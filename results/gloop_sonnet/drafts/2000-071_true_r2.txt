**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford's Denial of the Club's Application Did Not Violate the Free Speech Clause.**

Milford's Community Use Policy opens the school after hours for "social, civic and recreational meetings" but expressly withholds the forum from "religious purposes." This is a subject-matter line, drawn without regard to the theology of any particular sect, and it is precisely the kind of even-handed rule this Court has approved when government declines to make its property available for worship or proselytizing generally, rather than singling out one faith for disfavor. That distinguishes this case from *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), where Rhode Island's counsel conceded that "a religious service of Jehovah's Witnesses is treated differently than a religious service of other sects," and this Court explained: "That amounts to the state preferring some religious groups over this one." Milford's Policy draws no such distinction among sects; it excludes worship and religious instruction by any group, while allowing groups such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club to use the building for secular civic activities.

Nor does this case resemble *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), which presented the question whether a permit scheme was administered "so as to deny all applications made by religious organizations to hold religious meetings and deliver religious talks in the parks of Portsmouth." Milford's Policy is neutral on its face and in application; it was not adopted or enforced to target the Club's denomination or any other.

Respondent does not dispute that religious speech generally may not be excluded from a forum simply because it is religious. But a school need not convert its limited civic forum into a vehicle for religious instruction and evangelization directed at young children. This Court's approval of the released-time arrangement in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), turned on the fact that the program at issue "involves neither religious instruction in public school classrooms nor the expenditure of public funds," and that "[a]ll costs, including the application blanks, are paid by the religious organizations." Here, by contrast, the Club sought to conduct prayer and religious instruction inside the schoolhouse itself, minutes after the compulsory school day ends, before an audience of six-to-twelve-year-olds. Milford was entitled to decline to host that activity without engaging in viewpoint discrimination; it simply declined to make the schoolhouse a site for religious worship, for any group.

**II. Milford's Refusal Was, In Any Event, Justified by Its Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.**

Even if this Court concludes that Milford's Policy restricts the Club's speech, that restriction is justified by the interest schools have long been permitted to protect: keeping the schoolhouse free from the appearance that government sponsors religious indoctrination of children. In *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), this Court confronted a program in which "religious teachers, employed by private religious groups, were permitted to come weekly into the school buildings during the regular hours set apart for secular teaching, and then and there for a period of thirty minutes substitute their religious teaching for the secular education provided under the compulsory education law." The vice identified was that the instruction occurred within the schoolhouse itself, associated in young students' minds with the machinery of compulsory public education.

The same concern is present here. The Club's own lesson materials, part of the record below, direct leaders to tell unsaved children "[y]ou will be given an opportunity later in class today to believe on Jesus," and to invite them to pray using the words, "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved" (Acts 16:31). 202 F.3d 502, 509 (2d Cir. 2000). Elementary-school children attending a Club meeting held in their own classrooms, immediately after the school day, reasonably could perceive the school as endorsing that message.

This Court reinforced the same concern in *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), holding on the record there presented that "the practices at issue and the laws requiring them are unconstitutional under the Establishment Clause." The common thread running through *McCollum* and *Schempp* is that the schoolhouse itself must not become the situs of religious exercises conducted under circumstances suggesting official sanction.

This Court's decisions permitting indirect aid confirm the distinction. In *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370 (1930), the Court upheld the loan of secular textbooks to children attending religious schools because, as the state court had explained and this Court accepted, "the schools, however, are not the beneficiaries of these appropriations." The aid flowed to children, not to the religious enterprise. Similarly, in *Board of Education v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), this Court considered a New York statute lending textbooks to students in religious and secular schools alike. What distinguishes both cases from this one is that neither involved converting the schoolhouse itself, during hours associated with the school day, into the site of the religious activity. Milford's schoolhouse, used by the Club for prayer and religious instruction of young children within an hour of the closing bell, presents exactly the risk of perceived endorsement that *McCollum* and *Schempp* identified and that the child-benefit cases like *Cochran* and *Allen* were careful to avoid. See also Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947) (discussing the separation the Establishment Clause requires between government and religious exercise).

Milford's judgment that the Establishment Clause counseled exclusion of the Club's worship-centered activity from its schoolhouse was reasonable, neutral, and consistent with this Court's teaching that public schools may not become vehicles for religious indoctrination of the young. The judgment below should be affirmed.
