**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in interpreting the jury’s verdict as a denial of recovery to Unit Drilling Company, despite the jury’s explicit award of damages on Form 1 and a set-off on Form 3. The court’s interpretation—subtracting the set-off from the damages—was legally incorrect and violated the jury’s clear intent, as evidenced by the verdict’s arithmetic and the structure of the instructions. Moreover, the court’s refusal to clarify the ambiguous verdict deprived Unit of its constitutional right to a meaningful jury determination. The error was not waived, and the judgment must be reversed.

**I. The District Court’s Interpretation of the Verdict Was Legally Incorrect and Contrary to the Jury’s Intent**

The jury returned a verdict on Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 in damages and on Form 3 awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The district court concluded that because the set-off exceeded the damages, Unit recovered nothing. This interpretation misapprehends the nature of a set-off and the jury’s role in applying it. A set-off is not an independent damage award; it is a reduction of a plaintiff’s recovery. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 374 (1948) (“A set-off is a counterclaim which reduces the amount of the plaintiff’s recovery.”). The jury was instructed that if it found Enron had breached, it could award Unit damages up to $157,262.36, but that if Unit had only substantially performed, it must reduce that amount by the cost to Enron of correcting defects. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1—$86,494.30 less than the full invoice amount—demonstrates that it applied the set-off to Unit’s damages. The jury did not award Enron damages; it awarded Enron a set-off, which is a procedural mechanism to offset a plaintiff’s recovery, not a separate claim for damages.

The district court’s method of subtracting the set-off from the damages effectively nullified the jury’s award. But the jury did not award Enron damages; it awarded a set-off. The proper interpretation is that the jury awarded Unit $70,768.02 *after* applying the set-off. The court’s interpretation would only be valid if the jury had awarded Enron damages *in addition* to Unit’s damages, which it did not. The verdict forms were designed to allow the jury to determine liability and then apply the set-off in a single calculation. The jury’s arithmetic—$157,262.36 minus $86,494.30 equals $70,768.02—confirms that the jury intended to reduce Unit’s recovery by the cost of repairs, not to award Enron a separate sum. The court’s interpretation renders the verdict meaningless and contradicts the jury’s own calculation.

**II. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Clarify the Ambiguous Verdict**

Unit timely requested that the court question the jury to clarify the verdict before discharging it. Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(b) provides that “if the jury returns a general verdict and the court finds that the verdict is ambiguous, the court may direct the jury to return a special verdict or may ask the jury to clarify its general verdict.” The Tenth Circuit has held that “a trial court must permit the jury to clarify its verdict when the verdict is ambiguous.” *In re Estate of Hensley*, 87 F.3d 1145, 1150 (10th Cir. 1996). Here, the verdict was ambiguous: the jury awarded Unit damages on Form 1 and a set-off on Form 3, but the court’s interpretation rendered the verdict inconsistent with the jury’s own arithmetic. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1, which exactly equals the unpaid invoice amount minus the set-off, strongly suggests that the jury intended to apply the set-off to Unit’s damages. The court’s refusal to allow clarification deprived Unit of its right to have the jury’s intent honored.

The district court’s refusal was not harmless. The jury’s intent was not self-evident. The verdict forms were complex, and the jury was instructed to consider set-off only if it found a breach. The court’s failure to allow clarification left the jury’s intent unresolved. *See Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (“The right to a jury trial is fundamental, and the jury’s verdict must be given effect when possible.”). The court’s interpretation, which ignored the jury’s own calculation, violated this principle.

**III. The Jury Instructions Were Not So Confusing as to Require Reversal, and Unit Did Not Waive Its Right to Challenge the Verdict Interpretation**

Unit did not object to the instructions on the ground that they permitted the jury to award a set-off independently of damages. However, the failure to object to the instructions does not waive the right to challenge the court’s interpretation of the verdict. *See Fed. R. Civ. P. 51*; *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977). The error here is not in the instructions but in the court’s interpretation of the verdict. The instructions were not so confusing as to require reversal. The jury was told that if it found a breach, it could award damages up to $157,262.36, but that if Unit had only substantially performed, it must reduce that amount by the cost of correcting defects. The jury’s award of $70,768.02—precisely the amount after subtracting the set-off—shows that it applied the instructions correctly. The court’s error was in interpreting the verdict, not in instructing the jury.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in interpreting the jury’s verdict as a denial of recovery. The jury clearly intended to award Unit $70,768.02 after applying the set-off. The court’s refusal to clarify the verdict deprived Unit of its right to a meaningful jury determination. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for entry of judgment in Unit’s favor in the amount of $70,768
