**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 authorizes punitive damages only when a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This standard, drawn from the common-law tradition governing exemplary damages, requires proof of conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous,” not merely intentional discrimination.

At common law, punitive damages were never awarded for every intentional tort. As the Court explained in *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), such awards were reserved for cases in which the defendant’s conduct was “outrageous, because of the defendant’s evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others.” *Id.* at 46 (quoting Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979)). The same limitation appears in the legislative history of § 1981a. The House Report stated that the provision “demands of plaintiff ‘an even higher standard’ than simple proof of intentional discrimination.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 72 (1991). Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum likewise limited recovery to “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991).

Every circuit to address the question under either § 1981 or § 1981a has required this additional showing. *See, e.g.*, *Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 832 F.2d 194, 205-06 (1st Cir. 1987) (requiring “outrageous” conduct); *Yarbrough v. Tower Oldsmobile, Inc.*, 789 F.2d 508, 514 (7th Cir. 1986) (same); *Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy Health Corp.*, 816 F.2d 1104, 1109 (6th Cir. 1987) (same). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary view—that proof of intentional discrimination alone suffices—contradicts this uniform authority and the statute’s text.

Here, the record contains no evidence that ADA’s promotion decision was accompanied by the requisite outrageous conduct. The jury found only that the employer’s stated reasons were pretextual. That finding establishes intentional discrimination, but nothing more. Because Kolstad failed to prove conduct that was independently egregious or outrageous, the district court correctly refused to submit the punitive-damages issue to the jury. The judgment should be affirmed.
