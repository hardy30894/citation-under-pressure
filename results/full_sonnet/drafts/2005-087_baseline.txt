# ARGUMENT

Arizona's rule channeling evidence of mental disease and capacity to the insanity defense—rather than permitting it as freestanding rebuttal of the mens rea element—does not violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Constitution affords States broad latitude to define criminal responsibility, allocate the burdens of proving it, and structure the manner in which mental-disease evidence is received. Arizona's rule is a reasonable exercise of that latitude, fully consistent with this Court's precedents.

## I. States Possess Wide Latitude Under the Due Process Clause to Define Criminal Responsibility and Structure the Insanity Defense.

This Court has "long been reluctant to disturb a State's decision as to how to allocate the burdens of proof" concerning mental disease, and has recognized that "the definition of the elements of a criminal offense is entrusted to the legislature, particularly in the case of a State's efforts to distinguish between guilt and innocence." *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977). In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that "the science of psychiatry has not reached a point where its learning would compel us to require the states to eliminate the right and wrong test from their criminal law." *Id.* at 800–801. The Court further explained that adoption of any particular test of criminal responsibility "is not a matter of fundamental principle" mandated by due process. *Id.* at 801.

This deference reflects the plurality's teaching in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), that "formulation of a constitutional rule" governing criminal responsibility "would reduce, if not eliminate, the tentative, experimental, and correctible" quality of American criminal law, which depends on "a continuing process of adjustment" by the States. *Id.* at 536–37. Because "the definition of mental illness or disease is not static," it is for the States, "and not this Court, to resolve" how mental-disease evidence bears on guilt. *Id.* at 536.

*Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), confirms that a state procedural rule allocating burdens or channeling evidence in the criminal-responsibility context violates due process only if it "offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 445 (internal quotation marks omitted). Arizona's rule offends no such principle; to the contrary, "the processes for determining a defendant's mental state at the time of the offense" have varied widely among the States. *Id.* at 445–46.

## II. Arizona's Channeling Rule Is Analogous to, and No Less Permissible Than, the Evidentiary Restriction Upheld in *Montana v. Egelhoff*.

In *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), this Court upheld a Montana statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication in determining mens rea, even though such evidence was relevant to whether the defendant actually possessed the required mental state. The plurality explained that "the rule excluding consideration of voluntary intoxication has gained wide acceptance" and reflects a legitimate judgment that "the ‘cognitive incapacity' produced by voluntary intoxication does not exculpate." *Id.* at 56 (plurality opinion). The Court reasoned that a State may reasonably conclude that certain categories of mental-state evidence should be considered only through a defined doctrinal channel, without offending due process, because "the potentially exculpatory value of evidence of voluntary intoxication is a matter that has divided courts and legislatures" and is not settled by "fundamental principle." *Id.* at 56.

Arizona's rule reflects an analogous judgment: capacity evidence bearing on mental disease is channeled to the insanity defense—where it is tested against the *M'Naghten*-derived standard and the defendant bears the burden—rather than treated as an open-ended, freestanding challenge to mens rea untethered from any doctrinal framework. Just as Montana could conclude that intoxication evidence should be confined to a specific doctrinal use, Arizona may conclude that mental-disease evidence is more reliably and fairly evaluated within the structured insanity inquiry, given persistent and unresolved concerns about the reliability of psychiatric testimony offered to negate specific mental states directly. Because reasonable minds, and reasonable legislatures, continue to disagree about how such evidence should bear on culpability, this is precisely the area where, as in *Egelhoff*, the Court should defer to the State's judgment.

## III. Clark Received a Meaningful Opportunity to Present a Defense, Satisfying Due Process.

Due process guarantees a "meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense," *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986), but that guarantee is not unlimited; in *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), this Court struck down an evidentiary rule only because it excluded evidence bearing "persuasive assurances of trustworthiness" and was applied in a manner that left the defendant no other avenue for presenting reliable, exculpatory proof. *Id.* at 302. Clark suffered no such deprivation. He introduced extensive expert testimony regarding his mental illness and used it fully in support of his insanity defense—the very channel Arizona law provides for such evidence. Unlike *Chambers*, Clark was not denied any opportunity to present his evidence; he was simply required to present it within the structure Arizona's law establishes for mental-disease evidence. That structure comports with due process, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
