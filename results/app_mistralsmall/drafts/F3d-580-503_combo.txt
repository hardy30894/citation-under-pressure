**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY DENYING APPELLANT’S MOTION FOR A CONTINUANCE AND EXPERT WITNESS TO TEST THE GOVERNMENT’S FINGERPRINT EVIDENCE.

A. The denial of a continuance violated appellant’s Sixth Amendment right to a fair trial and due process.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “[t]he right to a speedy and public trial is a fundamental right of every accused person.” *Klopfer v. North Carolina*, 386 U.S. 213, 223 (1967) (citing *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948)). A trial court’s denial of a continuance may violate due process where it “prevent[s] the accused from having a fair trial or presenting his defense.” *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964). In *Ungar*, the Court held that a continuance should be granted where “the denial of a continuance ... makes it impossible for the accused to obtain counsel of his own choosing or to prepare his defense.” *Id.* at 589. Here, the district court denied appellant’s motion for a continuance filed just five days before trial, despite appellant’s showing that he needed additional time to secure an expert to challenge the government’s fingerprint evidence. This denial deprived appellant of the opportunity to meaningfully test the government’s forensic claims, thereby undermining his ability to present a full defense. *See id.*; *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 58 (1932) (holding that due process requires that an accused be afforded a reasonable opportunity to prepare his defense).

B. The denial of appellant’s motion for a fingerprint expert violated his Sixth Amendment right to compulsory process and to present a defense.

The Supreme Court has held that the Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to present witnesses in his favor and to compel their attendance at trial. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). This right includes the ability to present expert testimony to challenge the government’s forensic evidence. *See id.* at 23; *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (recognizing that due process may require the admission of expert testimony to rebut forensic evidence). Here, the government’s case relied heavily on the absence of fingerprints on the recovered evidence, and appellant sought an expert to test whether latent prints could have been lifted from the materials in question. The district court’s denial of this motion, made only days before trial, deprived appellant of the ability to test the government’s forensic claims and present a meaningful defense. *See Washington*, 388 U.S. at 19; *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. at 273.

C. The district court’s denial of appellant’s motion for a continuance and expert witness was an abuse of discretion.

The Seventh Circuit has held that a district court’s denial of a continuance is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Dellinger*, 472 F.2d 340, 344 (7th Cir. 1972) (citing *Ungar*, 376 U.S. at 589). A denial is an abuse of discretion where it “results in fundamental unfairness.” *Id.* Here, the district court’s denial of appellant’s motion for a continuance and expert witness, made only five days before trial, resulted in fundamental unfairness by depriving appellant of the opportunity to test the government’s forensic evidence. *See id.*; *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 58. The district court’s reliance on the lateness of the motion was misplaced, as appellant had no prior notice that the government would rely on fingerprint evidence until the filing of its pretrial disclosures. *See United States v. Augenblick*, 393 U.S. 348, 356 (1969) (holding that a continuance may be warranted where a party receives late notice of evidence).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING APPELLANT’S MOTION FOR A JUDGMENT OF ACQUITTAL ON THE FIREARM POSSESSION COUNTS.

A. The evidence was insufficient to support appellant’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm by a felon.

The Supreme Court has held that “[t]he standard for reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (emphasis in original). Here, the evidence showed that appellant fled from police and discarded his jacket, which contained the firearm, before he was apprehended. The government did not present evidence that appellant had constructive possession of the firearm at the time of his arrest. *See United States v. Manzella*, 791 F.2d 124, 127 (7th Cir. 1986) (holding that constructive possession requires a showing of dominion and control). The district court erred in denying appellant’s motion for a judgment of acquittal on this count, as the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction. *See Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319.

B. The evidence was insufficient to support appellant’s conviction for possession of a firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime.

The Supreme Court has held that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c) requires proof that the defendant “actively employed” the firearm in relation to the drug trafficking crime. *Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137, 143 (1995). Here, the government did not present evidence that appellant actively employed the firearm in furtherance of the drug trafficking crime. The evidence showed only that the firearm was found in appellant’s jacket, which he had discarded before his arrest. The district court erred in denying appellant’s motion for a judgment of acquittal on this count, as the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction. *See id.* at 143; *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319.
