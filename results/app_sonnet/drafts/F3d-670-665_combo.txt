ARGUMENT

I. The Writ of Habeas Corpus Has Always Functioned as an Equitable Remedy Whose Availability Turns on the Demands of Justice Rather Than Mechanical Rules.

Since its earliest exercise of jurisdiction, the Supreme Court has treated habeas corpus not as a creature of rigid procedural forfeiture but as "the fundamental instrument for safeguarding individual freedom against arbitrary and lawless state action." Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 401 (1963). The Court explained that "conventional notions of finality in criminal litigation cannot be permitted to defeat the manifest federal policy that federal constitutional rights of personal liberty shall not be denied without the fullest opportunity for plenary federal judicial review." Id. at 424. That principle applies whether the procedural bar is a failure to exhaust, as in Fay, or, as here, the running of a limitations period.

The Court reinforced this equitable understanding in Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), holding that even res judicata yields in habeas proceedings when "the ends of justice would ... be served by permitting the redetermination of the merits." Id. at 17. And in Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), the Court held that federal courts sitting in habeas retain an independent duty to examine the record for constitutional violation regardless of procedural regularity below. This tradition confirms that habeas is designed to reach manifest injustice — precisely what a credible claim of actual innocence represents.

II. A Credible Claim of Actual Innocence Implicates the Fundamental-Miscarriage-of-Justice Principle That Has Long Justified Habeas Relief Independent of Procedural Compliance.

Courts long ago recognized that punishing an innocent man is the paradigmatic miscarriage of justice habeas exists to correct. In Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), the Court held that a federal court could look behind a state conviction obtained through a fundamentally corrupted process, for no "perfection in the machinery for correction" could excuse allowing an unjust conviction to stand. Id. at 91. The same concern animates Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103 (1935), where the Court held that a conviction procured through "a deliberate deception of court and jury by the presentation of testimony known to be perjured" offends due process regardless of the procedural posture in which the claim arises. Id. at 112. Perkins's claim — that the prosecution's own eyewitness is the true killer — sits squarely within this tradition.

Nor is newly discovered evidence of innocence a novel basis for departing from ordinary procedural rules. In Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), the Court held that a federal habeas court must hold an evidentiary hearing where the petitioner presents "newly discovered evidence" bearing on guilt not adequately considered below. Id. at 312-13. That such evidence warrants special accommodation is the settled premise underlying decades of habeas practice.

III. The Equitable Doctrine of Tolling Does Not Impose a Freestanding Diligence Requirement Where the Underlying Equity Is Satisfied by a Showing of Actual Innocence.

Equitable tolling has never been a mechanical inquiry limited to diligence alone. As the Court explained in Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946), the doctrine that a limitations period may be tolled "is read into every federal statute of limitations" because "equity eschews mechanical rules; it depends on flexibility." Id. at 396. Diligence is one factor in that flexible calculus — not an independent precondition foreclosing relief whenever another overriding equity, such as innocence, is present.

This flexible approach traces to Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874), where the Court held that courts of equity will not permit a limitations defense "to be used as a means of accomplishing a fraud" and will look past delay when other circumstances render enforcement of the bar unconscionable. Id. at 349-50. Likewise, in Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal, 359 U.S. 231 (1959), the Court tolled a statutory period notwithstanding the petitioner's delay because the equities of the case, not a rigid diligence checklist, controlled the outcome. Id. at 232-33.

These authorities demonstrate that diligence has always been one equitable consideration among several, never an absolute gatekeeper. The Warden's position — that a credible, unrebutted showing of actual innocence must be disregarded because Perkins did not file sooner — inverts this equitable tradition. The severity of the underlying wrong, an innocent man's continued incarceration, is itself the controlling equity. Just as Bailey and Glus refused to let procedural delay immunize a deeper injustice, this Court should refuse to let the passage of time immunize a credible claim of actual innocence from merits review.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, warrants equitable tolling of AEDPA's statute of limitations, and should remand for consideration of Perkins's petition on the merits.
