The district court erred in admitting the opinion of American Steamship’s witness on the cause of the line’s parting. Expert testimony is admissible only when the subject matter “is not within the common knowledge of the average man” and the witness possesses “special skill or knowledge” that will assist the trier of fact. *United States Smelting Co. v. Parry*, 166 F. 407, 415 (8th Cir. 1909). The witness’s conclusion rested solely on an examination of the parted line after the fact and required no application of specialized training; it therefore constituted nothing more than a lay opinion on a matter within the jury’s ordinary competence. *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923).

The district court further erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness. A vessel is unseaworthy when its “appurtenances” are not “reasonably fit for their intended use.” *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 99 (1944). The undisputed evidence established that the line had been stored in a heated compartment, then rigged in sub-zero temperatures on a deck encrusted with ice, without any opportunity to inspect or replace it, and that the boom bearings had frozen because the crew had failed to substitute winter grease. These conditions rendered the bos’n’s-chair apparatus unfit for its intended purpose as a matter of law. *The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903); *Carlisle Packing Co. v. Sandanger*, 259 U.S. 255, 259 (1922). The Jones Act does not relieve the shipowner of this absolute duty. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960).

Because the erroneous admission of the non-expert opinion and the refusal to direct a verdict on unseaworthiness together infected the jury’s apportionment of fault, the judgment must be vacated and the cause remanded for a new trial.
