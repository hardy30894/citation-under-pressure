# ARGUMENT

## THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT RECOGNIZE A "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION ABSENT ALLEGED MEMBERSHIP IN A CLASS

The Equal Protection Clause commands that "[n]o State shall... deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This constitutional guarantee has always been understood as a *relational* protection—one that safeguards individuals from being treated differently than similarly situated members of an identifiable class. Respondent's theory, adopted by the Seventh Circuit below, would sever equal protection from its textual and doctrinal foundations by permitting suit whenever a single individual believes a government official acted with subjective ill will, regardless of any classification whatsoever.

### A. The Text and History of the Equal Protection Clause Require Classification

This Court's foundational equal protection jurisprudence has consistently analyzed governmental action in terms of classifications and the groups they burden. In *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), and later, in repudiating it, *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), the Court's entire analytical framework turned on the treatment of a *class*—there, a racial class—rather than the treatment of a solitary plaintiff divorced from any group identity. *Brown* held that segregating "children in public schools solely on the basis of race" violated equal protection, 347 U.S. at 495, precisely because the challenged classification separated one class of persons from another.

Similarly, in *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1 (1973), this Court explained that equal protection analysis requires identifying "some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation," *id.* at 17, language that presupposes a comparison between defined groups of similarly situated persons. The very structure of tiered scrutiny—strict scrutiny for suspect classifications, intermediate scrutiny for quasi-suspect classifications, and rational basis review for all other classifications, *see City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985)—depends upon the existence of a class being burdened or benefited.

### B. This Court Has Never Recognized a "Class of One" Claim

Respondent cannot point to any decision of this Court sanctioning an equal protection claim untethered from class membership. To the contrary, in *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), this Court reaffirmed that equal protection claims require proof that the government "selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part 'because of,' not merely 'in spite of,' its adverse effects upon an identifiable group," *id.* at 279. The Court's analysis presupposed the existence of a *group*—there, women—against whom discrimination was alleged.

Likewise, in *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), this Court held that a plaintiff alleging an equal protection violation must demonstrate discriminatory purpose directed at a cognizable class, explaining that disproportionate impact alone is insufficient absent proof that the action was "motivated by a racially discriminatory purpose," *id.* at 240. The entire discriminatory-purpose inquiry contemplated by *Davis* is meaningless without a class serving as the comparator.

### C. The Seventh Circuit's "Spite" Standard Improperly Converts Ordinary Tort and Contract Disputes into Constitutional Claims

The court of appeals' holding—that a plaintiff states an equal protection claim by alleging that government action was a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective"—collapses the distinction between garden-variety governmental decisionmaking and constitutional violation. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against transforming ordinary regulatory disputes into constitutional litigation. In *Parratt v. Taylor*, 451 U.S. 527 (1981), the Court emphasized that the Fourteenth Amendment was not intended to supplant traditional remedies available under state tort law, cautioning against reading constitutional provisions to encompass injuries adequately redressed through other legal avenues.

Moreover, in *Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520 (1979), this Court underscored the danger of permitting federal courts, under the guise of constitutional review, to second-guess the ordinary discretionary judgments of state and local officials. Extending equal protection to single-plaintiff claims premised solely on allegations of subjective "spite" would invite exactly this kind of judicial oversight into countless local zoning, permitting, and licensing decisions—decisions that Olech's own dispute over an easement requirement plainly resembles.

### D. Rational Basis Review Presupposes a Classification to Evaluate

Even under the most deferential tier of equal protection scrutiny, this Court's decisions confirm that some classification must exist for a court to assess. In *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court upheld a traffic regulation distinguishing between types of advertising, explaining that the Equal Protection Clause "does not require that things different in fact... be treated in law as though they were the same," *id.* at 110. That reasoning is incoherent without two classes to compare. Absent an alleged class, there is no metric by which a court can determine whether "things different in fact" have been improperly equated or whether similarly situated persons have been treated differently.

Respondent's failure to allege membership in any class—she claims only that *she individually* was treated differently from unspecified other property owners—asks this Court to invent a standard wholly foreign to the class-based framework that has governed equal protection jurisprudence since *Plessy* and *Brown*.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the Equal Protection Clause does not recognize a "class of one" cause of action, and should reverse the judgment of the Seventh Circuit.
