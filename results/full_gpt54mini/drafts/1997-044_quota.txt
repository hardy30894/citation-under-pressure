The Bankruptcy Code’s discharge protects the “honest but unfortunate debtor,” not one who has committed fraud. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). Section 523(a)(2)(A) therefore withholds discharge from “any debt” for money or property obtained by “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court should affirm that the phrase “any debt” is broad enough to encompass all liability arising from the fraud—not merely the out-of-pocket amount wrongfully obtained.

First, the statutory text controls. Congress used the expansive term “any debt,” not “any compensatory debt” or “the amount obtained by fraud.” In Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213 (1998), this Court held that once money is obtained by fraud, “any debt” arising from that fraud is nondischargeable. Id. at 218-19. The Court reasoned that the phrase “to the extent obtained by” modifies the money, property, services, or credit that was fraudulently acquired, not the word “debt.” Id. at 218. Thus, “any debt” includes treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs when those liabilities flow from the fraud. Id. at 223.

That reading is consistent with the structure of § 523. When Congress wanted to limit nondischargeability to particular kinds of liability, it did so expressly. See, e.g., 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(7) (fines, penalties, or forfeitures “payable to and for the benefit of a governmental unit”); 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(6) (debts for “willful and malicious injury”). Section 523(a)(2)(A) contains no comparable limitation. In Brown v. Felsen, 442 U.S. 127 (1979), this Court explained that discharge exceptions are designed to prevent dishonest debtors from escaping the consequences of fraud through bankruptcy. Id. at 138-39. And in Grogan, the Court reaffirmed that the Bankruptcy Code balances a fresh start against the need to deny discharge to fraudsters. 498 U.S. at 286-87.

Second, the liability here was not some independent punishment untethered to the fraud; it was the direct measure of the legal consequences of Cohen’s fraudulent conduct. In Cohen, this Court rejected the argument that punitive or exemplary components fall outside § 523(a)(2)(A) simply because they exceed the amount of money initially obtained. 523 U.S. at 223. The debt “arose from” the fraud and therefore was nondischargeable in full. Id. at 218-19, 223. That conclusion accords with Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1878), which distinguished technical from actual fraud, and with Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59 (1995), which confirmed that § 523(a)(2)(A) targets common-law fraud principles. See also Archer v. Warner, 538 U.S. 314, 321-23 (2003) (a settlement debt remains subject to § 523(a)(2)(A) when it replaces a fraud claim; bankruptcy cannot convert fraud into dischargeable contract liability).

Third, allowing discharge of fraud-based damages would create a perverse incentive: a debtor could keep the fruits of fraud while using bankruptcy to avoid the rest of the judgment. That would undermine the Code’s remedial purpose. In Cohen, this Court recognized that a broader reading of “debt” prevents a wrongdoer from obtaining a “free pass” on the full legal consequences of fraud. 523 U.S. at 223. Lower courts have likewise held that attorney’s fees and costs attached to fraud judgments are nondischargeable when state law makes them part of the liability. See, e.g., In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 678-80 (11th Cir. 1993); In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 199-200 (9th Cir. 1991); In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995).

Finally, petitioner’s reading would nullify the practical effect of anti-fraud statutes. Here, New Jersey law imposed treble damages and fees as part of the fraud remedy. The Bankruptcy Code does not authorize debtors to sever the compensatory portion from the statutory consequences of their misconduct. As Cohen makes clear, once the debt is “for” money obtained by fraud, the whole liability is excepted from discharge. 523 U.S. at 223.

The judgment should be affirmed.
