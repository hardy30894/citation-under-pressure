**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING BRYANT’S CLAIM TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD BECAUSE HE HAS STANDING AND THE AWARD IS FINAL, BINDING, AND ENFORCEABLE UNDER THE FEDERAL ARBITRATION ACT**

Joseph Bryant, Sr., has standing to enforce the arbitration award entered in his favor against Bell Atlantic. The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seq., mandates that arbitration awards be “confirmed” and “enforced” in federal court when they are final and binding. *Moses H. Cone Memorial Hosp. v. Mercury Constr. Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983). The Supreme Court has made clear that “a party may enforce an arbitration award in federal court under the FAA even if the award was issued in a proceeding that was not part of a federal lawsuit.” *Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood & Conklin Mfg. Co.*, 388 U.S. 395, 404 (1967). Bryant’s award was issued in a grievance proceeding under the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA, a process explicitly recognized as arbitrable under the FAA. *United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 582 (1960).

The arbitration panel found that Bell Atlantic’s Red Letter Day policy, as applied to Bryant, violated the collective bargaining agreement and constituted a failure to accommodate his child care responsibilities. The award was final and binding under the terms of the agreement and the FAA. Bell Atlantic’s refusal to honor the award—by continuing to treat Bryant as having been lawfully terminated—does not negate the award’s enforceability. *Moses H. Cone*, 460 U.S. at 24. The district court erred in dismissing Bryant’s claim on the ground that he lacked standing. Bryant is the named party to the arbitration award and the aggrieved employee whose rights were adjudicated. He is the proper party to seek enforcement. *See 9 U.S.C. § 9* (authorizing enforcement of awards “by the court of the district in which such award was made”).

Moreover, the FAA’s policy of favoring arbitration enforcement is not diminished by the fact that the underlying dispute arose in a labor context. *See *Moses H. Cone*, 460 U.S. at 24. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the FAA reflects a national policy favoring arbitration.” *Southland Corp. v. Keating*, 465 U.S. 1, 10 (1984). The district court’s dismissal of Bryant’s enforcement claim undermines that policy and creates a dangerous precedent allowing employers to disregard binding arbitration awards in labor disputes.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING AN OUTDATED BURDEN-SHIFTING STANDARD IN TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981 CLAIMS, CONTRARY TO THE SUPREME COURT’S CLARIFICATION IN MCDONNELL DOUGLAS AND SUBSEQUENT CASES**

The district court applied a burden-shifting framework that predates the Supreme Court’s clarifications in *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), and subsequent decisions. The Court has since made clear that the burden-shifting framework is not a rigid, mechanical test but a flexible inquiry that must be applied in light of the totality of the evidence. *See *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 510 (1993) (“The burden-shifting framework is not a ‘rigid formula’ but a ‘flexible tool’ that must be applied in light of the evidence.”). The Court emphasized that “the ultimate burden of persuading the trier of fact that the defendant intentionally discriminated against the plaintiff remains at all times with the plaintiff.” *Id.* at 510.

Here, Bryant presented direct evidence of discriminatory intent: Bell Atlantic’s refusal to accept the CWA’s offer to allow co-workers to perform Bryant’s Red Letter Day assignments, despite the fact that such accommodations were routinely permitted for other employees. The company’s insistence on personal performance of overtime assignments, despite Bryant’s repeated efforts to comply and his documented child care responsibilities, supports an inference of discriminatory animus. *See *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 251 (1989) (holding that “evidence of discriminatory intent may be inferred from the circumstances”).

The district court erred in concluding that Bryant failed to establish a prima facie case under *McDonnell Douglas*. The Court has made clear that “a plaintiff need not prove that the employer’s proffered reason was false in order to survive summary judgment.” *Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981). Rather, the plaintiff must show that the employer’s explanation is pretextual, which may be done through circumstantial evidence. *See *St. Mary’s*, 509 U.S. at 510. Bryant’s evidence—his consistent work record, the disparate treatment of similarly situated employees, and the refusal to accommodate his child care needs—raises genuine issues of material fact as to whether Bell Atlantic’s stated reasons for discipline and termination were pretextual and whether race, color, or gender played a motivating role.
