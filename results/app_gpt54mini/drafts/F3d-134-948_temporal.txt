**Argument**

I. Selam Was Denied Compulsory Process When the Tribal Court Failed to Secure the Attendance of Available Defense Witnesses Necessary to His Defense.

The right to compulsory process is not a formality; it is “an essential attribute of the right to a fair trial.” *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). When a defendant is prevented from obtaining witnesses whose testimony is material and favorable, the conviction cannot stand. *Id.* at 23; *Funk v. United States*, 290 U.S. 371, 381-82 (1933).

Here, Selam did everything the record required of him to invoke that right. He supplied counsel with names of five potential witnesses; the court and counsel advised him of his right to subpoena witnesses; and yet the tribal proceedings ended with no defense witnesses at all. On these facts, the failure was not Selam’s. A defendant’s right to compulsory process is violated when the state denies him “the right to present his own witnesses to establish a defense.” *Washington*, 388 U.S. at 19. The right is especially important where, as here, the accused faces serious criminal penalties and the prosecution’s proof depends largely on the testimony of the complaining witnesses. See *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) (a fair proceeding requires an opportunity to defend with the “assistance of counsel and compulsory process for witnesses”).

The district court treated the absence of subpoenas as a waiver. But waiver of a fundamental right cannot be presumed from a silent record. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The government must show an intentional relinquishment of a known right. *Id.* There is no such showing here. Selam was advised generally that he could compel witnesses, but he was represented by a lay spokesperson, not trained counsel, and he identified witnesses whose testimony could have been central to his defense. The record does not show a knowing and intelligent decision to forgo compulsory process. *Id.*; *Agnello v. United States*, 269 U.S. 20, 33 (1925) (courts will not lightly infer relinquishment of constitutional protections).

Nor can the denial be excused as harmless. Where the accused is deprived of the means to present available defense evidence, the error goes to the very heart of the adversary process. *Washington*, 388 U.S. at 23; *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (recognizing the centrality of the right to present witnesses), but because later authority is unavailable, the same principle is rooted in earlier due process cases. See *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. at 273.

II. Selam Did Not Waive His Confrontation Claim, and the Admission of Out-of-Court Accusations Without Live Testimony Violated Fundamental Fairness.

Selam’s confrontation claim was properly before the federal habeas court because the record shows no intentional relinquishment of that right, and because the tribal proceedings themselves failed to provide the procedural protections necessary to make waiver meaningful. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464; *Agnello*, 269 U.S. at 33. The right of confrontation has long been recognized as an indispensable incident of a fair criminal trial. *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 406-07 (1965); *Douglas v. Alabama*, 380 U.S. 415, 418 (1965).

The second prosecution rested heavily on hearsay. The five-year-old victim did not testify; instead, the court received her accusations through a physician, her parents, and an investigator. That procedure deprived Selam of the core protections of confrontation: the opportunity to test perception, memory, narration, and sincerity through cross-examination. *Pointer*, 380 U.S. at 404-06; *Douglas*, 380 U.S. at 418-19. As the Court explained in *Pointer*, confrontation is “a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” 380 U.S. at 404.

The government may argue that Selam should have raised the confrontation issue first in the tribal court of appeals. But exhaustion doctrine does not authorize federal courts to treat a fundamental-rights claim as waived where the petitioner lacked a meaningful opportunity to litigate it in the earlier proceeding. See *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 118 (1944) (habeas is available where state corrective processes are absent or ineffective). In any event, waiver of confrontation must be knowing and intelligent; the record here contains no such waiver. *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464.

Finally, the use of testimonial hearsay in place of live testimony was especially prejudicial because the five-year-old’s statements were recounted by multiple witnesses, magnifying the risk of distortion. The Court has long rejected criminal convictions built on procedures that “offend those canons of decency and fairness which express the notions of justice of English-speaking peoples.” *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941). This case presents precisely that sort of unfairness.

**Conclusion**

Because Selam was denied compulsory process and because his confrontation rights were violated and not knowingly waived, the judgment should be reversed and the writ granted.
