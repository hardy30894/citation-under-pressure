**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve substantial government interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 120 S.Ct. 2480, 147 L.Ed.2d 597 (2000), squarely governs this case and forecloses the petitioners’ challenge. The Court should affirm the judgment below.

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROTECT PROXIMITY-BASED COMMUNICATIONS THAT INTERFERE WITH THE RIGHT TO ACCESS ABORTION CLINICS**

The First Amendment protects speech, but not speech that is so coercive or obstructive in its physical manifestation that it infringes upon the fundamental right of individuals to enter and exit healthcare facilities. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court in *Hill* recognized that “sidewalks and areas outside healthcare facilities are *quintessential* public forums,” yet upheld a statute prohibiting “any person from approaching within 8 feet of another person” who is entering or leaving a healthcare facility, unless the person is engaged in a consensual interaction. Id. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court emphasized that the law was “content-neutral,” “narrowly tailored,” and “designed to protect the right of access to healthcare facilities.” Id. at 716, 120 S.Ct. 2480.

Massachusetts’ law is no different in substance or purpose. It creates a fixed 35-foot buffer zone around entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities (RHCFs), prohibiting anyone from knowingly entering or remaining within that zone—except for those exempted under the statute. See Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b), (c). Like the Colorado law in *Hill*, the Massachusetts statute is content-neutral: it applies equally to all speech, regardless of viewpoint, and does not target pro-life or pro-choice messages. It is also viewpoint-neutral, as the exemptions do not favor one ideology over another. The law is not a prior restraint, nor does it suppress speech based on its message. Rather, it regulates the *time, place, and manner* of speech to protect the right of patients and staff to access healthcare without harassment or intimidation.

The petitioners’ claim that the buffer zone “chills” their speech is belied by the record. They continue to engage in speech—through signage, prayer, literature distribution, and verbal outreach—outside the buffer zones. At the Boston clinic, McCullen displays signs on her car; Zarrella prays aloud; Cadin holds signs; and Smith uses a loudspeaker to amplify group prayers. At Worcester and Springfield, Bashour and Clark display signs and pray. Shea, at Springfield, continues to hold a sign reading “They’re Killing Babies Here.” These activities occur *outside* the buffer zones and are fully protected. The law does not prohibit speech; it merely limits where it may occur.

**II. THE LAW IS NARROWLY TAILORED TO SERVE SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENT INTERESTS**

The Massachusetts legislature has a compelling interest in ensuring the safe and unimpeded access of patients to reproductive health care facilities. The record demonstrates that without such a buffer zone, individuals seeking abortion services have been subjected to harassment, intimidation, and obstruction. The law is narrowly tailored to serve that interest. It applies only to the immediate vicinity of entrances, exits, and driveways—precisely where access is most vulnerable. It does not prohibit all speech near clinics; it merely prevents individuals from approaching patients within 35 feet of a facility’s entry points.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld similar regulations. In *Hill*, the Court upheld a 8-foot buffer zone, noting that “the State’s interest in protecting access to healthcare facilities is substantial.” 530 U.S. at 716, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court emphasized that “the right to access a healthcare facility is not a right to be free from all speech, but a right to be free from obstruction and intimidation.” Id. The Massachusetts law, which creates a larger buffer zone than *Hill*, is even more precisely tailored to that interest. It does not ban all speech; it only restricts proximity-based communication that may interfere with access.

The petitioners argue that the law is overbroad because it prevents them from speaking at “a normal conversational distance.” But the First Amendment does not guarantee a right to speak at a specific physical distance. *Hill* made clear that “the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech to ensure that the right of access is not compromised.” 530 U.S. at 716, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The law here does not prevent the petitioners from communicating; it only requires them to do so from a distance. They may still distribute literature, display signs, pray, and speak—just not within 35 feet of a patient’s path to the clinic.

**III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RE-LITIGATION OF THE FACIAL CHALLENGE**

The First Circuit correctly applied the law of the case doctrine. In *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009), this court held that the Massachusetts buffer zone law was a valid time-place-manner regulation, content-neutral, and consistent with *Hill v. Colorado*. The Supreme Court denied certiorari, 130 S.Ct. 1881, 176 L.Ed.2d 362 (2010), thereby affirming the precedent. The petitioners now seek to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law—despite having lost on that issue in the same case just five years earlier.
