# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) commands that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. That word is not a suggestion; it is, in this Court's words, "the language of command." *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Congress's choice of mandatory phrasing must be enforced as written, for "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Nothing in Section 1407(a) excuses the remand duty when a transferee judge decides, under the wholly separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be convenient. The statute's chosen words supply the "persuasive evidence of the purpose" that governs here: remand occurs "at or before the conclusion" of pretrial proceedings—not "unless the transferee court prefers otherwise." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute. A court may not nullify a mandatory legislative command simply because it judges that departure would serve efficiency or convenience. The remedy for whatever hardship the remand requirement may occasionally impose lies with Congress, not with ad hoc judicial revision of the text.

## II. Because Congress specifically confined multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve distinct purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a "federal judicial housekeeping measure," generally applicable to ordinary civil litigation and "intended, subject to the transferee district court's discretion, to protect litigants, witnesses, and the public against unnecessary inconvenience and expense." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636–37 (1964). It operates only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff at the outset—a limitation this Court has enforced strictly, even against arguments grounded in convenience or party consent. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the remand command. The general "convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice" standard that governs ordinary venue transfer, *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960), cannot be read to swallow the specific temporal limit Congress placed on multidistrict transfer.

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. As this Court has explained, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; it cannot be "controlled or nullified" by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a)—a statute that says nothing whatever about multidistrict litigation. *Id.*

Nor has this Court ever understood Section 1404(a) to authorize a court to displace a plaintiff's chosen forum for trial merely because the case happens to be before it for another, limited purpose. Section 1404(a) is "a federal statute dealing with the placement of litigation in the federal courts," designed for ordinary venue disputes between forums in which the action could independently have been brought—not a vehicle by which a transferee court may entrench itself in a case Congress committed to it solely for pretrial coordination. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting only by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The court below erred in treating this conflict as unresolved.

The Ninth Circuit acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on a Panel-promulgated rule and non-binding circuit precedent that had, by its own account, developed "willy-nilly" over a quarter century. But an administrative rule cannot rewrite the words Congress enacted, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command merely because compliance proves inconvenient in a given case. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, the "sole function" of a court confronting plain statutory text is to enforce it—not to weigh policy trade-offs that Congress itself has already resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
