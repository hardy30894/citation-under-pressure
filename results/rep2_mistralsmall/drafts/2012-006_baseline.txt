**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond the Statute of Limitations**

The government must prove every element of a conspiracy charge beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *Griffin v. United States*, 502 U.S. 46, 55 (1991) ("[E]very essential element of the offense must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt."). Withdrawal from a conspiracy does not shift the burden to the defendant to disprove participation; rather, it is an affirmative defense that the defendant must raise and prove by a preponderance of the evidence. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1233 (7th Cir. 1981) ("[W]ithdrawal is an affirmative defense which the defendant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence."); *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 444 (1978) (burden of proving an affirmative defense is on the defendant).

The government’s argument that withdrawal shifts the burden to disprove participation misreads the law. The Supreme Court has made clear that the government bears the burden of proving all elements of the offense, including the duration of the conspiracy. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995) ("[A]ll of the elements of an offense, including the mens rea and actus reus, must be established by the Government."). The government cannot avoid this burden by arguing that a defendant’s withdrawal relieves it of proving the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *See United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 16 (1994) (holding that the government must prove all elements of a conspiracy charge, including agreement and overt act, beyond a reasonable doubt).

**II. The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden to Prove Continuing Conspiracy**

The jury must be instructed on the government’s burden to prove the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 522 ("[T]he jury must find [every element] beyond a reasonable doubt."). Failure to instruct the jury on this burden is reversible error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 731 (1993) ("A constitutional error occurs, and the trial result is impaired, if the jury is not properly instructed on the burden of proof.").

Here, the jury was not instructed that the government bore the burden of proving the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. This omission violated the Fifth and Sixth Amendments by relieving the government of its burden to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275, 277 (1993) ("[A] reasonable-doubt instruction is constitutionally required, and a misdescription of the burden violates the Due Process Clause.").

**III. The Government’s Burden Does Not Shift to the Defendant to Prove Withdrawal**

The government argues that withdrawal shifts the burden to the defendant to prove lack of participation. This argument misapprehends the nature of withdrawal as an affirmative defense. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1233. The government must prove the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, and the defendant’s withdrawal does not alter this burden. *Griffin v. United States*, 502 U.S. at 55.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, is misplaced. *Shabani* held that the government must prove all elements of a conspiracy charge, including agreement and overt act, but it did not address the burden of proving the duration of the conspiracy. *Id.* at 16. The government’s burden to prove the conspiracy continued into the limitations period remains undisturbed.

**IV. The Jury’s Verdict Must Be Based on Sufficient Evidence of Continuing Conspiracy**

The jury’s verdict must be supported by sufficient evidence that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) ("[T]he relevant question is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt."). Here, the evidence was insufficient to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period because the leaders of the conspiracy had split up before that period. The jury’s verdict cannot stand where the evidence does not support a finding of continuing conspiracy. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a conspiracy charge, including that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. The jury must be instructed on this burden, and the verdict must be supported by sufficient evidence. The judgment below should be reversed.
