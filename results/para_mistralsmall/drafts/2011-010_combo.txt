**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The text of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") is clear: it extends Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage only to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" modifies "injury," not "operations." *See* *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955) (holding that statutory language must be read in its ordinary and natural sense); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (same). Thus, the injury itself must occur on the outer Continental Shelf.

This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA's structure and purpose. The OCSLA was enacted to provide a uniform federal workers' compensation scheme for injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 85-1452, at 3 (1958), *reprinted in* 1958 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4432, 4433. The Act defines the outer Continental Shelf as "all submerged lands lying seaward and outside the area of lands beneath navigable waters." 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act's coverage is thus geographically limited to the outer Continental Shelf itself. *See* *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97, 103 (1971) (noting that the OCSLA's coverage is limited to the outer Continental Shelf).

The legislative history further supports this interpretation. The House Report accompanying the OCSLA states that the Act "provides for the payment of workmen's compensation benefits to employees injured on the outer Continental Shelf." H.R. Rep. No. 85-1452, at 3 (emphasis added). This language confirms that Congress intended to limit coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

The Ninth Circuit's contrary interpretation—that the OCSLA extends coverage to injuries occurring on land where there is a "substantial nexus" to operations on the outer Continental Shelf—is unsupported by the statutory text or legislative history. *See* *Erlenbaugh v. United States*, 409 U.S. 239, 243 (1973) (holding that courts must give effect to the plain meaning of statutory language). The Ninth Circuit's interpretation would impermissibly extend the OCSLA's coverage beyond the outer Continental Shelf, contrary to the Act's clear geographic limitation.

**II. THE SUPREME COURT'S DECISION IN *OFFSHORE LOGISTICS, INC. v. TALLENTIRE* SUPPORTS THE CONCLUSION THAT THE OCSLA EXTENDS COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF.**

In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Supreme Court considered whether the OCSLA's choice-of-law provision applied to an accident occurring on the waters above the outer Continental Shelf. The Court held that the choice-of-law provision did not apply because the accident did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf. *Id.* at 219. In doing so, the Court noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." *Id.* (emphasis added). The Court further observed that "only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an 'employee' resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act." *Id.* at 219 n.2.

The Ninth Circuit misread *Tallentire* as dicta, but the Supreme Court's observation about § 1333(b) was not mere dicta. *See* *Hutto v. Davis*, 454 U.S. 370, 375 (1982) (holding that Supreme Court statements about the law are not dicta when they are necessary to the Court's holding). The Court's observation that § 1333(b) is an exception to the OCSLA's general situs requirement confirms that the OCSLA's coverage is otherwise limited by locale. *See* *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2.

**III. PRE-1970 CASE LAW SUPPORTS THE CONCLUSION THAT THE OCSLA EXTENDS COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF.**

Pre-1970 case law also supports the conclusion that the OCSLA extends coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Supreme Court considered whether a worker injured on a platform in state waters was engaged in "maritime employment" for purposes of LHWCA coverage. The Court held that the worker was not engaged in maritime employment because the platform was in state waters, not on the outer Continental Shelf. *Id.* at 422-24. The Court's reasoning confirms that the LHWCA's coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. *See id.*

Similarly, in *Rodriguez v. Shell Oil Co.*, 348 F.2d 709 (5th Cir. 1965), the Fifth Circuit held that the OCSLA did not extend coverage to an injury occurring on a vessel in the Gulf of Mexico because the vessel was not on the outer Continental Shelf. *Id.* at 711. The court reasoned that the OCSLA's coverage was limited to the outer Continental Shelf itself. *Id.* at 710-11.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit's contrary interpretation is unsupported by the statutory text, legislative history, or pre-1970 case law.
