**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding misapplies the statutory text, misinterprets Supreme Court precedent, and expands Exemption 2 beyond its narrow, constitutionally grounded limits. The Court should reverse.

**I. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices” Under Exemption 2**

Exemption 2 applies only to “matters related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that this exemption is “explicitly exclusive” and “must be narrowly construed.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841, 106 L.Ed.2d 112 (1989); *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from the D.C. Circuit’s *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc)—is inconsistent with this principle and exceeds the scope of the statute.

The ESQD arc maps at issue are not internal personnel rules or practices. They are technical safety calculations used to determine the safe storage and handling of explosives. As the Navy’s own commander acknowledged, these maps are “used to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not a matter of internal personnel management; it is a matter of engineering safety and operational planning. The maps are not used to train, discipline, or manage Navy personnel. They are not internal directives governing employee conduct. They are not even internal to the Navy in the sense of being internal to its administrative or personnel functions. Rather, they are technical tools used to comply with safety regulations—specifically, the OP-5 manual—which governs the handling and storage of explosives.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that Exemption 2 does not cover information that is “of genuine and significant public interest.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The public has a profound interest in knowing the potential blast ranges of explosive ordnance stored near populated communities. The Navy’s own actions confirm this: it voluntarily released ESQD arc maps for the Bangor base, located four to five miles from the nearest town, but refused to release identical maps for Indian Island, which lies less than two miles from Port Townsend and less than a mile from Port Hadlock and Irondale. This stark contrast reveals that the Navy’s refusal is not based on national security concerns, but on political sensitivity. The maps are not “internal” in any meaningful sense—they are public safety data.

**II. The Risk of Circumvention Is Not Established, and the “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Unmoored from Statutory Text**

Even if the maps were internal, the Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that disclosure would “risk circumvention of agency regulation” is unsupported by the record. The Navy’s sole justification is Commander Whitbred’s affidavit, which states that a person might “reverse engineer” the maps to discover information about “particular ammunition, explosive[s], and weapons systems.” But this is speculative. No evidence shows that such reverse engineering is possible, nor that it would lead to actual harm. The Navy has not classified the maps, nor has it demonstrated that their release would jeopardize national defense or foreign policy—precisely the kind of harm Exemption 1 is designed to prevent. *5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1)*.

The Supreme Court has held that “the risk of circumvention must be real and substantial, not merely speculative.” *U.S. Dep’t of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173, 112 S.Ct. 541, 116 L.Ed.2d 526 (1991). The Navy has not met this burden. The maps are not operational plans, nor are they tactical intelligence. They are static, geometric representations of safety zones. Their disclosure does not reveal how explosives are stored, how they are moved, or how they are secured. They do not reveal the types or quantities of ordnance stored. They do not reveal vulnerabilities in the Navy’s security posture. They are not “law enforcement” materials, nor are they compiled for law enforcement purposes. *Church of Scientology v. IRS*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979). Thus, Exemption 7(F) does not apply.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard is not only unsupported by the text of Exemption 2, but also undermines the very purpose of FOIA. The statute was enacted to “open agency action to the light of public scrutiny.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The public has a right to know the risks posed by military installations near civilian populations. The Navy’s refusal to disclose these maps—while releasing identical data for a less politically sensitive site—reveals a pattern of selective disclosure based on political pressure, not statutory justification.
