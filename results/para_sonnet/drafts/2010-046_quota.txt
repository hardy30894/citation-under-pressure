# ARGUMENT

## I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT'S POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER SATISFIES THE PLRA'S REQUIREMENTS FOR PROSPECTIVE RELIEF

The Prison Litigation Reform Act permits prospective relief that is "narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary," and represents "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court's order satisfies each requirement.

### A. Overcrowding Was Properly Found To Be the Primary Cause of the Constitutional Violations

This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy proven constitutional violations in institutional settings. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), this Court held that courts must fashion remedies adequate to redress constitutional harms, and in *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education*, 402 U.S. 1 (1971), this Court confirmed that "once a right and a violation have been shown, the scope of a district court's equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad." *Id.* at 15.

The three-judge court, after extensive evidentiary proceedings, found that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the systemic Eighth Amendment violations documented in this litigation. That finding tracks this Court's teaching in *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), that conditions of confinement violate the Eighth Amendment when they involve "the wanton and unnecessary infliction of pain," *id.* at 347, and that overcrowding may independently produce such unconstitutional conditions when it triggers cascading harms in food service, sanitation, and medical care.

### B. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and Extends No Further Than Necessary

The PLRA does not prohibit population caps; it requires that such relief be crafted with precision. As the Ninth Circuit's related proceedings in this very litigation demonstrate, the district court's remedial authority derives from findings of persistent, unremedied constitutional violations following years of failed alternative measures. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, No. 08-17412 (9th Cir. Mar. 25, 2009) (recounting the six-day evidentiary hearing in 2005 detailing "a long history of constitutional violations and a failure of California to comply with remedial orders").

This Court's decision in *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343 (1996), confirms that remedial orders in prison litigation must be tailored to the violation found, but does not foreclose systemic relief where the violation is itself systemic. Here, the district court considered and rejected less intrusive alternatives before ordering population reduction—precisely the analytical sequence the PLRA demands.

### C. The State's Own Consent to Antecedent Remedial Structures Undermines Its Present Objections

The State's belated PLRA challenge is difficult to reconcile with its litigation conduct. As the Ninth Circuit observed in the companion contempt proceedings, California stipulated to remedial orders in 2001, 2002, and 2004, did not appeal the 2006 order appointing a Receiver, and filed a "statement of non-opposition" to successive Plans of Action calling for construction of thousands of new beds. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088 (9th Cir. 2009) (unpublished disposition context). The Ninth Circuit specifically noted that the "State's original consent to the proceedings may affect or even negate its claim" regarding statutory limitations on relief. This Court has long held that parties may not acquiesce in remedial structures for years and then challenge their foundations only when compliance becomes burdensome. *See Local No. 93, Int'l Ass'n of Firefighters v. City of Cleveland*, 478 U.S. 501 (1986) (recognizing that consent decrees, once entered, carry binding force even where they exceed relief a court could have ordered after trial).

### D. Federalism Concerns Do Not Bar Relief Where Constitutional Violations Persist

Petitioners invoke principles of federalism and comity, but this Court has repeatedly held that such concerns yield when a State's own officials have failed, over a period of years, to correct proven constitutional deprivations. In *Missouri v. Jenkins*, 495 U.S. 33 (1990), this Court affirmed that district courts retain authority to order effective relief—including measures that intrude upon state prerogatives—when necessary to remedy constitutional violations that state officials have failed to cure. *Id.* at 55. Similarly, in *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), this Court upheld a federal court's authority to impose a population limit on an Arkansas prison system as a remedy for Eighth Amendment violations, recognizing that "the ordering of specific relief... may be essential" where a state has demonstrated an inability or unwillingness to comply with less intrusive orders. *Id.* at 687.

The record here reflects exactly the kind of protracted noncompliance that justified the population cap in *Hutto*. The State's failure to fund even court-approved remedial construction—conduct that precipitated the contempt proceedings addressed in the Ninth Circuit's opinion below—confirms that no remedy short of population reduction can adequately address the constitutional violations.

### E. The Procedural History Confirms the Necessity of Systemic Relief

The Ninth Circuit's opinion in the related appeal underscores that California's objections to the scope of relief were not properly preserved through direct challenge to the underlying consent decrees or receivership order, but were instead raised reactively when enforcement became imminent. The court there applied the standard from *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463 (1978), and *Bauman v. United States District Court*, 557 F.2d 650 (9th Cir. 1977), and found no basis for extraordinary relief, noting the absence of any showing that the district court's orders were "clearly erroneous." That same posture pervades the current appeal: the population order followed years of graduated, unsuccessful remedial efforts, precisely the deliberative process the PLRA contemplates.

## CONCLUSION

The three-judge court's order is narrowly tailored, follows years of exhausted alternatives, and remedies proven, persistent constitutional violations. The judgment below should be affirmed.
