# ARGUMENT

## I. GUAM'S SECTION 107 CLAIM IS TIMELY BECAUSE THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT TRIGGER SECTION 113(f)(3)(B)

This Court has recognized that CERCLA provides "two express avenues for contribution" under Section 113, while Section 107 remains available for direct cost-recovery actions. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). The statutory text of Section 113(f)(3)(B) is precise: it applies only when a party "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B) (emphasis added). The 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam's liability—it explicitly disclaimed it.

The Decree states it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." This Court has consistently held that consent decrees must be interpreted according to their plain terms, since they are "in some respects contractual in nature" and reflect the parties' bargained-for agreement. *United States v. ITT Continental Baking Co.*, 420 U.S. 223, 236-37 (1975) (consent decrees "should be construed basically as contracts"). Where a decree expressly disclaims any adjudication of liability, courts should not read into it a resolution of liability that the parties took pains to avoid.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on the Sixth Circuit's approach in *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015)—which found no resolution of liability precisely because "the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability"—actually supports Guam's position when properly applied. The panel's attempt to distinguish *Florida Power* by treating the disclaimer as insufficient "standing alone" inverts the proper analysis: the disclaimer is not a minor textual quirk to be overcome by other provisions, but rather the parties' express statement of what the agreement does and does not accomplish.

Moreover, the underlying action here arose under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. This Court has emphasized that CERCLA's remedial scheme should not be imported wholesale into settlements addressing entirely different statutory violations. *See Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 814-19 (1994) (carefully cabining CERCLA's remedies to their statutory text). The EPA complaint alleged violations of the Clean Water Act's permitting requirements for discharges into "waters of the United States," 33 U.S.C. § 1362(7)—not the release of "hazardous substances" that triggers CERCLA liability. A settlement resolving Clean Water Act permitting violations should not be deemed, without clear indication, to simultaneously resolve CERCLA liability for a distinct regulatory regime.

## II. THE COURT OF APPEALS' INTERPRETATION PRODUCES ABSURD AND INEQUITABLE RESULTS THAT CONGRESS COULD NOT HAVE INTENDED

This Court has long recognized that statutory interpretation must avoid results that Congress could not have intended. *Griffin v. Oceanic Contractors, Inc.*, 458 U.S. 564, 575 (1982) ("interpretations of a statute which would produce absurd results are to be avoided if alternative interpretations consistent with the legislative purpose are available"). CERCLA's purpose is to "ensure that responsible parties foot the bill" for environmental contamination. *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). The panel's decision inverts this purpose entirely.

The Navy operated the Ordot Dump for nearly fifty years, depositing military waste, munitions, and chemicals including DDT and Agent Orange, with no environmental safeguards whatsoever. Under the panel's ruling, Guam—which inherited this hazard only because the Navy relinquished sovereign control—now bears the entire $160 million cleanup cost, while the Navy, the party that actually created the contamination, faces no CERCLA liability at all. This result cannot be reconciled with CERCLA's core purpose of ensuring that "any potentially responsible party... shall be liable" for contamination costs. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a).

The panel below acknowledged this harsh outcome, admitting that "the United States deposited dangerous munitions and chemicals at the Ordot Dump for decades and left Guam to foot the bill." Yet the court felt constrained by what it deemed unambiguous statutory text. But where, as here, the statutory text is genuinely ambiguous regarding what constitutes "resolution" of liability, this Court should not adopt a construction that contradicts CERCLA's remedial purpose. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA was enacted "in response to the serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution").

## III. GUAM'S UNIQUE TERRITORIAL STATUS COUNSELS AGAINST A RIGID READING THAT FORECLOSES RECOVERY

Guam's status as an unincorporated territory, 48 U.S.C. § 1421a, created the very predicament at issue here. The Navy constructed and operated the Ordot Dump while exercising sovereign military control over the island. When Congress passed the Guam Organic Act in 1950, transferring civilian governance to Guam, it did not thereby transfer the environmental consequences of decades of military waste disposal. Guam did not consent to inherit this contamination; it was a structural consequence of Guam's unique political relationship with the United States.

This Court should be wary of interpreting CERCLA's limitations provisions in a manner that leaves territories without recourse against the federal government for hazards the government itself created during periods of direct federal control. Just as this Court has recognized that "unincorporated territories" occupy a distinct constitutional position warranting careful judicial attention, *see Downes v. Bidwell*, 182 U.S. 244 (1901), so too should this Court ensure that CERCLA's technical limitations provisions do not work a fundamental injustice against a territory whose contamination originated in federal military operations predating any meaningful territorial autonomy.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that Guam's Section 107 cost-recovery action remains timely.
