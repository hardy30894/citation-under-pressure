ARGUMENT

I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the Act.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may rebut that presumption with “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). When such doubt exists, the employer may withdraw recognition, petition for an RM election, or conduct its own poll. Id. at 778 n.1.

The Board applies the identical evidentiary threshold to all three options. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989). That equivalence renders polling pointless: an employer already possessing enough evidence to withdraw recognition has no need to poll, while an employer lacking that evidence is forbidden to poll. Three courts of appeals have therefore rejected the Board’s standard as inconsistent with the Act’s policies. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981) (employer may poll upon “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support” even if insufficient to justify withdrawal); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984) (same); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982) (same).

The Board’s defense—that polling remains useful because an employer may wish “more certain, precise information”—fails. Texas Petrochemicals, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063. The Act favors Board-supervised elections over unsupervised polls. Id. at 1061. Maintaining the same threshold for both practices therefore contradicts the Board’s own premise that elections are preferable. The Board’s further insistence that polling is “potentially disruptive” supplies no warrant for equating it with withdrawal of recognition, which carries no procedural safeguards at all. Id. at 1061-62. Because the Board’s standard “is not rational and consistent with the Act,” it is not entitled to deference. Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S. at 787.

II. The Board’s Finding That Allentown Lacked Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.

Allentown presented unrefuted statements from multiple employees indicating loss of union support, including declarations that the entire night shift opposed the union and that a vote would result in defeat. The Board discounted these statements on grounds that one employee’s report of others’ views is unreliable and that expressions of dissatisfaction voiced during hiring interviews are presumptively insincere. Those categorical exclusions lack support in the record and contradict the Board’s own precedent permitting consideration of objective evidence short of direct repudiation by a majority. See, e.g., Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988).

The record as a whole therefore does not contain “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate” to support the Board’s conclusion. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 477 (1951). The poll, conducted under Struksnes safeguards with a neutral observer, confirmed the loss of majority support. The Board’s refusal to credit that evidence was arbitrary.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
