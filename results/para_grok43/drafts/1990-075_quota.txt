The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provisions, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfy the nondelegation doctrine because Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); see Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting the same). The statute enumerates eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, 21 U.S.C. § 811(c), requires an evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, id. § 811(b), and limits temporary placement to substances posing “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” id. § 811(h)(1). These constraints mirror the “goals,” “factors,” and “limits” upheld in Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 376-77, and the “well-known and generally acceptable standards” approved in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

The same intelligible principle governs the rapidity of changes in drug abuse, a consideration this Court has long recognized as justifying broader delegation. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944); see also Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (availability of judicial review supports delegation). Although petitioners invoke Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), those cases involved statutes containing “no criterion to govern the President’s course.” Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 415. Section 811(h) supplies multiple criteria and therefore falls comfortably within the “wide latitude” the Court has afforded Congress since 1935. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 373.

The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is likewise valid. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has repeatedly sustained such internal reallocations when the delegating statute so provides. See, e.g., United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512-13 (1974); Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co., 331 U.S. 111, 121-22 (1947). The regulation effectuating the delegation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely implements that statutory permission and does not enlarge the substantive power Congress conferred.

Because the temporary scheduling order is subject to the same intelligible principle that cabins permanent scheduling, and because the Attorney General’s subdelegation rests on express statutory authority, the Third Circuit correctly rejected petitioners’ constitutional challenge. The judgment should be affirmed.
