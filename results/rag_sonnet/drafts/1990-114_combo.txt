# ARGUMENT

## I. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the ADEA

**A. Congress did not clearly express an intent to override a State's constitutional authority to structure its own judiciary.**

Federal statutes are not read to displace a State's traditional prerogatives absent a clear and manifest congressional purpose. As this Court has explained in construing the reach of a federal statute against an area of historic state regulation, "we start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). Few subjects lie closer to the core of state sovereignty than a State's power to organize its own courts and define the tenure of its own judges. This Court has long recognized that it is for each State to determine the composition, jurisdiction, and structure of its judicial department. *See Duncan v. Missouri*, 152 U.S. 377 (1894) (recognizing that it was "entirely competent for the people" of Missouri "to adopt such a change in their organic law" governing the composition of their own courts); *Layton v. Missouri*, 187 U.S. 356 (1902) (deferring to Missouri's own allocation of jurisdiction among its courts as a matter of state constitutional design). Likewise, in *Dreyer v. Illinois*, 187 U.S. 71 (1902), this Court examined a state constitution's tripartite separation of "legislative, executive, judicial" power and confirmed that the content and organization of "the judicial powers" of a State are matters committed to the State's own constitution. And in *Ohio ex rel. Bryant v. Akron Metropolitan Park District*, 281 U.S. 74 (1930), this Court treated a State's constitutional arrangement for its own courts as a matter for the State to resolve, refusing to treat internal structural choices about a state judiciary as automatically subject to displacement by external federal norms.

Nothing in the ADEA speaks with the clarity required to override this tradition. The statute excludes from coverage "any person elected to public office" and any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges—who exercise the sovereign judicial power of the State, resolve "questions previously unsettled," and thereby make law for the entire State—fall within the latter category. A judge exercising the judicial power of a sovereign State performs a function no less "policymaking" in character than an executive appointee; indeed, the accumulated decisions of a State's highest court establish the common law for the whole State. Congress's use of a general term like "policymaking level," without any specific reference to the judiciary, does not satisfy the demand for a clear statement before this Court will read a federal statute to reach into so sensitive an area of state governmental structure.

## II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Rational-Basis Review Under the Equal Protection Clause

Age classifications affecting public officeholding are reviewed under the deferential rational-basis standard, which requires only "some reasonable basis" for the line the State has drawn. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911) (a classification "having some reasonable basis does not offend" the Equal Protection Clause "merely because it is not made with mathematical nicety or because in practice it results in some inequality"). A State need not solve every facet of a problem at once, nor prove its classification empirically correct; "[i]t is enough that there is an evil at hand for correction, and that it might be thought that the particular legislative measure was a rational way to correct it." *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 488 (1955). A classification is invalid only if it "rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State's objective." *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425–26 (1961).

Missouri's line easily survives that review. The proposition that judicial faculties may decline with advancing age, and that a fixed retirement age spares the State the "unpleasantness" and difficulty of case-by-case competency inquiries into sitting judges, is not a novel or irrational judgment—it is one this Nation's own federal system has long embraced. Congress itself permitted federal judges to retire, with continued full salary, upon reaching the age of seventy after ten years of service. *See Booth v. United States*, 291 U.S. 339, 341 (1934) (describing 28 U.S.C. § 260's provision allowing a federal judge who "having served continuously for ten years and attained the age of seventy years" to retire from active service). If Congress itself judged seventy a rational marker for the retirement of Article III judges holding office for life, Missouri cannot be said to act irrationally in fixing the same age for its own judges. So too, this Court has recognized the propriety of age- and tenure-based retirement classifications in other public service, sustaining a scheme distinguishing officers' retired pay according to length of service and cause of retirement. *See Hannum v. United States*, 226 U.S. 436 (1913). And where a State's classification bears a plausible relationship to a legitimate objective—here, preserving the vigor and efficient administration of the courts—this Court's precedents counsel deference to the State's own judgment about the wisdom of its internal governmental arrangements. *Cf. Joseph S. Finch & Co. v. McKittrick*, 305 U.S. 395 (1939).

Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is rational and lawful. This Court should affirm.
