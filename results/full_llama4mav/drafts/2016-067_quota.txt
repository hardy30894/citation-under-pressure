The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The distinction between the two is critical in determining whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander) is subject to the FDCPA. As the Fourth Circuit correctly held, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting debts on its own behalf, not on behalf of another.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The plain language of this definition makes clear that a debt collector is one who collects debts for others, not for itself. See Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)); Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015) (holding that a person must regularly collect debts for others to qualify as a debt collector).

The petitioners argue that Santander is a debt collector because it acquired defaulted debts from CitiFinancial Auto. However, this argument is unpersuasive. The FDCPA's definition of "creditor" excludes a person who receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). The fact that Santander acquired defaulted debts does not automatically make it a debt collector. Instead, the key factor is whether Santander was collecting debts on its own behalf or on behalf of another. See Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (holding that a debt collector is one who collects debts owed or due another at the time of collection).

The Fourth Circuit's decision is consistent with the FDCPA's purpose of regulating abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Santander, as a consumer finance company, is not a debt collector when it collects debts on its own behalf. See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698 (stating that the FDCPA is intended to regulate "third persons who regularly collect debts for others").

Other courts have reached similar conclusions. In Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012), the Sixth Circuit held that the terms "debt collector" and "creditor" are mutually exclusive. However, this holding does not support the petitioners' argument, as the Fourth Circuit correctly applied the statutory definitions to the facts of this case. See also Ruth v. Triumph P'ships, 577 F.3d 790, 796-97 (7th Cir. 2009) (discussing the distinction between debt collectors and creditors); FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007) (same).

The petitioners' argument that Santander's status as a debt collector before purchasing the debts somehow makes it a debt collector after the purchase is also unpersuasive. As the Fourth Circuit held, Santander's status as a creditor or debt collector depends on its actions at the time of the alleged FDCPA violation. See Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318 (holding that the inquiry under § 1692a(6) is whether the person regularly collects debts owed or due another at the time of collection).

In conclusion, the Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting debts on its own behalf. The FDCPA's definitions and the case law support this conclusion. The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
