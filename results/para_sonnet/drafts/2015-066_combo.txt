# ARGUMENT

## I. The Sixth Amendment Guarantees Counsel Whenever Imprisonment May Result, and a Conviction Obtained Without Counsel Cannot Be Used to Establish Guilt in a Later Prosecution.

The right to counsel is not a technicality; it is the foundation upon which the reliability of any criminal adjudication rests. In *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), this Court explained that "the right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel." *Id.* at 68-69. Three decades later, in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court made that guarantee binding wherever a defendant faces the loss of liberty, observing that lawyers in criminal courts are "necessities, not luxuries." *Id.* at 344. And in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described the Sixth Amendment as "a constant admonition that, if the constitutional safeguards it provides be lost, justice will not 'still be done.'" *Id.* at 462.

These cases establish that counsel is indispensable precisely because its absence undermines confidence in the correctness of the resulting judgment. That principle controls here. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), this Court held that a prior conviction obtained without counsel cannot be introduced to prove an element of a later offense or to enhance punishment, because "[t]o permit a conviction obtained in violation of *Gideon v. Wainwright* to be used against a person either to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense ... is to erode the principle of that case." *Id.* at 115. Burgett's rule was not confined to state-court convictions obtained without appointed counsel; it rests on the categorical premise that an uncounseled conviction is too unreliable to bear the weight the government would place upon it. That reasoning applies with equal force where, as here, the government seeks to use tribal-court convictions—obtained in proceedings that afforded no Sixth Amendment counsel whatsoever—as an essential element of guilt under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).

## II. Tribal Convictions, Though Valid Exercises of Sovereignty, Were Never Subject to the Sixth Amendment and Cannot Satisfy a Federal Guilt-Phase Predicate Without Compromising the Reliability the Amendment Protects.

Tribal courts exercise a sovereignty that predates the Constitution and is not bound by its provisions. As this Court recognized in *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), the powers of tribal government are "of a local nature" that existed before the Constitution and are therefore not measured against the Bill of Rights. *Id.* at 384. That doctrine, reaffirmed in *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375 (1886), and traceable to *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556 (1883), reflects a considered judgment that tribal adjudication proceeds under a distinct legal order.

Respect for that distinct order, however, does not require—and has never required—that federal courts treat tribal judgments as automatically equivalent to Sixth-Amendment-compliant convictions for purposes of establishing guilt in a federal prosecution. The comity that federal courts extend to the judgments of other sovereigns, as this Court described in *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113 (1895), is "the recognition which one nation allows within its territory to the ... judicial acts of another nation." *Id.* at 164. Comity permits recognition of a foreign or tribal judgment for its own purposes; it does not convert that judgment into proof, sufficient to satisfy the federal Constitution, of an element the government must establish beyond a reasonable doubt in an entirely separate federal prosecution. Using an uncounseled tribal conviction as a building block of a federal felony charge does not disrespect tribal sovereignty—it simply declines to import a proceeding lacking constitutional safeguards into a context, federal criminal adjudication, where those safeguards are indispensable.

## III. The Heightened Reliability Required to Prove an Element of Guilt, as Opposed to a Sentencing Factor, Confirms That Uncounseled Convictions Cannot Serve as § 117(a) Predicates.

This Court has long distinguished the reliability demanded at the guilt phase from that tolerated elsewhere in the criminal process. In *Reck v. Pate*, 367 U.S. 433 (1961), the Court emphasized that due process requires special vigilance where the reliability of the evidence used to establish guilt is in doubt. *Id.* at 440-41. Similarly, in *In re Gault*, 387 U.S. 1 (1967), the Court held that even juvenile proceedings, though nominally civil, require counsel because "the assistance of counsel is ... essential for the determination of the facts." *Id.* at 36. And in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), and *Chandler v. Fretag*, 348 U.S. 3 (1954), this Court repeatedly recognized that the right to counsel safeguards the very accuracy of criminal proceedings, not merely their formal fairness.

Section 117(a) does not use prior convictions as a sentencing enhancement of a single offense; it makes the fact and validity of two prior convictions an element the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt to obtain a conviction at all. Given the heightened reliability that guilt determinations demand under *Gault*, *Reck*, *Douglas*, and *Chandler*, and given *Burgett's* direct holding that uncounseled convictions cannot support guilt, the court of appeals correctly held that Bryant's uncounseled tribal convictions cannot serve as § 117(a) predicates.
