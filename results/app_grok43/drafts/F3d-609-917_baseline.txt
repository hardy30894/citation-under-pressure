ARGUMENT

The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 preempts Ray’s state-law claims for false imprisonment and negligence. The Act provides that a State “may not enact or enforce a law, regulation, or other provision having the force and effect of law related to a price, route, or service of an air carrier.” 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). The Supreme Court has held that the phrase “related to” expresses a broad preemptive purpose: “State enforcement actions having a connection with or reference to airline ‘rates, routes, or services’ are pre-empted.” Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc., 504 U.S. 374, 384 (1992). Claims that “relate to” an airline’s boarding procedures, handling of flight delays, and decisions whether to permit passengers to deplane fall squarely within this prohibition. See id. at 390; American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens, 513 U.S. 219, 226-28 (1995).

Ray’s complaint alleges that American “intentionally or negligently” failed to provide adequate personnel and equipment and, “for its own financial benefit,” refused to allow passengers to deplane after the buses departed. These allegations directly implicate the airline’s “services” in managing tarmac delays, communicating with passengers, and controlling access to the aircraft—core operational decisions Congress intended to insulate from state tort regulation. See Morales, 504 U.S. at 378, 390 (state consumer-protection claims concerning fare advertising preempted because they related to airline rates and services).

The Eighth Circuit has likewise recognized that state claims arising from an airline’s decisions about flight operations and passenger accommodations are preempted when they have a “connection with” the carrier’s services. See, e.g., Botz v. Omni Air Int’l, 286 F.3d 488, 494-95 (8th Cir. 2002) (state whistleblower claim based on refusal to fly preempted). Ray’s false-imprisonment and negligence theories seek to impose state-law duties on precisely those services. Allowing such claims to proceed would permit juries to second-guess the same operational judgments the Act reserves to federal regulation and airline management.

Because the claims are preempted, the district court lacked authority to reach their merits. The judgment should be affirmed on preemption grounds alone.
