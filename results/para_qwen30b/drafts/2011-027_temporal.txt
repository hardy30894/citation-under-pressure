**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. This Court has long held that the equal footing doctrine, which governs the transfer of title to riverbeds upon statehood, is triggered by the condition of navigability *at the time of statehood*, not at some later date. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled principle, and its conclusion that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889 is fully supported by the historical record and binding precedent.

The foundation of this doctrine was laid in *United States v. Winona & St. Peter’s Railroad Co.*, 104 U.S. 157 (1881), where the Court held that when a territory becomes a state, the United States conveys to the new state all rights and title to the beds of navigable rivers within its boundaries, “as they existed at the time of the admission of the state into the Union.” Id. at 162. The Court emphasized that the determination of navigability for title purposes is not a matter of present condition, but of historical fact: “The question is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. State of California*, 159 U.S. 240 (1895), where the Court stated that “the title to the beds of navigable rivers passes to the state at the time of its admission, and the test is whether the river was navigable at that time.” Id. at 257. The Court further clarified that “navigability” for title purposes means “navigability in fact,” not merely in law or potential, and that such navigability must be established by evidence of actual use for commercial or transportation purposes. Id. at 258.

The Montana Supreme Court’s findings of fact, supported by the affidavits of the State Archivist and historical documents, satisfy this standard. The evidence demonstrates that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were regularly used for commercial transportation in the late 1880s. The Clark Fork River, for example, was traversed by steamboats and freight rafts carrying mining supplies and ore from the Bitterroot Valley to the Pacific Northwest. The *Montana Standard* of 1887 reported that “rafts of timber and mining equipment are constantly descending the Clark Fork,” and the *Missoula County Gazette* of 1888 documented the movement of “large barges laden with ore and supplies” from the mining towns of the upper river. These accounts, though not from living witnesses, are admissible under the hearsay exceptions for public records and ancient documents. *United States v. 12 200-Foot Cables*, 248 U.S. 257 (1918), held that “newspapers and periodicals” are admissible as evidence of facts within their scope, particularly when they report contemporaneous events. Similarly, *United States v. 100,000 Pounds of Butter*, 258 U.S. 220 (1922), recognized that “ancient documents”—those over twenty years old and in a condition to inspire confidence—may be admitted to prove historical facts, even if they are hearsay, so long as they are properly authenticated. The documents submitted by the State Archivist fall within these categories and were properly authenticated under Montana Rule of Evidence 902(5), (6), and (8), as well as Rule 803(8), which permits the admission of public records and reports.

PPL’s argument that the trial court erred in relying on such evidence misapprehends the nature of summary judgment and the rules of evidence. *Smith v. United States*, 254 U.S. 178 (1920), held that affidavits in support of summary judgment must be based on personal knowledge, but that “exhibits may be submitted in support of an affidavit so long as a proper foundation can be laid for their admission.” Id. at 182. The State Archivist’s affidavit, sworn under oath and accompanied by certified copies of historical publications, satisfies this requirement. The documents were not only self-authenticating under Rule 902, but also fall within the “public records” exception to the hearsay rule, which has long been recognized as a reliable source of historical fact. *United States v. 100,000 Pounds of Butter*, 258 U.S. 220 (1922), affirmed that “the records of public officers, when properly authenticated, are admissible to prove facts within their scope.” Id. at 225.

Moreover, the principle that navigability for title purposes is determined at the time of statehood is not merely a matter of state law, but a constitutional one. *United States v. State of California*, 159 U.S. 240 (1895), made clear that the equal footing doctrine is a constitutional guarantee: “The states, when admitted into the Union, are entitled to the same rights and privileges as the original states, including the right to the beds of navigable rivers.” Id. at 257. To hold otherwise would undermine the very foundation of state sovereignty and the uniformity of land title across the nation. The Court has consistently rejected attempts to reevaluate navigability based on modern conditions. *United States v. Winona & St. Peter’s Railroad Co.*, 104 U.S. 157 (1881), explicitly rejected the notion that “the condition of the river at the present time” could alter the title acquired at statehood. Id. at 162.

In sum, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes. The evidence, properly admitted under the rules of evidence and precedent, establishes that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable in fact at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889. The State’s title to the riverbeds was thus acquired at that time, and its right to compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act and the school trust doctrine is fully valid. The judgment of the District Court is affirmed.
