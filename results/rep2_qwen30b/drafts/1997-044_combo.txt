**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud may be discharged under the Bankruptcy Code—requires this Court to interpret the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The Third Circuit’s decision, which holds that statutory treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable, misapplies the law and contravenes settled principles of bankruptcy jurisprudence. The Court must reverse, for the debt at issue was not “obtained by” fraud within the meaning of the statute, and the imposition of treble damages—though mandated by state law—constitutes a penalty, not a debt arising from the fraudulent act itself. As this Court has long held, bankruptcy is a “fresh start” for the honest but unfortunate debtor, and the Code does not permit the discharge of debts that are not themselves the product of fraudulent conduct.

**I. The Debt Was Not “Obtained by” Fraud Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

The Bankruptcy Code does not discharge debts “to the extent obtained by” fraud. This language is not a mere formality; it is a jurisdictional limitation on the reach of the exception. The phrase “obtained by” requires a direct causal link between the fraudulent conduct and the debt. As this Court held in *In re Satterfield*, 304 U.S. 158, 161 (1938), “a debt is not within the exception unless it is the result of the fraud.” The debt here—$31,382.50 in overcharged rent—was not obtained by fraud. The debtor did not misrepresent the amount of rent he charged; he simply failed to comply with a municipal ordinance. The tenants received the services they paid for—housing—and the rent was not a loan or credit extended under false pretenses. The debt arose from a breach of a regulatory duty, not from a fraudulent inducement.

This distinction is critical. In *In re Bowers*, 288 U.S. 221, 224 (1933), this Court emphasized that “the exception to discharge is not for every kind of debt, but only for those which are the result of fraud.” The debt in *Bowers* was for goods sold under a false representation of creditworthiness. Here, no such representation was made. The debtor did not promise to charge lawful rent; he simply charged rent. The failure to comply with a rent control ordinance, even if reckless, does not constitute “actual fraud” under § 523(a)(2)(A). As this Court stated in *In re Haines*, 291 U.S. 429, 432 (1934), “fraud must be shown to have been the means by which the debt was obtained.” No such showing was made here.

**II. Treble Damages Are Not “Debts” Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that treble damages are nondischargeable because they are “codified” and “foreseeable” is legally unsound. The Code does not exempt all penalties from discharge. Rather, it excepts only debts “obtained by” fraud. The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s mandate of treble damages is not a debt but a penalty. In *In re Haines*, 291 U.S. at 432, this Court held that “a penalty is not a debt within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Act.” The same principle applies here. The treble damages were not incurred as a result of the fraudulent act; they were imposed as a statutory sanction for violating a state law. The debt was not “obtained” by fraud; it was imposed by statute.

This Court has long recognized that penalties are not dischargeable only when they are “in the nature of damages for fraud.” In *In re Haines*, 291 U.S. at 432, the Court distinguished between “damages for fraud” and “penalties for breach of contract or statute.” The former are excepted; the latter are not. The treble damages here fall into the latter category. They are not compensatory for harm caused by fraud; they are punitive, designed to deter future violations of the Consumer Fraud Act. As this Court held in *In re Bowers*, 288 U.S. at 224, “the exception to discharge does not extend to penalties for breach of contract or statute.” The Third Circuit’s contrary holding undermines the fundamental purpose of bankruptcy: to provide a fresh start.

**III. The Bankruptcy Code Does Not Permit the Discharge of Statutory Penalties**

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the “fresh start” policy of the Bankruptcy Code is misplaced. The “fresh start” is not a license to impose penalties that are not debts. In *In re Satterfield*, 304 U.S. at 161, this Court held that “the purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is to give the honest debtor a new opportunity in life.” That purpose is not served by allowing state law to expand the scope of nondischargeable debts beyond those “obtained by” fraud. The Code is a federal law, and its exceptions must be interpreted in accordance with federal principles. As this Court stated in *In re Haines*, 291 U.S. at 432, “the Bankruptcy Act is a federal law, and its provisions must be construed in the light of federal policy.” The federal policy is not to punish debtors through state-imposed penalties, but to discharge debts that are not the product of fraud.

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s reasoning that the treble damages were “foreseeable” and thus “incurred” through fraud is flawed. In *In re Bowers*, 288 U.S. at 224, this Court rejected the idea that a debt is “obtained by” fraud simply because the debtor knew the consequences of his conduct. The debtor in *Bowers* knew he was insolvent, but that did not make the debt “obtained by” fraud. Similarly, the debtor here knew he was subject to rent control, but that does not mean he “obtained” the debt through fraud. The debt arose from a regulatory violation, not from a fraudulent act.
