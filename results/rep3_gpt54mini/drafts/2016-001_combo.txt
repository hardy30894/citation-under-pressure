Samsung’s damages award cannot stand because the Patent Act does not authorize a patentee to recover an infringer’s entire profits when the patented design covers only a component of a multicomponent product. The governing statute, as construed by this Court, limits design-patent recovery to “the profit made from the article of manufacture to which the design has been applied,” not profits traceable to unpatented features of the accused product. 35 U.S.C. § 289; see Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444-45 (1885) (rejecting recovery of profits untethered to the patented design); Untermeyer v. Freund, 58 F. 205, 209 (2d Cir. 1893) (design-patent damages must reflect the value of the design, not the entire article absent proof of that equivalence).

This Court has long recognized that damages in patent cases must be compensatory, not punitive or confiscatory. Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), held that a patentee “must show in what what [sic] manner, and to what extent, his profits are to be accounted for by reason of the use of his invention.” The Court further explained that when the patent covers only part of a product, the patentee must provide evidence that separates or apportions the value of the patented feature from the whole. Id. at 121-22. That principle applies with special force here, where the asserted design patents cover ornamental aspects of smartphones, not the devices as a whole.

The Court’s apportionment decisions in utility-patent cases reinforce that rule. In Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490-91 (1854), the Court refused to award profits based on the entire machine where the invention covered only an improvement. The Court again rejected whole-product recovery in Dobson, 114 U.S. at 444-45, emphasizing that damages must be limited to the patented contribution. Similar reasoning appears in Elizabeth v. Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126, 137-38 (1877), where the Court measured recovery by the value attributable to the patented pavement improvement, not the full roadway. See also Littlefield v. Perry, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 205, 223-24 (1874) (where patent covers only part of a machine, profits must be apportioned to the patented part); Seymour, 57 U.S. at 490-91.

The same apportionment logic governs accountings in equity. In Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912), the Court held that when a patent is only one element of a larger product, the patentee bears the burden of showing the profits attributable to the patented feature. If the patentee cannot make that showing, recovery is limited accordingly. Id. at 617-18. The Court reaffirmed in Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 648-49 (1915), that profits must be apportioned where the patent covers only a component; the patentee may not recover for value created by unpatented elements. And in Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. at 619, the Court stressed that the burden-shifting rule does not eliminate apportionment; it presupposes it.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach in this case cannot be squared with those precedents. A design patent does not automatically entitle the patentee to the infringer’s total profits from a finished product merely because the design is visually embodied in the product. The older cases teach that where the patented feature is only part of the whole, the factfinder must identify the profits caused by that feature. Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121-22; Dowagiac, 235 U.S. at 648-49; Westinghouse, 225 U.S. at 614-18. Otherwise, damages become a windfall unrelated to the actual contribution of the patented design.

Nor does the statute’s reference to “the article of manufacture” compel whole-product disgorgement here. The phrase identifies the relevant profit base; it does not erase the Court’s longstanding rule that recovery must be confined to the value of the patented contribution when the design is only part of a larger article. See Dobson, 114 U.S. at 444-45; Untermeyer, 58 F. at 209. The better reading harmonizes § 289 with the Court’s apportionment cases: the patentee may recover the infringer’s profits on the relevant article of manufacture, but when that article is a component, the profits must be limited to the component’s contribution to the accused product.

Because Apple sought and obtained Samsung’s entire profits from multicomponent smartphones without proving that the patented designs accounted for those profits, the judgment should be reversed. At minimum, the case should be remanded for proper apportionment consistent with Garretson, Seymour, Dobson, Littlefield, Westinghouse, Dowagiac, Elizabeth, and Untermeyer.
