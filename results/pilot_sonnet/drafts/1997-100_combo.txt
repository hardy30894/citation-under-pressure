**ARGUMENT**

**The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial of the Prior-Conviction Allegation Because the State's Own Procedures Made the Sentencing Proceeding the Functional Equivalent of a Trial on Guilt.**

The Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, guarantees that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." This guarantee is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). As this Court has explained, the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. It protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after conviction. And it protects against multiple punishments for the same offense." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). California's rule—that a defendant may be forced to relitigate a sentencing enhancement after an appellate court has held the evidence insufficient to sustain it—offends all three of these protections.

*First*, the proceeding below was not an informal, discretionary exercise of sentencing judgment of the kind approved in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949). California required the prosecution to prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, applied the ordinary rules of evidence, and afforded petitioner the right to confront the State's witnesses. A proceeding bearing these "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" cannot be treated as though it were merely an exercise of sentencing discretion immune from constitutional scrutiny. *Williams* rested on the premise that a sentencing judge could rely on information never subjected to adversarial testing; California, by contrast, chose to try the enhancement allegation with the very safeguards that define a criminal trial. Having done so, it may not now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend such a trial.

*Second*, the Court of Appeal's determination that the evidence was insufficient to prove the prior conviction is the functional equivalent of an acquittal, and this Court has long held that an acquittal—however erroneous, and however it is styled—is absolutely final. In *Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141, 143 (1962), the Court held that a trial court's acquittal, even one entered "on the basis of erroneous foundations," could not be reviewed "without putting the defendant twice in jeopardy." That principle traces to *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), which held that "a verdict of acquittal, although not followed by any judgment, is a bar to a subsequent prosecution for the same offence." And in *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904), the Court held that an appeal from a judgment of acquittal is itself a second jeopardy, because the defendant "would be placed in jeopardy a second time for the same offense" if the government could obtain a new trial after failing to prove its case. A finding that the prosecution's evidence was legally insufficient is indistinguishable in substance from an acquittal; retrial after such a finding "would be to hold him twice in jeopardy for the same offense in plain violation of the Constitution." *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957). This Court in *Green* explained the deeper policy at stake: the Double Jeopardy Clause exists because "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity." *Id.* at 187–88. That rationale applies with undiminished force whether the "offense" at issue is the substantive crime or, as here, a sentencing allegation tried with the full panoply of trial-type protections.

*Third*, retrial here would also risk the multiple-punishment evil that the Clause forbids. In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1873), the Court held that once a defendant has suffered a lawful judgment, the Constitution forbids the State from imposing a second, harsher punishment for the same conduct. Permitting the State a second opportunity to prove the enhancement—after having already litigated and lost that very issue—invites precisely the kind of piecemeal, repeated punishment-seeking that *Lange* and *Pearce* condemn.

Nor is there any principled basis for confining these protections to capital sentencing. The California Supreme Court's reliance on the capital/noncapital distinction finds no support in this Court's own historical practice. In *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court treated a jury's sentencing-related determination in a capital case as bound up with jeopardy principles precisely because the jury's finding functioned as an adjudicative fact-finding process, not because capital cases are categorically unique. What matters is not the label "sentencing," but whether the State has chosen to conduct a trial-like adjudication of a discrete factual issue. Where, as here, the State has done so—with proof beyond a reasonable doubt, evidentiary rules, and confrontation—the resulting proceeding must carry the same finality that attaches to any other criminal adjudication.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars California from retrying the prior-conviction allegation that a court of competent jurisdiction has already found unproven.
