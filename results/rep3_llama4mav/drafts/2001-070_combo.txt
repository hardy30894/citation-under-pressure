The execution of Daryl Renard Atkins, who is mentally retarded, does not constitute "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that his mental retardation bars his execution. Although Penry was later superseded by statute, the principle that mental retardation is a mitigating factor, not a categorical bar to execution, remains valid under the Eighth Amendment as interpreted by prior Supreme Court decisions.

The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual," a standard that has been interpreted by the Court to prohibit punishments that are "barbarous" or "shocking to the conscience." Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (plurality opinion). In Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court held that a punishment is cruel and unusual if it is "greatly disproportioned to the offense charged." Id. at 367. However, the Court has also recognized that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit capital punishment per se. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890).

The execution of mentally retarded individuals is not "cruel and unusual" under the Eighth Amendment. The Court has long recognized that the states have significant latitude in determining the appropriate punishment for crimes. See Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 134-35 (1878). In re Kemmler established that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual" in the sense that they are "barbarous" or "shocking to the conscience." 136 U.S. at 447. The execution of a mentally retarded individual, while potentially tragic, is not necessarily "barbarous" or "shocking to the conscience."

Furthermore, the Court has held that the Constitution does not require that the states adopt a uniform punishment for a particular crime. See Collins v. Johnston, 237 U.S. 502, 509-10 (1915). The fact that some states may prohibit the execution of mentally retarded individuals does not establish a national consensus against such executions. See Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 602 (1900).

In Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "excessive" in relation to the crime committed. Id. at 100 (plurality opinion). However, the Court has not established a clear standard for determining when a punishment is "excessive." See O'Neil v. Vermont, 144 U.S. 323, 339-40 (1892). In the absence of a clear standard, it is difficult to argue that the execution of a mentally retarded individual is "excessive" in relation to the crime of capital murder.

The respondent respectfully submits that the execution of Daryl Renard Atkins does not constitute "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied the relevant law in affirming Atkins' death sentence, and this Court should affirm that judgment.
