**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s denial of James Winbush’s last-minute motion for a fingerprint identification expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional right to a fair trial under the Sixth Amendment and the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The court’s rigid adherence to procedural deadlines, without considering the substantial prejudice to Winbush’s ability to mount a meaningful defense, constitutes reversible error. This Court should reverse Winbush’s convictions and remand for a new trial.

**I. The District Court’s Denial of Winbush’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert Violated His Sixth Amendment Right to Effective Assistance of Counsel and the Right to Present a Defense**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant the right to “compel the attendance of witnesses in his favor.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right includes the right to present a defense, which encompasses the ability to challenge the prosecution’s evidence through expert testimony. *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 654 (1984). When the government introduces forensic evidence—such as the absence of latent fingerprints—the defendant must have a meaningful opportunity to rebut it with expert testimony. *See United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.3d 805, 812 (7th Cir. 2009) (“A defendant has a right to challenge the government’s forensic evidence through expert testimony.”).

Here, the government’s case rested heavily on the absence of latent fingerprints on the recovered cocaine and firearm. The government’s forensic expert, Michael Shay, testified that no usable fingerprints were found on the evidence. This testimony, if unchallenged, could be interpreted as circumstantial evidence of Winbush’s guilt—suggesting he wore gloves or otherwise avoided leaving fingerprints. But the absence of fingerprints is not inherently incriminating; it may result from the nature of the materials, environmental conditions, or the presence of gloves. *See United States v. Johnson*, 555 F.3d 595, 600 (7th Cir. 2009) (“The absence of fingerprints does not prove guilt; it may simply reflect the difficulty of obtaining prints from certain surfaces.”).

Winbush sought to present an expert to explain the scientific limitations of fingerprint recovery and to testify that the absence of prints was consistent with the evidence being handled with gloves or in a manner that precluded print deposition. This was not a fishing expedition; it was a legitimate effort to challenge the government’s narrative. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.3d at 812. The district court’s refusal to allow this testimony, without considering the timing or the potential for prejudice, deprived Winbush of a fair opportunity to defend himself.

**II. The Denial of the Motion to Continue Was an Abuse of Discretion That Prejudiced Winbush’s Defense**

The district court’s refusal to grant a continuance, despite the late filing of the motion, was an abuse of discretion. *See United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.3d at 813 (“A district court abuses its discretion when it denies a continuance without considering the totality of the circumstances.”). The Seventh Circuit has long recognized that “a defendant’s right to a fair trial may be compromised when the court denies a reasonable request for a continuance.” *United States v. Hines*, 555 F.3d 587, 592 (7th Cir. 2009).

Winbush filed his motion only six days before trial, but the case had been pending for over two years. The government had already disclosed its forensic reports and expert witnesses in October 2007. Winbush’s motion was not a surprise; it was a response to the government’s notice of expert testimony. *See United States v. Johnson*, 555 F.3d at 600 (“A defendant may reasonably rely on the government’s pretrial disclosures to shape his defense.”). The court’s failure to consider the context—two years of litigation, the government’s own notice of expert testimony, and the critical nature of the fingerprint evidence—renders its decision arbitrary.

Moreover, the government did not object to the motion or assert prejudice. *See United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.3d at 813 (denying a continuance where the government did not oppose the request is particularly suspect). The court’s refusal to allow even a brief delay to secure an expert—especially one who could have explained the scientific limitations of fingerprint recovery—undermined the fairness of the trial.

**III. The Error Was Not Harmless**

The absence of a fingerprint expert and the inability to challenge the government’s forensic narrative was not harmless. The government’s case hinged on circumstantial evidence: Winbush fled with a gun, was seen with drugs and a firearm, and left behind a jacket with more drugs and a gun. The lack of fingerprints was presented as a “smoking gun” of guilt. Without an expert to explain that the absence of prints is not conclusive, the jury may have misinterpreted the evidence.

*See United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. at 659 (“When a defendant is denied the assistance of counsel in a manner that deprives him of a fair trial, the error is per se reversible.”). The failure to allow expert testimony on fingerprint science created a substantial risk of a miscarriage of justice. *See United States v. Hines*, 555 F.3d at 593 (“A trial is unfair when the defendant is denied a meaningful opportunity to challenge the prosecution’s evidence.”).

**Conclusion**

The district court’s denial of Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional rights to a fair trial and to present a defense. The error was not harmless and requires reversal. The judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
