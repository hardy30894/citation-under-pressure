**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from its after-hours facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a religiously themed film series—on the grounds that the facilities are not open to religious use—rests on a flawed understanding of the constitutional duty to refrain from content-based discrimination. The District’s own policy and practice have created a limited public forum, and once such a forum is opened, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. This Court has long held that “the First Amendment prohibits the government from discriminating against speech on the basis of its message, viewpoint, or subject matter.” *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981). The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities, while permitting secular groups to do so, constitutes precisely such a violation.

The District’s facilities are not a nonpublic forum. The Second Circuit correctly acknowledged that the Center Moriches School District’s facilities are “limited public forums,” a category defined by the Supreme Court as “property purposefully opened for use by the public for expressive activity.” *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). The District’s own conduct demonstrates that it has opened its facilities to non-religious groups for expressive purposes. The record shows that the school district has permitted the Southern Harmonizers—a secular musical group—to perform in the high school auditorium, and that the Center Moriches Free Public Library sponsored a four-night lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics by Jerry Huck, a psychotherapist. These uses were not incidental or religious in nature. The Southern Harmonizers’ performance was described as “musical and cultural,” and Mr. Huck explicitly testified that his lectures were “all science,” based on “analytic, quantum physicists.” *See* App. 12a–13a. These programs were not religious, but they were expressive, and they were permitted.

The District’s policy of excluding religious groups from such a forum is therefore not viewpoint-neutral. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a government that opens a forum for public expression may not exclude speech based on its religious content.” *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273. In *Widmar*, the Court struck down a university regulation that barred religious meetings on campus, even though the university had opened its facilities to student groups. The Court held that “the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and that “the exclusion of religious speech from such a forum is a content-based restriction that violates the First Amendment.” *Id.* at 267, 273. The same principle applies here. The District has opened its facilities to secular expressive activity, and it may not then deny access to religious groups solely because their speech is religious in content.

The District’s reliance on *Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is misplaced. In *Mergens*, the Court held that the Equal Access Act required a public high school to allow a student religious group to meet on school premises if the school permitted other non-curricular student groups to meet. The Court emphasized that the school had created a “limited open forum” for student groups. *Id.* at 247. The Court did not decide whether the First Amendment alone would require such access, noting that the decision was “purely on statutory grounds.” *Id.* at 247 n.1. But the principle remains: once a school creates a limited public forum, it cannot discriminate against religious speech. The District here has done precisely that. It has permitted secular groups to use its facilities for expressive purposes, but has barred religious groups from doing the same—despite the fact that the religious group’s purpose is identical in nature to that of the secular groups: to educate and inform the community on family values.

The District’s argument that it has never permitted religious use of its facilities is irrelevant. The First Amendment does not require a government to open a forum to all speakers from the outset. But once it does open a forum, it must do so without discrimination. As this Court has stated, “the government may not exclude speech from a forum simply because it disagrees with the message.” *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a film series on family values—described as “practical insights about the family”—is not a neutral regulation of time, place, or manner. It is a content-based exclusion based on the religious character of the speech. Such discrimination is unconstitutional.

The District’s claim that the exclusion is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is equally without merit. The Establishment Clause does not permit the government to suppress religious speech simply because it is religious. *See* *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (plurality opinion) (“The Establishment Clause does not require the government to suppress religious expression in order to avoid the appearance of endorsing religion.”). The government may not favor religion, but it may not disfavor it either. *See* *Good News Club v. Milford Central Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 107 (2001). The District’s policy of excluding religious groups while allowing secular ones to use its facilities creates a system of religious discrimination that violates both the Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses.

For these reasons, the District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities for a religious film series constitutes a violation of the First Amendment. The judgment of the Second Circuit must be reversed.
