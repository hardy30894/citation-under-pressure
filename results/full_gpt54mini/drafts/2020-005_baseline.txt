Respondent, the injured plaintiff, is properly subjecting Ford to specific jurisdiction because Ford deliberately created and served a market for the very product that injured Gullett in Montana. The Due Process Clause requires only that Ford have “minimum contacts” with the forum such that suit there does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). In the specific-jurisdiction setting, the defendant’s suit-related conduct must create a substantial connection with the forum. Walden v. Fiore, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014). But the Court has never required a strict causal chain between the defendant’s forum contacts and the particular injury. To the contrary, jurisdiction may lie where the suit “arise[s] out of or relate[s] to” the defendant’s forum activities. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017).

That “or relate to” language is dispositive. In Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 592 U.S. 351 (2021), the Court held unanimously that specific jurisdiction exists where Ford “systematically served a market in Montana for the very vehicles that allegedly malfunctioned and injured the plaintiffs.” Id. at 364. Ford had extensively advertised, sold, and serviced the same models in the forum, and the Court rejected Ford’s argument that jurisdiction fails unless the plaintiff’s exact car was first sold in the forum or the particular vehicle was designed, manufactured, or originally sold there. Id. at 367-68. The Court explained that the Constitution does not demand “a strict causal relationship” and that the phrase “relate to” “contemplates that some relationships will support jurisdiction without a causal showing.” Id. at 362-63.

The same result follows here. Ford’s Montana activities were not random, isolated, or fortuitous. Ford extensively marketed, sold, and serviced Explorer vehicles and parts in Montana, establishing a sustained commercial presence in the state. Gullett was killed in Montana by the alleged malfunction of a Ford Explorer that Ford had designed for nationwide sale and placed into the stream of commerce anticipating resale, use, and service in states like Montana. Under Ford Motor Co., that is enough. The forum connection is not attenuated merely because the vehicle was first sold in Washington and later resold before reaching Montana. The relevant question is whether Ford’s forum conduct relates to the same model and the same kind of alleged defect causing the injury. Ford Motor Co., 592 U.S. at 369-70.

Ford’s contrary theory would collapse specific jurisdiction into a narrow causation test the Court has already rejected. That position conflicts with Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 472-76 (1985), which looks to purposeful availment and the relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation, not to a rigid “but-for” or proximate-cause requirement. It also conflicts with World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286, 297-98 (1980), which recognized that a manufacturer may reasonably anticipate being haled into court where it serves a market for its product. Ford did exactly that in Montana.

Nor is there any unfairness. Once minimum contacts are shown, the remaining reasonableness factors favor jurisdiction: Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its resident, the plaintiff has a strong interest in convenient relief, and Ford plainly can litigate in a state where it does substantial business. Burger King, 471 U.S. at 476-77. Ford is a global corporation that cannot plausibly claim surprise when sued in a state where it deliberately cultivated the market for the very vehicle line at issue.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed. Under Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 592 U.S. 351 (2021), a state court may exercise specific jurisdiction when the defendant’s forum contacts relate to the plaintiff’s claims even if none of those contacts caused the injury.
