**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN NATIONAL PARK SYSTEM UNITS IN ALASKA IS UNAFFECTED BY ANILCA § 103(c).**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not strip the National Park Service (NPS) of its statutory authority to regulate activities on nonfederal lands and waters within conservation system units (CSUs) in Alaska. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 822 F.3d 1066, 1073 (9th Cir. 2016). Section 103(c) provides:

> *"Only those lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] which are owned by the United States, by the State of Alaska, or by any Native organization, shall be deemed to be included as a part of such unit."* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added).

This provision merely clarifies which lands are *included* within CSU boundaries—not which lands the NPS may regulate. *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s construction is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding principle that Congress does not “hide elephants in mouseholes.” *Whitman v. Am. Trucking Ass’ns*, 531 U.S. 457, 468 (2001). If Congress intended to divest the NPS of regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters, it would have done so expressly. *See id.* (requiring clear statement before concluding Congress intended to alter the federal-state balance).

The legislative history of ANILCA confirms this reading. Congress enacted ANILCA to protect Alaska’s unique natural resources while respecting state sovereignty. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 96-622, at 15 (1979). However, Congress also recognized that the NPS retained its general regulatory authority under the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act and other federal statutes. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (preserving existing federal regulatory authority). The Ninth Circuit correctly deferred to the NPS’s reasonable interpretation of its own authority. *See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Nat. Res. Def. Council*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984).

**II. THE NPS’S HOVERCRAFT BAN APPLIES TO ALL LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN YUKON-CHARLEY, INCLUDING STATE-OWNED PORTIONS OF THE NATION RIVER.**

The NPS’s hovercraft regulation, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), prohibits the operation of hovercrafts “within the boundaries of any unit of the National Park System.” This regulation is not limited to federally owned lands. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 1.2(a)(3) (defining “unit of the National Park System” to include all lands and waters within park boundaries, regardless of ownership). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the regulation applies to state-owned portions of the Nation River because the river lies within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. *Sturgeon*, 822 F.3d at 1074.

Sturgeon’s reliance on *Katie John I*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Katie John I*, the Ninth Circuit held that ANILCA § 103(c) precluded the NPS from regulating subsistence fishing on state-owned waters. *Id.* at 701. However, the court later clarified that *Katie John I* was limited to subsistence regulations under ANILCA § 3114. *Katie John III*, 720 F.3d at 1220. The hovercraft ban is not a subsistence regulation and is instead grounded in the NPS’s general authority to regulate park use. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 1 (establishing the NPS’s mission to “conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations”).

**III. STURGEON HAS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE NPS’S REGULATIONS, BUT HIS CLAIMS FAIL ON THE MERITS.**

Sturgeon has established standing because he has suffered an injury-in-fact—his inability to use his hovercraft on state-owned portions of the Nation River—and the NPS’s enforcement actions are fairly traceable to his injury. *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1992). However, his claims fail on the merits because the NPS’s regulations are valid exercises of its statutory authority. *See Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 842-43.

**IV. ALASKA LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE NPS’S REGULATIONS.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Alaska lacks standing because its alleged injuries are not redressable. *Sturgeon*, 822 F.3d at 1077. Alaska’s claim that the NPS’s regulations interfere with its sovereign interests is insufficient because Alaska has not identified any actual conflict between its own laws and the NPS’s regulations. *See Alfred L. Snapp & Son, Inc. v. Puerto Rico ex rel. Barez*, 458 U.S. 592, 601-02 (1982). Moreover, Alaska’s claim that the regulations have a “chilling effect” on its citizens is impermissible because it seeks to protect private parties’ interests. *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971 (9th Cir. 2009).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s judgment. The NPS’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within CSUs in Alaska is unaffected by ANILCA § 103(c), and the hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of the NPS’s statutory authority. Sturgeon’s claims fail on the merits, and Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s regulations.
