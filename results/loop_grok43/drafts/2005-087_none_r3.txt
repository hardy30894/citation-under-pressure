The Fourteenth Amendment does not confer a due process right to present expert evidence of mental condition to negate mens rea apart from an insanity defense. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952), upheld a state rule placing the burden of insanity on the defendant and affirmed the Clause’s respect for state procedural choices. Arizona’s limitation is consistent with that holding.

States possess broad authority to prescribe the elements of crimes and the scope of defenses. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895), required proof of every element beyond a reasonable doubt but allowed states to direct mental-condition evidence to a distinct defense. The same approach is reflected in Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 420 (1902), and Matheson v. United States, 227 U.S. 540, 543 (1913).

Due process requires fundamental fairness but does not demand admission of all relevant evidence. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934), protects against fundamentally unfair proceedings. An exclusionary rule violates the Clause only when it contravenes “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Arizona’s statute meets this test.

The Constitution does not compel acceptance of every evidentiary proffer. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), and Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 235 (1940), prohibit sham trials but leave states free to regulate expert testimony on intent. Arizona’s rule therefore accords with the Fourteenth Amendment.
