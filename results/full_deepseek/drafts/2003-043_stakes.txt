ARGUMENT

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed because the Internal Revenue Service cannot collect a partnership’s tax deficiency from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The IRS’s timely assessment against the partnership extended the statute of limitations only for the partnership, not for the individual partners, who are separate taxpayers. Because the IRS failed to assess the partners within the three-year period provided by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), its claims are time-barred.

**I. The IRS Must Assess Each Individual Taxpayer to Extend the Statute of Limitations for Collection from That Taxpayer**

The Internal Revenue Code is clear: an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The term “taxpayer” includes “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). A partnership is a distinct taxpayer from its individual partners. The implementing regulation requires that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (emphasis added). An assessment against the partnership identifies only the partnership as the taxpayer. It does not identify the individual partners.

The limitations period for assessment is three years after the return is filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). If an assessment is timely made, the period for collection extends to ten years from the date of assessment. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But that extension applies only to “the taxpayer” against whom the assessment was made. Here, the IRS assessed only the partnership. The partners were never assessed. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, the assessment against the partnership “extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership, but it left unaltered the limitations period applicable to Debtors.” *United States v. Galletti*, 299 F.3d 913, 920 (9th Cir. 2002). Because the IRS did not assess the partners within three years after their returns were filed, the statute of limitations bars collection from them.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s Precedent Compels Affirmance**

This court’s decision in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), supports the conclusion that an assessment against a partnership does not suffice for collection from partners. In *Coson*, the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership and later claimed a lien against the property of an alleged partner. The district court held that the lien was invalid because the partner had never been individually assessed. *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958) (“[S]uch a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to [§§ 6321 and 6322] unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.”). This court affirmed, albeit on other grounds. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 462-63. The reasoning of the district court, which the Ninth Circuit did not repudiate, underscores that an individual assessment is a prerequisite to collection from a partner.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. There, the court held only that a partner who voluntarily paid taxes assessed against the partnership could not obtain a refund. *Id.* at 910. The court expressly noted that the partner was “personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership,” but it did not address whether the IRS could *collect* from a partner who refused to pay without an individual assessment. As the Ninth Circuit observed, “the district court acknowledged that to collect the tax for which the partner was liable, the IRS would have had to either resort to court action or individually assess the taxes against the partner.” *Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 921. *Young* does not authorize collection without assessment.

**III. State Law Does Not Provide an Independent Basis for Collection**

The IRS argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this liability permits collection without an individual assessment. But California law explicitly requires a judgment against the partner before a creditor may satisfy a partnership debt from the partner’s assets. “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS has not obtained a judgment against any partner. The time to do so has expired because the limitations period for bringing an action to collect from the partners was three years. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The assessment against the partnership did not extend that period as to the partners.

**IV. *United States v. Wright* Is Distinguishable**

The IRS cites *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), but that case is inapposite. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only issue was whether the statute of limitations was tolled as to the partners during the partnership’s bankruptcy. The Seventh Circuit had no occasion to decide whether an assessment against the partnership alone would suffice. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. Here, no assessment was made against the partners, so *Wright* does not apply.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations bars collection from them. The assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period only for the partnership. California law does not allow collection without a judgment against the partner, and no such judgment exists. The court of appeals correctly affirmed the disallowance of the IRS’s claims. The judgment should be affirmed.
