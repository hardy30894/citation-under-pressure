**ARGUMENT**

**I. The State’s Use of the Prosecutor’s Vouching for Riddle’s Credibility Violated Due Process Under Napue v. Illinois, 360 U.S. 264 (1958).**

The Fifth Circuit has long recognized that a prosecutor’s vouching for a witness’s credibility violates due process. *See, e.g., United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.,* 310 U.S. 150, 240 (1940) (holding that prosecutorial misconduct undermines the fairness of a trial). In *Napue*, the Supreme Court held that a prosecutor’s knowing use of false evidence to secure a conviction violates the Due Process Clause. *Napue*, 360 U.S. at 269. Here, the prosecutor (Hill) testified that Riddle was a liar, effectively vouching for his own credibility in rebutting Riddle’s claim that Hill had threatened him. This amounted to an improper injection of the prosecutor’s personal opinion into the trial, depriving Riddle of a fair proceeding. *See id.* at 269-70.

**II. The Trial Court’s Failure to Instruct the Jury on the Lesser-Included Offense of Murder Violated Due Process Under In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970) (precedent recognized in earlier cases).**

While *Winship* was decided in 1970, its principle—that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt—was firmly established in earlier cases. *See, e.g., Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895) (holding that the presumption of innocence requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt). Here, the trial court failed to instruct the jury on the lesser-included offense of murder, despite evidence suggesting that Riddle acted in the heat of passion or under provocation. This omission deprived Riddle of a fair opportunity to have the jury consider all reasonable alternatives to capital murder. *See id.* at 453-54.

**III. The State’s Withholding of Exculpatory Evidence Violated Due Process Under Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963).**

The Supreme Court has long held that the suppression of material exculpatory evidence violates due process. *See, e.g., Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (holding that prosecutorial misconduct in withholding evidence undermines due process). In *Brady*, the Court reaffirmed this principle, holding that the suppression of evidence favorable to the defense violates the Due Process Clause. *Brady*, 373 U.S. at 87. Here, the State failed to disclose evidence that could have impeached Bybee’s credibility, including Bybee’s prior inconsistent statements. This suppression undermined Riddle’s ability to mount an effective defense. *See id.* at 87-88.

**IV. The Trial Court’s Admission of Unreliable Evidence Violated Due Process Under Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736 (1948).**

The Supreme Court has held that the admission of unreliable evidence violates due process. *Townsend*, 334 U.S. at 741. Here, the trial court admitted evidence of Riddle’s prior escape attempts, which were irrelevant to the charged offense and highly prejudicial. This admission deprived Riddle of a fair trial. *See id.* at 741-42.

**V. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle an Evidentiary Hearing Under Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963) (precedent recognized in earlier cases).**

While *Townsend v. Sain* was decided in 1963, its principle—that a habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing when factual disputes exist—was firmly established in earlier cases. *See, e.g., Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 462 (1953) (holding that a habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing when factual disputes exist). Here, the district court denied Riddle an evidentiary hearing despite unresolved factual disputes, including the credibility of Bybee’s testimony. This denial was error. *See Brown*, 344 U.S. at 462.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Riddle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s denial of habeas relief and remand for an evidentiary hearing.
