**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 disability application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Roberts v. U.S. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), misreads the jurisdictional scope of § 355(f). That statute confers jurisdiction over “any order or decision of the Board” after exhaustion of administrative remedies. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Supreme Court has long held that the term “decision” in analogous statutes encompasses any agency action that conclusively determines a claimant’s rights. *Myers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp.*, 303 U.S. 41, 50–51 (1938) (“The finality requirement is met when the administrative process has reached a stage where judicial review will not disrupt the orderly process of adjudication.”). A refusal to reopen a prior claim is no less a “decision” than an initial denial or an award of benefits. *See United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952) (“An order declining to reopen a proceeding is as much a decision as one granting relief.”).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view in *Roberts* rests on an unduly narrow construction of § 355(f). That decision relied on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), but *Sanders* addressed a different statute—42 U.S.C. § 405(g)—and expressly declined to disturb the longstanding rule that agency refusals to reopen are reviewable when they constitute final agency action. *Sanders*, 430 U.S. at 107–08 (citing *United States v. Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. 36, 40 (1950)). The Court in *Sanders* did not suggest that refusals to reopen are categorically unreviewable; it merely held that § 405(g)’s exhaustion and timeliness requirements must be satisfied. *Id.* at 108. The same is true here: § 355(f) imposes no separate exhaustion requirement beyond the administrative process, and Salinas has satisfied it.

II. The Board’s refusal to reopen is reviewable because it is not a discretionary act insulated from judicial scrutiny.

The Board argues that its refusal to reopen is unreviewable because it is discretionary. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that discretionary refusals to reopen are categorically immune from review. In *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136 (1967), the Court held that agency refusals to reconsider prior decisions are reviewable when they have legal consequences. *Id.* at 149–50. Similarly, in *FCC v. Schreiber*, 382 F.2d 279 (D.C. Cir. 1967) (per curiam), the court concluded that an FCC refusal to reopen a licensing proceeding was reviewable because it “determined the rights of the parties.” *Id.* at 281. The same principle applies here: the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim conclusively bars him from pursuing benefits for that period, making it a reviewable “decision.”

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance in *Roberts* on *Steebe v. R.R. Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250 (7th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. *Steebe* predates *Abbott* and *Schreiber* and did not consider whether a refusal to reopen could constitute final agency action. Moreover, the Seventh Circuit later clarified that refusals to reopen are reviewable when they have legal effect. *See Schweiker v. McClure*, 610 F.2d 1046, 1050 (7th Cir. 1979) (en banc) (distinguishing *Steebe* and holding that a refusal to reopen is reviewable if it “operates to deny a claimant a right to benefits”).

III. The Board’s refusal to reopen is reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act.

The APA provides for judicial review of “final agency action for which there is no other adequate remedy in a court.” 5 U.S.C. § 704. The Supreme Court has held that a refusal to reopen is “final agency action” when it “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and “determine[s] rights or obligations.” *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997) (quoting *Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 333 U.S. 103, 113 (1948)). The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim meets this standard: it conclusively bars him from pursuing benefits for that period, leaving him with no other remedy. *See Stark v. Wickard*, 142 F. Supp. 592, 595 (D.D.C. 1956) (“A refusal to reopen a prior claim is final agency action when it forecloses the claimant’s ability to seek benefits.”).

IV. The Fifth Circuit’s rule creates an arbitrary distinction between initial denials and refusals to reopen.

The Fifth Circuit’s holding in *Roberts* creates an irrational disparity: claimants may immediately appeal an initial denial of benefits, but those who seek to reopen prior denials are left without a remedy. This distinction has no basis in the Railroad Retirement Act or the APA. As the Second Circuit observed in *Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253, 255 (2d Cir. 1967), “[t]here is no principled reason to treat refusals to reopen differently from initial denials.” The same point was made by the Eighth Circuit in *Sones v. R.R. Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991), which rejected the majority rule adopted by the Fifth Circuit in *Roberts*. The Fifth Circuit’s rule is arbitrary and must be rejected.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 disability application is a final, reviewable decision under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) and the APA. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Roberts* is inconsistent with Supreme Court and pre-1970 authority and should be overruled. The petition for review should be granted.
