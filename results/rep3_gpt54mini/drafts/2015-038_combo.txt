ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of regulatory authority over non-federal lands and waters merely because they lie within the exterior boundaries of a park unit. The statute instead preserves “the jurisdiction and responsibility of the State of Alaska over fish and wildlife” and limits only the treatment of “public lands” within conservation system units. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). Read in context, that provision does not create an enclave in which state, Native, corporate, or private lands become immune from otherwise applicable federal regulation. Congress knew how to withdraw federal authority over nonfederal property when it wanted to do so, and it did not do so here.

The controlling principle is that federal power does not depend on naked title when Congress has acted pursuant to an enumerated power. In United States v. Gratiot, 39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 526, 537 (1840), the Court recognized that Congress may regulate federal interests even when private rights are implicated. Likewise, Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States, 243 U.S. 389, 404-05 (1917), held that the United States may impose regulations necessary to protect public lands and adjacent uses. That understanding has deep roots in the public lands cases. Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525-26 (1897), upheld federal regulation reaching privately owned land where necessary to protect adjacent federal land from injury. The Court explained that Congress may not be “defeated by the simple expedient of extending their fences.” Id. at 525. So too here: ANILCA cannot be read to disable federal rules merely because regulated conduct occurs on land or water that is not itself federally owned.

The Court has long recognized that federal regulation may reach state and private conduct when the subject lies within federal authority. In McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 436 (1819), the Court held that states may not control valid federal action by indirect means. In Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 196-97 (1824), the Court sustained federal supremacy over navigation, a principle especially relevant where the Nation River is navigable. United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935), confirms that federal authority over navigable waters coexists with state interests but is not displaced by them. And United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 426-27 (1940), emphasized the breadth of federal control over navigation and related uses. These cases all confirm that state ownership of submerged lands does not by itself bar federal regulation of conduct on or affecting those waters.

Nothing in ANILCA’s text suggests a departure from those settled rules. Section 103(c) states that no lands within conservation system units “which are not owned by the United States” are “subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). That language is a narrow choice-of-law instruction, not a broad immunity for nonfederal lands from generally applicable federal regulation. Statutes are not read to work implied repeals absent a clear statement. The Court has repeatedly required “a clear and manifest” congressional purpose before finding that federal law has surrendered a traditional field or curtailed federal power. United States v. Texas, 507 U.S. 529, 534 (1993); see also United States v. Greathouse, 166 U.S. 601, 605-06 (1897). ANILCA contains no such clear withdrawal.

The statutory structure reinforces that conclusion. Congress enacted ANILCA against a background of broad federal authority over national parks and preserves, not as a wholesale divestiture of that authority. The Court has often read statutes in light of their object and the practical consequences of contrary interpretations. Sutherland v. Mayer, 271 U.S. 272, 275-76 (1926). A construction that would permit private or state parcels within park boundaries to become federal-law-free zones would defeat ANILCA’s evident purpose of conserving park resources. That is especially so where the challenged regulation is not targeted at state ownership as such, but at a particular use—hovercraft operation—within the park unit.

Petitioner’s reading also cannot be squared with the Court’s cases respecting federal regulation of activities adjacent to or affecting federal property. In Hunt v. United States, 278 U.S. 96, 100-01 (1928), the Court upheld federal action necessary to protect federal lands from fire and related harm. In United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co., 174 U.S. 690, 703-08 (1899), the Court recognized federal authority to regulate navigable waters to protect the public interest. And in Butte City Water Co. v. Baker, 196 U.S. 119, 124-25 (1905), the Court sustained federal control over uses affecting federal interests in western lands and waters. These decisions reflect the consistent rule that federal power may operate on nonfederal property when needed to protect federal land, water, and resources.

Finally, petitioner’s argument would transform a jurisdictional boundary into a substantive immunity, contrary to first principles. The Constitution does not require Congress to surrender regulatory authority simply because federal lands are geographically intermingled with state or private holdings. See Tennessee v. Davis, 100 U.S. 257, 262-63 (1879). ANILCA’s text does not say otherwise. Because the hovercraft ban is a regulation of park use, not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands,” it remains valid as applied here. The judgment should be affirmed.
