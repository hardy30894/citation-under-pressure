### Argument

The court of appeals correctly held that punitive damages under Title VII do not require proof that the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the statutory mental state. The governing statute authorizes punitive damages when the employer acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That text focuses on state of mind, not on an additional, free-standing requirement of exceptional misconduct. This Court’s Title VII decisions likewise tie liability to intentional discrimination and Congress’s chosen mental-state standard, not to a separate “outrage” element. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973); St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993).

Under McDonnell Douglas, a plaintiff proves discrimination by showing a prima facie case and then demonstrating that the employer’s stated reason is pretextual. 411 U.S. at 802-05. Hicks held that once the employer’s explanation is disbelieved, “no additional proof of discrimination is required.” 509 U.S. at 511. Those cases establish the baseline Title VII framework: intentional discrimination may be inferred from pretext. They do not impose an extra threshold of outrage for damages. To the contrary, Congress added punitive damages in the Civil Rights Act of 1991 precisely because “additional remedies under Federal law are needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a note; see Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994).

The statutory text confirms the point. “Malice” and “reckless indifference” are traditional punitive-damages concepts that refer to the defendant’s subjective culpability. Section 1981a(b)(1) asks whether the employer acted with the requisite mental state toward federally protected rights. It does not say that punitive damages are available only if the conduct was, in addition, “egregious” or “outrageous.” Where Congress includes a standard, courts should not graft on a second, more demanding limitation. See West Virginia Univ. Hospitals, Inc. v. Casey, 499 U.S. 83, 98-99 (1991); Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 286.

The courts of appeals’ pre-1991 punitive-damages decisions also show that “egregiousness” is not an independent element. In Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), this Court held in the § 1983 context that punitive damages may be awarded when the defendant acts “with reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” Id. at 56. The operative inquiry is culpable state of mind, not a separate classification of the conduct as outrageous. Likewise, Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc., 453 U.S. 247 (1981), recognized that punitive damages turn on deterrence and culpability. Id. at 266-70. Congress borrowed this settled punitive-damages vocabulary when it enacted § 1981a. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 257-58.

Nor does Thompson v. Pharmaceutical Corp., 888 F.2d 673 (10th Cir. 1989), or EEOC v. American Nat’l Bank, 652 F.2d 1176 (4th Cir. 1981), support an independent “outrage” requirement. Those cases involved pre-1991 law and the unavailability or narrow availability of punitive damages under Title VII. Congress changed that law. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 252-55. The question now is what Congress enacted, not what some pre-1991 court thought Title VII implied.

The court of appeals also properly rejected the proposition that only “extraordinarily egregious cases” qualify. Legislative-history snippets cannot override statutory text. See Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). The statute asks whether the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference; that standard already screens out ordinary discrimination and negligence. Requiring separate “egregious” conduct would collapse the statute’s mental-state test into an amorphous and judge-made superrequirement. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against adding words to an enacted statute. See Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135, 147-48 (1994).

The D.C. Circuit’s rule is also consistent with Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), and Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775 (1998). Those decisions emphasize that Title VII turns on employer responsibility, agency principles, and deterrence. Ellerth, 524 U.S. at 764-65; Faragher, 524 U.S. at 802-05. Punitive damages serve that same deterrent function where the employer acts with conscious disregard of protected rights. Nothing in those cases suggests a separate, free-floating outrage test.

Finally, the rule urged by petitioner would create arbitrary line-drawing and undermine the statute’s remedial design. Intentional discrimination accompanied by reckless indifference is already serious enough to justify punitive damages; conduct need not also be “outrageous” in some undefined sense. The better reading is the one the court below adopted: if the plaintiff proves intentional discrimination and the requisite malice or reckless indifference, punitive damages may be submitted to the jury. That interpretation faithfully applies the statute, respects this Court’s Title VII precedents, and preserves the jury’s role in assessing punitive damages. See McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. 792; Hicks, 509 U.S. 502; Smith, 461 U.S. 30; Landgraf, 511 U.S. 244.
