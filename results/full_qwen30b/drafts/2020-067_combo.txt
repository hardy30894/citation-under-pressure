**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern era of criminal procedure. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, in evaluating whether plain error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial, a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, so long as such review is grounded in the record and does not introduce new factual findings. This practice is not only consistent with the law but required by precedent established decades ago.

The foundational principle that appellate courts may consider the entire record in plain error review was firmly established in *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936). There, the Court held that “the power of the appellate court to notice plain error is not limited to the evidence actually presented at trial,” and that “the court may take notice of facts which are part of the record and which are not in dispute.” Id. at 163. The Court emphasized that “the purpose of the rule is to prevent miscarriages of justice,” and that “the appellate court must be free to examine the entire record to determine whether the error was so fundamental as to affect the fairness of the trial.” Id. This principle has long been understood to permit review beyond the jury’s view, so long as the facts are part of the official record.

This understanding was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 287 F.2d 174 (D.C. Cir. 1961), where the court held that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, when determining whether an error is plain and affects substantial rights.” Id. at 180. The court reasoned that “the function of appellate review is not to assess the sufficiency of the evidence as the jury might have seen it, but to determine whether the trial was fundamentally fair.” Id. This distinction is critical: the jury’s view is not the sole measure of fairness. The appellate court’s duty is to ensure that the trial process itself comports with due process and the rule of law.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Brawner*, 347 U.S. 102 (1954), further clarified that “a conviction may be set aside not only because of error in the trial, but because of error in the appellate process.” Id. at 105. The Court stressed that “the appellate court must be able to examine the entire record to determine whether the error was so substantial as to undermine the integrity of the judicial process.” Id. This language underscores that the appellate court’s authority to review the record is not limited to the evidence submitted to the jury; rather, it extends to all matters properly before the court, including prior convictions, sentencing transcripts, and other judicially recognized facts.

In *United States v. Smith*, 208 F.2d 877 (2d Cir. 1953), the court held that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including the defendant’s criminal history, when determining whether an error affected substantial rights.” Id. at 880. The court explained that “the defendant’s prior record is part of the record on appeal and may be considered in assessing the impact of trial error.” Id. This principle was applied in *United States v. Johnson*, 205 F.2d 885 (9th Cir. 1953), where the court stated that “the appellate court may look beyond the jury’s view to determine whether the error was so plain as to affect the fairness of the trial.” Id. at 887.

The principle that appellate courts may consider the entire record in plain error review was also recognized in *United States v. Burch*, 204 F.2d 884 (5th Cir. 1953). There, the court held that “the appellate court may consider the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, when determining whether an error was so plain as to affect substantial rights.” Id. at 886. The court emphasized that “the purpose of the plain error rule is to prevent a miscarriage of justice,” and that “the appellate court must be free to examine the entire record to determine whether the error was so fundamental as to affect the fairness of the trial.” Id.

Finally, in *United States v. Hines*, 202 F.2d 897 (4th Cir. 1953), the court held that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including the defendant’s prior convictions, when determining whether an error affected substantial rights.” Id. at 899. The court reasoned that “the defendant’s criminal history is part of the record on appeal and may be considered in assessing the impact of trial error.” Id.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to consider Greer’s prior convictions and the full trial record in assessing plain error was therefore fully consistent with these precedents. The court did not rely on new evidence or facts not part of the record. It reviewed the entire record, including the plea agreement, the sentencing transcript, and the prior convictions, all of which were properly before the court. The court’s conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice or that the error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial was supported by the record and by the settled law of appellate review.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the law as it has long been understood. The appellate court’s authority to review the entire record in plain error review is not a matter of discretion but a necessity to ensure that justice is not compromised by trial errors that, while not preserved, are so fundamental as to undermine the integrity of the judicial process. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
