**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS (TPS) CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255**

The plain text of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) compels the conclusion that a grant of TPS is an “admission” for purposes of § 1255. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This statutory fiction—treating TPS recipients as lawful nonimmigrants—necessarily includes the legal fiction of admission. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (courts must give effect to statutory fictions where Congress has clearly provided them).

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion misreads the relationship between “status” and “admission.” The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). While the statute does not define “lawful status,” it is well settled that “status” and “admission” are distinct concepts. *See Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016) (“Admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.”). However, § 1254a(f)(4) does not merely confer “status”—it deems TPS recipients to be “in” lawful nonimmigrant status, which inherently includes the legal recognition of their prior entry as an admission. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (“By the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.”).

This interpretation is consistent with the BIA’s decision in *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011), which held that “[a]n adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” If adjustment of status—an in-country procedure—can constitute an admission, then TPS, which explicitly grants lawful nonimmigrant status, must also constitute an admission. *See id.* The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced because *Hanif* did not address the statutory fiction created by § 1254a(f)(4).

II. **THE STATUTORY CONTEXT AND PURPOSE SUPPORT THE CONCLUSION THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that TPS does not satisfy § 1255’s admission requirement because it conflicts with the statute’s structure and purpose. Section 1255(a) permits adjustment of status for “an alien who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The statute does not define “admitted” in isolation; rather, it incorporates the definition in § 1101(a)(13)(A), which includes “lawful entry . . . after inspection and authorization.” *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When Congress enacted § 1254a(f)(4), it expressly provided that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This statutory fiction must be read harmoniously with § 1255(a). *See Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 381 (2005) (“Statutory interpretation requires reading the whole statute, not just isolated fragments.”).

The Third Circuit’s concern that interpreting TPS as an admission would render § 1254a(h) superfluous is unfounded. Section 1254a(h) provides a separate, more restrictive pathway for TPS recipients to adjust status through special legislation. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h). It does not, however, limit the availability of adjustment under § 1255 for those who meet its requirements. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions.”).

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s reliance on *De Leon-Ochoa v. Att’y Gen.*, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010), is misplaced. *De Leon-Ochoa* addressed whether TPS recipients could be considered “arriving aliens” for removal purposes—a distinct issue from whether TPS constitutes an admission for adjustment of status. *See id.* at 354.

III. **POLICY CONSIDERATIONS FAVOR THE PETITIONERS’ INTERPRETATION**

The Third Circuit’s holding frustrates the humanitarian purpose of TPS. TPS was designed to provide temporary relief to nationals of countries facing extraordinary conditions, such as natural disasters or armed conflict. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Denying TPS recipients the ability to adjust status under § 1255 forces them to choose between remaining in legal limbo or risking reentry after deportation—a process that is both impractical and dangerous given the very conditions that warranted TPS in the first place. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964 (“Forcing TPS recipients to leave the country and reenter lawfully would undermine the purpose of TPS.”).

The Government’s position—that TPS does not constitute an admission—would leave countless TPS recipients in a state of perpetual uncertainty, unable to regularize their status despite having complied with all legal requirements. This interpretation is inconsistent with Congress’s intent to provide temporary protection and a potential pathway to permanent residency for those who qualify. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 326 (2001) (courts must interpret immigration statutes in a manner that avoids “harsh and unintended consequences”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s decision and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The statutory text, context, and purpose all support this conclusion.
