# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLRA PERMITS A POPULATION-REDUCTION ORDER WHEN, AS HERE, IT IS THE ONLY REMEDY THAT WILL CURE PROVEN CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS

This Court has long recognized that severe prison overcrowding may itself constitute the moving force behind conditions that violate the Eighth Amendment. In *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), this Court reviewed a district court order directing prison officials "to reduce the inmate population" at a correctional facility "until the population fell to the prison's approximate design capacity." *Id.* at 340. This Court did not suggest that a population cap was an impermissible remedy; it addressed only whether the underlying double-celling practice itself violated the Eighth Amendment on the facts presented. The remedial mechanism—capping population at "design capacity"—was accepted as a legitimate judicial tool where overcrowding produces constitutional harm.

Similarly, in *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1979), this Court upheld a comprehensive remedial order addressing conditions that included "continued overcrowding" among the "interdependence of the conditions producing the violation." This Court explained that "[i]n fashioning a remedy, the District Court had ample authority to go beyond earlier orders and to address each element contributing to the violation," and that "taking the long and unhappy history of the litigation into account, the court was justified in entering a comprehensive order." *Id.* The three-judge court's population order here rests on precisely this authority: after years of failed remedial efforts, including the very receivership at issue in the companion proceedings below, the court found that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and that no lesser remedy could succeed.

Petitioners' suggestion that overcrowding claims present some categorically different, disfavored species of Eighth Amendment claim finds no support in this Court's precedent. In *Costello v. Wainwright*, 430 U.S. 325 (1977), this Court recognized without hesitation that prisoners could attack "the overcrowding in Florida's prisons as violative of the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause," and that a district court could properly "order[] the Division of Corrections either to reduce the inmate population or to increase prison capacity." *Id.* at 325-26. The remedial architecture approved in *Costello*—reduce population or increase capacity—is the same architecture Congress permitted to survive in the PLRA and that the three-judge court employed below.

## II. THE PLRA'S NEED-NARROWNESS-INTRUSIVENESS REQUIREMENTS ARE SATISFIED BY FINDINGS OF SYSTEMWIDE, INTERLOCKING CONSTITUTIONAL FAILURE

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be narrowly drawn, extend no further than necessary, and be the least intrusive means to correct the violation. These requirements do not forbid systemwide relief where, as here, the violation is itself systemwide. This Court's decision in *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 (1991), reaffirmed that Eighth Amendment analysis under *Rhodes* asks "whether the deprivation" was "sufficiently serious," and that courts may examine the totality of prison conditions rather than isolated incidents. Where, as the three-judge court found, overcrowding pervades every prison in the system and drives deficiencies in medical care, mental health care, and physical safety alike, a systemwide population remedy is not overbroad—it is coextensive with the violation itself.

Petitioners' own conduct confirms the necessity of the remedy. The receivership proceedings, detailed in the Ninth Circuit's opinion below, document years of California's admitted inability to comply with stipulated remedial orders, culminating in the district court's finding that the State "admitted that it was unable to comply with the injunctive relief to which it had stipulated." *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, No. 08-17412 (9th Cir. Mar. 25, 2009). This history of non-compliance—capital-project funding disputes, contempt motions, and repeated failed turnaround plans—demonstrates precisely the kind of entrenched institutional failure that justifies comprehensive relief. As this Court observed in *Hutto*, when officials have been given "repeated opportunities" to remedy unconstitutional conditions and have failed, courts are "justified in entering a comprehensive order to insure against the risk of inadequate compliance." 437 U.S. at 687.

## III. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT PROPERLY DEFERRED TO STATE OFFICIALS WHILE ENSURING AN EFFECTIVE REMEDY

The order under review does not micromanage California's penal system; it sets an outer population ceiling and leaves to the State the choice of means—new construction, parole reform, sentencing adjustments, or release—by which to comply. This structure honors the deference this Court's precedents demand. In *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78 (1987), this Court emphasized that "courts are ill equipped to deal with the increasingly urgent problems of prison administration," counseling deference to prison officials' choice of methods. The population cap here does exactly that: it leaves California free to select its preferred method of compliance, exactly as the *Costello* remedy allowed Florida "either to reduce the inmate population or to increase prison capacity." 430 U.S. at 326.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the three-judge district court should be affirmed.
