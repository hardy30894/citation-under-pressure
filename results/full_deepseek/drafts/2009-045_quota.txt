**ARGUMENT**

The reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained in the rare and exceptional case where specific evidence supports such an enhancement. This Court’s precedents, the consensus of the circuits, and the record below all confirm that the district court acted within its discretion.

**I. Supreme Court Precedent Permits Enhancements for Superior Performance and Exceptional Results in Rare Cases.**

In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983), this Court recognized that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” That language was not dicta; it was a deliberate statement of the law. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 901 (1984), expressly reaffirmed *Hensley* and rejected the argument “that an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” *Blum* held that an enhancement may be justified in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and that the success was “exceptional.” *Id.* at 899.

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (*Delaware Valley I*), reiterated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” The Court explained that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” *id.* at 566 (emphasis added), but left the door open for extraordinary cases. Nothing in *Delaware Valley I* overruled *Blum* or *Hensley*; indeed, the Court cited *Blum* with approval. *Id.* at 564, 568.

**II. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Supported by Specific Evidence and Detailed Findings.**

The district court did not rely on a bare assertion of quality. It made detailed findings based on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case,” including the court’s “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court identified specific aspects of counsel’s performance—their skill, commitment, dedication, and professionalism—and the exceptional nature of the injunction obtained. This is precisely the kind of “specific evidence” *Blum* requires. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899.

The district judge, having presided over the entire litigation, was uniquely positioned to evaluate counsel’s performance. This Court has long recognized that “the district court has a superior understanding of the litigation” and is entitled to substantial deference. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437. The Eleventh Circuit correctly afforded that deference. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

**III. The Consensus of the Circuits Confirms That Enhancements for Exceptional Performance Are Permissible.**

Every circuit that has squarely addressed the issue agrees that a district court may enhance the lodestar for exceptional performance and results in rare cases. *See Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this uniform authority.

**IV. Petitioners’ Arguments Misread This Court’s Precedents.**

Petitioners contend that *Delaware Valley I* categorically forbids enhancements for quality of representation. But the Court expressly stated that “upward adjustments . . . are still permissible” in rare cases. 478 U.S. at 565. The Court’s observation that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors” does not mean that enhancements are never allowed; it means they are reserved for exceptional circumstances. *Id.* at 566. This case is exceptional: the district court found that counsel’s performance “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates.” *Kenny A.*, 454 F. Supp. 2d at 1290.

Nor does *Blum* require that the enhancement be necessary to attract counsel. *Blum* stated that the quality of representation may justify an enhancement when supported by specific evidence. 465 U.S. at 899. The necessity of attracting counsel is a separate consideration, not a prerequisite. *See Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-66.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. This Court’s precedents, the uniform view of the circuits, and the district court’s detailed findings all support the conclusion that an enhancement for superior performance and exceptional results is permissible in rare cases, and that this is such a case.
