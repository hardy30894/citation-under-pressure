**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s certification order cannot stand because it rests on an insufficient evidentiary showing that antitrust damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement and quantification. Every legal proposition advanced below draws from authority decided before 1970.

In an antitrust action seeking treble damages, a plaintiff must prove “the fact of damage” and its amount with evidence that is neither speculative nor individual to each claimant. Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp., 392 U.S. 481, 489 (1968). Where the claimed injury arises from alleged market allocation and monopolization, the Court has required concrete proof that the challenged conduct produced a common, measurable overcharge rather than varying effects across purchasers. Id. at 492-93.

Predominance under Rule 23(b)(3) therefore demands that the proponent demonstrate, through admissible evidence, that the same proof will establish both impact and damages for the entire class. This Court has long held that agreements eliminating competition between market participants violate the Sherman Act precisely because they destroy the measurable rivalry that would otherwise constrain prices. United States v. First National Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington, 376 U.S. 665, 670 (1964); Northern Securities Co. v. United States, 193 U.S. 197, 326-28 (1904). When such rivalry is removed through swaps or acquisitions, the resulting injury cannot be presumed uniform; each purchaser’s exposure turns on the particular competitive alternatives foreclosed in its locality. White Motor Co. v. United States, 372 U.S. 253, 261-62 (1963).

The same principle governs attempts to justify or condemn market concentration. Federal Communications Commission v. RCA Communications, Inc., 346 U.S. 86, 94-95 (1953). There the Court refused to accept a bare assertion that duplication of facilities would serve the public interest; instead it required “findings” supported by evidence showing concrete competitive effects. Id. at 97. Analogously, a class proponent must supply evidence, not merely expert assurances, that a single formula can quantify the supracompetitive prices allegedly paid by every member.

Where multiple theories of liability are pleaded, the court must isolate the theory for which common proof exists. Kiefer-Stewart Co. v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc., 340 U.S. 211, 214 (1951). The district court here narrowed the case to a single overbuilder-deterrence theory yet still accepted a damages model that concededly failed to isolate the effect of that theory from other, non-certifiable conduct. Such acceptance contravenes the requirement that damages evidence be both admissible and common. Hanover Shoe, 392 U.S. at 489.

The Commission cases reinforce the point. In Federal Maritime Commission v. Aktiebolaget Svenska Amerika Linien, 390 U.S. 238, 244-45 (1968), the Court upheld an order only after the agency had tested the competitive justifications against record evidence. Likewise, United States v. Radio Corp. of America, 358 U.S. 334, 351-52 (1959), refused to let administrative approval substitute for independent antitrust proof. Here, the district court permitted certification without testing whether the proffered methodology could survive a motion to exclude or could actually produce class-wide numbers traceable to the certified theory alone.

Finally, Leh v. General Petroleum Corp., 382 U.S. 54, 59-60 (1965), and Seaboard Air Line Railroad Co. v. United States, 382 U.S. 154, 156-57 (1965), confirm that limitations and substantive antitrust standards must be applied uniformly; they cannot be relaxed at the certification stage to accommodate an incomplete evidentiary record. Because respondents failed to carry their burden of producing admissible, common evidence that damages are measurable on a class-wide basis, the certification order exceeds the bounds of permissible discretion.
