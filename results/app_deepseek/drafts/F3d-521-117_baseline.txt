**ARGUMENT**

**THE PAT-FRISK OF SOARES VIOLATED THE FOURTH AMENDMENT BECAUSE THE OFFICERS LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION THAT HE WAS ARMED AND DANGEROUS.**

The district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress because the officers lacked the requisite reasonable suspicion to justify a pat-frisk. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), a lawful investigatory stop may be accompanied by a protective frisk only when “the officer is justified in believing that the individual whose suspicious behavior he is investigating at close range is armed and presently dangerous.” This standard requires “specific and articulable facts” that would warrant a reasonably prudent officer to believe the suspect is armed. *Id.* at 21. The government cannot meet this burden.

**A. The “Furtive Movements” Observed Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion of Danger.**

The officers observed passengers moving down and to their left before the car stopped. However, such movements, without more, do not establish reasonable suspicion that a person is armed. *See United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d 38, 40 (1st Cir. 2005) (holding that “furtive movements” alone, in a high-crime area, are insufficient to justify a frisk). The movements here were ambiguous—they could have been consistent with reaching for a seatbelt, adjusting clothing, or any number of innocent actions. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989) (requiring that the totality of circumstances support a particularized suspicion). The officers did not see any object or bulge that suggested a weapon. *Cf. Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 111-12 (1977) (upholding frisk only after officer saw a bulge). Without more, these movements cannot justify the intrusion.

**B. Soares’s Agitation and Verbal Abuse Did Not Justify a Frisk.**

Soares’s behavior—rocking, flailing his hands, and using profanity—was disruptive but did not give rise to reasonable suspicion that he was armed. The Supreme Court has held that “nervous, evasive behavior” is a factor, but it is not dispositive. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000). Here, Soares’s agitation was a reaction to being detained and ordered at gunpoint. His verbal abuse, while offensive, is not a reliable indicator of dangerousness. *See United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d 57, 66 (1st Cir. 2007) (holding that “mere refusal to cooperate” or “hostile demeanor” does not create reasonable suspicion). The officers had already ordered Soares to keep his hands visible, and he initially complied. His later movements, though erratic, did not suggest he was reaching for a weapon. *See United States v. Ruidiaz*, 529 F.3d 25, 30 (1st Cir. 2008) (finding no reasonable suspicion where suspect’s movements were “not directed toward a particular location” that could conceal a weapon).

**C. The Officers’ Subjective Unease Does Not Satisfy the Objective Standard.**

Officer Reid testified that he “got a little uneasy” and “felt really uneasy” about Soares’s behavior. However, the Fourth Amendment requires an objective assessment of whether the facts available to the officer at the moment of the frisk would warrant a person of reasonable caution to believe the suspect is armed. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21-22. An officer’s subjective fear, without more, is insufficient. *See United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 273 (2002) (emphasizing objective reasonableness). Here, the officers had already removed Soares from the car, and he was surrounded by multiple officers. The risk of harm was minimal, and the frisk was not justified. *See United States v. Schiavo*, 29 F.3d 6, 9 (1st Cir. 1994) (holding that frisk was unreasonable where suspect was “surrounded by officers” and posed no immediate threat).

**D. The Presence of Backup Officers Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion.**

The arrival of two additional officers did not retroactively justify the frisk. The frisk was based on the same facts that existed before their arrival. *See United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981) (requiring that suspicion be based on “the whole picture” at the time of the frisk). The backup officers merely responded to Reid’s request; they did not observe any new threatening behavior. *Cf. United States v. Johnson*, 256 F.3d 895, 904 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (holding that frisk was unreasonable where officers “did not articulate any specific facts” suggesting the suspect was armed).

**CONCLUSION**

The pat-frisk of Soares was not supported by reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous. The district court’s denial of the motion to suppress must be reversed, and Soares’s conviction must be vacated.
