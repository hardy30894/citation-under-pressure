**ARGUMENT**

**Agent Jones's Testimony That Hill Was Lying and Behaved Like a Guilty Man Was Plain Error Requiring Reversal**

**A. Standard of Review**

Because Hill did not object to Agent Jones's testimony at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Reversal is warranted where there is (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affected the defendant's substantial rights, and (4) that "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* at 732, 736 (quoting *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985)). Hill satisfies each element.

**B. Admission of Agent Jones's Credibility Testimony Was Error**

It is fundamental that a witness—expert or lay—may not tell the jury that another witness is lying or that his conduct reflects guilt. Credibility determinations belong exclusively to the jury. *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1266 (10th Cir. 1999) (expert may not offer an opinion that vouches for or against the credibility of another witness because that determination is reserved for the jury). This Court has repeatedly enforced this rule in the context of law-enforcement testimony purporting to identify "deception" through training and experience. *See United States v. Toledo*, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470–71 (10th Cir. 1993) (expert testimony that effectively tells the jury a witness is truthful or untruthful invades the jury's province). Similarly, expert opinion testimony is confined by Federal Rule of Evidence 608(a), which permits only testimony about a witness's general "reputation for having a character for truthfulness or untruthfulness," or opinion "about that character"—not opinion testimony that a specific statement, on a specific occasion, was a lie. Fed. R. Evid. 608(a).

Agent Jones's testimony crossed every one of these lines. He did not testify to Hill's general reputation for truthfulness; he told the jury, as an FBI-trained expert, that Hill's specific answers during interrogation "do[] not make sense," that Hill's evasiveness was "common among the criminal element to keep law enforcement at bay," that he had "[n]ever in [his] career" seen an innocent person express a willingness to die rather than face charges, and that Hill's invocations of God were themselves "deceptive statements." This was not testimony about demeanor or background; it was a direct, guilt-oriented credibility assessment delivered with the imprimatur of federal law-enforcement expertise. Such testimony is precisely what this Court has condemned as invading the jury's exclusive role. *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1266; *see also United States v. Rodriguez-Felix*, 450 F.3d 1117, 1123–24 (10th Cir. 2006) (discussing the limits Rule 704 and related evidentiary principles place on expert testimony that effectively decides an ultimate issue reserved for the jury). By labeling Hill's statements as the deceptive utterances "common among the criminal element," Agent Jones did not merely comment on demeanor—he told the jury, in the guise of expertise, that Hill was guilty.

**C. The Error Was Plain**

The impropriety of a law-enforcement witness opining that a defendant's statements are lies, and that his behavior matches that of guilty persons rather than innocent ones, is not a novel or debatable proposition; it follows directly from *Charley* and *Toledo*. An error is "plain" when it is "clear" or "obvious" under current law. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. Because this Court's precedent squarely forecloses an expert witness from vouching for or against a defendant's truthfulness, the district court's failure to exclude Agent Jones's testimony sua sponte was clear and obvious error.

**D. The Error Affected Hill's Substantial Rights**

An error affects substantial rights when it had a prejudicial effect on the outcome of the proceedings. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. The record here demonstrates precisely that kind of prejudice. This was not an overwhelming case against Hill: the first trial on nearly identical evidence ended in a deadlocked jury and mistrial as to Hill, even though his brother Vernon was convicted on the same set of facts. The government's own theory of the case changed between trials—Hill went from an alleged principal robber to an alleged getaway driver—reflecting genuine uncertainty about his role. In a case this closely balanced, an FBI agent's direct assurance that Hill's own words were lies, and that his behavior mirrored that of the "criminal element," supplied exactly the kind of credibility-tipping testimony capable of swinging a jury that had previously been unable to reach a verdict. The testimony did not merely color the jury's assessment of collateral matters; it went to the heart of whether Hill was truthful about his own innocence.

**E. The Error Seriously Affected the Fairness and Integrity of the Proceedings**

Allowing a federal agent to tell a jury, cloaked in the authority of training and experience, that a defendant's protestations of innocence—including his references to his faith—were themselves markers of deception "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. Permitting such testimony to stand, particularly where it likely proved decisive in a case where a properly instructed jury had previously failed to convict, would sanction the erosion of the jury's role as sole arbiter of credibility. *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1266.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, Hill's convictions should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
