## ARGUMENT

### I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before Its Enactment

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) provides that a prior deportation order “shall be reinstated” when an alien “reenters” the United States illegally. The present-tense verb “reenters” unambiguously refers to reentries that occur after the statute’s effective date. When Congress intends a statute to apply to past conduct, it typically uses language such as “has reentered” or “reentered.” *See Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994) (explaining that the “presumption against retroactive legislation” is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence). The statute lacks any express command that it apply to reentries that occurred before 1997, and this Court must give effect to that silence. *See Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) (“Congressional enactments and administrative rules will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.”).

The Tenth Circuit erred by reading the statute to reach pre-enactment reentries. The court relied on the general purpose of the statute to “expedite the removal of aliens who have already been ordered removed.” But a statute’s purpose cannot override its clear text. *See Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 949 (1997) (declining to apply a statute retroactively where the text did not clearly require it). Because the operative conduct—Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry—occurred in 1982, the reinstatement statute does not authorize his removal.

### II. Even If the Statute Applies, Its Retroactive Application Is Impermissible

If this Court finds the statute ambiguous, the presumption against retroactivity compels a construction that avoids retroactive effect. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 286–87. A statute is retroactive if it “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Id.* at 280. The reinstatement statute does exactly that: it attaches new and severe legal consequences—automatic removal with no possibility of relief—to the pre-enactment act of reentry.

Before 1997, Fernandez-Vargas had a settled expectation that he could seek discretionary relief from removal, such as adjustment of status based on his marriage to a United States citizen. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 321–22 (2001) (holding that the presumption against retroactivity barred the elimination of § 212(c) relief for aliens who pleaded guilty to deportable offenses before the statute’s effective date). The reinstatement statute eliminates that opportunity entirely. As this Court recognized in *St. Cyr*, the “important and valuable” right to apply for discretionary relief cannot be stripped away retroactively without a clear congressional statement. *Id.* at 322.

The Government may argue that the statute merely restores the status quo ante by reinstating an existing deportation order. But that argument ignores the critical change: before 1997, an alien who reentered illegally could seek relief from removal; after 1997, he cannot. The statute thus “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269–70. This is a quintessentially retroactive effect. *See Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 358–59 (1999) (finding that a statute reducing attorney’s fees for work performed before enactment was impermissibly retroactive).

No “protectable expectation” analysis is required; the presumption against retroactivity operates automatically. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Even if such an expectation were required, Fernandez-Vargas had a reasonable expectation that the legal consequences of his 1982 reentry would remain as they were—namely, that he could apply for adjustment of status. That expectation was “settled” and “reliable.” *See Kaiser Aluminum & Chem. Corp. v. Bonjorno*, 494 U.S. 827, 837 (1990) (observing that the presumption against retroactivity protects “settled expectations” that parties have relied upon). The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion ignored this Court’s repeated admonition that “the presumption against retroactivity is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence.” *United States v. Sec. Indus. Bank*, 459 U.S. 70, 79 (1982).

Finally, the Government’s reliance on the “reinstatement” label does not change the analysis. A statute is not saved from retroactivity simply because it is labeled “procedural” or “remedial.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 275. The statute here eliminates a substantive right to seek relief—a right that existed at the time of Fernandez-Vargas’s reentry. *Cf. Miller v. Florida*, 482 U.S. 423, 431 (1987) (rejecting a procedural label where the practical effect was to alter substantive rights). Because the statute attaches new disabilities to past conduct, it cannot be applied retroactively.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that § 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before its effective date, or, in the alternative, that retroactive application of the statute is impermissible. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
