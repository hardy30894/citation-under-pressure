**Argument**

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander was not a “debt collector” within the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act when it collected debts it had purchased for its own account. The Act targets third-party collection activity, not creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. The statutory text must be read in light of that basic distinction, which has long been recognized in this Court’s interpretive decisions.

The starting point is the Act’s language. A statute is construed according to its “natural and most obvious import.” *Jarecki v. G.D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961). Here, Congress defined “debt collector” to cover persons who regularly collect debts “owed or due another,” while defining “creditor” as one to whom the debt is owed. 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), (6). That distinction is decisive. This Court has repeatedly instructed that words are to be given effect in context, not isolated from the statutory scheme. *United States v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co.*, 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929); *Atlantic Cleaners & Dyers, Inc. v. United States*, 286 U.S. 427, 433 (1932).

Nothing in the FDCPA suggests that Congress meant to transform a purchaser into a “debt collector” simply because the debt was already in default when acquired. The better reading is that the relevant question is whether the entity is collecting for another or for itself at the time of collection. In *Reed v. Hinderland*, 21 F.2d 490, 492 (3d Cir. 1927), the court explained that statutory terms must be read in their operative setting, not by remote antecedents. Likewise, *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486-87 (1869), rejected interpretations that would produce unreasonable practical consequences contrary to the statute’s purpose.

That principle matters here because petitioners’ theory would collapse the creditor/debt-collector distinction Congress wrote into the statute. A company that purchases delinquent receivables becomes the owner of the debt; after purchase, it seeks payment for itself, not “another.” The law has long recognized that ownership changes the character of the claim. See *Mulligan v. Smith*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 206, 210 (1855); *Brock v. Cornell*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 414, 428 (1869). Petitioners’ default-based theory would ignore that transfer of ownership and reclassify a creditor as a debt collector solely because the account was delinquent when bought.

The statutory structure confirms that result. This Court has held that exceptions and provisos are not to be used to rewrite an enactment’s main clause. *Gooch v. United States*, 297 U.S. 124, 128 (1936). The FDCPA’s exclusion for those collecting debts “owed or due another” does not convert every purchaser of defaulted debt into a debt collector; it assumes the person is collecting for another in the first place. That is consistent with the settled rule that a proviso narrows, rather than expands, the scope of a statute’s principal definition. *United States v. Dickson*, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 141, 165 (1841); *Great Northern Railway Co. v. United States*, 208 U.S. 452, 465 (1908).

Petitioners rely on the debt’s default status, but default alone is not the statutory trigger. Courts do not “add to a statute something which Congress did not include.” *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 250 (1926). Nor may they “torture the language” to reach a preferred policy outcome. *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 60 (1930). The FDCPA could have regulated all purchasers of defaulted debt; it did not. Instead, Congress limited the Act to debt collectors, a category historically tied to agents and third parties pursuing another’s obligation. *Madden v. Kentucky*, 309 U.S. 83, 88 (1940); *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. 221, 231 (1920).

Petitioners’ contrary construction would also create unnecessary inconsistency in the statutory scheme. This Court has long favored interpretations that avoid rendering statutory distinctions meaningless. *Rainey v. United States*, 232 U.S. 310, 317 (1914). If every assignee of defaulted debt were a debt collector, the Act’s separate definition of “creditor” would be largely drained of content whenever a debt was purchased after delinquency. Courts should not adopt readings that make one statutory category swallow another. *Wood v. United States*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 342, 362 (1842); *United States v. Fisher*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805).

Finally, petitioners’ position would extend the FDCPA beyond its object. The Act addresses abusive third-party debt collection practices. Statutes aimed at regulating a particular evil must be applied to that evil, and no further. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931). Santander purchased the accounts and then collected them for itself; that is creditor conduct, not third-party collection. Because Santander was not acting “for another,” it was not a debt collector within the meaning of the Act. The judgment should be affirmed.
