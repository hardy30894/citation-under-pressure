**Argument**

The order below does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act. The PLRA does not bar all population-reduction remedies; it permits prospective relief that is “narrowly drawn,” extends no further than necessary to correct a federal-rights violation, and is the “least intrusive means” necessary to correct that violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). Where a court makes the predicate findings required by the statute, it may order release or population reduction if overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violation and no other relief will remedy it. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i), (ii). The three-judge court made exactly those findings here. It determined after a full evidentiary record that severe overcrowding in California’s prisons was the primary cause of continuing Eighth Amendment violations and that no other relief would cure them. That is the precise circumstance Congress addressed in the PLRA’s “prisoner release order” provisions. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3); see Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12 (2011).

This Court has long recognized that prison overcrowding may violate the Eighth Amendment when it produces denial of adequate medical care or other basic necessities. Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687-88 (1978); Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347-50 (1981); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 505-06. Once such a constitutional violation is established, federal courts have not only the power but the duty to enter effective equitable relief. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971); Milliken v. Bradley, 433 U.S. 267, 281-82 (1977). The PLRA does not eliminate that remedial authority; it channels it. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-12, 531-33.

California’s contrary reading would collapse the statute’s carefully drawn distinction between ordinary prospective relief and prisoner release orders. Congress specifically authorized a three-judge court to enter a release order when overcrowding is the primary cause of the violation and no other relief will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A), (E). The court below complied with that framework. It did not impose a generalized policy preference for lower incarceration rates. Rather, it ordered population reduction only after finding that California’s prisons were operating at levels that made constitutionally adequate care impossible. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-12, 529-30.

Nor does the PLRA require a court to await the continued failure of every lesser remedy before ordering population reduction. The statute asks whether “crowding is the primary cause of the violation” and whether “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i), (ii). The record here showed that years of injunctions, stipulations, and administrative measures had failed to cure the systemic violations. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 507-11. The three-judge court was therefore entitled to conclude that relief short of population reduction would be ineffective. Id. at 533-36.

Finally, the scope of the order was constitutionally and statutorily measured. The PLRA requires the least intrusive remedy necessary, not the least politically inconvenient one. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 533-34. The court selected a cap tied to prison design capacity and left California discretion as to how to comply. That kind of remedial flexibility is consistent with equitable practice and with the PLRA’s command to respect federalism while vindicating federal rights. Milliken v. Bradley, 433 U.S. at 281-82; Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-12.

The order should be affirmed.
