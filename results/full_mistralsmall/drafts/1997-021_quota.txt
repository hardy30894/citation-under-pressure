**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Employee Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it possesses “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status is an unreasonable construction of the Act and warrants no deference under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843–45 (1984). The Board’s standard conflates the evidentiary thresholds for polling with those for withdrawing recognition, despite the fact that polling is a less intrusive and more procedurally safeguarded means of testing employee sentiment. *See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786–87 (1990) (recognizing that employers may rebut the presumption of majority status through objective evidence). The Board’s insistence on the same standard for both actions ignores the distinct interests at stake: polling allows an employer to verify its reasonable doubt without prematurely withdrawing recognition or disrupting bargaining relationships. *See Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982) (explaining that polling serves as a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with sincere doubts).

The Board’s position is further undermined by the fact that polling is a statutorily cognizable method of testing employee support. *See Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967) (permitting polling where an employer has a “good-faith reasonable doubt” based on objective evidence). The Board’s attempt to cabin polling within a narrow evidentiary framework ignores the statutory purpose of § 9(c) of the Act, which encourages voluntary resolution of disputes through employee verification of union support. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 610–11 (1969) (recognizing the importance of informal methods of gauging employee sentiment). The Board’s rule is thus inconsistent with the Act’s remedial scheme and should be rejected. *See NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co.*, 416 U.S. 267, 275 (1974) (holding that the Board’s interpretation must align with the Act’s purposes).

**II. The Board Erred in Concluding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt of the Union’s Majority Status.**

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board improperly discounted several employees’ statements expressing dissatisfaction with the union, including those of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, who indicated during job interviews that they saw no need for union representation. *See Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB*, 882 F.2d 19, 22 (1st Cir. 1989) (holding that dissatisfaction with union services does not equate to opposition to representation). The Board also erred in disregarding statements by Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr regarding the views of other employees, despite the fact that such hearsay may constitute substantial evidence if it is probative and reliable. *See NLRB v. Tennessee Packers, Inc.*, 339 F.2d 203, 206 (6th Cir. 1964) (upholding the Board’s reliance on hearsay where it is corroborated by other evidence).

Moreover, the Board’s temporal limitation—focusing on employee statements made as of January 25, 1991—was arbitrary. The Board failed to account for the fact that the bargaining unit had been significantly reduced in size following the sale of the dealership, weakening the presumption of the union’s continuing majority status. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987) (explaining that the presumption is rebuttable where the bargaining unit has been altered). The Board’s refusal to consider the cumulative effect of employee statements and the changed composition of the unit led to an unreasonable conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt. *See Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing the Board where it ignored substantial evidence of employee dissatisfaction).

**III. The Board’s Standard for Polling Is Inconsistent with the Statutory Framework and Should Be Overruled.**

The Board’s insistence on a “reasonable doubt” standard for polling, while permitting lower thresholds for other employer actions, is irrational. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (requiring that agency rules be reasonable and consistent with the statute). The Board’s approach creates an anomaly: employers are permitted to withdraw recognition or seek an RM election with less evidence than is required to conduct a poll, despite polling being a less disruptive method of testing employee sentiment. *See Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989) (acknowledging the Board’s preference for elections over polling). This inconsistency undermines the Board’s stated goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships. *See NLRB v. J. Weingarten, Inc.*, 420 U.S. 251, 260 (1975) (emphasizing the importance of preserving the collective-bargaining process).

The Board’s rule also conflicts with the decisions of several courts of appeals, which have held that employers may poll employees if they have “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. *See A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1140, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 867. This Court should adopt the reasoning of these circuits and reject the Board’s unduly restrictive standard. *See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. at 786 (deferring to the Board’s reasonable interpretations but rejecting those that are arbitrary or inconsistent with the Act).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is arbitrary, inconsistent with the Act, and unsupported by substantial evidence in this case. This Court should reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and hold that Allentown Mack’s poll was lawfully conducted under a proper standard.
