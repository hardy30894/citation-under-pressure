# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

### A. The Act Is Content-Neutral Because It Applies Regardless of What Anyone Says

A regulation is content-neutral if it applies "without reference to the content of the regulated speech." *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Act's thirty-five-foot buffer zone applies to everyone approaching a reproductive health care facility entrance, exit, or driveway—counselors, protestors, and supporters alike—"without reference to" what any speaker intends to say. This Court's decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), controls. There, the Court upheld a Colorado statute creating a 100-foot buffer zone around health care facilities, holding that the statute was content-neutral because "[i]t is not a regulation of speech. Rather, it is a regulation of the places where some speech may occur." *Id.* at 719. The Massachusetts Act does precisely the same thing.

Petitioners' argument that the Act is content-based because it burdens only those who wish to approach patients—and abortion opponents are more likely to want to do so—fails under *Hill*. The Court there rejected an identical argument, explaining that "the statute's purpose is to protect those who enter health care facilities—not to burden the expression of a particular viewpoint." *Id.* at 719-20. The Act's exemptions for clinic employees and patients do not render it content-based; as this Court recognized in *Hill*, such exemptions reflect the reality that "the statute is not limited to the antiabortion message." *Id.* at 723.

### B. The Act Serves Significant Governmental Interests Unrelated to the Suppression of Expression

Massachusetts enacted the buffer zones to protect "public safety, patients' access to healthcare, and the unobstructed use of public sidewalks and roadways." *See McGuire v. Reilly*, 260 F.3d 36, 40 (1st Cir. 2001). These interests mirror those this Court validated in *Hill*: "The State's interests in protecting access and privacy, and providing the police with clear guidelines, are unrelated to the content of the demonstrators' speech." 530 U.S. at 725. A state's interest in "ensuring the public safety and order, in promoting the free flow of traffic on public streets and sidewalks... [is] all content neutral." *Id.* at 715 (quoting *Boos v. Barry*, 485 U.S. 312, 321 (1988)).

The record here—developed over years of litigation including a full bench trial—establishes that these buffer zones responded to documented histories of congestion, harassment, and obstruction outside Massachusetts clinics. *See McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 174 (1st Cir. 2009). The legislature was entitled to rely on this record, just as the Colorado legislature's judgment was entitled to deference in *Hill*. 530 U.S. at 729-30.

### C. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored and Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels of Communication

Narrow tailoring under intermediate scrutiny does not require the least restrictive means; it requires only that the regulation "not burden substantially more speech than is necessary to further the government's legitimate interests." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. A thirty-five-foot zone is narrower than the buffer this Court approved in *Hill*, and it leaves petitioners free to engage in "oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups." *McCullen v. Coakley*, No. 12-1401, slip op. at 33-34 (1st Cir. 2013).

The trial record confirms this. Petitioner McCullen herself testified that she persuaded approximately eighty women to forgo abortions during the period the buffer zones were in effect—hardly evidence that the zones silence her message. Petitioners' insistence on "normal conversational distance," Pet. Br. 22, seeks a constitutional right to unwanted physical proximity that this Court has never recognized. To the contrary, in *Hill* the Court upheld an even larger zone precisely because unwilling listeners retain the right "to be let alone" from unwanted, close-quarters confrontation. 530 U.S. at 717 (quoting *Rowan v. Post Office Dept.*, 397 U.S. 728, 738 (1970)).

## II. THIS COURT SHOULD NOT OVERRULE HILL v. COLORADO

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* disregards the "basic legal principle of stare decisis." *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 377 (2010) (Roberts, C.J., concurring). Stare decisis "promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles." *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991). Departure requires "special justification," *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000), and none exists here.

Petitioners rely on *Citizens United*, *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), to suggest a doctrinal shift disfavoring speaker- and content-based restrictions. But as the First Circuit correctly observed, these cases articulate "conventional First Amendment principles" applied in "factual scenarios far different than the scenario at issue here." *McCullen*, slip op. at 14. *Citizens United* addressed political speech restrictions targeting corporate speakers—a context with no analog to safety buffers around medical facility entrances. Nothing in these decisions undermines *Hill*'s core holding that a state may regulate the location of speech near health care facilities to protect unimpeded access.

*Hill* itself remains sound. It correctly recognized that even in traditional public fora, the government retains authority to impose reasonable place restrictions when necessary to protect substantial governmental interests. 530 U.S. at 715. Overruling *Hill* would destabilize buffer-zone laws nationwide, inviting exactly the kind of clinic-entrance obstruction and harassment that prompted Colorado's, and Massachusetts's, legislatures to act in the first place.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
