**ARGUMENT**

I. **The district court erred in admitting American Steamship’s “expert” opinion on the cause of the line’s parting.**

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 permits expert testimony only when “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” will assist the trier of fact. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993); *Salem v. U.S. Lines Co.*, 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962). The question here was whether the line parted because of a shipboard condition during a hurried rigging operation, not a matter requiring specialized expertise beyond the ken of a jury. Where a witness simply offers a conclusory explanation untethered to reliable methodology, the testimony is not expert proof within Rule 702. *United States v. L.E. Cooke Co.*, 991 F.2d 336, 341-42 (6th Cir. 1993); *United States v. Simmons*, 154 F.3d 765, 770 (8th Cir. 1998).

The record reflects that American Steamship’s witness did not apply any recognized technical method to the failure of the line; instead, he speculated about causation from the visible aftermath. That is precisely the sort of opinion juries can evaluate without expert assistance. *Samaan v. St. Joseph Hosp.*, 670 F.3d 21, 32 (1st Cir. 2012); *Berry v. City of Detroit*, 25 F.3d 1342, 1350-51 (6th Cir. 1994). Because the opinion lacked a reliable foundation and invaded the jury’s role on a straightforward factual issue, its admission was an abuse of discretion. *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 146 (1997).

II. **Cook was entitled to judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness.**

A vessel is unseaworthy if its appurtenances are not reasonably fit for their intended use, and liability is absolute; negligence need not be shown. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960); *Usner v. Luckenbach Overseas Corp.*, 400 U.S. 494, 499 (1971). The evidence here established that the line supporting the bos’n’s chair failed during normal shipboard use. A rope that parts while being used to lower a seaman is, as a matter of law, not reasonably fit for its intended function. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550; *Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 373 U.S. 206, 213-14 (1963).

The district court was required to grant judgment as a matter of law when “there is no legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for” the nonmovant. Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a); *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 149-50 (2000). American Steamship offered no evidence that the line was fit for the task once it parted under ordinary rigging conditions, and the speculative alternative explanations did not create a genuine fact issue. Where the maritime appliance fails in service, the vessel is unseaworthy. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100-01 (1944); *Crumady v. The J.H. Fisser*, 358 U.S. 423, 428 (1959). The denial of Cook’s Rule 50 motion on unseaworthiness therefore was error.

III. **The court properly refused to take comparative negligence from the jury.**

Comparative fault applies under the Jones Act and general maritime law, but only when the plaintiff’s negligence is supported by evidence and reasonable inferences. *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406, 408-09 (1953); *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). Cook was the experienced deckhand assigned to the task under dangerous weather conditions and under time pressure imposed by the master. The evidence showed that he used the tools and procedures directed by the ship’s crew, including deicing the frozen bearings so the boom could pivot. A jury could reasonably find that his conduct was prudent under the circumstances, not negligent. *Rogers v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506 (1957); *CSX Transportation, Inc. v. McBride*, 564 U.S. 685, 691-92 (2011).

Rule 50 relief is unavailable where the evidence permits competing inferences. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 250-51 (1986); *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150-51. The district court therefore correctly allowed the jury to decide comparative negligence.

IV. **The evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was admissible.**

Relevant evidence is admissible unless excluded by a specific rule, and Rule 403 bars it only when unfair prejudice “substantially outweighs” probative value. Fed. R. Evid. 401, 403; *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180-81 (1997). Cook’s claimed damages included loss of earning capacity and future impairment. Evidence that he suffered alcohol-related ailments bore directly on life expectancy, work life, and alternative causes of disability. Such evidence is plainly relevant. *Monessen Southwestern Ry. Co. v. Morgan*, 486 U.S. 330, 336 (1988); *Haglund v. Seafirst Corp.*, 486 F.3d 1112, 1120 (9th Cir. 2007).

A district court’s Rule 403 ruling is reviewed for abuse of discretion, and exclusion is reserved for the exceptional case. *Sprint/United Management Co. v. Mendelsohn*, 552 U.S. 379, 384-85 (2008); *United States v. Caldwell*, 760 F.3d 267, 286 (3d Cir. 2014). The court acted within that discretion in allowing the jury to hear evidence bearing on damages and causation.  

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
