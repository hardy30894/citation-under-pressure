# ARGUMENT

## I. The Rule Announced in *Johnson* Is Substantive and Therefore Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review

This Court has long distinguished, for purposes of retroactivity, between constitutional rules that regulate the manner of trial and rules that place certain conduct or persons entirely beyond the State's power to punish. *See Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965). A rule of the latter kind cannot be confined to future cases, because a State has no power—today or yesterday—to imprison a person under a judgment rendered pursuant to a law the Constitution forbids. *See Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880).

*Johnson v. United States* did not adopt a new trial procedure. It held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act's definition of "violent felony" could not stand, because assessing the "hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version" of a crime made the clause impermissibly unpredictable under the Due Process Clauses. That holding rests on the same fair-warning principle this Court applied in *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347 (1964), where the Court explained that "[t]he basic principle that a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime has often been recognized by this Court." *Id.* at 350. The Court in *Bouie* held that the State, by applying an unforeseeable construction of its statute to the petitioners' conduct, "has punished them for conduct that was not criminal at the time they committed it, and hence has violated the requirement of the Due Process Clause that a criminal statute give fair warning of the conduct which it prohibits." *Id.* at 350–51. This Court reached a similar conclusion in *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399 (1965), invalidating a statute for "vagueness and the absence of any standards sufficient to enable defendants to protect themselves against arbitrary and discriminatory" enforcement. *Id.* at 402–03.

*Johnson*'s residual-clause holding is of the same character. It does not merely regulate how courts decide sentencing facts; it removes an entire category of conduct from the universe of conduct that may lawfully trigger a fifteen-year mandatory minimum. A defendant sentenced under that clause was punished more severely than the substantive law permits, exactly the defect that *Siebold* and *Bouie* treat as void, not merely erroneous.

## II. Habeas Corpus Has Traditionally Been Available to Test Whether a Sentence Exceeds the Court's Lawful Power, Including Sentences Enhanced by Prior-Conviction Statutes

This Court's historic practice confirms that collateral review reaches precisely this kind of defect. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that habeas corpus lies to test whether a court had constitutional power to impose the sentence it imposed; a sentence entered without that power cannot stand. This same principle governed *Reynolds v. Cochran*, 365 U.S. 525 (1961), where this Court entertained a habeas petition challenging confinement under a recidivist enhancement on the ground that the sentence "was not authorized by the Florida second-offender statute" and violated federal constitutional guarantees. *Id.* at 526. That case is Welch's case: a habeas petitioner attacking an enhanced sentence built on prior convictions that could not lawfully support it.

*Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. 471 (1945), likewise entertained a collateral habeas attack on a sentence entered upon a guilty plea, because the petitioner alleged that he was "deprived of counsel contrary to the requirements of the due process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment." *Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. at 472. That constitutional defect, like the one here, was discovered only after the judgment was final, yet this Court permitted the collateral attack to proceed.

And in *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), the Court reviewed on collateral attack a life sentence imposed under a recidivist statute predicated on prior convictions, confirming that this Court has never treated sentence-enhancement statutes as immune from later constitutional testing simply because the underlying convictions were already final. *See also Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963) (vindicating, through collateral proceedings, a constitutional right to counsel violated at a trial that had already produced a final judgment).

## III. *Johnson*'s Rule Must Apply to Welch's Collateral Challenge

Welch's fifteen-year mandatory minimum rests entirely on the residual clause this Court held infirm in *Johnson*. A rule announcing that a criminal statute failed to give fair warning, *Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 350–51, and could not be applied consistent with due process, is substantive: it withdraws the government's power to punish, not merely its chosen method of proof. As *Siebold*, *Zerbst*, *Reynolds*, *Williams*, and *Graham* establish, habeas corpus has always been the proper vehicle for testing whether a sentence exceeds a court's lawful authority. Welch's sentence does exactly that. *Johnson* therefore governs his case, and the judgment below should be reversed.
