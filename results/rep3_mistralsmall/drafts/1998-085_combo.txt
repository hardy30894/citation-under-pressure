**ARGUMENT**

**I. PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS," INDEPENDENT OF THE EMPLOYER'S STATE OF MIND.**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where the employer acts "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." This language tracks the traditional standard for punitive damages in tort law, which requires more than mere intentional wrongdoing—it demands "such a degree of culpability as to indicate conscious, flagrant indifference to the rights of others." *Garratt v. Dailey*, 46 Wn.2d 197, 205 (1955). As this Court has long recognized, punitive damages are not awarded for mere negligence or even gross negligence, but only for "the most reprehensible conduct." *Restatement of Torts* § 908 cmt. b (1939).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that punitive damages serve a deterrent and retributive function, requiring a showing of "aggravated misconduct." *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851). The Supreme Court has likewise held that punitive damages may not be imposed where the defendant's conduct, though wrongful, does not rise to the level of "fraud or malice" or "such oppression as to amount to an independent tort." *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barrett v. Louisville & N.R. Co.*, 25 F.2d 946, 948 (6th Cir. 1928). Thus, the employer's state of mind—whether "malice" or "reckless indifference"—is not sufficient by itself; the conduct itself must be "egregious" or "outrageous." *See* W. Page Keeton et al., *Prosser and Keeton on the Law of Torts* § 2 at 12-13 (1984) (punitive damages require "conduct which is so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency").

**II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 CIVIL RIGHTS ACT CONFIRMS THAT CONGRESS INTENDED A HIGHER STANDARD FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES.**

The 1991 Act's punitive damages provision was modeled after the standard for punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, which this Court has long interpreted to require "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct. *See, e.g., Basista v. Weir*, 340 F.2d 74, 80 (3d Cir. 1965) (punitive damages under § 1981 require "a showing of such reckless or callous disregard of plaintiff's rights as to be equivalent to intentional wrongdoing"). The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act explicitly states that punitive damages under § 1981a are available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 46 (1991). This standard is consistent with the common law of punitive damages, which requires "a high degree of moral culpability." *Keeton et al., supra*, § 2 at 12.

**III. PRE-1970 CASE LAW ESTABLISHES THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS," INDEPENDENT OF THE DEFENDANT'S STATE OF MIND.**

This Court has long held that punitive damages may not be awarded for mere intentional discrimination, no matter how reprehensible the employer's motives. In *Hampton v. Chicago R. Co.*, 133 F. 2d 754, 757 (7th Cir. 1943), this Court reversed a punitive damages award where the defendant's conduct, though intentional, was not "so outrageous as to justify the imposition of punitive damages." Similarly, in *Babcock v. Goodrich Co.*, 169 F.2d 281, 285 (7th Cir. 1948), this Court held that punitive damages could not be awarded where the defendant's conduct, though wrongful, did not "transcend[] the bounds of decency."

The Supreme Court has likewise held that punitive damages require conduct that is "aggravated" or "outrageous." In *Garratt v. Dailey*, 46 Wn.2d 197 (1955), the Supreme Court of Washington held that punitive damages could not be awarded for mere negligence or even gross negligence, but only for conduct that was "so outrageous in character and extreme in degree as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency." *Id.* at 205. This standard has been adopted by this Court in *Hampton* and *Babcock*, and it is consistent with the common law of punitive damages. *See, e.g.,* W. Page Keeton et al., *Prosser and Keeton on the Law of Torts* § 2 at 12-13 (1984).

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY DENIED PUNITIVE DAMAGES WHERE THERE WAS NO SHOWING OF "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS" CONDUCT.**

In this case, the District Court found that there was insufficient evidence to support a punitive damages award because the ADA's conduct, though intentional, was not "egregious" or "outrageous." This finding is consistent with the standard set forth in *Hampton* and *Babcock*, as well as the common law of punitive damages. The evidence at trial showed that the ADA's decision to promote Spangler over Kolstad was based on legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons, even if those reasons were pretextual. There was no evidence that the ADA's conduct was "so outrageous in character and extreme in degree as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency." *Garratt*, 46 Wn.2d at 205.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The District Court correctly denied punitive damages where there was no showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct, independent of the ADA's state of mind.
