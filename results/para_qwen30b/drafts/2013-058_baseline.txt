**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as an “exempt retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and therefore is not protected from creditors’ claims in bankruptcy. The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds is not a relic of the original owner’s intent or a mere label attached to an account; it is a functional protection for funds that are *currently* dedicated to the debtor’s future retirement. The Court of Appeals’ interpretation—rooted in statutory text, structure, and policy—must be affirmed.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key inquiry is not whether the funds *once* served as retirement savings, but whether they *currently* function as such in the hands of the debtor. As this Court has long held, “the meaning of a statute is not to be determined by the subjective intent of its drafters, but by the language of the statute and the context in which it appears.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The phrase “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but its meaning is informed by the statutory scheme and the economic reality of the account.

An inherited IRA, by its very design, is not a vehicle for the beneficiary’s retirement. Unlike a traditional IRA, which is intended to accumulate funds for the owner’s post-employment years, an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral mechanism. Under 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A), the beneficiary must begin distributions within one year of the original owner’s death, and the account must be fully distributed within five years (or over the life expectancy of the beneficiary, if permitted). These mandatory distributions are not optional; they are triggered by the death of the original owner, not by the beneficiary’s retirement. The funds are not preserved for future use after the beneficiary stops working—they are *required* to be spent before retirement, often decades earlier.

This structural distinction is decisive. The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that “distribution precedes the owner’s retirement.” *Clark v. Rameker*, 705 F.3d 718, 723 (7th Cir. 2013). If the funds were truly “retirement funds,” they would be held until the beneficiary reaches retirement age. But they are not. They are instead a vehicle for *current consumption*, as the Court of Appeals noted: “To treat this account as exempt under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) would be to shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption.” Id. at 724. This is not the kind of asset Congress intended to protect from creditors.

The dissenting views in *Chilton* and *Nessa* rely on a strained reading of the statute’s language. They argue that because the account was once “retirement funds” in the decedent’s hands, it remains so in the beneficiary’s. But this ignores the fundamental principle that *exemptions are not perpetual*. The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt assets based on their historical status; it protects those that serve a current, functional purpose. As this Court has held, “a statute must be interpreted in light of its purpose, but not at the expense of its text.” *United States v. Ron Pair*, 489 U.S. at 241. The purpose of the retirement fund exemption is to protect funds that are *currently* set aside for the debtor’s future retirement—not to preserve the tax-advantaged status of an account that has lost its retirement function.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is instructive. Under §522(b)(3)(A), a debtor may exempt a “domicile” only if it was the debtor’s home for at least 730 days before filing. *Clark*, 705 F.3d at 724. A beneficiary who inherits a house from a parent cannot claim it as exempt simply because it was once the parent’s home. The exemption depends on *current use*, not past ownership. The same logic applies here: an inherited IRA is not exempt simply because it once served as a retirement account for the decedent. The account’s function has changed. It is no longer a retirement fund; it is a liquid asset subject to mandatory distribution.

The Clarks’ argument—that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage saving and bequests—misunderstands the scope of the exemption. Congress did not create a general rule that all inherited assets are exempt. It created a *narrow* exemption for funds that are *currently* used for retirement. To extend the exemption to inherited IRAs would undermine the policy of protecting only those funds that are truly reserved for the debtor’s future. As the Seventh Circuit correctly noted, “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Clark*, 705 F.3d at 725 (quoting *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525 (1987)). The Court of Appeals did not sacrifice the purpose of the exemption to promote bequests; it upheld the statutory limit.

Finally, the conflict among the circuits is not a reason to reverse. The Seventh Circuit’s interpretation is the only one grounded in the functional reality of inherited IRAs. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Chilton* rests on a textualist misreading that ignores the account’s economic purpose. The Eighth Circuit’s *Nessa* decision, while similarly flawed, was not binding. The Seventh Circuit’s reasoning is sound, consistent with the Bankruptcy Code’s structure, and faithful to the principle that exemptions must be interpreted in light of their purpose and function.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
