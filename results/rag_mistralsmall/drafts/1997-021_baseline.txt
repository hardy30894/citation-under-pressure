**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Employer Polls Is Unlawful and Contrary to the NLRA.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status is arbitrary and capricious. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987) (recognizing rebuttable presumption of union majority status). The Board’s own rationale—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—does not justify imposing a higher evidentiary threshold for polls than for withdrawal of recognition or RM elections. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061–62 (1989). This Court has repeatedly deferred to the Board’s policy judgments only where they are “rational and consistent with the Act.” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). The Board’s standard fails this test.

The Board’s approach renders polling a “marginally useful” tool, as the D.C. Circuit acknowledged. *Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc. v. NLRB*, 83 F.3d 1483, 1490 (D.C. Cir. 1996). Yet polling serves a critical function: it allows employers to obtain “more certain, precise information” about union support before taking drastic action. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063. The Board’s insistence on the same standard for polls as for withdrawal of recognition ignores the fact that polling is a less intrusive means of testing employee sentiment. *See id.* at 1062–63.

Moreover, the Board’s standard conflicts with the statutory goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *See id.* at 1061. By forcing employers to either withdraw recognition prematurely or risk an unfair labor practice charge, the Board’s rule destabilizes bargaining units unnecessarily. This is particularly unjust where, as here, the employer’s poll revealed a clear lack of majority support. *See infra* Part II.

**II. The Board Erred in Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt.**

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status is unsupported by substantial evidence. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (substantial evidence standard). The Board discounted key statements by employees, including:

- **Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack**, who unequivocally stated that the union had lost support. The Board improperly excluded these employees from its analysis, despite their status as bargaining-unit members at the time of the poll. *See NLRB v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co.*, 340 U.S. 498, 502 (1951) (Board must consider all probative evidence).

- **Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh**, who expressed dissatisfaction with the union during job interviews. The Board’s reliance on *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888 (1978), was misplaced. Ridgick’s prior inquiry about decertification and Marsh’s complaint about union dues demonstrated a lack of support for the union’s continued representation. *See Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB*, 882 F.2d 19, 22 (1st Cir. 1989) (employee dissatisfaction may evidence loss of majority support).

- **Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr**, who reported that multiple employees opposed the union. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements—because Bloch and Mohr did not testify—was arbitrary. *See NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 406 (1962) (Board must consider all credible evidence, even if untested by cross-examination).

The Board’s selective reliance on evidence ignored the totality of the record. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 490. The ALJ’s findings, adopted by the Board, were “arbitrary and capricious” in light of the overwhelming unrefuted evidence of the union’s loss of majority support. *See Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 868 (6th Cir. 1982) (Board’s findings must be upheld only if supported by substantial evidence).

**III. The Board’s Standard for Polling Conflicts with Decisions of Other Circuits and Undermines the NLRA’s Purpose.**

Three circuits—the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth—have rejected the Board’s “reasonable doubt” standard, holding that employers may poll if they have “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.” *See A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1140, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 867; *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). These courts recognized that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with “sincere doubts” about union support. *Thompson*, 651 F.2d at 1145.

The Board’s refusal to adopt this standard is irrational. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 845 (1984) (agency must provide reasoned explanation for policy choice). The Board’s stated preference for RM elections is undermined by its insistence on identical evidentiary standards for polls and withdrawals of recognition. *See Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061–62. This Court should reject the Board’s arbitrary rule and adopt the standard applied by the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits.

**Conclusion**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polls is unlawful, unsupported by substantial evidence in this case, and inconsistent with the NLRA. This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and hold that Allentown Mack’s poll was lawfully conducted.
