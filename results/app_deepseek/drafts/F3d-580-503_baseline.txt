**ARGUMENT**

**The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Winbush’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert, Thereby Violating His Rights to Due Process and a Fair Trial Under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments.**

A defendant in a criminal case is entitled to a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense. *See California v. Trombetta*, 467 U.S. 479, 485 (1984). This includes the right to access expert assistance where the government’s case relies on scientific evidence and the defendant’s ability to rebut that evidence is compromised. *See Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 77 (1985) (holding that due process requires access to a competent psychiatrist where sanity is a significant factor at trial). While *Ake* addressed psychiatric assistance, its reasoning extends to other expert services when the defendant’s defense is critically undermined without them. *See, e.g., Little v. Armontrout*, 835 F.2d 1240, 1243 (8th Cir. 1987) (applying *Ake* to a fingerprint expert).

Here, the government’s case was built on physical evidence—the Tennessee crack, the Kentucky crack, the handgun, and the jacket—all recovered after Winbush fled. The government’s fingerprint expert, Michael Shay, was prepared to testify that no latent fingerprints of value were found on any of these items. The government also planned to call Deputy Commander Reilly to explain why fingerprints might not be recoverable from certain surfaces. This testimony was designed to preempt any defense argument that the absence of Winbush’s fingerprints on the evidence suggested he never possessed those items.

Winbush sought a fingerprint expert to examine the same evidence and, if no latent prints were present, to testify “as to its meaning.” This was not a frivolous request. The absence of fingerprints could have been used by the defense to argue that Winbush never touched the drugs or the gun—a critical point given that the government’s theory of constructive possession relied on proximity and flight. *See United States v. Kitchen*, 57 F.3d 516, 520 (7th Cir. 1995) (possession may be constructive, but the government must prove knowledge and intent to control). An expert could have explained that the absence of prints, particularly on items like plastic baggies and a firearm, was inconsistent with recent handling, thereby undermining the government’s inference of possession.

The district court denied the motion solely because it was filed “woefully late”—six days before trial in a case pending for over two years. While timeliness is a legitimate consideration, a court must balance it against the defendant’s constitutional rights. *See United States v. Alden*, 527 F.3d 653, 660 (7th Cir. 2008) (reviewing denial of expert for abuse of discretion). Here, the government had disclosed its expert witnesses on October 3, 2007—over a month before trial. Winbush did not move for his own expert until November 8, 2007. But the record shows that Winbush had agreed to stipulate to Shay’s testimony at the November 2 pretrial conference, and he did not learn until later that the government would also call Reilly to bolster the fingerprint evidence. The government’s strategy to use expert testimony to explain away the absence of fingerprints was not fully apparent until the eve of trial.

Moreover, the prejudice to Winbush was substantial. The government’s fingerprint experts were permitted to testify that the absence of prints was unremarkable, leaving the jury to infer that the evidence was nonetheless connected to Winbush. Without a defense expert, Winbush had no means to challenge that inference. *See Ake*, 470 U.S. at 82 (noting that without expert assistance, the defendant is “unable to respond” to the state’s case). The denial of a continuance compounded the error, forcing Winbush to trial without a critical defense tool. *See United States v. Miller*, 499 F.3d 711, 716 (7th Cir. 2007) (continuance should be granted when necessary to avoid prejudice).

Because the district court’s denial of the motion for a fingerprint expert and the motion to continue deprived Winbush of a fair opportunity to present his defense, his conviction must be reversed.
