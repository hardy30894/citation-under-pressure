**Argument**

**I. The district court erred by transferring the case to itself for trial under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) because § 1407(a) mandates remand to the original district.**

The text of § 1407(a) is unambiguous. It authorizes the Panel to transfer cases for "coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings" and commands that the case "shall be remanded" to the original district for trial. *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124 (2d Cir. 1971). The Second Circuit in *Pfizer* candidly admitted that "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. Sec. 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district." *Id.* at 125. This statutory silence has been noted by courts across the country, with the District of New Hampshire stating that the statute "does not directly allow" such a transfer. *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971). While the Third Circuit in *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), suggests that § 1404(a) can be used to transfer a case to the transferee court, this interpretation ignores the explicit command of § 1407(a) that the case "shall be remanded." The Panel’s Rule 14(b) cannot override the clear statutory mandate.

**II. The "illicit practice" of using § 1404(a) to retain cases for trial undermines the purpose of § 1407 and causes prejudice to litigants.**

The purpose of § 1407 is to centralize pretrial proceedings, not to strip plaintiffs of their right to choose a forum for trial. *Bauman v. United States Dist. Court*, 557 F.2d 650, 653 (9th Cir. 1977). The remedy of mandamus is a drastic one, to be invoked only in extraordinary situations. *Wash. Pub. Util. Group v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 843 F.2d 319, 323 (9th Cir. 1987). However, the district court’s order is clearly erroneous as a matter of law. *United States v. Harper*, 729 F.2d 1216, 1222 (9th Cir. 1984). The Ninth Circuit’s majority opinion acknowledges that the statutory mandate is clear but suggests that the transferee court has the power to transfer the case to itself. This "illicit practice" has been widely criticized. *Pfizer*, 447 F.2d at 124. Furthermore, the petitioners have shown substantial prejudice. *Varsic v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 607 F.2d 245, 252 (9th Cir. 1979). The petitioners were dragged hundreds of miles from their home district to appear before a judge in Arizona with no familiarity with the case. *Sunshine Beauty Supplies v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 872 F.2d 310, 311 (9th Cir. 1989). This prejudice is of a type not correctable on appeal. *Id.* Therefore, mandamus is appropriate to correct this clear error.
