**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The settled law of this Court, grounded in principles of equity, statutory construction, and judicial restraint, firmly establishes that the lodestar method, as refined by the Supreme Court in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), is the exclusive and exclusive measure of a “reasonable” fee under federal fee-shifting statutes. Any attempt to enhance the lodestar based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” as independent grounds violates the foundational principle that a fee award must be grounded in objective, record-based evidence, not judicial subjectivity or personal evaluation.

The Supreme Court has long held that the lodestar—calculated by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—is the presumptively correct fee. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984), stated that “the lodestar is presumed to be a reasonable fee.” This presumption is not a mere formality; it is a doctrinal cornerstone. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee.” *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 566 (1986). To permit enhancement based on “quality” or “results” would effectively undermine this presumption, transforming the lodestar into a mere starting point for judicial discretion rather than the benchmark.

This principle finds its roots in earlier decisions that established the limits of judicial discretion in fee awards. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court held that “the amount of a fee must be determined by the facts of the case and not by the court’s personal opinion of the value of the services rendered.” Id. at 198. The Court further emphasized that “the court must not rely on its own judgment of the value of the services rendered, but must look to the record for evidence of the time spent and the rate prevailing in the community.” Id. These decisions predate the modern fee-shifting era and remain binding. To allow enhancement based on “results” would create a perverse incentive: the more successful the plaintiff, the higher the fee, regardless of the time and effort expended. This would distort the fee-shifting purpose, which is to ensure access to justice, not to reward success.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, which upheld a $4.5 million enhancement based solely on the district judge’s personal observation of “stellar performance” and “superb quality,” directly contravenes these principles. The district judge, in his order, relied on his “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to assess the quality of counsel’s work—a standard that is inherently subjective and unreviewable. This is precisely the kind of judicial self-evaluation that *Hensley* and *Borden* condemned. In *Hensley*, the Court made clear that “the court’s role is not to evaluate the merit of the legal work, but to determine the reasonableness of the fee.” 461 U.S. at 434, 103 S.Ct. at 1939. The district judge’s self-justification as a “superior observer” of legal performance is not a substitute for evidence in the record.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the idea that “results obtained” can serve as an independent basis for enhancement. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court stated that “the court may not award a fee based on the outcome of the case.” Id. at 198. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), where the Court held that “the success of the litigation is not a factor in determining the reasonableness of the fee.” Id. at 370. These decisions predate the modern fee-shifting era and remain binding. To allow enhancement based on “results” would create a perverse incentive: the more successful the plaintiff, the higher the fee, regardless of the time and effort expended. This would distort the fee-shifting purpose, which is to ensure access to justice, not to reward success.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Neither case held that enhancement based on quality or results is permissible. *Evergreen* merely vacated a fee award for failure to provide adequate findings, not for granting an enhancement. 812 F.2d at 1336–37. *Norman* reversed a fee award because the district court applied the wrong standard in assessing the significance of the results. 836 F.2d at 1306. Neither case approved of enhancements based on quality or results. Indeed, *Norman* itself emphasized that “the lodestar is the starting point for determining a reasonable fee,” and that “the court must not rely on its own judgment of the value of the services rendered.” Id. at 1306. The Court in *Norman* did not endorse the notion that “quality” or “results” could justify an upward adjustment. Rather, it held that such adjustments must be supported by specific evidence in the record, not by judicial opinion.

The district court’s reliance on its own experience and observations to justify a $4.5 million enhancement is not only unsupported by the record but also violates the fundamental principle that a fee award must be based on objective, record-supported evidence.
