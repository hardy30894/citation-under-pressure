# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE REQUIRES A SINGLE COMPETENCY STANDARD, NOT A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD

### A. This Court Has Established One Standard for Competency to Proceed

The competency standard this Court announced in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), draws the constitutional line: a defendant is competent if he has "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him" and the ability to consult with counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), described the same inquiry as "the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense." This standard governs every stage of a criminal proceeding. Nothing in the Due Process Clause suggests that the competence required to make a particular decision depends on which decision the defendant makes.

The court of appeals nevertheless held that a defendant who pleads guilty or waives counsel must demonstrate the capacity for "reasoned choice" among available alternatives, a more demanding measure of mental functioning than *Dusky* requires. That holding finds no support in this Court's cases. It originated in the Ninth Circuit's decision in *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and has been rejected by other courts of appeals. The competency inquiry exists to ensure that a defendant has the capacity to participate in the proceedings against him. If he has the capacity to understand the proceedings and consult with counsel, he is competent to decide whether to stand trial, plead guilty, or defend himself—even if the court thinks his choice is unwise.

### B. The Knowing-and-Intelligent Waiver Inquiry Is Separate from Competency

The court of appeals' confusion is revealed by its conflation of competency with the separate requirement that a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea be knowing and intelligent. The waiver must be "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). This inquiry asks whether the defendant was adequately informed of the rights he would forgo and the consequences of his decision. *North Carolina v. Alford*, 400 U.S. 25, 31 (1970); *Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637, 645 (1976). It does not require a higher level of mental capacity; it requires that the defendant's decision be made with sufficient information and without coercion. A competent defendant may make a waiver that is not knowing and intelligent, just as he may make a waiver that is knowing and intelligent but, in hindsight, regrettable. The remedy for the former is to invalidate the waiver; the remedy for the latter is none.

Moran's decision to discharge counsel and plead guilty to prevent the presentation of mitigating evidence may have been poor strategy. But the Fourteenth Amendment does not empower courts to refuse a competent defendant's informed choice merely because the court considers that choice insufficiently reasoned. Accepting the Ninth Circuit's rule would convert every guilty plea and waiver of counsel into an invitation for post-conviction litigation over the quality of the defendant's judgment—a task the competency standard was never designed to serve.

## II. THE STATE COURT'S FINDING OF COMPETENCY UNDER THE CORRECT STANDARD IS SUPPORTED BY THE RECORD

Applying *Dusky*, the state court's competency finding is sound. Two court-appointed psychiatrists examined Moran in September 1984 and concluded he was competent. Dr. O'Gorman reported that Moran was "knowledgeable of the charges being made against him," could "assist his attorney, in his own defense, if he so desires," and was "fully cognizant of the penalties if convicted." At the plea hearing, Moran responded to the court's questions, affirmed his understanding of his rights, and explained his deliberate decision to forgo mitigating evidence. Although Moran was taking medication at the time, he told the court so, answered coherently, and gave no indication that he lacked a rational understanding of the proceedings. His monosyllabic answers to the trial court's leading questions reflect a defendant who had made a choice, not one who lacked the capacity to choose.

This Court confronted similar facts in *Demosthenes v. Baal*, 495 U.S. 731 (1990), a Nevada capital case in which a defendant pleaded guilty after psychiatrists found him competent. The Court declined to disturb the state court's judgment. The same deference is warranted here. The state post-conviction court held an evidentiary hearing and found Moran competent, a factual determination presumptively correct under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The Ninth Circuit did not find the state court's factual findings clearly erroneous; it reversed solely because the state court applied the ordinary *Dusky* standard rather than the heightened "reasoned choice" standard. Because that legal premise is wrong, the reversal collapses.

## III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S STANDARD IS UNWORKABLE AND UNDERMINES FINALITY

A heightened standard invites collateral attacks on virtually every guilty plea and waiver of counsel. A defendant who has been found competent but now regrets his decision need only argue that his choice was not sufficiently "reasoned." Such a rule would impose an intolerable burden on state courts and undermine the finality of criminal judgments. The Due Process Clause requires competency, not clairvoyance; it demands that a defendant be able to understand the proceedings and assist in his defense. It does not require that his decisions please a reviewing court.

# CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
