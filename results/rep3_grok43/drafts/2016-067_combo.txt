The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only those who collect debts owed or due another. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). When Santander purchased the loans, it became the owner and therefore collected debts owed to itself. The statute’s text admits of no other reading. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917) (“If the language be plain * * * it is the duty of the courts to enforce it according to its obvious meaning.”).

The phrase “owed or due another” requires an agency relationship at the moment of collection. United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“Words * * * should be given their ordinary meaning.”). An assignee who takes title steps into the shoes of the original creditor and ceases to act for another. Carpenter v. Longan, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 271, 274 (1872) (“The assignment of a debt carries with it the security.”). No language in the Act converts subsequent ownership into perpetual third-party collection.

Statutory context confirms the distinction. The Act excludes from the definition of “debt collector” persons collecting debts not in default when obtained. That exclusion presupposes the threshold requirement of collection for another; it does not create an independent category of liability. Crooks v. Harrelson, 282 U.S. 55, 58 (1930) (“The plain meaning of the words is not to be disregarded.”). Courts may not enlarge the statute beyond its terms. Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250 (1926) (“It is not for the courts to supply what Congress has omitted.”).

The same principle appears throughout the Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence. Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (statutes are to be read according to their letter when the letter is unambiguous); McBoyle v. United States, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (“The words * * * must be taken in their ordinary sense.”); United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (“The intention of the legislature is to be collected from the words they employ.”); Schooner Paulina’s Cargo v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 52, 60 (1812) (penal and regulatory statutes receive strict construction).

Because the complaint alleges only that Santander collected debts it owned, petitioners failed to plead that Santander acted as a “debt collector.” The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
