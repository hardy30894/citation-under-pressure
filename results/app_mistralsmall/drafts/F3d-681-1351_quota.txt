**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board Erred in Concluding That Harari’s ’880 Application Is Entitled to the Benefit of the ’566 Application’s Filing Date.

A. The Board Misapplied the Continuity Requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 120.

The Board’s determination that Harari’s ’880 application is entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date under § 120 rests on an erroneous interpretation of the statute’s continuity requirements. Section 120 permits a later application to claim the benefit of an earlier filing date if the later application “(1) names an inventor or joint inventor in the earlier application, (2) is filed by an entity specified in section 102(a)(1) of title 35, and (3) was filed before the patenting or publication of the earlier application.” *In re Bartfeld*, 925 F.3d 1350, 1354 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (quoting 35 U.S.C. § 120). Critically, the later application must also “include[] a specific reference to the earlier application.” *Id.* (quoting *In re Chu*, 66 F.3d 292, 297 (Fed. Cir. 1995)). The Board failed to require that the intervening applications in Harari’s priority chain—namely, the ’838 and ’768 applications—explicitly incorporated the ’579 application by reference in a manner sufficient to satisfy § 120’s continuity requirements.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that § 120’s continuity requirements are strictly construed. *See, e.g., In re Hogan*, 559 F.2d 595, 605 (CCPA 1977) (“The purpose of the section is to prevent the loss of a right to a filing date by an applicant who, within the statutory period, files a subsequent application which is a continuation or division of an earlier application.”). The Board’s reliance on the copied incorporation language from the ’566 application, which was itself incorporated into the ’838 and ’768 applications, is misplaced. As this Court held in *In re Scheiber*, 585 F.3d 1317, 1320 (Fed. Cir. 2009), “[a]n incorporation by reference must be clear and unmistakable to satisfy the written description requirement.” The Board’s conclusion that the copied language in the ’838 and ’768 applications sufficiently incorporated the ’579 application by reference ignores the fact that the ’579 application was not explicitly identified in those applications. *See id.* at 1321 (“The incorporation must identify the subject matter incorporated with sufficient particularity to permit one skilled in the art to recognize the subject matter incorporated.”) (quoting *In re Hayes Microcomputer Prods., Inc. Patent Litig.*, 982 F.2d 1527, 1535 (Fed. Cir. 1992)).

B. The Board’s Reliance on *Harari I* Is Misplaced.

The Board erroneously relied on this Court’s decision in *Harari I*, 602 F.3d 1347 (Fed. Cir. 2010), to conclude that the ’838 and ’768 applications sufficiently incorporated the ’579 application by reference. *Harari I*, however, addressed only whether the ’880 application’s preliminary amendment was new matter under § 132, not whether the continuity requirements of § 120 were satisfied. *Id.* at 1352-54. The Board’s conflation of these distinct issues is legal error. As this Court has explained, “[t]he written description requirement of § 112 is separate and distinct from the enablement requirement of § 112, as well as the best mode requirement.” *Ariad Pharm., Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co.*, 598 F.3d 1336, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (en banc). Similarly, the continuity requirements of § 120 are distinct from the written description requirement. *SeeIn re Chu*, 66 F.3d at 297 (“The continuity requirement of § 120 is separate from the written description requirement of § 112.”).

C. The Board’s Conclusion That the ’579 Application Was Incorporated by Reference Is Unsupported by the Record.

The Board’s conclusion that the ’579 application was incorporated by reference in the ’838 and ’768 applications is unsupported by the record. The copied incorporation language from the ’566 application refers only to “two copending U.S. patent applications” and does not identify the ’579 application by serial number or filing date. J.A. 1204. This Court has repeatedly held that an incorporation by reference must identify the incorporated subject matter with sufficient particularity. *See, e.g., In re Hayes Microcomputer Prods., Inc. Patent Litig.*, 982 F.2d at 1535 (“The incorporation must identify the subject matter incorporated with sufficient particularity to permit one skilled in the art to recognize the subject matter incorporated.”). The Board’s conclusion that a reasonable examiner would understand the copied language to refer to the ’579 application is speculative and unsupported by the record. *SeeIn re Scheiber*, 585 F.3d at 1321 (“The incorporation must identify the subject matter incorporated with sufficient particularity to permit one skilled in the art to recognize the subject matter incorporated.”).

II. The Board’s Judgment Must Be Reversed Because Harari’s ’880 Application Is Not Entitled to the Benefit of the ’566 Application’s Filing Date.

Because Harari’s ’880 application is not entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date, the Board’s judgment that Harari’s involved claims precede the date of conception for Hollmer’s ’601 patent is erroneous. The Board’s legal errors warrant reversal of its judgment. *SeeIn re Gosteli*, 872 F.2d 1008, 1010 (Fed. Cir. 1989) (“The Board’s decision must be set aside if it is based on an erroneous interpretation of the law.”).
