**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Was Permissible Viewpoint-Neutral Subject Matter Discrimination in a Limited Public Forum.**

Milford Central School created a limited public forum for “social, civic, and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” The School expressly reserved the forum for secular purposes, prohibiting use “for religious purposes.” When the government creates a limited public forum, it may restrict speech to the designated subject matter so long as the restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. *See, e.g., Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (recognizing that the government may regulate the use of its property for expressive activities consistent with the purpose of the forum). The School’s policy reasonably limits use to non-religious activities, and the Club’s proposed meetings—featuring prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—fall squarely within the excluded category of “religious purposes.” The School therefore properly denied access.

**II. The Club’s Activities Constitute Religious Worship and Instruction, Not a Viewpoint on a Secular Subject.**

The Club’s meetings are not merely discussions of morals from a religious perspective; they are religious exercises. The Club’s lesson plans explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children, invite children to pray to “receive Jesus as their personal Savior,” and include “challenge and invitation” segments designed to lead children to conversion. This is religious instruction and worship, not a viewpoint on a secular topic. *See Davis v. Beason*, 133 U.S. 333, 342 (1890) (defining religion as “one’s views of his relations to his Creator, and the obligations they impose of reverence for his being and character, and of obedience to his will”). The School’s policy reasonably excludes such activities, just as it would exclude a purely secular political rally or commercial solicitation. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575-76 (1941) (upholding reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions on speech in public forums).

**III. The School’s Distinction Between Secular Moral Development and Religious Instruction Is Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The School permitted the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club to use its facilities for moral and character development. Those organizations do not engage in prayer, scripture reading, or proselytization. The School’s exclusion of the Good News Club is not based on disapproval of the Club’s religious viewpoint but on the subject matter of its activities—religious worship and instruction. *See, e.g., Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571-72 (1942) (holding that certain categories of speech, including “the lewd and obscene, the profane, the libelous, and the insulting or ‘fighting’ words,” are not protected by the First Amendment). The School’s policy does not discriminate against religion; it simply reserves the forum for secular uses. *See, e.g., West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943) (recognizing that the state may not compel speech but may regulate the use of its property for secular purposes).

**IV. The School’s Policy Is Reasonable and Does Not Violate the First Amendment.**

The School’s policy is a reasonable exercise of its authority to manage its property. *See, e.g., Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1966) (upholding the state’s right to control the use of its property for non-expressive purposes). The policy serves the legitimate interest of avoiding the appearance of endorsing religion and ensuring that school facilities are used for community welfare, not religious indoctrination. *See, e.g., Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947) (recognizing the state’s interest in maintaining neutrality toward religion). The Club remains free to meet elsewhere, as it did at the Milford Center Community Church. The School’s denial of access does not suppress the Club’s speech; it merely declines to open the forum to religious activities.

**V. The School’s Policy Does Not Violate the Establishment Clause, but Even If It Did, the School’s Concern Justifies Its Action.**

The School’s concern that permitting the Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause is well-founded. *See, e.g., McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 209-12 (1948) (holding that religious instruction in public school facilities violates the Establishment Clause). Allowing a religious club to conduct worship and proselytization on school premises, even after hours, could be perceived as state endorsement of religion. *See, e.g., Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312-13 (1952) (upholding released-time programs only because they occurred off school premises). The School’s decision to exclude the Club is a reasonable precaution to avoid constitutional violation.

**VI. The Club’s Free Speech Rights Are Not Absolute and Must Yield to the School’s Reasonable Forum Restrictions.**

The First Amendment does not guarantee access to all government property for all speech. *See, e.g., Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88-89 (1949) (upholding restrictions on sound trucks in public forums). The School’s policy is a content-neutral restriction on the subject matter of speech in a limited public forum. *See, e.g., Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (recognizing that the government may regulate the use of its property for expressive activities). The Club’s speech is not prohibited; it is merely excluded from a forum reserved for secular uses.

**VII. The School’s Policy Is Consistent with Longstanding Precedent Upholding Governmental Control Over Its Property.**

This Court has consistently held that the government may reserve its property for specific uses. *See, e.g., United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 376-77 (1968) (upholding restrictions on symbolic speech when the government’s interest is unrelated to the suppression of expression). The School’s policy is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restriction that serves the legitimate purpose of maintaining the secular character of its facilities. *See, e.g., Pickering v. Board of Education*, 391 U.S. 563, 568 (1968) (recognizing that the state’s interest in managing its property may outweigh an individual’s speech rights).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
