**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in stretching FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its text and this Court’s precedents. FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Its exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)); *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. Exemption 2, however, applies only to matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency,” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), and cannot be expanded to cover any document an agency prefers not to disclose.

1. **The ESQD documents are not “internal personnel rules and practices.”**  
The Ninth Circuit treated explosive-effects maps as exempt because they were “predominantly” for internal use. That approach rewrites the statute. Congress chose the phrase “internal personnel rules and practices,” not “predominantly internal agency materials.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has never held that operational safety maps, long-term planning tools, or facility-design information fall within Exemption 2 merely because agency personnel use them. To the contrary, *Rose* construed the exemption to cover mundane employee matters such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave.” 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The Navy’s ESQD materials are not analogous to those sorts of housekeeping rules; they are substantive technical documents about the effects of explosions and the siting of ordnance storage facilities.

The Ninth Circuit relied on its own prior decisions, but those cases do not support the ruling here. In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), the court addressed a raids-and-searches manual for law enforcement officers. That case involved operational tactics for executing searches, not public-safety engineering data about blast effects. Later, in *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997), the court held that goshawk nesting data did not “relate ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.’” *Id.* at 1085. The same is true here: ESQD data concerns how the Navy designs munitions storage and how Navy operations affect surrounding communities. It is not a personnel rule or practice at all.

2. **The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test is incompatible with the statute and this Court’s FOIA cases.**  
The Ninth Circuit explicitly adopted the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” formulation from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc). But *Crooker* is a statutory gloss, not a textual command, and this Court has never endorsed it. FOIA exemptions cannot be expanded by judicial policy preferences. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. The words Congress used are limited and exacting: information must relate “solely” to internal personnel rules and practices. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). A “predominantly internal” standard dilutes that restriction into something much broader.

This Court’s cases likewise caution against such expansion. In *Rose*, the Court rejected a broad reading of Exemption 2 and emphasized the statute’s narrow scope. 425 U.S. at 360-61, 369. In *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989), the Court described FOIA as a “careful balance” between disclosure and protection, which Congress struck through specific exemptions. The Ninth Circuit upset that balance by creating a new class of withheld records based on the agency’s own assertion that disclosure might aid someone in avoiding regulation.

3. **The Navy’s circumvention theory cannot justify withholding documents that fall outside Exemption 2’s text.**  
The Ninth Circuit’s approach also makes the circumvention rationale do too much work. *Rose* recognized that some internal materials may be withheld when disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” 425 U.S. at 369. But that principle operates only after the material first qualifies as internal personnel rules and practices. Here, the ESQD documents are technical safety records, not internal personnel rules. Risk of misuse cannot convert nonexempt records into exempt ones. If it could, any public document with security implications could be withheld under Exemption 2, contrary to *Rose* and *Tax Analysts*.

That concern is especially acute here because the Navy has not invoked or satisfied other, more specific exemptions that Congress designed for sensitive information. FOIA does not allow agencies to avoid those narrower provisions by relabeling technical safety data as personnel practices. See *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

In short, the Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with FOIA’s text and this Court’s teaching that exemptions are exclusive and narrow. The ESQD documents are not “internal personnel rules and practices,” and they cannot be withheld under Exemption 2 merely because the Navy thinks disclosure might create a circumvention risk. The judgment should be reversed.
