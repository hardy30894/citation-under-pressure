**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a)'s Command to Remand Is Mandatory and Admits No § 1404(a) Exception**

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." That directive is not advisory. This Court has long insisted that statutory text controls over judicial convenience, even where a contrary practice has become entrenched. In *Herb v. Pitcairn*, 325 U.S. 77 (1945), the Court examined the operative language of a federal statute closely rather than defer to a state court's gloss on it, underscoring that courts must give effect to the words Congress chose. The word "shall" in § 1407(a) leaves no room for a transferee court to retain a case for trial simply because it finds that course efficient. Congress could have made remand discretionary, as it did with venue transfer under § 1404(a), which "may" be granted "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice." *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960). It did not. The deliberate contrast between "shall" in § 1407(a) and "may" in § 1404(a) confirms that Congress assigned the Panel, not the transferee judge, the exclusive power to decide whether and when a case leaves the transferee district.

**II. Section 1404(a) Does Not Authorize a Court to Transfer a Case to Itself**

Section 1404(a) speaks of transferring an action "to any other district or division where it might have been brought." This Court's precedents demonstrate that § 1404(a) operates only between two distinct courts, one that could properly have entertained the suit as an original matter and another to which venue is more convenient. In *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335 (1960), the Court held that a § 1404(a) transferee district must be one "where the action ‘might have been brought,'" a limitation that this Court refused to relax even by consent of the parties. The Arizona district court could not satisfy that limitation with respect to itself, because Lexecon's action was never "brought" in Arizona at all; it arrived there solely by operation of the Panel's pretrial-consolidation order under § 1407(a), a wholly separate statutory mechanism serving a wholly separate purpose.

That separateness matters. In *Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co.*, 345 U.S. 379 (1953), this Court explained that § 1404(a), "by its very terms, speaks to federal courts" and "addresses itself only to that federal forum in which a lawsuit has been initiated," its function being "to vest such a federal forum with the power to transfer a transitory cause of action to a more convenient federal court." *Id.* at 384. Section 1404(a) thus presupposes a forum of proper initiation exercising a power to send the case elsewhere. It confers no power on a forum that received the case only for pretrial coordination to declare itself the permanent venue. The Arizona district court exercised precisely the power *Pope* said § 1404(a) does not confer: it used the statute not to transfer the case away from the forum of initiation, but to convert an involuntary way-station into a permanent destination.

**III. The Statutory Scheme Preserves the Plaintiff's Choice of Forum for Trial**

This Court has repeatedly treated the plaintiff's selection of forum as an interest that Congress protected even while authorizing convenience-based transfers. In *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612 (1964), the Court held that a § 1404(a) transfer for the convenience of parties and witnesses should not work a change in the substantive rights of the parties, reasoning that Congress intended the transferee court to apply the law of the transferor forum precisely to protect the plaintiff's original choice. That solicitude for the plaintiff's forum is incompatible with allowing a transferee court under § 1407(a)—a court chosen by the Panel, not the plaintiff, for the limited purpose of pretrial coordination—to strip the plaintiff of the very forum it was entitled to select for trial. Likewise, in *Ex Parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), the Court recognized that § 1404(a) codifies the doctrine of forum non conveniens for the benefit of a defendant seeking a more convenient forum than the one the plaintiff chose; it was never conceived as a vehicle for a court to entrench itself over a case that a plaintiff had not chosen to bring there in the first place.

Nor can Milberg justify the Arizona court's self-transfer as an incidental exercise of case-management discretion. In *Koehring Co. v. Hyde Construction Co.*, 382 U.S. 362 (1966), this Court cautioned that transfer authority under § 1404(a) must be exercised consistent with its statutory text, even while recognizing limited extraordinary departures from ordinary transfer procedure; no comparable extraordinary circumstance justifies overriding § 1407(a)'s express remand mandate here. And in *Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co.*, 375 U.S. 71 (1963) (per curiam), this Court reversed a court of appeals for disregarding statutory limits on transfer discretion, reaffirming that § 1404(a) discretion operates within, not beyond, the boundaries Congress drew.

Because § 1407(a) commands remand and § 1404(a) supplies no authority for a transferee court to retain a case for trial by transferring it to itself, the judgment below should be reversed.
