**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether judicial review of a Merit Systems Protection Board (“MSPB”) decision dismissing a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds lies in federal district court or the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The answer, compelled by the plain text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702 and this Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), is that review lies in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), cannot survive *Kloeckner*.

**I. The Statutory Scheme Commits Review of All Mixed-Case Decisions to District Court**

Under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), a “mixed case” exists when an employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the [MSPB], and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” The statute then provides that “any decision of the Board” in such a case shall be reviewed in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). This Court has recognized that the phrase “any decision” is “expansive” and “does not carve out an exception for cases dismissed on procedural grounds.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 48. Neither does it carve out an exception for dismissals on jurisdictional grounds.

The D.C. Circuit erred by treating the jurisdictional nature of the MSPB’s dismissal as removing Perry’s case from the mixed-case definition. But the statutory test for a mixed case focuses on the *action* affecting the employee and the *allegation* of discrimination—not on the Board’s ultimate jurisdictional ruling. As this Court stated in *Kloeckner*, “the only question is whether the employee (A) has been affected by an action that is appealable to the Board and (B) alleges discrimination.” *Id.* at 49. Perry’s retirement and suspension were actions that “may appeal” to the Board if involuntary; indeed, the Board’s entire jurisdictional inquiry turned on that very question. Under *Kloeckner*, the Board’s conclusion that the action was voluntary does not retroactively erase the fact that Perry was “affected by an action which [he] may appeal.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A); see *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 49-50 (rejecting the argument that an untimely appeal is not “appealable”).

**II. *Kloeckner* Eviscerates the Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals**

The D.C. Circuit clung to *Powell* on the theory that *Kloeckner* involved only a procedural dismissal, not a jurisdictional one. But *Kloeckner’s* reasoning applies with equal force here. This Court unanimously held that “when an employee brings a mixed case, and the MSPB issues a decision on the merits or a procedural dismissal, the employee may seek judicial review in district court.” *Id.* at 44. The Court’s logic—that the statute’s “plain language” requires district court review for all mixed-case decisions—admits no exception for jurisdictional dismissals. *Id.* at 48.

The D.C. Circuit attempted to differentiate jurisdictional dismissals on the ground that a jurisdictionally barred appeal was never “appealable to the MSPB.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). But this Court already considered and rejected a similar argument in *Kloeckner*. There, the government argued that an untimely appeal was not properly “appealable.” This Court responded that the “action” itself—a removal—was the kind of action “that an employee may appeal to the Board,” and the Board’s subsequent procedural ruling did not change that fact. 568 U.S. at 50. The same is true here: Perry’s retirement and suspension were actions “appealable” to the Board if the employee challenges them as involuntary. The Board’s jurisdictional ruling on that factual question does not alter the nature of the action. *See Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 713 F.3d 111, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013) (acknowledging that *Kloeckner* “arguably” casts doubt on the jurisdictional-procedural distinction).

Moreover, the jurisdictional inquiry into voluntariness is inextricably intertwined with the merits of Perry’s discrimination claim. The same evidence that would show discrimination—coercion, misrepresentation, or duress—also bears on whether the settlement was voluntary. To require Perry to litigate these overlapping issues in the Federal Circuit, which lacks jurisdiction over discrimination claims, defeats the purpose of the mixed-case provisions. Congress intended discrimination claims to be heard in district court, where employees have access to a jury trial and broader remedies. *See Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 48-49.

**III. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Creates an Unworkable and Inconsistent Jurisdictional Regime**

The D.C. Circuit’s approach forces litigants and courts to guess the proper forum based on the label the MSPB affixes to its dismissal. But the line between “jurisdictional” and “procedural” is far from clear. This Court has cautioned against such “arbitrary distinctions” that “serve no purpose other than to generate confusion.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 48. Here, the MSPB’s jurisdictional dismissal rested on a factual finding that Perry’s retirement was voluntary—a determination that could just as easily be characterized as a merits ruling on the coercion claim. The forum for judicial review should not turn on such “questions of characterization.” *Id.* at 51.

For these reasons, the D.C. Circuit’s transfer of Perry’s case to the Federal Circuit was error. The proper forum for review is federal district court.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to transfer to the appropriate district court.
