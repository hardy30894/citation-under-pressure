**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE APPLIES TO NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS.**

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has held that the Clause applies to any sentencing proceeding where the state seeks to increase a penalty based on facts not found by a jury. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). In *Rumsey v. Arizona*, the Court extended this holding to noncapital cases, holding that the Clause applies to any sentencing proceeding where the state has the power to increase the penalty based on facts not found by a jury. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209. Here, the state sought to double Monge’s sentence based on a prior conviction. This is precisely the type of proceeding *Rumsey* identified as subject to Double Jeopardy protection.

**II. THE "SAME EVIDENCE" RULE BARS RETRIAL.**

Even if the proceeding is considered a sentencing hearing, the "same evidence" rule of *Green v. United States* bars retrial. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 188. *Green* held that if the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction, the state cannot use that same evidence to support a sentence enhancement. *Id.* at 188-89. The California Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to prove the prior assault conviction. Therefore, the state cannot retry the allegation using the same evidence. This rule is equally applicable in the context of a guilty plea, as established in *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 87, 98 (1978), and in civil proceedings, as noted in *United States v. Martin Linen Supply Co.*, 430 U.S. 564, 579 (1977).

**III. THE "SAME OFFENSE" ANALYSIS CONFIRMS THE PROHIBITION.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits a second prosecution for the "same offense." *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299, 304 (1932). A prior conviction is a distinct offense from the current drug charges. *Id.* at 303. Because the prior conviction is a separate offense, the state is barred from retrying it after an acquittal or insufficient evidence finding. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 70 (1906) (recognizing the prohibition against using the same evidence to support a second prosecution). While *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), allows for retrial after a conviction if the sentence is increased, *Green* provides the necessary exception when the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction in the first instance.
