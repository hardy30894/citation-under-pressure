**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause forbids the State from trying again to prove a prior conviction allegation after it has already failed to carry its burden. U.S. Const. amend. V; *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794-96 (1969); *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The constitutional protection is against being “twice put in jeopardy,” not merely against being twice tried on a formal charge. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 128-29 (1904); *Ball v. United States*, 163 U.S. 662, 671-72 (1896). Once the State has litigated the truth of a sentencing allegation and lost because the evidence was insufficient, it may not take another shot at the same allegation. *United States v. Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. 85, 87-88 (1916); *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957).

This Court’s cases have consistently rejected an empty formalism in double jeopardy analysis. The Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. It also protects against the “embarrassment, expense and ordeal” of repeated prosecutions and against allowing the sovereign repeated efforts to secure conviction. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. Those dangers are present here. A prior conviction allegation is not an abstract administrative matter. It is a factual predicate for a materially greater punishment, and the State sought to prove it in a formal adversarial proceeding. When that proof failed, the Constitution did not authorize a second proceeding to relitigate the same allegation. *Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. at 87-88; *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173-74 (1873).

The State cannot avoid the Double Jeopardy Clause by calling the proceeding “sentencing.” The relevant question is whether the defendant has already been exposed to a final determination on the fact the State must prove to obtain the enhanced punishment. This Court has treated finality on the relevant issue as the touchstone. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72; *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29; *Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. at 87-88. Here, the prior conviction allegation was tried separately, evidence was received, and the court made a true finding. After the Court of Appeal held the evidence insufficient, the State sought to try the allegation again. That is the very sort of second attempt the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Sanges v. United States*, 144 U.S. 310, 312-13 (1892).

Nor is there any principled basis for limiting the Clause’s protection to capital sentencing. The Fifth Amendment contains no such exception. U.S. Const. amend. V. To the contrary, the Supreme Court’s cases show that the Clause’s application turns on the nature of the sovereign’s successive effort, not on the label attached to the proceeding. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72. If the government is permitted to retry a prior conviction allegation simply because the proceeding occurs at sentencing, then the State may circumvent the constitutional bar whenever it chooses to attach punishment to a fact in a bifurcated hearing. Nothing in the Constitution permits that evasion. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 794-96.

The danger is especially acute where, as here, the State’s first attempt failed on insufficiency grounds. A ruling of insufficiency means the prosecution did not prove its case. To allow retrial after such a ruling would convert the Double Jeopardy Clause into a procedural suggestion rather than a constitutional command. That would contradict *Ball* and *Oppenheimer*, which hold that the sovereign does not get unlimited attempts to establish a fact once it has been adjudicated. 163 U.S. at 671-72; 242 U.S. at 87-88. It would also undermine the finality and repose the Clause guarantees. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 173-74.

The judgment below should be reversed. Once the State failed to prove petitioner’s prior conviction allegation, the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial of that allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 794-96; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72.
