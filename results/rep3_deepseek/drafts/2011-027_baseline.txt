**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Requires That Title to Riverbeds Pass to the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood, But the Test Is Applied to Discrete Segments, Not the Entire River.**

The “equal footing doctrine” provides that upon admission to the Union, a State acquires title to the beds of waterways that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Oregon ex rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co.*, 429 U.S. 363, 370 (1977). Navigability for title purposes is a federal question determined by reference to the “navigable in fact” test: a river is navigable if it was “used, or [was] susceptible of being used, in their natural and ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted.” *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. 557, 563 (1871). Critically, the determination must be made “as of the time of statehood.” *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926). The Court has long recognized that navigability is assessed on a segment-by-segment basis, because a river may be navigable in part and non-navigable in others. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75–76 (1931) (holding that the Colorado River’s navigability “must be considered separately for each of the reaches”); *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922) (examining “the navigability of the Red River at the points where the state line crosses it”).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this established framework. The trial court evaluated the historical evidence of navigability for each of the three river segments—the Clark Fork, Missouri, and Madison Rivers—at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. The court relied on voluminous documentary evidence, including expedition logs, steamboat records, and government surveys, demonstrating that these stretches were in fact used for commercial transportation of goods and passengers. That evidence was admissible under standard hearsay exceptions for ancient documents and public records. *See* M. R. Evid. 803(8), 902(4)–(6), (8). The court’s finding that the relevant segments were navigable in 1889 is therefore a factual determination entitled to deference.

**II. The Historical Record Demonstrates That Each Relevant Stretch Was Navigable at Statehood, and PPL’s Segment-by-Segment Challenge Is Unsupported by the Record.**

PPL argues that the trial court failed to conduct a sufficiently granular analysis—e.g., examining the specific reaches where its dams are located. But the equal footing doctrine does not require a court to parse every mile of river; it requires only a practical assessment of whether the river, “in its natural and ordinary condition,” was capable of commercial use at statehood. *The Montello*, 20 Wall. 430, 442 (1874). The trial court’s findings are amply supported by evidence that each of the three rivers was used by steamboats and other vessels for significant distances during the relevant period. For example, the Missouri River segment was regularly navigated by steamboats from Fort Benton, Montana, to the Mississippi River. *See United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 409 (1940) (navigability does not require “a highway over which the commerce of the United States actually moves”). The Clark Fork and Madison Rivers were similarly used for logging and supply transport. The trial court did not err in concluding that these stretches satisfied the *Daniel Ball* test.

Moreover, the segment-by-segment approach urged by PPL would contravene the settled principle that “navigability is not destroyed because the stream is not generally navigable at all seasons or at all points.” *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 23 (1935). The Court has consistently rejected a “hairline” approach that would require proof of continuous commercial use at every precise location. *Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 411. The trial court’s methodology—considering the historical use of the rivers as whole segments—fully comports with this Court’s precedents.

**III. The Trial Court’s Reliance on Historical Evidence Was Proper and Did Not Violate Evidentiary Standards.**

PPL’s evidentiary objections are meritless. The hearsay rule does not bar the use of ancient documents or public records to prove navigability, as this Court has repeatedly recognized. *See, e.g., Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. at 592 (relying on “maps, surveys, and reports” to assess navigability). The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the documents attached to the State Archivist’s affidavit were self-authenticating under M. R. Evid. 902 and admissible under the ancient-documents and public-records exceptions. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in considering this evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court properly applied that test, and its factual findings are supported by the record. The judgment should be affirmed.
