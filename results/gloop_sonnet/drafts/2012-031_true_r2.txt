**ARGUMENT**

**Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement obligates a district court to determine, through admissible evidence, that damages are susceptible of class-wide measurement by a methodology tied to the theory of antitrust injury actually accepted for trial.**

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b)(3) requires that questions of law or fact common to the class predominate over questions affecting only individual members. That standard is not satisfied merely because common evidence might establish that a defendant engaged in some unlawful restraint; the court must separately determine, with equal rigor, that a common methodology exists to measure the damages flowing from the particular restraint on which liability rests. Antitrust damages, unlike liability, must be moored to the specific competitive injury found unlawful — not to the totality of a defendant's conduct.

This Court's antitrust decisions have long treated liability and damages as analytically distinct inquiries. In *Hanover Shoe, Inc.* v. *United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 392 U.S. 481 (1968), the Court of Appeals "affirmed the finding of liability but disagreed with the District Court on certain questions relating to the damage award." *Id.* at 481–82. That separation of inquiries confirms that a finding of common liability evidence does not automatically supply, or excuse the need for, common proof of damages. Likewise, in *Kiefer-Stewart Co.* v. *Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 211 (1951), a damages award followed only after "evidence was introduced tending to show" the specific unlawful price-fixing agreement at issue, *id.* at 211–12 — damages were tethered to the particular violation proved, not to defendants' conduct generally. The same discipline is required at certification: a methodology may not measure damages attributable to conduct or theories broader than the single theory certified for class treatment.

That correspondence between liability theory and damages proof follows from the structure of Sherman Act claims themselves. In *United States* v. *First National Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. 665 (1964), this Court found a violation because "the elimination of significant competition between First National and Security Trust constitutes an unreasonable restraint of trade." *Id.* at 665–66. The Court tied the violation — and necessarily the resulting injury — to the specific competitive relationship destroyed, grounding its holding in *Northern Securities Co.* v. *United States*, 193 U.S. 197, and *United States* v. *Union Pacific R. Co.*, 226 U.S. 61 (1913), both of which condemned combinations because they eliminated an identifiable competitive relationship "without reference to the strength or weakness of whatever competition remained." *First Nat. Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. at 665. The corollary for damages is unavoidable: where a complaint advances multiple discrete theories of anticompetitive harm, and only one theory — overbuilder deterrence — survives for class treatment, a damages model that computes overcharges attributable to all theories indiscriminately cannot serve as common proof of the one theory actually certified. It measures something other than the injury the class is permitted to litigate.

Precision in defining the relevant competitive relationship is equally indispensable to any resulting damages calculation. In *Seaboard Air Line Railroad Co.* v. *United States*, 382 U.S. 154 (1965), this Court explained that a three-judge District Court "set aside the order and remanded the case to the Commission for further proceedings," concluding that "the Commission's analysis of the competitive effects of the merger was fatally defective because the Commission had not determined whether the merger violated" the Clayton Act "by reference to the relevant product and geographic markets." *Id.* at 154. A regression model that calculates overcharges by reference to an undifferentiated regional class footprint, rather than to the relevant geographic market in which the certified overbuilder-deterrence theory actually operated, suffers the identical defect: it cannot supply admissible, common proof of damages traceable to the accepted theory of harm.

Nor may that burden be relaxed for administrative convenience. This Court has recognized that Sherman Act plaintiffs bear a distinctive burden of proof relative to other statutory schemes. *See Leh* v. *General Petroleum Corp.*, 382 U.S. 54, 55 (1965) (recounting the argument that, "because of the greater burden of proof under the Sherman Act," Sherman Act claims could not simply be equated with parallel Clayton Act claims). A model that overstates injury by folding in damages from non-common, individualized, or ultimately unaccepted theories of harm does not satisfy that heightened burden merely because it is uniform in form; uniformity of methodology is not equivalent to accuracy of measurement.

Finally, even under the deferential standard ordinarily applied to factual findings, a court of appeals may not affirm findings resting on a mistaken application of the governing legal standard. As this Court explained in *United States* v. *United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), a finding of fact may be overturned when the reviewing court is left with "the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395. Here the mistake is legal, not merely factual: the courts below allowed a single, undifferentiated damages model to stand as common proof of a narrowed, singular theory of antitrust impact, without requiring that the model isolate damages actually attributable to that theory.

Rule 23(b)(3) demands more. This Court should hold that class certification requires admissible, common evidentiary proof of damages tethered to the specific theory of antitrust injury accepted for class treatment — proof that respondents never supplied — and should reverse the judgment below.
