# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT BAR RETRIAL OF PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS IN NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment provides that "[n]o person shall...be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). By its plain terms, the Clause applies to prosecutions for offenses—not to sentencing enhancements based on recidivism. Petitioner's prior conviction allegation was never an "offense" for which he was prosecuted; it was a sentencing factor. This Court should reject petitioner's invitation to extend double jeopardy protections beyond their historical and textual mooring.

### A. Sentencing Proceedings Traditionally Fall Outside the Double Jeopardy Clause's Protection

This Court's precedents establish that sentencing determinations, including those based on prior convictions, do not carry the same constitutional protections as trials on guilt or innocence. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), this Court upheld a death sentence imposed after a judge considered information about the defendant's uncharged prior conduct—information obtained without affording the defendant the right to confront or cross-examine witnesses. This Court explained that the procedural protections applicable at trial "traditionally" have not applied at sentencing, and cautioned that "[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure." *Id.* at 251.

This Court reaffirmed *Williams* as recently as its decision in *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), and in *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389 (1995), where it held that due process does not require "that courts throughout the Nation abandon their age-old practice of seeking information from out-of-court sources to guide their judgment toward a more enlightened and just sentence." *Id.* at 398. If due process itself does not mandate trial-like procedures for sentencing determinations, the more specific protections of the Double Jeopardy Clause—which applies only to prosecutions "for the same offence"—cannot be read to constrain noncapital sentencing proceedings.

### B. *Bullington* and *Rumsey* Are Limited to the Unique Context of Capital Sentencing

Petitioner relies principally on *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), which held that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a capital sentencing determination where a jury has effectively "acquitted" the defendant of a death sentence. But this Court's reasoning in those cases rested on the singular characteristics of capital sentencing proceedings that "resemble a trial." *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10.

Critically, this Court has already signaled that *Bullington*'s reasoning does not extend to noncapital sentencing. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), this Court observed that "[b]oth *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding." *Id.* at 392. This Court further noted that "a reasonable jurist reviewing our precedents at the time respondent's conviction and sentence became final would not have considered the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to a noncapital sentencing proceeding to be dictated by our precedents." *Id.* at 393.

The capital sentencing context in *Bullington* possessed distinctive features absent here: the Missouri capital sentencing scheme required the jury to make findings governed by evidentiary standards "similar to those used at the trial itself" on guilt, and the choice between life and death implicated the "acute need for reliability" this Court has consistently demanded in capital proceedings. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438. As this Court recognized in *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349 (1977), the constitutional protections applicable to sentencing may properly differ between capital and noncapital contexts precisely because of "the qualitative difference between death and other penalties." *Id.* at 357-58.

### C. Extending Double Jeopardy Protection to Noncapital Recidivism Determinations Would Improperly Constitutionalize a Matter of Legislative Grace

Because a State need not provide any trial-like procedure for determining prior conviction allegations in noncapital cases, see *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 245-46, a State that voluntarily provides such procedures—as California has done through Penal Code section 1025—need not extend the full panoply of trial protections, including double jeopardy. This Court has recognized that recidivism enhancements are fundamentally different from elements of the underlying offense. In *Almendarez-Torres v. United States*, 523 U.S. 224 (1998), decided the same year as the proceedings below, this Court held that the fact of a prior conviction is a traditional sentencing factor rather than an element of the offense, reasoning that recidivism "is as typical a sentencing factor as one might imagine." *Id.* at 230.

Treating prior conviction findings as subject to double jeopardy would create an anomalous and unworkable rule: a sentencing court's erroneous evidentiary ruling on the sufficiency of proof of a prior conviction—a matter readily correctable on remand—would forever insulate a defendant from the enhanced sentence the legislature has prescribed for recidivists. Nothing in this Court's jurisprudence compels such a result, and the "same offense" language of the Fifth Amendment forecloses it.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Supreme Court of California should be affirmed.
