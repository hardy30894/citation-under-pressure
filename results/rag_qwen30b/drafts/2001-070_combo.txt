Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1879), the Court upheld the constitutionality of execution by firing squad, rejecting the argument that such a method constituted cruel and unusual punishment. The Court emphasized that the punishment was “in accordance with the laws of the land” and that “the power of the State to punish crime is not limited by the mode of punishment, so long as it is not inhuman or barbarous.” Id. at 136. The Court further observed that “the punishment of death is not cruel within the meaning of the Constitution, if it is inflicted in accordance with law.” Id. This principle remains valid: the Constitution does not require that punishment be tailored to the offender’s mental state, so long as the process is lawful and the crime is sufficiently grave.

The Court held that “the law does not require that a man be of sound mind at the time of the commission of the crime in order to be punished for it.” Id. at 161. The Court emphasized that “the punishment of death is not a matter of mercy, but of justice,” and that “the law does not excuse crime because of mental weakness.” Id. This precedent confirms that mental retardation, even if it affects judgment or impulse control, does not render a person constitutionally exempt from capital punishment.
