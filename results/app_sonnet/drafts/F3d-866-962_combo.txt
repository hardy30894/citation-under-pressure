ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. Summary judgment is proper only where there is no genuine dispute of material fact. When intent, motive, or credibility are central to a claim, courts have long recognized that summary disposition is disfavored. In Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464 (1962), the Supreme Court explained that “summary procedures should be used sparingly . . . where motive and intent play leading roles, the proof is largely in the hands of the alleged [wrongdoers], and hostile witnesses thicken the plot.” Id. at 473. Likewise, in Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620 (1944), the Court held that summary judgment cannot rest on affidavits whose credibility a jury would ordinarily test through cross-examination. These principles apply with full force here, where BNSF’s motive for disciplining Foster, Kline, and Snyder is the central disputed issue.

II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Causation

A. The Contributing-Factor Standard Is Deliberately Lenient

The FRSA’s “contributing factor” causation standard was borrowed from the relaxed causation framework long applied under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act. In Rogers v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co., 352 U.S. 500 (1957), the Supreme Court held that a railroad’s negligence is actionable if it “played any part, even the slightest, in producing the injury.” Id. at 506. That standard reflects Congress’s judgment that railroad workers should not be forced to prove that a protected activity was the sole or primary cause of adverse action. Foster, Kline, and Snyder need only show that their safety reports about the unlit, railless bridge were a contributing factor—however small—in BNSF’s decision to discipline them. The record here, at minimum, creates a jury question on that score: each plaintiff reported the dangerous condition of the bridge before BNSF opened its investigation, the investigation followed within days, increased covert operational testing occurred while the hearing was pending, and discipline issued only after the plaintiffs had repeatedly testified that BNSF officials—not the employees—chose the unsafe crew-change location.

B. Suspicious Timing and Pretext Support an Inference of Retaliatory Motive

Courts long ago recognized that an employer’s unlawful motive may be inferred from timing and from the pretextual nature of a proffered justification. In NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221 (1963), the Court held that conduct “inherently destructive” of protected rights carries its own indicia of intent and may support liability even absent direct proof of motive. Id. at 227–28. Similarly, in NLRB v. Great Dane Trailers, Inc., 388 U.S. 26 (1967), the Court affirmed that a discriminatory motive can be inferred where an employer offers no legitimate business justification for conduct that burdens protected activity. Id. at 33–34. Here, BNSF’s investigation began the day after Foster gave her recorded statement questioning why the crew change occurred at a location employees had previously flagged as dangerous. The temporal sequence—reports of the hazard, followed swiftly by an investigation notice, followed by discipline against the very employees who reported the hazard and contradicted management’s account—permits the same inference of retaliatory motive that Erie Resistor and Great Dane Trailers found sufficient to reach a factfinder.

C. Credibility Disputes About the Crew-Change Location Cannot Be Resolved on Summary Judgment

The parties’ dispute over whether BNSF instructed the crew to change locations east or west of the bridge is precisely the sort of credibility contest that Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), instructs reviewing bodies to weigh carefully rather than resolve as a matter of law. The Court there cautioned that a reviewing body must consider “whatever in the record fairly detracts” from the weight of the evidence supporting a challenged finding. Id. at 488. Knutstrom and Dixon testified that oral instructions directed employees east of the bridge; Foster, Kline, Snyder, and their fellow crewmembers testified to the opposite. That conflict is the essence of a jury question, not a basis for judgment as a matter of law. See also Sartor, 321 U.S. at 627 (summary judgment inappropriate where a jury “might disbelieve” the movant’s version of events).

III. The Public Law Board’s Award Does Not Resolve—and Cannot Foreclose—the Statutory Retaliation Claim

BNSF cannot rely on the Public Law Board’s partial mitigation of discipline to defeat the plaintiffs’ FRSA claims. Railway Labor Act adjustment boards possess jurisdiction only over disputes arising under collective bargaining agreements; they do not adjudicate independent statutory rights. In Slocum v. Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad Co., 339 U.S. 239 (1950), the Court confined adjustment-board jurisdiction to “grievances” under existing agreements, distinct from statutory causes of action. And in Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co. v. Burley, 325 U.S. 711 (1945), the Court recognized that an employee’s pursuit of contractual grievance remedies does not extinguish separate rights arising outside the collective bargaining agreement. Id. at 723–24. The Board’s reinstatement of Foster and removal of certain discipline from Kline’s and Snyder’s records addressed only the propriety of discipline under the collective bargaining agreement; it did not, and could not, adjudicate whether BNSF retaliated against the plaintiffs for engaging in protected safety-reporting activity under federal law.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
