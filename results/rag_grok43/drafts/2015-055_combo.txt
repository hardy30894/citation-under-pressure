The residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause. Giaccio v. Pennsylvania, 382 U.S. 399, 402-03 (1966) (statute invalid where it "authorizes juries to assess costs against acquitted defendants... without prescribing definite standards"); Bouie v. City of Columbia, 378 U.S. 347, 350-51 (1964) (conviction violates due process when it rests on judicial construction that "fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice"); Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196, 201-02 (1948) (due process requires that convictions rest on the offense actually tried and defined at the time of the conduct).

A statute lacking such notice cannot support a valid conviction or sentence. Reynolds v. Cochran, 365 U.S. 525, 529-30 (1961) (invalidating enhanced sentence resting on prior convictions obtained without required procedural safeguards); Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616, 623-24 (1912) (habitual-offender proceeding must afford full opportunity to contest identity and validity of predicates).

Because the clause supplied no ascertainable standard, any sentence imposed under it is void. Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 339-40 (1963) (denial of fundamental procedural protection renders conviction invalid); Williams v. Kaiser, 323 U.S. 471, 475-76 (1945) (same). The same principle governs when a later decision reveals the statute's constitutional infirmity. Lynumn v. Illinois, 372 U.S. 528, 534-35 (1963) (constitutional violation infecting the original proceeding must be redressed on collateral attack); Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242-43 (1969) (guilty plea and resulting sentence cannot stand where record fails to show compliance with due-process requirements at the time of acceptance).

Welch's 1996 robbery conviction was counted solely under the residual clause. The clause's invalidity therefore renders his enhanced sentence unconstitutional, and the rule announced in Johnson must apply to his case.
