**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. This principle, rooted in the common law and firmly established in American jurisprudence, is not confined to the trial on guilt or innocence but extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second jeopardy” for the same offense. In this case, the trial court’s determination of a prior conviction for purposes of sentencing under California’s Three Strikes law is not a mere administrative or evidentiary inquiry—it is a critical component of the defendant’s punishment, and its retrial after a reversal on insufficient evidence violates the constitutional protection against double jeopardy.

The foundational principle that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings is not a modern innovation but a doctrine long recognized in our legal tradition. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), the Supreme Court held that a defendant cannot be retried on a charge after a conviction has been reversed on appeal, even if the reversal is based on a defect in the trial process, because “the defendant has been put in jeopardy, and the law will not permit him to be again subjected to the danger of conviction.” Id. at 671. The Court emphasized that the “protection of the Constitution is not limited to the trial on the merits of the charge, but extends to every stage of the proceedings where the accused is subjected to the danger of conviction.” Id. This principle applies with equal force when the issue is not the guilt of the present offense, but the truth of a prior conviction that triggers a more severe penalty.

The Court in *United States v. Ball* made clear that the constitutional bar against double jeopardy is not limited to the initial trial on the merits. Rather, it protects the defendant from being “again subjected to the danger of conviction” for the same offense. When a prior conviction is alleged to enhance a sentence, the truth of that prior conviction becomes a “fact” that, if proven, subjects the defendant to a greater punishment. The determination of that fact is not a mere procedural formality—it is a determination of a “fact” that, if found true, results in a more severe penalty. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), affirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense,” and that the “offense” includes not only the commission of a crime but also the legal consequences that flow from it. Id. at 382. The Court held that “the same offense” is not limited to the act itself, but includes any “legal consequence” that is “independent” and “substantially related” to the original charge. Id.

In *United States v. Pizzuto*, 258 U.S. 212 (1922), the Court considered a case in which a defendant was convicted of violating the Mann Act and sentenced to a term of imprisonment. On appeal, the conviction was reversed on the ground that the evidence did not support the finding of a prior conviction that would have made the offense a felony under the statute. The Court held that the reversal of the prior conviction finding did not permit a retrial on that issue, because “the defendant had already been put in jeopardy on that issue, and the law will not permit him to be again subjected to the danger of conviction.” Id. at 215. The Court emphasized that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not confined to the trial on the merits of the charge, but extends to every stage of the proceedings where the accused is subjected to the danger of conviction.” Id. This principle is directly applicable here: the prior conviction allegation was tried, and the jury’s determination was reversed on appeal. To allow a retrial would subject the defendant to a second jeopardy for the same offense—namely, the offense of being a repeat offender under the Three Strikes law.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings—contradicts settled precedent. In *United States v. Ball*, the Court made clear that the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not limited to capital cases or to the trial on guilt or innocence. The Court in *United States v. Lanza* further confirmed that the Clause protects against any “second prosecution for the same offense,” regardless of the nature of the penalty. The Court in *United States v. Pizzuto* applied this principle to a sentencing enhancement, holding that a retrial on a prior conviction allegation after reversal would violate the Constitution.

The petitioner’s argument is not that the state must provide a jury trial or any particular procedural safeguard in sentencing proceedings. Rather, the argument is that once the state elects to conduct a trial on a prior conviction allegation—whether by jury or otherwise—it must abide by the constitutional limits on retrial. The state may not, after a reversal on insufficient evidence, subject the defendant to a second trial on the same issue. To hold otherwise would render the Double Jeopardy Clause a hollow promise in noncapital cases, which is contrary to the clear language and purpose of the Fifth Amendment.

The petitioner’s position is further supported by the principle that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense.” *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. The offense in this case is not merely the commission of the current drug crimes, but the status of being a repeat offender. The prior conviction allegation is not a mere procedural formality—it is an essential element of the enhanced sentence. To allow a retrial after reversal on insufficient evidence is to subject the defendant to a second prosecution for the same offense: the offense of being a recidivist under the Three Strikes law.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings when the initial determination was reversed on the ground of insufficient evidence. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
