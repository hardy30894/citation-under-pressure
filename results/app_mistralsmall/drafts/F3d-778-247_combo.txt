**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Search Warrant Failed to Establish a Sufficient Nexus Between the Alleged Crime and the Premises to Be Searched.**

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. This Court has long held that a warrant must demonstrate a “nexus” between the criminal activity and the location to be searched. *United States v. Lucarz*, 414 F.2d 187, 189 (9th Cir. 1969) (citing *Agnello v. United States*, 269 U.S. 20, 33 (1925)). The affidavit here did not supply probable cause to believe that evidence of sexual exploitation of children would be found at Joubert’s parents’ home.

The affidavit relied on generalized assertions that offenders “often maintain possession” of such materials, but it did not link Joubert’s alleged crimes to the specific location. *See United States v. Halsey*, 257 F.2d 167, 171 (7th Cir. 1958) (holding that conclusory statements without specific facts fail to establish probable cause). The affidavit merely recited that Joubert had “contact” with minors and that he had “tear[ed] apart” a computer, but it did not state where that computer was located or that he kept materials at his parents’ home. *Cf. United States v. Ventresca*, 380 U.S. 102, 108 (1965) (requiring that affidavits provide “a substantial basis” for concluding evidence will be found at the place to be searched).

Because the warrant lacked a sufficient nexus, the evidence seized should have been suppressed. *See Agnello*, 269 U.S. at 33 (holding that evidence seized under an invalid warrant is inadmissible).

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting Testimony Regarding Uncharged Child Molestation.**

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) prohibits the admission of evidence of “other crimes, wrongs, or acts” to prove character. *See Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475 (1948) (holding that such evidence is inadmissible unless it serves a proper purpose). The government introduced testimony about uncharged acts of molestation to show Joubert’s propensity to commit the charged offenses, which was plainly improper.

The district court’s admission of this evidence was an abuse of discretion. *See Nash v. United States*, 54 F.2d 1006, 1007 (2d Cir. 1932) (holding that courts must carefully scrutinize Rule 404(b) evidence to prevent prejudice). The testimony was not probative of any material issue other than character, and its prejudicial effect substantially outweighed any minimal probative value. *See United States v. Bozza*, 365 F.2d 206, 214 (2d Cir. 1966) (requiring a balancing test under Rule 403).

**III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of the Federal Statutes to Joubert.**

The government’s theory of prosecution under 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) relies on the Commerce Clause. However, the Supreme Court has long held that the Commerce Clause does not permit federal regulation of purely local conduct. *See United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 551 (1995) (citing *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 119 (1941), for the proposition that Congress may regulate only activities that substantially affect interstate commerce). Here, the government failed to demonstrate any substantial effect on interstate commerce.

The statutes at issue criminalize local possession and production of child pornography, not activities that implicate interstate commerce. *See United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 613 (2000) (holding that Congress cannot regulate noneconomic, intrastate activity under the Commerce Clause). Joubert’s conduct—alleged local exploitation of a child in New Hampshire—falls outside Congress’s commerce power. *See Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241, 258 (1964) (limiting the Commerce Clause to activities with a “substantial and harmful effect” on interstate commerce).

**IV. Joubert’s 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable.**

A sentence is substantively unreasonable if it is “shocking” or “monstrous.” *See United States v. Goff*, 529 F.3d 56, 66 (1st Cir. 2008) (quoting *United States v. Smith*, 440 F.3d 725, 731 (6th Cir. 2006)). Here, the district court imposed a sentence far exceeding the Sentencing Guidelines range for Joubert’s offenses. The court relied on uncharged conduct and improperly considered the dismissed counts as relevant conduct. *See United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 157 (1997) (holding that acquitted conduct may not be used to enhance a sentence).

The 480-month sentence is substantively unreasonable because it is grossly disproportionate to the offense conduct and fails to account for the lack of prior criminal history. *See United States v. Jackson*, 46 F.2d 675, 677 (2d Cir. 1931) (holding that sentences must be proportionate to the offense). The district court’s error in considering improper factors warrants reversal. *See United States v. Cunningham*, 429 F.2d 673, 675 (2d Cir. 1970) (holding that sentences based on improper factors are reversible).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Joubert’s conviction and sentence should be reversed.
