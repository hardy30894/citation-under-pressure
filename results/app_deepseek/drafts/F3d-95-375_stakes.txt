ARGUMENT

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Plaintiffs’ Expert Witnesses as a Discovery Sanction Without Finding Willfulness or Bad Faith and Without Considering Lesser Sanctions.**

A court may dismiss a claim or strike expert testimony as a discovery sanction only upon a finding of willfulness, bad faith, or fault. *National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc.*, 427 U.S. 639, 642–43 (1976) (per curiam) (dismissal under Rule 37 requires “clear record of delay or contumacious conduct”). Here, Plaintiffs’ counsel faced logistical hurdles: multiple pro se litigants, a compressed schedule, and experts who needed additional time to review voluminous documents. The district court acknowledged these difficulties at the June 3, 1993 hearing but nonetheless imposed the “draconian” sanction of striking four experts without exploring lesser alternatives such as extending the deadline or ordering the experts to appear for depositions on a revised schedule. *See Chilcutt v. United States*, 4 F.3d 1313, 1320 (5th Cir. 1993) (sanctions must be “proportionate to the circumstances”). The court’s blanket order violated the principle that dismissal is a “sanction of last resort.” *Marshall v. Sielaff*, 492 F.2d 917, 919 (5th Cir. 1974) (per curiam). Because the record reveals no evidence of intentional delay or bad faith, the district court abused its discretion.

**II. The District Court Erred in Excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson’s Testimony Under *Daubert* Because Their Opinions Were Based on Reliable Methodology and the Court Applied an Overly Rigid Standard.**

Under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 593–94 (1993), the trial court must make a “flexible inquiry” into the reliability of expert testimony, focusing on methodology rather than conclusions. The court here improperly demanded that the experts have fully formed opinions before completing their review of all relevant documents, ignoring that differential diagnosis and epidemiological analysis are accepted methodologies for determining causation. *See Heller v. Shaw Industries, Inc.*, 167 F.3d 146, 154 (3d Cir. 1999) (differential diagnosis is “a valid method for determining causation”). Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson had not yet had the opportunity to examine all case-specific data due to the tight deadlines and Defendants’ own discovery delays. The district court’s exclusion effectively required a degree of certainty that the *Daubert* framework does not demand. *See Joiner v. General Electric Co.*, 522 U.S. 136, 146 (1997) (abuse of discretion review applies; trial court may not “exclude expert testimony simply because it finds the expert’s conclusions unconvincing”). The court’s cursory analysis—without a hearing on the experts’ methodology—was an abuse of discretion. *See Watkins v. Telsmith, Inc.*, 121 F.3d 984, 988–89 (5th Cir. 1997) (district court must provide reasoned explanation for *Daubert* ruling).

**III. Without the Improperly Excluded Expert Testimony, Summary Judgment Was Premature and Inappropriate.**

Summary judgment is proper only when no genuine dispute of material fact exists. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322–23 (1986). Here, Plaintiffs’ experts were prepared to offer opinions that exposure to the chemicals at the superfund sites caused their injuries and property damage. The district court’s striking and exclusion of these experts left Plaintiffs without any admissible evidence on causation, leading to summary judgment. But because the exclusion was erroneous, the summary judgment cannot stand. *See Elkins v. Richardson-Merrell, Inc.*, 8 F.3d 1068, 1071 (6th Cir. 1993) (reversal of *Daubert* exclusion requires reversal of summary judgment). Moreover, the district court should have granted Plaintiffs additional time to complete their experts’ opinions under Rule 56(f) before granting summary judgment. *See Celotex*, 477 U.S. at 326 (nonmovant must have adequate opportunity for discovery). Plaintiffs had not yet completed depositions or received all necessary documents; the court’s refusal to allow further discovery was an abuse of discretion. *See Resolution Trust Corp. v. Sharif-Munir-Davidson Dev. Corp.*, 992 F.2d 1398, 1403 (5th Cir. 1993) (summary judgment premature when party has not had “adequate time for discovery”).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s order striking and excluding Plaintiffs’ expert testimony, vacate the grant of summary judgment, and remand for further proceedings.
