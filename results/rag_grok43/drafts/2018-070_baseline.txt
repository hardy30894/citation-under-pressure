**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” This Court has consistently read the provision to adopt state law only where federal law leaves a gap. In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court held that state wrongful-death remedies applied on fixed drilling platforms precisely because “the [federal] admiralty action under the [High] Seas Act no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave.” *Id.* at 366. The absence of a federal remedy removed “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law.” *Ibid.*

The same premise governed *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971). There the Court explained that OCSLA “adopts the laws of the adjacent States as surrogate federal law” only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law, confirming that state rules fill voids left by the federal scheme. *Id.* at 100-01. *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), reiterated the point: “OCSLA . . . mandates that state laws apply as federal laws ‘[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.’” *Id.* at 481 (quoting 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary view—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable” and not expressly preempted—cannot be reconciled with these holdings. Under *Rodrigue* and *Huson*, the FLSA’s comprehensive regulation of wages and hours on the Outer Continental Shelf leaves no gap for California’s more protective rules to occupy. Because the statute supplies a federal remedy, state law is not assimilated as surrogate federal law. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
