**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional guarantee is not limited to trials on the underlying criminal charge; it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a defendant may not be retried on a sentencing enhancement—specifically, a capital sentencing aggravating factor—after a prior proceeding on that issue ended in an acquittal or reversal due to insufficient evidence. The Court emphasized that such a proceeding “involves the same kind of inquiry into the defendant’s past conduct and character as a trial on guilt or innocence,” and thus “the same constitutional protections” apply. *Id.* at 439, 441.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding—directly contradicts *Bullington* and its progeny. The majority’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it involved a capital case is legally untenable. The Court in *Bullington* did not limit its reasoning to capital cases. Rather, it grounded its holding in the nature of the proceeding itself: a determination of facts that could result in a more severe punishment, including death, and that is conducted with the same adversarial characteristics as a trial on guilt. *Id.* at 439. The Court explicitly stated that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” and that “the same constitutional protections” apply when the issue is “whether the defendant is subject to a more severe penalty.” *Id.* at 439, 441. Nothing in the opinion suggests that this protection vanishes in noncapital cases.

The lead opinion’s reliance on *Caspari v. Stewart*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), to support the proposition that *Bullington* does not apply in noncapital cases is misplaced. In *Caspari*, the Court did not decide whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing enhancements. Instead, it addressed whether a federal habeas court could apply a new rule of law—namely, that *Bullington* applies to noncapital cases—retroactively. In doing so, the Court observed that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases and that their reasoning “was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392. This observation was not a holding, nor did it purport to overrule or limit *Bullington*’s broader principles. Indeed, the Court in *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995), reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause “protects against multiple punishments for the same offense,” and that “the Clause applies to sentencing determinations that are part of the same criminal proceeding.” *Id.* at 398. The Court has never held that the Clause is inapplicable to noncapital sentencing enhancements.

The California Supreme Court’s assertion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because the prior conviction proceeding is “not a trial on guilt or innocence” ignores the fundamental purpose of the Clause: to protect against the government’s power to harass or punish a defendant repeatedly for the same conduct. The determination of a prior conviction is not a mere administrative formality. It is a factfinding proceeding that directly affects the severity of punishment. As the Court recognized in *Bullington*, such proceedings “involve the same kind of inquiry into the defendant’s past conduct and character as a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. The defendant is entitled to the same protections—such as the right to a fair hearing, the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the right to confront witnesses—because the outcome may result in a significantly longer prison term. *See People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 228 (1996) (holding that the rules of evidence and confrontation apply in prior conviction trials).

The majority’s argument that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because the protections in *Penal Code* § 1025 are statutory, not constitutional, is equally flawed. The fact that California has chosen to provide a jury trial and other procedural safeguards for prior conviction allegations does not diminish the constitutional significance of those proceedings. On the contrary, the very existence of such safeguards underscores that these are not mere administrative determinations, but fact-intensive inquiries with serious consequences. The Constitution does not permit a state to circumvent its obligations by granting procedural rights through statute. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949) (“The due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.”). But that does not mean the Constitution is silent when the state chooses to provide a trial-like proceeding.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply in noncapital cases is not only inconsistent with *Bullington*, but also undermines the integrity of the constitutional protection against multiple prosecutions. If a defendant may be retried on a prior conviction allegation after a reversal due to insufficient evidence, the government may use the threat of retrial to coerce a defendant into waiving his right to a jury trial or to plead guilty to the enhancement. This is precisely the kind of prosecutorial overreach the Double Jeopardy Clause was designed to prevent.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings that determine the truth of a prior conviction allegation. The California Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise. The judgment of the Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
