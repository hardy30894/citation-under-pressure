**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tribal Court's Failure to Afford Meaningful Compulsory Process Violated Selam's Rights Under 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6).**

The Indian Civil Rights Act provides that no tribe exercising powers of self-government shall "deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right ... to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This guarantee mirrors the Sixth Amendment, and the values it protects are not technical. As the Supreme Court explained in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), "[t]he right to offer the testimony of witnesses, and to compel their attendance, if necessary, is in plain terms the right to present a defense, the right to present the defendant's version of the facts as well as the prosecution's to the jury so it may decide where the truth lies." Id. at 19. The right is meaningless if it consists only of a pro forma recitation to a criminal defendant that he "may" subpoena witnesses, unaccompanied by any practical mechanism ensuring that an unrepresented or lay-represented defendant understands how to invoke it. See Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 408-09 (1988) (compulsory process clause protects the right "to secure the attendance ... and the presentation" of favorable testimony, not merely a nominal opportunity to request it).

Here, Selam was represented by a lay Tribal Spokesperson, not a lawyer, in proceedings where the government sought to convict him of two serious sexual-offense charges. Selam was simply told, in the abstract, that he could compel witnesses; he was never afforded tribal-court process — such as a continuance, appointed investigative assistance, or clear instruction on how to secure a subpoena — that would have translated that abstract right into a usable one. A bare judicial advisement does not discharge the tribe's affirmative obligation under Section 1302(6), any more than a bare recitation of the right to counsel discharges the government's obligation to make that right effective. Because the tribal courts treated Selam's failure to formally request a subpoena as an implicit waiver, without ensuring that a defendant assisted only by lay counsel understood the mechanics of compulsory process, the conviction rests on a proceeding that did not satisfy Section 1302(6).

**II. Selam's Failure to Raise His Confrontation Clause Claim Before the Tribal Court of Appeals Does Not Bar Federal Habeas Review Because He Can Show Cause and Prejudice.**

Habeas corpus under 25 U.S.C. § 1303 is the exclusive federal remedy for violations of the Indian Civil Rights Act. Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 58 (1978). Because that remedy exists to vindicate individual rights secured against tribal action, principles governing federal habeas review of state convictions — including the treatment of unpreserved claims — provide the appropriate analytical framework. Federal courts require exhaustion of tribal remedies as "a matter of comity," not as a jurisdictional bar, so that tribal forums have "the first opportunity to evaluate the factual and legal bases" for a challenge. National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 856 (1985); see also Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 16 (1987) (tribal courts should be given "the first opportunity to evaluate the factual and legal bases" of claims arising within their jurisdiction).

Because exhaustion in this context, like exhaustion in the state habeas context, is prudential rather than absolute, a procedural default may be excused where the petitioner demonstrates cause for the default and actual prejudice resulting from the underlying violation. Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) (cause requires "some objective factor external to the defense" that impeded the ability to raise the claim). Selam can make both showings.

Cause exists because Selam was represented before the tribal court of appeals by the same system of lay advocacy that failed to identify or preserve the confrontation issue at trial. A criminal defendant proceeding through a tribal system without access to trained legal counsel cannot fairly be charged with the same standard of issue-spotting expected of licensed appellate counsel. That external limitation on Selam's ability to recognize and articulate a federal constitutional-style claim constitutes cause under Murray, 477 U.S. at 488.

Prejudice is equally clear. In the second prosecution, the five-year-old complainant never testified. Her entire accusation reached the tribal court solely through the testimony of her parents, an examining physician, and a police investigator repeating her out-of-court statements. Selam was convicted and sentenced to a year's imprisonment based entirely on hearsay accounts of a witness he never had the opportunity to confront, cross-examine, or test before the fact-finder. This is precisely the kind of proceeding that Section 1302(6)'s confrontation guarantee — modeled on the Sixth Amendment — was designed to prevent. A default that forecloses review of a claim going to the very reliability of the evidence supporting conviction satisfies the prejudice requirement of Wainwright and Murray.

Because Selam has shown both cause for his failure to raise the confrontation claim before the tribal court of appeals and actual prejudice from the underlying violation, the district court erred in holding the claim waived without reaching its merits.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand with instructions to grant the writ, or, alternatively, to address the merits of Selam's confrontation clause claim.
