# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 7702(a)(1)'s Text Draws No Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals of Mixed Cases

The Civil Service Reform Act directs that judicial review of a "case of discrimination" arising from Board proceedings lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). A "case of discrimination" is defined as one in which the employee "has been affected by an action which the employee . . . may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board" and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." *Id.* § 7702(a)(1). This Court held in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), that this definition turns on the nature of the *case*, not the *reason for the Board's disposition* of it. As the Court explained, mixed cases are simply those "appealable to the MSPB and alleging discrimination"—and "when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case, . . . the employee should seek judicial review in district court, not in the Federal Circuit." 568 U.S. at 44, 50.

Nothing in that definition distinguishes dismissals resting on timeliness from dismissals resting on voluntariness. Both categories involve the same operative fact: the Board declines to adjudicate the merits because it concludes, on a threshold basis, that the matter is not properly before it. The D.C. Circuit's effort to distinguish the two—reasoning that a procedurally barred appeal remains "appealable" in some abstract sense while a jurisdictionally barred one never was, Pet. App. 14a—finds no footing in the statutory text. Section 7702(a)(1)(A) asks only whether the employee "has been affected by an action which the employee . . . may appeal," not whether the Board, after the fact, agrees that the appeal was properly filed. A retirement or suspension coerced by discrimination is no less an "action" appealable to the Board than an untimely removal claim; in both instances the Board ultimately concludes it lacks power to proceed.

*Kloeckner* itself confirms this reading. The Court there rejected precisely the kind of forum-splitting the D.C. Circuit reintroduces, explaining that Congress intended "a simple test" for identifying the proper forum—"the nature of the mixed case, not the ground of the Board's decision." 568 U.S. at 44. The Court emphasized that "MSPB review of mixed cases . . . is to proceed in the same way regardless of the ground of the Board's decision," 568 U.S. at 50, and that fracturing review based on the Board's rationale would produce exactly the confusion Congress sought to avoid.

## II. *Kloeckner*'s Reasoning Extends Squarely to Jurisdictional Dismissals

The D.C. Circuit reasoned that *Kloeckner* addressed only "procedural" dismissals and thus left undisturbed *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which routes jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit. Pet. App. 8a–9a. But *Kloeckner*'s rationale sweeps more broadly than its facts. The Court grounded its holding in three considerations, each of which applies with equal force here.

**First**, the Court relied on the statute's presumption of a "single, integrated review scheme." 568 U.S. at 47. Splitting review based on whether the Board's dismissal is characterized as jurisdictional or procedural creates the very bifurcation *Kloeckner* condemned, forcing employees to guess—often before the Board rules—which appellate forum will ultimately have authority over their case.

**Second**, the Court stressed practical administrability, noting that "[b]ifurcated review . . . would needlessly complicate and delay the resolution of MSPB appeals." 568 U.S. at 50. A voluntariness dismissal is no less susceptible to that concern than a timeliness dismissal; indeed, voluntariness determinations frequently require the same factual inquiry into coercion and discriminatory motive that pervades the merits of the discrimination claim itself. Perry's case illustrates the point precisely: whether his retirement was "voluntary" depended on whether the settlement was procured through discriminatory pressure—the identical question a district court would examine were the case bifurcated.

**Third**, *Kloeckner* found it decisive that Congress vested district courts, not the Federal Circuit, with expertise in discrimination law. 568 U.S. at 47–48 ("the Federal Circuit has no expertise in employment discrimination law"). The Federal Circuit possesses no greater competence to assess whether a retirement was coerced by disability discrimination than to assess whether an appeal was untimely for reasons related to that same discrimination.

## III. The D.C. Circuit's Rule Produces the Uncertainty *Kloeckner* Sought to Eliminate

*Powell*'s jurisdictional/procedural distinction cannot be squared with *Kloeckner*'s core teaching that the forum for review must be ascertainable from the "nature of the mixed case" alone, not from the Board's eventual—and often contestable—characterization of its own ruling. 568 U.S. at 44. Boards and reviewing courts frequently disagree about whether a given threshold dismissal is "jurisdictional" or "procedural," a distinction this Court has cautioned is often elusive even in the Article III context. *See Arbaugh v. Y & H Corp.*, 546 U.S. 500, 511 (2006) (courts have been "less than meticulous" in distinguishing jurisdictional from merits-related prerequisites). Importing that unstable line into the mixed-case context recreates the "confusing" bifurcated system Congress rejected. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 44.

Perry's own procedural history demonstrates the problem: the administrative judge twice recharacterized the voluntariness question, first as jurisdictional, and Perry was shuttled from the D.C. Circuit toward the Federal Circuit only because of that label—not because of anything about the substance of his discrimination claim, which remains, at bottom, a claim that discriminatory coercion produced his retirement.

## CONCLUSION

Because Perry's case was a mixed case within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1), and because *Kloeckner* directs that all such cases—regardless of the ground on which the Board disposes of them—must be reviewed in district court, the judgment of the Board should be reviewed there, not in the Federal Circuit.
