# ARGUMENT

## I. Under *Teague v. Lane*, New Rules of Criminal Procedure Do Not Apply Retroactively on Collateral Review

The framework governing this case is well established. In *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), this Court held that "a new rule generally will not apply retroactively to convictions that were already final by the time the new rule was announced." *Id.* at 310. That rule reflects the settled principle that "habeas corpus is not a substitute for direct review," *id.* at 306, and that finality of criminal judgments serves indispensable interests of the state and of the individuals whose cases have been concluded.

*Teague* recognizes only two narrow exceptions. First, a new rule applies retroactively if it is "substantive," meaning it "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). Second, a new rule applies retroactively if it is a "watershed rul[e] of criminal procedure" implicating "the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding." *Saffle v. Parks*, 494 U.S. 484, 495 (1990). This Court has since narrowed the second category nearly to the vanishing point, explaining that it is "unlikely" that any such rule "ha[s] yet to emerge." *Whorton v. Bockting*, 549 U.S. 406, 417 (2007). Johnson's rule fits within neither exception.

## II. *Johnson*'s Rule Is Procedural, Not Substantive

*Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), held that the ACCA's residual clause—covering offenses that "otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another"—was unconstitutionally vague because it required courts to assess "a judicially imagined 'ordinary case' of a crime, not to real-world facts or statutory elements." *Id.* at 596. That holding does not identify which offenders fall outside the ACCA's reach; it invalidates only a method of analysis that this Court found too indeterminate to satisfy due process.

This distinction matters under *Schriro*. A rule is substantive when it "narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms" so that "conduct that the statute purports to criminalize" falls outside its reach, *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351–52 (citing *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998)). *Johnson* does no such thing. It leaves entirely intact the ACCA's elements clause and its enumerated-offense clause, § 924(e)(2)(B)(i)–(ii), both of which continue to render offenders like petitioner subject to the fifteen-year mandatory minimum if their predicate convictions qualify under those provisions. *Johnson* simply removes one analytical pathway—the residual clause's "serious potential risk" standard—because that pathway was too vague to administer, not because Congress lacked power to punish the underlying conduct. As this Court has explained, "rules that regulate only the manner of determining the defendant's culpability are procedural." *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353. A rule eliminating an indeterminate *method* of classifying prior convictions is precisely such a procedural rule; it says nothing about the substantive class of "violent felonies" Congress intended to reach, only that one clause used to identify them was unconstitutionally standardless.

This case is unlike *Bousley*, where the Court's interpretation of "knowingly" in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(1) actually narrowed the substantive elements the government had to prove, 523 U.S. at 616, and unlike the sentencing-eligibility rules discussed in *Schriro* that "prohibit[ed] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants," 542 U.S. at 352. *Johnson* prohibits nothing; it merely forecloses a vague method of proof while leaving two independent, substantively valid clauses of the ACCA in force.

## III. *Johnson*'s Rule Is Not a Watershed Rule of Criminal Procedure

Nor can *Johnson* qualify under the second *Teague* exception. A watershed rule must be one "without which the likelihood of an accurate conviction is seriously diminished," and must "alter our understanding of the bedrock procedural elements essential to the fairness of a proceeding." *Whorton*, 549 U.S. at 418 (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311, 313). This Court has identified only the right to counsel recognized in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), as an example of such a rule, and has since declined to add to that list even for rules of considerable procedural importance. *Whorton*, 549 U.S. at 421 (declining retroactive effect for the *Crawford* confrontation rule). The elimination of one clause among three alternative bases for an enhanced sentence—leaving the elements and enumerated-offense clauses fully operative—does not approach the transformative character required for watershed status.

## Conclusion

Because *Johnson*'s rule neither narrows the substantive class of conduct or persons subject to punishment nor constitutes a watershed procedural rule, it does not apply retroactively to cases, like petitioner's, that were already final under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
