**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over every case that requires application of patent law principles, even when the patent issue is neither the plaintiff's cause of action nor determinative of any right beyond the parties before the court. This Court's precedents answer that question no. Minton's suit against his former attorneys sounds entirely in Texas tort law; the patent-law question it happens to require the state court to answer is neither the source of his claim nor a matter of any consequence beyond this litigation. Under settled doctrine, that is not enough to divest the state courts of jurisdiction.

**I. Federal "arising under" jurisdiction is narrow, and a state malpractice claim does not become a patent case merely because it requires resolution of a hypothetical patent defense.**

Congress granted federal district courts jurisdiction over cases "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), but this Court has consistently resisted readings of "arising under" so broad that any state-created claim touching a federal issue is swept into an exclusive federal forum. As Justice Holmes explained, "a suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action." *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Minton's suit is a Texas tort—legal malpractice—created entirely by Texas law. That resolving it requires the state court to ask what would have happened had counsel timely raised the experimental-use exception does not transform the claim into one "arising under" federal patent law, any more than any backward-looking causation inquiry transforms state tort law into federal law.

The well-pleaded-complaint doctrine confirms this limit: "a suit arises under the Constitution and laws of the United States only when the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action shows that it is based upon those laws or that Constitution." *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Minton's petition pleads the elements of Texas malpractice law; it states no patent cause of action. And even an embedded federal issue must "really and substantially involve[] a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). The experimental-use question here is a fact-bound inquiry confined to two former litigants in a case that has already ended; it does not implicate the validity, construction, or effect of the patent statute for anyone beyond them.

This Court has likewise cautioned against opening the arising-under door indiscriminately, favoring instead "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). A hypothetical defense that was never actually litigated before the Patent Office or any patent tribunal is not "substantial" in the sense *Gully* demands—it is a single strand in the web of an ordinary malpractice case, not a cause weighty enough to displace the States' traditional authority over attorney conduct.

**II. Longstanding precedent confirms that suits requiring only incidental application of patent principles remain within the States' traditional jurisdiction.**

Decades before the modern "arising under" framework took shape, this Court already recognized that a suit requiring courts to apply patent concepts to resolve a contract or property dispute does not thereby become a federal patent case. In *Wilson v. Sandford*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 99 (1850), the Court held that a dispute over rights under a patent license did not arise under the patent laws, because the plaintiff's claim rested on the contract rather than on the patent grant itself. *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255 (1897), reaffirmed that a suit to enforce contractual obligations tied to a patent license need not proceed in federal court merely because resolving it requires reference to patent-law principles. And *New Marshall Engine Co. v. Marshall Engine Co.*, 223 U.S. 473 (1912), confirmed once more that state courts may adjudicate disputes over rights derived from patents where the plaintiff's right to relief flows from something other than the patent statute itself—there, contract; here, the duty of care an attorney owes a client.

The same principle controls this case. Minton's right to relief flows entirely from Texas's law of professional negligence; the patent question is, at most, background necessary to prove or disprove causation—just as license terms were mere background to the contract claims in *Wilson*, *Pratt*, and *New Marshall Engine Co.* If patent-adjacent contract and license disputes may remain in state court under this settled line of authority, a fortiori a state malpractice claim resting on the same kind of backward-looking, party-specific inquiry may as well.

**III. Exercising exclusive federal jurisdiction here would disturb, not preserve, the balance between federal and state judicial responsibilities.**

Embedded-issue jurisdiction is warranted only where a federal forum may entertain the question without disturbing the balance Congress has struck between state and federal courts. That balance tips toward federal jurisdiction only in the rare case carrying broad, prospective importance—such as *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180 (1921), which involved the constitutionality of a federal bond program affecting countless future transactions. Cf. *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 671–73 (1950) (declining to enlarge jurisdiction merely because resolution turns on an antecedent federal question). No comparable stakes exist here. The state court's resolution of Minton's hypothetical, unlitigated defense binds no one but the parties before it, affects no other patent, and will not disturb the uniform administration of the patent laws nationwide.

Nor does patent law's interest in nationwide uniformity require a different result. That interest is vindicated when the Patent and Trademark Office issues patents and when the Federal Circuit reviews patent judgments on direct appeal—as it already did in Minton's underlying infringement suit. It is not served by relocating a malpractice claim, arising after that litigation concluded and binding no one but a client and his former lawyers, into federal court. Texas courts are fully competent to apply settled patent doctrine to decide this fundamentally backward-looking claim, consistent with the States' substantial and traditional interest in regulating the practice of law within their own borders. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117–18; *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

For these reasons, the Texas courts possessed—and should have exercised—jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim, and the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas dismissing it for want of jurisdiction should be reversed.
