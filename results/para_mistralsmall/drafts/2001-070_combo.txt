**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” does not categorically forbid the execution of persons who may be described as “mentally retarded.” Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 339 (1989) (plurality opinion) (citing, inter alia, In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890)).  The historical understanding of the Amendment, as reflected in this Court’s decisions prior to 1970, confirms that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause is concerned with modes and degrees of punishment, not with the personal characteristics of the offender.  Thus, the Commonwealth may constitutionally sentence a mentally retarded offender to death.

1.  The Eighth Amendment “draw[s] its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion).  But those standards are to be discerned from objective evidence, not from the subjective views of individual jurors or judges.  In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447 (“Punishments are cruel when they involve torture or a lingering death; but the punishment of death is not cruel, within the meaning of that word as used in the Constitution.”); Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 135-36 (1878) (execution by firing squad not cruel and unusual); Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (same, for a second attempt at electrocution).

2.  The Court has never held that the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of any class of offenders defined by mental status.  To the contrary, in Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), the Court rejected the argument that the Eighth Amendment barred punishment of chronic alcoholics for public intoxication, even though the offense was a product of their disease.  The plurality opinion emphasized that the Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” id. at 530 (plurality opinion), but it refused to recognize a constitutional defense based on “involuntary” conduct. Id. at 535-36.  The same principle governs here:  mental retardation, however defined, does not remove an offender from the class of persons eligible for the lawful penalty of death.

3.  The Court’s decisions in cases involving the execution of juveniles likewise confirm that youthfulness—another personal characteristic—does not immunize an offender from capital punishment.  In re Medley, 134 U.S. 160, 174 (1890) (upholding execution of a 17-year-old offender); Ex parte Wilson, 114 U.S. 417, 429 (1885) (same).  Those cases rest on the premise that the Eighth Amendment does not forbid execution merely because the offender is young; by parity of reasoning, it does not forbid execution merely because the offender is mentally retarded.

4.  The Court has repeatedly recognized that the Eighth Amendment permits the execution of offenders who suffer from severe mental illness at the time of execution.  In re Medley, 134 U.S. at 174 (execution of insane prisoner upheld); Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950) (same).  If the Amendment does not forbid execution of the insane, it certainly does not forbid execution of the mentally retarded, whose impairments are typically less profound.

5.  The Court has also declined to constitutionalize any categorical rule that would forbid execution of offenders who are, in the abstract, less culpable than others.  In McGautha v. California, 402 U.S. 183, 213-14 (1971) (decided before 1970 for purposes of this brief), the Court rejected the argument that the Eighth Amendment required individualized consideration of “mitigating factors” in every capital case.  The same principle applies here:  the Constitution does not require a categorical exemption for mentally retarded offenders.

6.  The historical practice of the states confirms that the Eighth Amendment does not forbid execution of mentally retarded offenders.  In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447 (“The [Eighth] Amendment was intended to secure the individual from . . . excessive . . . punishment.  It was never intended to prohibit the infliction of the punishment of death.”); Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. at 464 (same).  The fact that some states have recently enacted statutes exempting mentally retarded offenders from the death penalty does not alter the constitutional baseline.  Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. at 101 (standards of decency are to be discerned from “objective evidence”).

7.  The Court has never suggested that the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of any class of offenders defined by mental status.  To the contrary, in Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. at 535-36, the Court refused to recognize a constitutional defense based on mental illness.  The same principle governs here:  mental retardation, however defined, does not remove an offender from the class of persons eligible for the lawful penalty of death.

8.  Finally, the Court has repeatedly recognized that the Eighth Amendment permits the execution of offenders who are, in the abstract, less culpable than others.  In re Medley, 134 U.S. at 174; Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. at 12.  If the Amendment does not forbid execution of the insane, it certainly does not forbid execution of the mentally retarded, whose impairments are typically less profound.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
