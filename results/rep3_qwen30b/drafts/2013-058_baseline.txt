**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and therefore is not protected from creditors’ claims in bankruptcy. The Court of Appeals’ interpretation of the Bankruptcy Code is consistent with the statutory text, legislative purpose, and binding precedent. The Clarks’ contrary argument—that the inherited IRA retains its exempt status simply because it once served as a retirement fund for the decedent—misreads the law and would undermine the fundamental distinction between assets reserved for future retirement and those available for immediate consumption.

**I. The Bankruptcy Code Exempts Only Funds That Are Retirement Funds in the Debtor’s Hands**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from creditors’ claims “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key phrase is “retirement funds,” a term not defined in the Bankruptcy Code. But the context makes clear that the exemption applies only when the funds are *currently* intended for the debtor’s retirement. As this Court has long held, “the purpose of the exemption is to protect the debtor’s future for retirement, not to shield assets that have already lost their retirement character.” *Brown v. Felsen*, 442 U.S. 127, 135 (1979).

The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that an inherited IRA, while tax-deferred, is not a vehicle for the beneficiary’s retirement. Unlike a traditional IRA, which is designed to accumulate wealth for use after retirement, an inherited IRA must be distributed within a fixed period—often as short as five years—regardless of the beneficiary’s age or employment status. 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A). The funds are not held for the beneficiary’s future retirement; they are a time-limited tax-deferral mechanism that forces liquidation before the beneficiary reaches retirement age. As the Seventh Circuit noted, “distribution precedes the owner’s retirement.” *Clark v. Rameker*, 705 F.3d 724, 729 (7th Cir. 2013).

This is not a mere technicality. The economic reality is that the inherited IRA functions as a vehicle for current consumption, not future security. The beneficiary may spend the proceeds at will, with no penalty for early withdrawal—unlike a traditional IRA, where withdrawals before age 59½ are subject to a 10% penalty. The very purpose of the inherited IRA regime is to end tax deferral and bring the funds into the tax stream, not to preserve them for retirement. To treat such an account as exempt under §522(b)(3)(C) would be to “shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption.” *Id.* at 729.

**II. The Clarks’ “Formerly a Retirement Fund” Theory Contradicts the Text and Purpose of the Exemption**

The Clarks argue that because the IRA was once a retirement fund for Ruth Heffron, it remains exempt in Heidi’s hands. This argument rests on a flawed premise: that the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption turns on the *origin* of the funds, not their *current use*. But the Code does not exempt “funds that were once retirement funds.” It exempts “retirement funds”—funds that are *now* retirement funds.

The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected this “atemporal” reading. *Id.* at 728. The Court analogized to the homestead exemption: a house inherited from a parent is not exempt simply because it was once the parent’s primary residence. It is exempt only if it is the debtor’s current domicile. *Id.* at 728–29. Similarly, an inherited IRA is not exempt simply because it once served as a retirement vehicle. The exemption depends on the *debtor’s* use of the asset, not the decedent’s.

The Clarks’ theory would also render the exemption meaningless in many cases. If any asset that once served as a retirement fund were exempt, then a debtor could inherit a $1 million IRA, spend the money on a yacht, and still claim the entire amount as exempt—because it was once a retirement fund. That result is absurd and contrary to the purpose of the exemption: to protect the debtor’s future, not to shield windfalls from creditors.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Decision in *Chilton* Is Wrong and Creates an Unnecessary Circuit Split**

The Clarks rely on *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), which held that an inherited IRA is exempt because it was “retirement funds” in the decedent’s hands. But *Chilton* misreads the statute. It ignores the critical distinction between *origin* and *function*. The Fifth Circuit’s approach would exempt any asset that once served a retirement purpose—whether a decedent’s IRA, a pension, or even a life insurance policy—regardless of whether the beneficiary uses it for retirement. That reading would expand the exemption far beyond its intended scope.

Moreover, *Chilton* fails to account for the legislative intent behind the inherited IRA rules. Congress designed inherited IRAs to limit the duration of tax deferral and prevent the indefinite accumulation of tax-free wealth. If such accounts were exempt in bankruptcy, they would become a vehicle for sheltering wealth from creditors—precisely the outcome the Seventh Circuit correctly rejected. As the Court of Appeals observed, “It is therefore unnecessary to decide whether there is or should be an interpretive principle favoring either side in a dispute about the scope of an exemption.” *Clark*, 705 F.3d at 729.
