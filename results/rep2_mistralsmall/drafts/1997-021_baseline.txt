**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board’s own rationale—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—does not justify imposing a higher evidentiary threshold for polling than for withdrawal of recognition or for seeking an RM election. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-62 (1989). The Board’s approach renders polling a “marginally useful” tool, *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984), despite the fact that polling serves the same purpose as an RM election—testing employee support—without the procedural safeguards of a Board-conducted election. The Board’s rule is therefore arbitrary and capricious. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (agency action must be reasonable and reasonably explained).

The Board’s standard also conflicts with the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). By forcing employers to choose between withdrawing recognition without certainty or risking an unfair labor practice charge, the Board’s rule destabilizes bargaining relationships. The Board’s approach is therefore inconsistent with the Act’s mandate. *See id.*

**II. The Board’s Factual Determination That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board improperly discounted the statements of employees who indicated a loss of union support. For example, the Board refused to credit statements by Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack because they were not members of the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991—the date of Allentown Mack’s letter refusing recognition. *See NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (Board’s factual findings must be supported by substantial evidence). However, the Board’s reliance on the January 25 date is arbitrary. The employees’ statements were made before the sale to Allentown Mack and reflected a general loss of union support in the bargaining unit. The Board’s refusal to consider these statements is unsupported by the record.

The Board also improperly discounted the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick’s statement during a job interview that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union” was dismissed as “equivocal.” *See Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB*, 882 F.2d 19, 22 (1st Cir. 1989) (Board must give due weight to employee statements). Marsh’s complaint about union dues was similarly dismissed. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements is arbitrary and capricious. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n*, 463 U.S. at 43.

Finally, the Board improperly discounted the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr. Bloch’s statement that the night shift did not want the union was dismissed because he did not testify. *See NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 406 (1962) (Board must consider all probative evidence). Mohr’s statement that the union would lose a vote was dismissed as “unverified.” The Board’s refusal to credit these statements is unsupported by substantial evidence. *See Columbian Enameling*, 306 U.S. at 300.

**Conclusion**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is arbitrary and contrary to the Act. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
