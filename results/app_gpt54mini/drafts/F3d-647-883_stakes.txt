**ARGUMENT**

## I. Buckles’s § 2255 motion was timely because the Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007 order recalling and reissuing the mandate restarted the period for seeking certiorari.

A motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 is timely if filed within one year of “the date on which the judgment of conviction becomes final.” 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1). A federal conviction becomes final when the time for filing a petition for certiorari expires, or, if certiorari is sought, when the Supreme Court denies review. *Clay v. United States*, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003); *United States v. Garcia*, 210 F.3d 1058, 1060-61 (9th Cir. 2000).

Buckles’s certiorari petition was timely if the 90-day period ran from June 12, 2007, the date this Court recalled the mandate and denied his motion for appointment of certiorari counsel. Supreme Court Rule 13(3) provides that if “the lower court appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing,” the time to seek certiorari runs from the denial of rehearing. The rule turns on whether the court “appropriately entertains” the request; it does not turn on formal labels. *Missouri v. Jenkins*, 495 U.S. 33, 45 (1990) (explaining that a postjudgment motion that the court entertains can affect finality for certiorari purposes); *Sup. Ct. R. 13(3)*.

Here, Buckles’s April 24, 2007 request was made on the 21st day after denial of rehearing, as Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) contemplated. He asked retained counsel to assist in filing a motion for appointment of counsel, and he forwarded the request to the Clerk. He then filed a “Supplemental Motion for Appointment of Counsel to File a Petition for Writ of Certiorari,” asking the Court to construe his April 24 letter as a timely Rule 4-1(e) motion. The Court did just that, issuing an order that stated: “So construed, we DENY the motion.”

That order necessarily “entertained” Buckles’s request. A court “entertains” a filing when it considers it on the merits rather than ignoring it. *Jenkins*, 495 U.S. at 45. Because Buckles’s motion was timely under the local rule and was expressly construed and denied by the Court, the June 12 order restarted the 90-day certiorari period under Rule 13(3). Buckles then had until September 10, 2007, to petition for certiorari. His September 5, 2007 petition was therefore timely.

Because the certiorari petition was timely, Buckles’s conviction became final only when the Supreme Court denied certiorari on October 15, 2007. *Clay*, 537 U.S. at 527. His § 2255 motion, filed within one year of that date, was timely. 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1).

## II. At minimum, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling because he diligently relied on misinformation from the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office.

Even if the Court concludes that the June 12 order did not restart the certiorari clock, dismissal as untimely was still improper unless equitable tolling was unavailable as a matter of law. The one-year limitations period in § 2255 is subject to equitable tolling. *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 645-49 (2010); *United States v. Battles*, 362 F.3d 1195, 1196-97 (9th Cir. 2004). A petitioner is entitled to tolling upon a showing that he has been pursuing his rights diligently and that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way and prevented timely filing. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 649; *Miles v. Prunty*, 187 F.3d 1104, 1107 (9th Cir. 1999).

Buckles made that showing. He diligently sought certiorari review, repeatedly contacted retained counsel, and promptly pursued appointment of counsel under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e). He also alleges that his sister called the Clerk’s office and was told that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” If true, that misinformation came from an official source charged with administering appellate filings, and Buckles reasonably relied on it in timing his petition.

Equitable tolling is particularly appropriate where a litigant is affirmatively misled by an officer of the court. *Spitsyn v. Moore*, 345 F.3d 796, 800-01 (9th Cir. 2003); *Sossa v. Diaz*, 729 F.3d 1225, 1230-31 (9th Cir. 2013). This Court has also held that reliance on incorrect official advice can constitute an extraordinary circumstance. *Ford v. Pliler*, 590 F.3d 782, 784-85 (9th Cir. 2009). Buckles’s declaration squarely raises that issue, and the record does not permit dismissal without addressing it.

At minimum, the district court should have permitted factual development on whether the Clerk’s office provided inaccurate advice and whether Buckles reasonably relied on it. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 653; *Doe v. Busby*, 661 F.3d 1001, 1012-13 (9th Cir. 2011). Because the petitioning deadline may have been tolled by official misinformation, the district court erred in dismissing the § 2255 motion as untimely on the existing record.

## CONCLUSION

The Court should reverse the dismissal of Buckles’s § 2255 motion and remand for further proceedings, or at minimum for an evidentiary hearing on equitable tolling.
