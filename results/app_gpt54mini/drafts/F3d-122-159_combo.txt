**Argument**

The district court erred in sustaining the Bureau of Prisons’ refusal to consider Mr. Roussos for the early-release benefit Congress authorized in 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The statute permits a reduction only for a prisoner “convicted of a nonviolent offense.” Congress did not say that every drug offender who received a sentencing enhancement for firearm possession becomes retroactively convicted of a violent crime. The Bureau’s contrary Program Statement rewrites the statute and the governing regulation, which both focus on the inmate’s “current offense,” not on an administrative characterization untethered to the offense of conviction.

I. **The Bureau may not deny a statutory benefit by redefining a nonviolent drug conviction as violent based on a sentencing enhancement.**

A criminal judgment “must stand or fall” on the offense of conviction and the lawful sentencing findings attached to it; administrative agencies may not enlarge criminal consequences beyond what Congress authorized. See *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (criminal laws “are not to be extended by implication”); *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 345–46 (1880) (governmental power affecting liberty must rest on lawful authority). Here, Mr. Roussos stands convicted of conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846, a drug offense. The firearm enhancement did not change the elements of that offense, and no firearm was charged as an element of conviction. A sentencing factor cannot be converted into the defining feature of the conviction itself. See *United States v. Hudson*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 34 (1812) (federal criminal jurisdiction depends on authority conferred by law); *In re Wong Wing*, 159 U.S. 651, 653–54 (1896) (punishment affecting liberty requires lawful process).

The Bureau’s Program Statement also conflicts with the plain structure of § 3621(e)(2)(B). Congress used the phrase “convicted of a nonviolent offense,” not “sentenced without a firearm enhancement.” That distinction matters. As the Supreme Court long has held, “[w]hen a statute limits a thing to be done in a particular mode, it includes the negative of any other mode.” *Botany Worsted Mills v. United States*, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929). The statute specifies the relevant category—conviction of a nonviolent offense—and the Bureau may not substitute a different criterion. See *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868) (statutes should not be read to produce absurd or unauthorized results); *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459, 472 (1892) (courts and agencies must avoid constructions that defeat legislative purpose).

II. **Under the Bureau’s own regulation, the “current offense” is not a crime of violence because the offense of conviction does not have violence as an element and does not, by nature, involve a substantial risk of force.**

The regulation in effect states that an inmate may be eligible “unless the inmate’s current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. The incorporated statutory definition turns on the offense itself: whether it “has as an element” the use or threatened use of force, or whether it “by its nature” involves a substantial risk that force “may be used in the course of committing the offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The focus is categorical. See *Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 383–84 (1937) (words of a statute are read in context and in relation to the whole enactment); *United States v. Hess*, 124 U.S. 483, 486–87 (1888) (statutory language must be given its ordinary and contextual meaning).

A narcotics conspiracy under § 846 does not, by its elements, require force or threatened force. The Bureau’s decision therefore must rest on subsection (B)—that the offense “by its nature” involves substantial risk of force. But that language cannot be stretched to cover every drug conspiracy enhanced for a firearm. The relevant inquiry is not whether the defendant possessed a gun, but whether the offense of conviction itself is violent in nature. See *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) at 95; *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221–22 (1876) (courts may not enlarge statutory language to reach conduct not fairly within it). Congress knew how to exclude offenders whose crimes involved violence; it did not authorize the Bureau to exclude those whose convictions remain nonviolent but who received sentencing enhancements. See *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (criminal statutes require fair notice and ordinary meaning).

III. **The Program Statement cannot override the statute and regulation.**

An agency interpretation that conflicts with the governing enactment has no force. See *Bates & Guild Co. v. Payne*, 194 U.S. 106, 109–10 (1904) (administrative practice cannot control where contrary to statute); *United States v. Eaton*, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892) (subordinate regulations must be within delegated authority). Program Statement 5162.02 effectively creates a new disqualification: any § 841 or § 846 conviction with a firearm enhancement is deemed violent. That is not what Congress enacted, and it is not what the regulation says. The Bureau may administer the statute, not amend it. See *The Aurora*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813) (executive action must conform to statutory limits); *Goldsmith v. United States Board of Tax Appeals*, 270 U.S. 117, 122 (1926) (administrative power must be exercised within lawful bounds).

Because Mr. Roussos was convicted of a nonviolent drug offense, and because the Bureau’s contrary classification rests on an unlawful program statement rather than the statute or regulation, the denial of early-release eligibility should be reversed and the petition granted.
