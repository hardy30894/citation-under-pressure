ARGUMENT

The sole question presented is whether judicial review of a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds lies in federal district court or in the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The answer is district court. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), compels that result and effectively overrules the D.C. Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

**I. The Statutory Framework Resolves the Question**

The Civil Service Reform Act defines a “mixed case” as one in which an employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Judicial review of a mixed case “shall be filed in district court.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 44. The statute focuses on the employee’s *allegations* at the time of filing, not the Board’s ultimate jurisdictional ruling. *Id.* at 48-49. When an employee alleges discrimination and an action that is *appealable* to the Board—such as a removal or suspension for more than 14 days, 5 U.S.C. § 7512—the case is a mixed case regardless of how the Board later disposes of it.

Perry’s allegations satisfy both elements. He alleged that his suspension and retirement resulted from discrimination based on race, age, and disability. He also alleged that those actions were involuntary, rendering them appealable to the Board. *See Garcia v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (voluntariness is a jurisdictional prerequisite). The Board’s subsequent finding that the settlement was voluntary did not retroactively erase Perry’s allegations. Congress did not condition district court review on the Board’s agreement with the employee’s jurisdictional allegations.

**II. *Kloeckner* Controls and Abrogates *Powell***

In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that “when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, the employee may seek judicial review in district court.” 568 U.S. at 44. The Court emphasized that the “action” referenced in § 7702(a)(1)(A) is the employer’s adverse *action*, not the Board’s ruling. *Id.* at 48-49. The Board’s dismissal on a pre-merits ground—whether procedural or jurisdictional—does not change the fact that the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Board.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that jurisdictional dismissals present a different case. As the Court noted, the statutory language “does not require the employee to file a *successful* appeal to the Board.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 48 (emphasis in original). A jurisdictional dismissal is simply a failed appeal. The employee’s allegations remain the same.

The D.C. Circuit’s distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals finds no support in *Kloeckner*. The Court there recognized that the “critical point” is that the employee’s case “was a mixed case under § 7702(a)(1)” because the employee alleged discrimination and an appealable action. *Id.* at 50. The Board’s dismissal on untimeliness did not alter that status. *Id.* The same reasoning applies to jurisdictional dismissals. The Board’s conclusion that it lacked jurisdiction does not erase the employee’s allegations; it merely means the Board chose not to adjudicate them. The case remains a mixed case, and review lies in district court.

**III. The *Powell* Rationale Is Incompatible with *Kloeckner***

*Powell* held that jurisdictional dismissals must go to the Federal Circuit because “the boundaries of the Board’s [own] jurisdiction should be subject to uniform interpretation in a single forum.” 158 F.3d at 600. But *Kloeckner* rejected that rationale. The Court held that the statutory scheme deliberately routes mixed cases to district court, even when the Board’s ruling involves legal questions about its own authority. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50-52. The uniform-interpretation rationale cannot override the plain text of § 7702(b)(2). Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s monopoly on jurisdictional questions is not absolute; district courts routinely review Board jurisdiction in mixed cases. *See, e.g., Harms v. IRS*, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007-08 (10th Cir. 2003) (distinguishing jurisdictional from procedural dismissals but acknowledging that district courts review jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases). *Kloeckner* implicitly overruled any precedent that hinged the forum on the ground of dismissal.

**IV. The Overlap Between Jurisdictional and Merit Questions Confirms the Result**

Jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases often turn on the same facts as the discrimination claim. Here, the Board’s determination that Perry’s retirement was voluntary required evaluating whether the agency coerced him—a question that overlaps with his discrimination and retaliation claims. The D.C. Circuit itself acknowledged that “jurisdictional dismissals grounded in the voluntariness of the challenged employment action . . . can overlap with the merits of a discrimination claim.” *Perry v. MSPB*, slip op. at 18. Routing such cases to district court, which specializes in discrimination law, ensures coherent resolution of the entire case. *Kloeckner* recognized that the statutory scheme prioritizes that coherence over the Federal Circuit’s exclusive jurisdiction. 568 U.S. at 50-52.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that the MSPB’s dismissal of a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in federal district court, not the Federal Circuit. The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed, and the case transferred to the appropriate district court.
