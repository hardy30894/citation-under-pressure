**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). The Supreme Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 690-91. The Court held that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." *Id.* at 701.

The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that § 1231(a)(6) permits detention so long as the Government continues "good faith efforts to effectuate deportation." *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999), *rev’d*, 533 U.S. 678 (2001). This standard improperly shifts the burden to the alien to prove that deportation is impossible, rather than requiring the Government to justify continued detention. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 705. The Court explicitly rejected this approach, noting that it "would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable." *Id.*

The Court established a six-month presumptively reasonable period for post-removal detention, after which the Government must rebut a showing that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 705. The Court further explained that "as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink." *Id.* Here, Zadvydas has been detained for years beyond the removal period with no realistic prospect of removal. The Government has failed to justify his continued detention under the statute.

II. INDEFINITE DETENTION VIOLATES SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS

The Government’s indefinite detention of Zadvydas violates substantive due process. The Supreme Court has long recognized that "[f]reedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that the Due Process Clause protects." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987). The Court has repeatedly invalidated statutes authorizing prolonged detention without individualized justification. *See Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 77 (1992) (holding that continued detention must be justified by a legitimate purpose); *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972) (limiting detention to the purpose of effectuating a valid state goal).

The Court in *Zadvydas* expressly recognized that indefinite detention of removable aliens raises serious constitutional concerns. 533 U.S. at 690. The statute’s breadth—applying to aliens ordered removed for "many and various reasons," including minor visa violations—underscores the need for a temporal limit. *Id.* at 699. The Court declined to defer to the political branches on this issue, noting that the statute "does not apply narrowly to ‘a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,’ say suspected terrorists." *Id.* at 699 (citation omitted).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien held at the border, not a resident alien with a final removal order. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700. The Court distinguished *Mezei*, emphasizing that the alien in that case "had not effected entry into the United States." *Id.* at 700-01. Unlike *Mezei*, Zadvydas has lived in this country for decades and has strong ties to the community. His indefinite detention is constitutionally infirm.

III. THE HABEAS COURT PROPERLY ORDERED ZADVYDAS’ RELEASE

The district court correctly determined that Zadvydas’ continued detention violated the Constitution. The court found that removal was not reasonably foreseeable and that indefinite detention would violate substantive due process. *Zadvydas v. Caplinger*, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion was based on an erroneous interpretation of the statute and an unduly deferential standard. *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d at 297.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Zadvydas* confirms that the habeas court must assess whether detention exceeds a reasonable time to secure removal. 533 U.S. at 704. Here, removal is not reasonably foreseeable, and the Government has not rebutted that showing. The district court’s order of supervised release, conditioned on compliance with reporting and other requirements, is a permissible alternative to detention. *Id.* at 701-02. The Government has not challenged the conditions of release, and Zadvydas has complied with them. His continued detention is unlawful.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court below should be affirmed. The statute does not authorize indefinite detention, and Zadvydas’ continued custody violates substantive due process. The district court properly ordered his release under conditions designed to ensure his presence for removal if circumstances change.
