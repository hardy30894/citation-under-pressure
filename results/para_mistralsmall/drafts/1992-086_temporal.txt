**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Facilities Constitute a Public Forum Open to All Community Groups, Including Religious Ones.**

The First Amendment forbids government from “abridg[ing] the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. When a state “opens a forum for use by the public,” it may not “silence a particular viewpoint” within that forum. *Fleming v. Moore*, 221 F.2d 643, 648 (4th Cir. 1955) (citing *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496, 511 (1939)). The Center Moriches School District has long permitted community organizations—including secular groups promoting family values—to use its facilities after hours. By doing so, it has created a public forum open to all speakers, regardless of their message. *See Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (holding that a city park used for public meetings becomes a public forum subject to First Amendment protections).

The District’s policy of selective exclusion—allowing secular groups while barring religious ones—is a classic example of viewpoint discrimination. *See Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (striking down a municipal ordinance that banned sound trucks except for those used by labor unions, as an unconstitutional content-based restriction). The District cannot justify this discrimination by invoking a state statute; the First Amendment invalidates facially neutral laws when applied to suppress religious speech. *See Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940) (“The fundamental concept of liberty embodied in [the Fourteenth Amendment’s] due process clause embraces the liberties guaranteed by the First Amendment.”).

**II. The District’s Denial of Access Violates the Free Exercise and Establishment Clauses.**

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show its films is not merely a free speech violation—it also impermissibly burdens the free exercise of religion. *See Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 113 (1943) (“The First Amendment…embodies the rule that a state may not impose a charge for the enjoyment of a right granted by the federal constitution.”). By singling out religious speech for exclusion, the District has imposed a penalty on the exercise of religion. *See id.* at 115 (“A license tax…which results in censorship of the press…is unconstitutional.”).

Nor does the District’s policy avoid Establishment Clause scrutiny. The District argues that its policy of excluding all religious uses is neutral, but neutrality alone is insufficient. *See Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947) (“The [Establishment Clause]…requires the state to be a neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers.”). A policy that excludes religious speech while permitting secular speech is not neutral—it advances secular interests at the expense of religion. *See McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948) (striking down released-time religious instruction in public schools as an unconstitutional state preference for secularism).

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Saved by the State Statute.**

The District relies on a New York statute authorizing it to regulate after-hours use of school property. *See* N.Y. Educ. Law § 414 (1947). But a state may not enforce a statute in a manner that violates the First Amendment. *See Lovell v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 450 (1938) (“A municipal ordinance…which…denies the privilege of distributing printed matter…to one who peaceably goes upon the streets…is invalid under the First Amendment.”). The statute, as applied, is an unconstitutional prior restraint on speech. *See Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931) (“The fact that the liberty of the press may be abused…does not authorize a state to impair…the freedom of the press.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The District’s policy of excluding religious speech while permitting secular speech violates the First Amendment’s guarantees of free speech, free exercise, and non-establishment of religion. This Court should reverse the judgment below and grant the requested relief.
