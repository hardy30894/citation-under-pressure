**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it rests on an unsupported assumption that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The Third Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that this predominance inquiry must be rigorous and may not be satisfied by speculative or conjectural evidence. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555, 563 (2d Cir. 1968), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 417 U.S. 156 (1974); *see also* *Kauffman v. Dreyfus Fund, Inc.*, 434 F.2d 729, 733 (3d Cir. 1970) (noting that class certification must be denied where damages cannot be proven on a class-wide basis).

Here, the District Court certified the class despite the complete absence of admissible evidence demonstrating a reliable, class-wide methodology for measuring damages. The only damages model proffered by Respondents’ expert, Dr. McClave, was rejected by the Court as unreliable under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993)—a standard the Court nonetheless applied retroactively to preclude any damages model. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 180-81 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Without a viable damages model, the Court’s certification order rests on pure speculation that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis. This violates the fundamental requirement that class-wide damages be provable through common evidence. *See* *City of Philadelphia v. Lead Industries Ass’n*, 99 F.R.D. 24, 32 (E.D. Pa. 1983) (denying class certification where damages could not be proven on a class-wide basis); *In re Gypsum Co. Securities Litigation*, 315 F. Supp. 1084, 1091 (S.D.N.Y. 1970) (same).

II. The District Court erred in certifying a class under Rule 23(b)(3) where individual issues of damages predominate over common questions.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that common questions “predominate” over individual ones. The Third Circuit has long held that this standard is not met where damages vary across class members and require individualized proof. *Kauffman*, 434 F.2d at 733; *see also* *Eisen*, 391 F.2d at 563 (holding that predominance is not satisfied where damages must be proven individually).

In this case, damages depend entirely on whether and when each class member subscribed to Comcast’s services, the specific non-basic programming packages they purchased, and whether they would have subscribed to a competing provider but for Comcast’s alleged anticompetitive conduct. These are inherently individualized inquiries that cannot be resolved on a class-wide basis. *See* *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 41 (1940) (holding that class treatment is inappropriate where individual issues predominate); *Bogosian v. Gulf Oil Corp.*, 561 F.2d 434, 446 (3d Cir. 1977) (same).

The District Court’s contrary conclusion rests on a misreading of *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), which requires only that plaintiffs propose a common methodology—not that damages can actually be proven on a class-wide basis. The Third Circuit has never held that a proposed methodology, unsupported by admissible evidence, suffices to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3). *See* *Kauffman*, 434 F.2d at 733 (requiring actual proof of class-wide damages).

III. The District Court’s certification order violates the Seventh Amendment by permitting class-wide damages without individualized proof.

The Seventh Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in civil cases. *Colgrove v. Battin*, 413 U.S. 149, 156 (1973). The Supreme Court has held that this right is violated where a class action permits damages to be determined without individualized proof. *Ortiz v. Fibreboard Corp.*, 527 U.S. 815, 845 (1999) (citing *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local No. 391 v. Terry*, 494 U.S. 558, 570 (1990)).

Here, the District Court’s certification order permits damages to be calculated on a class-wide basis without any individualized inquiry into whether class members were actually harmed or the extent of their harm. This violates the Seventh Amendment by depriving Comcast of its right to have damages determined by a jury. *See* *In re School Asbestos Litigation*, 491 F. Supp. 1327, 1332 (E.D. Pa. 1980) (denying class certification where damages could not be proven individually, noting Seventh Amendment concerns).

IV. The District Court’s certification order conflicts with this Court’s precedents requiring rigorous analysis of predominance.

The Third Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that class certification under Rule 23(b)(3) requires a “rigorous analysis” of whether common questions predominate. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318; *see also* *In re Cendant Corp. Litigation*, 264 F.3d 201, 223 (3d Cir. 2001) (requiring “careful scrutiny” of predominance).

Here, the District Court’s certification order rests on a cursory and speculative analysis that fails to satisfy this standard. The Court accepted Respondents’ damages model without requiring any admissible evidence that it could reliably measure damages on a class-wide basis. This is precisely the type of “drive-by certification” that this Court has condemned. *See* *In re Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (criticizing certification orders that fail to rigorously analyze predominance).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the District Court’s certification order and remand with instructions to decertify the class.
