**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court erred in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3) because Plaintiffs failed to present admissible evidence demonstrating that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis.

A. Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this standard, a plaintiff must show that common evidence can be used to prove both liability and damages. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011) (“What matters to class certification . . . is not the raising of common ‘questions,’ but the capacity of a class-wide proceeding to generate common answers apt to drive the resolution of the litigation.”).

B. The Supreme Court has made clear that individualized damages inquiries are incompatible with class certification. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013) (“The Court of Appeals concluded that respondents’ damages model could be used to show that damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis. But the model was deficient in that respect.”). Here, the District Court improperly relied on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which the Third Circuit later found to be “deficient” and “flawed.” *Id.* at 35. The model failed to isolate damages attributable to the alleged anticompetitive conduct from other factors, rendering it incapable of proving class-wide damages. *Id.* at 34-35.

C. The Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), requires that a plaintiff demonstrate a “methodology” for proving damages on a class-wide basis. *Id.* at 318. The methodology must be “consistent with [the] liability theory” and capable of “demonstrat[ing] that damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend*, 569 U.S. at 34. Dr. McClave’s model did neither. It assumed that all price increases were caused by Comcast’s conduct, without accounting for lawful price increases due to inflation, improved service quality, or other market factors. *Id.* at 35. This failure to tie damages to the alleged violation violates the predominance requirement. *Id.*

D. The District Court’s certification order rested on a clearly erroneous factual finding. The court credited Dr. McClave’s model despite its fatal flaws, including its failure to exclude supracompetitive prices caused by factors other than Comcast’s alleged anticompetitive conduct. *Behrend*, 569 U.S. at 35. Under Rule 52(a), findings of fact must not be set aside unless clearly erroneous. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). Here, the District Court’s reliance on an unproven damages model was an abuse of discretion. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312.

II. The District Court improperly resolved merits issues at the class certification stage, conflating the predominance inquiry with the merits of Plaintiffs’ antitrust claim.

A. Rule 23 does not permit a merits inquiry disguised as a class certification analysis. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) (“We find nothing in either the language or history of Rule 23 that gives a court any authority to conduct a preliminary inquiry into the merits of a suit in order to determine whether it may be maintained as a class action.”). The Third Circuit has recognized this principle, cautioning that courts must not “delve into the merits” when assessing class certification. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317.

B. Here, the District Court improperly evaluated the merits of Plaintiffs’ antitrust claim by accepting their theory of liability and damages methodology without rigorous scrutiny. The court’s “rigorous analysis” amounted to a merits determination, in violation of Rule 23 and *Eisen*. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350-51 (warning against certifying classes based on “common” questions that are not genuinely common).

C. The Supreme Court has emphasized that class certification must not become a “backdoor merits trial.” *Id.* at 351. The District Court’s certification order did precisely that by resolving contested issues of antitrust impact and damages methodology. This was reversible error.

**CONCLUSION**
The District Court abused its discretion in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3) because Plaintiffs failed to present admissible evidence demonstrating that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. The Court also improperly resolved merits issues at the certification stage. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to decertify the class.
