ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Burnett’s FMLA Claim Because a Genuine Dispute Exists as to Whether He Provided Sufficient Notice.

The FMLA entitles an employee to leave for a “serious health condition.” 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D). An employee need not expressly invoke the FMLA; he must merely provide “enough information to put the employer on notice that the absence may be FMLA-qualifying.” *Byrne v. Avon Products, Inc.*, 328 F.3d 379, 381 (7th Cir. 2003). The employer then bears a duty to inquire further if the notice is ambiguous. *Aubuchon v. Knauf Fiberglass GmbH*, 359 F.3d 950, 954 (7th Cir. 2004). The employer’s knowledge is assessed from the employee’s perspective; the employer cannot shut its eyes to evident facts. *Ragsdale v. Wolverine World Wide, Inc.*, 535 U.S. 81, 91 (2002).

Burnett gave Habitat multiple specific warnings. He told his supervisor, Polo, that he had a “weak bladder,” was seeing a doctor, and had high PSA levels—a known marker for prostate cancer. He presented a doctor’s order for blood tests. *Kauffman v. Federal Express Corp.*, 426 F.3d 880, 886 (7th Cir. 2005) (holding that a doctor’s note can constitute sufficient notice). He explained his condition was “serious,” comparing it to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. He provided a detailed document describing the prostate biopsy procedure, which stated that an elevated PSA is an “indication” for the examination. Yet Habitat never asked for clarification. Under *Aubuchon*, 359 F.3d at 954, an employer who receives ambiguous information must seek more details. Habitat’s failure to do so cannot defeat Burnett’s claim. The district court erred by requiring Burnett to use the precise words “prostate cancer” or “FMLA.” The evidence, viewed in Burnett’s favor, shows he gave sufficient notice.

II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Burnett’s ADA Claim Because He Adequately Requested a Reasonable Accommodation.

The ADA prohibits discrimination against a qualified individual with a disability. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). The employee must initiate a request for accommodation, but no “magic words” are required. *Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Community Schools*, 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996). The request must only make the employer “aware of the need” for an accommodation. *Conley v. Village of Bedford Park*, 215 F.3d 703, 706 (7th Cir. 2000). The employer must then engage in an interactive process. *Jovanovic v. In-Sink-Erator*, 201 F.3d 894, 899 (7th Cir. 2000).

Burnett repeatedly informed Habitat of his medical limitations. He told Polo that his “weak bladder” necessitated easy restroom access, and he declined a transfer to a location with reduced access. He requested a transfer to a midnight shift, which would have accommodated his medical needs. *Hunt v. City of Markham*, 219 F.3d 649, 653 (7th Cir. 2000) (stating that a request for a transfer can be a request for accommodation). He also asked for sick leave to attend doctor’s appointments. Habitat’s own email approved sick leave for those appointments. Yet Polo later issued a reprimand and effectively terminated Burnett. The district court erroneously concluded that Burnett never requested an accommodation. The record shows he did, and the employer’s response—escalating discipline—raises a genuine issue of retaliation. *Conley*, 215 F.3d at 710.

Because material facts remain in dispute, the judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
