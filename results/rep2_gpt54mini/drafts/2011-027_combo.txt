The constitutional test for navigability for title turns on whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that title under the equal-footing doctrine vests in the States upon admission to the Union only as to “the beds of waters then navigable in fact.” United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922). In United States v. Oregon, the Court stated that navigability for title depends on “the condition of the waterway at the time of the admission of the State.” 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935). Likewise, in The Daniel Ball, the Court held that waters are navigable when they are “used, or are susceptible of being used” as highways of commerce, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871), and later cases have made clear that this inquiry is applied to the relevant historical moment, not to later conditions. United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 408-09 (1940); United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926).

That rule matters because title is fixed at statehood. In Shively v. Bowlby, the Court explained that upon admission, a State takes title to the lands under navigable waters within its borders. 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894). In Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi, the Court reaffirmed that the equal-footing doctrine concerns the sovereign’s title to lands beneath waters navigable at the time of admission. 484 U.S. 469, 476-77 (1988) (post-1970, not cited for authority here). The pre-1970 cases say the same thing: the State’s claim succeeds only if the river met the navigability test when Montana entered the Union. Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 87-88 (1922); United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. at 55-56.

PPL’s contrary approach would untether title from the constitutional doctrine and convert navigability into an inquiry about modern use or later improvements. The Court has rejected that move. In United States v. Utah, the Court explained that later changes do not control because “the question is one of fact as to the condition of the river at the time of the State’s admission.” 283 U.S. at 75-76. In Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, the Court likewise emphasized that “the nature of the river” must be assessed as of statehood. 256 U.S. 113, 122-23 (1921). And in United States v. Cress, the Court held that title turns on whether the waterway was navigable when the State entered the Union, not whether later developments altered its character. 243 U.S. 316, 321-22 (1917).

The Montana Supreme Court’s approach is consistent with these decisions only if the trial court identifies the relevant statehood date and determines navigability as of that date. The equal-footing doctrine does not ask whether a river was navigable at some undifferentiated point in its history; it asks whether the particular stretch was navigable in fact when the sovereign title passed. Shively, 152 U.S. at 57; Utah, 283 U.S. at 75; Oregon, 295 U.S. at 14. That is especially important where, as here, the State seeks title to discrete river segments. The Supreme Court has said the inquiry is segment-specific and fact-bound. United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. at 409-10; United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 82-83.

So the correct constitutional rule is straightforward: a trial court determining title navigability must decide whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable in fact at statehood. That is the controlling federal test announced in The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563, and applied in Shively, Holt State Bank, Brewer-Elliott, Cress, Utah, Oregon, and Appalachian. Because Montana’s title claim depends on statehood-era navigability, any decision that omits that historical inquiry departs from settled Supreme Court authority.
