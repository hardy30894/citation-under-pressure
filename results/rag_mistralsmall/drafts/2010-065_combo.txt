**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE FEDERAL STATUTE**

A. The Tenth Amendment “is but a truism” that does not confer any substantive rights on individuals.

The Tenth Amendment provides: “The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const. amend. X. This Court has long held that the Amendment “is but a truism,” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941), and does not “add[] anything to the [Constitution] as originally ratified,” *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 733 (1931). The Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered,” *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Auth.*, 297 U.S. 288, 341 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring), and thus “does not purport to limit the powers of the national government.” *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis*, 301 U.S. 548, 589 (1937). Because the Tenth Amendment is not a source of rights, individuals may not invoke it to challenge the validity of federal legislation. *See Tennessee Elec. Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Auth.*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939) (holding that private parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to federal legislation).

B. Private parties may not assert a Tenth Amendment claim in federal court.

This Court has repeatedly held that private litigants may not challenge federal legislation on Tenth Amendment grounds. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, the Court rejected a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private utility companies against the Tennessee Valley Authority, explaining that the companies “have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment].” 306 U.S. at 144. Similarly, in *Ashwander*, the Court dismissed a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private parties, noting that the Amendment “is but a truism” and does not confer standing. 297 U.S. at 341 (Brandeis, J., concurring). The Court has also held that private parties may not challenge the federal government’s exercise of its enumerated powers, as such challenges are properly brought by the States. *See Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 482 (1923) (holding that private parties may not challenge the constitutionality of federal legislation on Tenth Amendment grounds, as such challenges are “reserved for the States”).

C. Bond’s reliance on *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), is misplaced.

Bond argues that *Missouri v. Holland* permits private parties to challenge federal legislation implementing a treaty. However, *Holland* does not support her position. In *Holland*, the Court held that the federal government’s treaty power, combined with the Necessary and Proper Clause, permits Congress to enact legislation to enforce a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed Congress’s enumerated powers. 252 U.S. at 432-33. The Court did not address whether private parties have standing to challenge such legislation. Moreover, *Holland* does not alter the rule that private parties may not assert Tenth Amendment claims. *See Tennessee Elec. Power Co.*, 306 U.S. at 144 (rejecting a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private parties, even in the context of federal legislation implementing a treaty).

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER**

A. Congress’s power to implement treaties is plenary and not subject to Tenth Amendment limitations.

This Court has long recognized that Congress’s power to implement treaties is broad and not constrained by the Tenth Amendment. In *Missouri v. Holland*, the Court held that Congress may enact legislation to enforce a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed Congress’s enumerated powers. 252 U.S. at 432-33. The Court explained that the treaty power, combined with the Necessary and Proper Clause, permits Congress to enact laws necessary to effectuate the treaty, regardless of whether those laws would otherwise fall within Congress’s enumerated powers. *Id.* at 432-33. This principle has been repeatedly reaffirmed by the Court. *See United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 318 (1936) (holding that the federal government’s treaty power is “exclusive and plenary” and not subject to Tenth Amendment limitations).

B. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power.

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power. The Convention was ratified by the United States pursuant to the Treaty Clause, U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2, and Congress enacted § 229 to implement its obligations under the Convention. *See* 22 U.S.C. § 6701; *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432-33. As this Court has held, Congress may enact legislation to enforce a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed Congress’s enumerated powers. *Id.* Thus, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power and does not violate the Tenth Amendment.

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

A. A statute is unconstitutionally vague only if it fails to provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits.

This Court has held that a statute is unconstitutionally vague only if it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden.” *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399, 402 (1965). A statute is not unconstitutionally vague merely because it is broad or because it may be difficult to apply in particular cases. *Id.* at 403. Instead, the statute must be so vague that “men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.” *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 387, 391 (1926).

B. § 229 provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits.

Section 229 prohibits the knowing use or possession of a “chemical weapon,” defined as “any toxic chemical and its precursors” used for non-peaceful purposes. 18 U.S.C. § 229F(1)(A). The statute explicitly excludes from its coverage chemicals used for peaceful purposes, such as “individual self-defense devices,” industrial activities, agricultural activities, research, medical activities, and pharmaceutical activities. *Id.* § 229C, 229F(1)(B). Thus, the statute provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits and is not unconstitutionally vague. *See Giaccio*, 382 U.S. at 402 (holding that a statute is not unconstitutionally vague if it provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
