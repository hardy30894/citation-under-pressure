**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED “REASONED CHOICE” STANDARD FOR COMPETENCY TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a novel, heightened competency standard that finds no support in this Court’s precedents. The proper standard for determining competency to plead guilty or waive counsel is the same standard applied to competency to stand trial: whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This Court has never required a separate, more demanding “reasoned choice” inquiry.

**A. This Court’s Competency Jurisprudence Establishes a Unitary Standard.**

In *Dusky*, this Court held that the test for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant has “rational understanding” of the proceedings. 362 U.S. at 402. That standard was reaffirmed in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), which held that a defendant is competent if he has “the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” Nothing in *Dusky* or *Drope* suggests that a different, higher standard applies when a defendant pleads guilty or waives counsel.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard—requiring that a defendant be capable of making a “reasoned choice” among alternatives—is a judicial invention. This Court has never held that the Due Process Clause demands such a heightened inquiry. To the contrary, in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966), this Court held that a competency hearing is required only when there is a “bona fide doubt” as to the defendant’s competence under the *Dusky* standard. The Court did not create a sliding scale of competency depending on the nature of the defendant’s decision.

**B. The Waiver of Constitutional Rights Does Not Alter the Competency Inquiry.**

The Ninth Circuit reasoned that because pleading guilty and waiving counsel involve the waiver of fundamental rights, a higher competency standard is required. But this Court has consistently held that the validity of a waiver depends on whether it is “voluntary, knowing, and intelligent,” *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969), not on whether the defendant possesses a heightened level of mental functioning. The competency inquiry is distinct from the voluntariness inquiry. A defendant may be competent under *Dusky* yet still make an involuntary or unknowing waiver. Conversely, a defendant who is incompetent cannot make a valid waiver at all. But the competency threshold remains the same.

In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), this Court held that waiver of counsel must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” That standard focuses on the defendant’s knowledge and voluntariness, not on a heightened capacity for reasoned choice. Similarly, in *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), this Court held that a defendant need only be “competent to stand trial” to waive counsel. *Faretta* did not impose a higher standard.

**C. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents.**

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard effectively requires trial courts to conduct a second, more searching competency hearing whenever a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has never imposed such a requirement. In *Drope*, the Court emphasized that the decision to hold a competency hearing is committed to the trial court’s discretion, guided by whether there is a “bona fide doubt” as to competence. 420 U.S. at 180. The Ninth Circuit’s rule would strip trial courts of that discretion and mandate hearings in cases where no bona fide doubt exists.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s standard conflates competency with the quality of the defendant’s decision. A defendant may make a poor or even irrational choice—such as refusing to present mitigating evidence—yet still be competent. This Court has never held that the Due Process Clause requires a defendant to make a “reasoned” choice; it requires only that the defendant be competent to make a choice at all.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
