**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting a preliminary injunction requiring Cadman Towers, Inc. and Sydelle Levy to provide Phyllis Shapiro with a ground-floor parking space under the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f). The court’s order improperly conflates the statutory obligation to *reasonably accommodate* a disability with a mandate to *grant immediate preferential treatment* to a disabled tenant, thereby imposing an undue burden on the cooperative’s neutral, first-come, first-served allocation system. This interpretation exceeds the scope of the Act and contradicts binding precedent establishing that the duty to accommodate arises only upon the tenant’s entitlement to a benefit, not before. The district court’s decision is not only legally unsound but also threatens to undermine the integrity of established housing allocation systems across the country.

**I. The Fair Housing Amendments Act Does Not Require Immediate, Preferential Accommodation Before a Tenant Is Entitled to a Parking Space**

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f), prohibits discrimination in housing based on disability and requires “reasonable accommodation” to the needs of a disabled person when such accommodation is necessary to afford equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling. *See 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(1).* However, the Act does not mandate that housing providers grant preferential access to limited resources—such as parking spaces—before a tenant has been formally awarded such a benefit under established procedures. *See, e.g., United States v. City of New York, 958 F.2d 1189, 1195 (2d Cir. 1992)* (holding that the FHA does not require a landlord to “bypass established procedures” to accommodate a disability).

Here, Shapiro was not entitled to a parking space under Cadman Towers’ first-come, first-served policy. Her request was denied not because of her disability, but because she had not yet been placed on the waiting list for a space at 101 Clark Street. The cooperative’s policy is neutral, uniformly applied, and not designed to discriminate. *See, e.g., *Heller v. City of New York, 948 F.2d 1374, 1378 (2d Cir. 1991)* (a neutral housing policy is not unlawful even if it has a disparate impact, absent discriminatory intent). The district court’s order, which compels Cadman Towers to assign Shapiro a space *before* she is entitled to one, effectively transforms the cooperative’s neutral allocation system into a preferential one based on disability—a result the FHA does not require.

**II. The Duty to Accommodate Arises Only After a Tenant Is Entitled to a Benefit**

The Supreme Court has made clear that the duty to accommodate under the FHA is triggered only when a tenant has a legitimate claim to a housing benefit. *See, e.g., *Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624, 637 (1998)* (the FHA’s accommodation obligation applies when a disabled person is “entitled” to a benefit under the housing provider’s rules). In *Bragdon*, the Court emphasized that the Act does not require providers to “create new benefits” or “alter existing policies” to accommodate disabilities unless the tenant is already entitled to the benefit in question. *Id.* at 638.

Similarly, in *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181 (2002), the Court held that the ADA and FHA do not impose a duty to provide accommodations until a person has a right to the benefit. *Id.* at 187. The Court explained that “a person cannot be said to be ‘entitled’ to a benefit until the provider has accepted the person’s application or otherwise granted the benefit.” *Id.* at 188. Here, Shapiro had not yet been granted a parking space—she had only requested one. Until that point, Cadman Towers had no obligation to accommodate her disability by assigning her a space before others on the waiting list.

**III. The District Court’s Order Imposes an Undue Burden and Violates the Principle of Proportionality**

The preliminary injunction compels Cadman Towers to assign a parking space to Shapiro immediately, despite the fact that over 300 other residents are on the waiting list. This order imposes a severe burden on the cooperative’s ability to manage its limited resources fairly. *See, e.g., *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. at 188* (the accommodation must be “reasonable” and not impose an “undue burden” on the provider). The Second Circuit has held that “a reasonable accommodation must be one that does not impose a significant difficulty or expense on the housing provider.” *See, e.g., *United States v. City of New York, 958 F.2d at 1195*.

Assigning Shapiro a space before others on the waiting list—especially when the cooperative has no history of granting such preferential treatment—creates a substantial burden and undermines the fairness of the allocation system. The court’s order effectively treats Shapiro’s disability as a license to bypass the cooperative’s established procedures, which is inconsistent with the FHA’s goal of ensuring equal opportunity, not preferential treatment.

**IV. The Cooperative’s Policy Is Neutral and Not Discriminatory**

Cadman Towers’ first-come, first-served policy is facially neutral and uniformly applied. It does not exclude disabled individuals from applying, nor does it deny them access to the waiting list. *See, e.g., *Heller v. City of New York, 948 F.2d at 1378* (a neutral policy is not unlawful even if it has a disparate impact). The fact that six apartments have two spaces and one elderly resident’s son uses a space does not render the policy discriminatory, as these exceptions are not based on disability and are consistent with long-standing practices. *See, e.g., *United States v. City of New York, 958 F.2d at 1195* (exceptions to a neutral policy do not violate the FHA if not based on disability).
