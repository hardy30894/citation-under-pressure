**ARGUMENT**

**I. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist as to Whether BNSF Retaliated Against Plaintiffs for Engaging in Protected Activity.**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment *de novo*. *Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014). The Federal Railroad Safety Act prohibits a railroad from disciplining an employee for reporting a hazardous safety condition. 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show (1) protected activity, (2) a materially adverse action, and (3) a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789. The burden then shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason, and the plaintiff must show that reason is pretextual. *Id.* at 790.

**A. Plaintiffs Engaged in Protected Activity.**

On April 2, 2012, Foster, Kline, and Snyder reported John Moore’s fall and the unsafe bridge conditions—including the lack of handrails and lighting—to Trainmaster Dixon. App. at 45-47. Kline and Snyder’s written statements expressly noted these hazards. *Id.* at 46-47. Foster provided a recorded statement to BNSF’s claims representative on April 5, 2012, and again noted the dangerous location. *Id.* at 49. Reporting a hazardous safety condition falls squarely within the FRSA’s protected activity. *See* 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C); *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790.

**B. BNSF Subjected Plaintiffs to Materially Adverse Actions.**

Within days of these reports, BNSF initiated an investigation and began subjecting plaintiffs to “increased operations testing”—unannounced observations that created a hostile work environment. App. at 50-51. Kline and Snyder felt compelled to avoid inexperienced coworkers and signed up for less lucrative work to avoid blame. *Id.* at 51. Ultimately, BNSF disciplined all three: Snyder received a formal reprimand and a one-year probationary period; Kline received a thirty-day record suspension and probation; Foster was fired. *Id.* at 53, 56. Termination is the quintessential adverse action, and the other actions—reprimands, suspensions, and probation—are materially adverse under FRSA. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790 (holding that “any action that would dissuade a reasonable worker from engaging in protected activity” is adverse).

**C. The Temporal Proximity and Evidence of Pretext Create a Genuine Dispute of Causation.**

The protected activity occurred in early April 2012. BNSF’s investigation began the very next day. App. at 49. Although the hearing was not held until January 2013, the record shows that BNSF immediately subjected plaintiffs to increased testing and that the investigation was itself a tool of retaliation. The district court erred in requiring a tight temporal link when the investigation was ongoing and when BNSF’s stated reasons for discipline were contradicted by the record itself.

BNSF claims it disciplined plaintiffs for rule infractions—failing to blow the whistle, use flashlights, improperly turning off the headlight, and fouling the tracks. App. at 52-53. But the crew testified that they were told to stop west of the bridge, and that the bridge was dark and dangerous. *Id.* at 45-46. Trainmaster Dixon and Road Foreman Knutstrom claimed the crew change was supposed to occur east of the bridge, but no written instruction existed. *Id.* at 52. This factual dispute is material: if the crew followed oral instructions, then the alleged rule violations were not willful but were the result of BNSF’s own contradictory orders. A jury could reasonably find that BNSF manufactured the infractions to retaliate against plaintiffs for reporting unsafe conditions.

Moreover, the severity of the discipline—especially firing Foster, who had a prior review period—compared to the alleged minor infractions, raises a triable inference of pretext. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) (holding that evidence of pretext may include “the employer’s departure from normal procedures” or “the employer’s inconsistent explanations”). The Public Law Board later reinstated Foster, removed Snyder’s reprimand, and removed Kline’s suspension, further undermining BNSF’s justification. App. at 56-57. While the Board’s decision is not dispositive, it is evidence that BNSF’s discipline was unfounded.

**II. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Evidence of Increased Operations Testing as Non-Adverse.**

The district court treated the increased operations testing as unactionable. But such testing, when imposed in response to protected activity, constitutes an adverse action because it subjects employees to heightened scrutiny and fear of discipline. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790 (“[A]dverse action need not be employment-related.”). Kline and Snyder testified that they changed their work assignments to avoid blame, resulting in lost income. App. at 51. A reasonable jury could find that this testing was retaliatory.

**III. The Existence of a Legitimate Investigation Does Not Bar a Retaliation Claim.**

BNSF argued that its investigation and discipline were based on objective evidence. But the question is not whether BNSF had *some* evidence; it is whether the stated reason is pretextual. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790 (plaintiff must show “that the employer’s proffered reason is not the true reason for the adverse action”). Here, the investigation itself was tainted by the disputed facts about where the crew change was to occur. The investigating witnesses—Dixon and Knutstrom—were the same officials who gave the oral instructions. Their credibility is a question for the jury.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. This Court should reverse and remand for trial.
