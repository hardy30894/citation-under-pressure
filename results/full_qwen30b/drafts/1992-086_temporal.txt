**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from the use of its school facilities—despite the facilities being opened to non-religious community organizations—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a religiously themed film series on family values constitutes a content-based restriction on speech that is not justified by any compelling state interest. This case falls squarely within the principle established in *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), that the government may not suppress speech based on its religious content, even when the speech occurs on government property.

The District’s facilities are not nonpublic forums, as the Second Circuit concluded. Rather, they are limited public forums created by the District’s own policy of allowing non-religious community groups to use school premises after hours. The Supreme Court has long held that when a government creates a forum for public expression, it must do so on a viewpoint-neutral basis. In *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516 (1945), the Court emphasized that “the right to speak, to publish, to assemble, and to petition the Government for redress of grievances” is “a fundamental right” that cannot be abridged by the government simply because the speech is religious in nature. The District’s policy of permitting secular groups—such as the Southern Harmonizers and the Center Moriches Free Public Library’s lecture series—demonstrates that it has opened its facilities to public discourse. Once a forum is opened, the government may not exclude speech based on its content, including religious content.

The District’s argument that its policy of excluding religious use is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is foreclosed by *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947). There, the Court held that the Establishment Clause does not require the government to suppress religious speech when it is otherwise permitted in a public forum. The Court stated: “The First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state. That wall must be kept high and impregnable.” Id. at 15. But the Court also made clear that “the government may not aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.” Id. at 16. The District’s refusal to allow religious groups to use its facilities does not advance neutrality—it actively discriminates against religious expression. As the Court recognized in *Cantwell*, “the state may not interfere with the free exercise of religion, nor may it discriminate against religious beliefs.” 310 U.S. at 303. The District’s policy does precisely that by allowing secular groups to use the facilities while denying access to religious ones.

The District’s reliance on *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599 (1961), is misplaced. In that case, the Court upheld a state law requiring businesses to close on Sundays, even though it burdened religious observance. But the Court did not hold that the government may exclude religious speech from a forum it has opened to the public. In fact, *Braunfeld* involved a general law of general applicability, not a content-based exclusion of religious speakers from a limited public forum. Here, the District has chosen to allow non-religious groups to use its facilities, thereby creating a forum. It may not then exclude religious groups from that same forum simply because their speech is religious.

The District’s claim that it has not opened a forum is contradicted by the record. The Southern Harmonizers performed at the school in a non-religious context. The library’s lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics was held in the school auditorium. These uses were not incidental or isolated; they were part of a consistent practice of allowing community groups to use school facilities. The District’s own rules, which permit use by “community organizations,” do not distinguish between religious and non-religious groups. When the District allows secular groups to use its facilities, it must allow religious groups to do the same. As the Court held in *Cantwell*, “the state may not impose a burden on the free exercise of religion that is not imposed on other beliefs.” 310 U.S. at 303.

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show its film series—designed to promote family values from a Christian perspective—constitutes a denial of equal access based on the content of speech. This is precisely the kind of discrimination the First Amendment forbids. In *Thomas v. Collins*, the Court struck down a law that required labor organizers to register and disclose their affiliations, holding that “the right to organize and to speak in behalf of one’s cause is a fundamental right.” 323 U.S. at 530. The same principle applies here: the right to speak on matters of family, child rearing, and moral values is fundamental, and the government may not deny that right to religious speakers.

The District’s policy is not only unconstitutional under the Free Speech Clause, but also under the Free Exercise Clause. The Court has long recognized that “the free exercise of religion includes the right to communicate religious beliefs.” *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities to share its religious message on family values constitutes a substantial burden on the free exercise of religion. The government may not impose such burdens simply because it fears the appearance of endorsing religion. As the Court stated in *Everson*, “the First Amendment does not permit the government to discriminate against religious speech.” 330 U.S. at 15.

For these reasons, the District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities violates the First Amendment. The District has created a limited public forum by allowing non-religious groups to use its facilities. It may not then exclude religious groups from that forum based on the content of their speech. The judgment of the Second Circuit must be reversed.
