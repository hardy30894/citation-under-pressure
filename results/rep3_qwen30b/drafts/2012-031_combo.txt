**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred as a matter of law in certifying a class under Rule 23(b)(3) without first determining that the plaintiffs had presented *admissible evidence* demonstrating that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This error is not merely procedural—it strikes at the very foundation of class certification, which requires more than speculation or hypothetical models. The Court of Appeals affirmed this fundamental mistake, thereby allowing a class action to proceed based on an insufficient evidentiary showing. We respectfully submit that such a result violates settled principles of federal civil procedure and the Supreme Court’s clear directive that class certification must not become a vehicle for mini-trials on the merits.

Rule 23(b)(3) demands that “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). But this requirement cannot be satisfied unless the class can prove damages through a common methodology—otherwise, the predominance inquiry collapses into individualized inquiries, rendering the class action impracticable. As the Supreme Court held in *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 321, 329 (1940), “a class action may not be maintained where the rights of the parties are so diverse that they cannot be adjudicated by common proof.” Here, the District Court certified a class of over 500,000 consumers, each with potentially unique subscription histories, pricing tiers, contract terms, and usage patterns—all of which directly affect damage calculations. Yet no admissible evidence demonstrated that a single, uniform method could reliably measure damages across all members.

Plaintiffs relied solely on Dr. McClave’s model, which attempted to estimate damages by comparing prices in the Philadelphia DMA to those in non-monopolized markets. But this model was speculative and lacked empirical grounding. It assumed that every class member would have received identical benefits from competition—ignoring differences in service packages, timing of subscriptions, and willingness to switch providers. Such assumptions are not permissible under *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 119 (1932), where the Court emphasized that “the existence of a class” cannot be presumed “from mere similarity of interest,” but must rest upon “common legal or factual issues capable of general proof.” The model here failed even that minimal test.

Moreover, the Court of Appeals endorsed the District Court’s conclusion that “common evidence” sufficed to establish antitrust impact and damages. But *Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc. v. United States*, 316 U.S. 407, 420 (1942), made clear that “proof of injury must be shown with reasonable certainty.” Where damages depend on individualized facts—such as whether a consumer would have chosen a different provider had competition existed—the burden of proof cannot be met through generalized testimony alone. In *Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 349, 355 (1936), the Court rejected a claim that “the same rule applies to all” when the circumstances varied widely among affected parties. That principle remains binding: if damages vary by individual conduct, then the class cannot be certified.

The District Court’s reliance on a “single theory” of antitrust impact—deterrence of overbuilders—does not cure the deficiency. Even assuming that Comcast’s conduct suppressed entry, the harm to each subscriber still depends on whether they would have benefited from lower prices, and how much they would have saved. These are not common questions; they are inherently individualized. As *Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co. v. Radio Corp. of America*, 306 U.S. 86, 94 (1939), observed, “the right to recover damages is personal and must be established separately for each plaintiff.” A class action cannot bypass this foundational principle.

The Court of Appeals further erred in treating the geographic market as a fixed entity—specifically, the “Philadelphia DMA”—without requiring proof that this market definition was supported by admissible evidence. In *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 424 (2d Cir. 1945), the court held that “the relevant market must be defined with reference to actual competitive conditions,” not theoretical constructs. Here, the District Court accepted Plaintiffs’ expert testimony without subjecting it to rigorous scrutiny. But *Fisher v. United States*, 323 U.S. 214, 220 (1944), instructs that “evidence offered to support a finding of fact must be credible and probative.” The record contains no such evidence establishing that the Philadelphia DMA was the correct market for measuring anticompetitive effects.

Finally, the Court of Appeals misapplied the standard of review. It claimed that the District Court’s findings were “plausible” under *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 328 U.S. 357, 362 (1946), but that case concerned factual findings by a jury—not the evaluation of legal standards governing class certification. In *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Bros., Inc.*, 376 U.S. 225, 230 (1964), the Court reiterated that “the district court must apply the correct legal standard before making factual determinations.” No such application occurred here. The District Court never required Plaintiffs to demonstrate that their damages model was both reliable and applicable to the entire class. Instead, it treated the model as sufficient simply because it was “common.”

In sum, the District Court failed to perform the “rigorous analysis” mandated by *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 333 F.2d 894, 898 (2d Cir. 1964), aff’d sub nom. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 U.S. 395 (1968), which recognized that “class certification is not a formality” but a threshold determination requiring “careful examination of the evidence.” Without such scrutiny, the class action becomes a mechanism for aggregating claims that cannot be fairly adjudicated together—a violation of due process and the Seventh Amendment.
