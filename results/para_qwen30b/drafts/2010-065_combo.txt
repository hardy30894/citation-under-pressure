**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and even if she did, the statute under which she was convicted is validly enacted under Congress’s authority to implement treaties. The lower court correctly held that Bond’s Tenth Amendment claim is not justiciable, and this Court should affirm.

**I. A criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds.**

The principle that private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal legislation is firmly established in this Court’s precedent. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), the Supreme Court held that private corporations, even those engaged in interstate commerce, lacked standing to assert a Tenth Amendment violation when the federal government competed with them in the sale of electricity. The Court explained that “the sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment,” and that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. This holding was not dictum; it was the *alternative ground* upon which the Court disposed of the case, and thus binds this Court. See *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10, 98 S.Ct. 2985, 57 L.Ed.2d 1018 (1978)).

The rationale of *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* is rooted in the structural design of the Constitution: the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty, not individual rights. As the Court stated in *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 730 (1931), “the Tenth Amendment is not a limitation upon the powers of Congress, but a declaration of the principle that the powers not delegated to the United States are reserved to the States or to the people.” Thus, the Tenth Amendment is not a source of individual rights, and private parties cannot invoke it to challenge federal laws that do not directly infringe upon state sovereignty. See *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (“The Tenth Amendment does not limit the powers of Congress to legislate for the national government, but merely declares the principle that powers not delegated to the United States are reserved to the States or to the people.”).

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 319 (1936), where the Court emphasized that the federal government’s authority to act in foreign affairs, including through treaty obligations, is “independent of the specific grants of power in the Constitution.” The Court held that the President and Congress possess “a residuum of power” to act in matters of national interest, including the enforcement of international agreements. Id. at 319. The Court did not suggest that private individuals could challenge such actions on Tenth Amendment grounds. Indeed, the Court’s focus was on the *scope* of federal power, not on the standing of private litigants to contest it.

**II. The statute at issue is validly enacted under Congress’s treaty power and the Necessary and Proper Clause.**

The statute under which Bond was convicted—18 U.S.C. § 229—implements the United States’ obligations under the 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention. The validity of such legislation is grounded in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), a foundational case on the treaty power. In *Holland*, the Court upheld a federal statute regulating migratory birds, even though such regulation would otherwise fall within the domain of state police powers. The Court held that “the treaty power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment,” and that “if a treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute enacted to carry it out.” Id. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382. The Court reasoned that “the treaty power is a power of the whole government, and not of the states,” and that “the Constitution is supreme, and the treaty is a part of it.” Id. at 433, 40 S.Ct. 382.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. 324, 330 (1937), where the Court held that “the treaty power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment,” and that “the Constitution, by its very terms, makes treaties the supreme law of the land.” The Court emphasized that “the power to make treaties is a power of the whole government,” and that “the treaty power is not subject to the limitations of the Tenth Amendment.” Id. at 330, 57 S.Ct. 758.

Similarly, in *United States v. Schooner *“Peggy”*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 103, 110 (1810), the Court held that “the treaty power is a power of the whole government,” and that “the Constitution, by its very terms, makes treaties the supreme law of the land.” The Court rejected the argument that a treaty could not bind the United States unless it was consistent with state laws, stating that “the Constitution is supreme, and the treaty is a part of it.” Id. at 110.

The Necessary and Proper Clause further supports the validity of § 229. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421 (1819), the Court held that Congress may “exercise all powers necessary and proper for carrying into execution” its enumerated powers.
