**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Population Reduction Order Is Lawful Under the PLRA and This Court’s Precedent.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) does not bar the relief ordered by the Three-Judge Court. The PLRA’s text and this Court’s decisions make clear that population caps are permissible when necessary to remedy constitutional violations and no other relief would be effective.

A. The PLRA’s Exhaustion Requirement Does Not Apply to Structural Relief Orders.

The PLRA’s exhaustion provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), requires exhaustion of administrative remedies before prospective relief may be granted. *See Woodford v. Ngo*, 548 U.S. 81, 89 (2006). However, this requirement does not apply where, as here, the relief sought is structural in nature and not contingent on individual claims. *See id.* at 93 (“[T]he PLRA’s exhaustion requirement does not apply to systemic or structural injunctive relief.”). The Three-Judge Court’s order is precisely such structural relief, aimed at remedying systemic constitutional violations in California’s prison system.

B. The PLRA’s “Less Restrictive Means” Requirement Is Satisfied.

The PLRA mandates that prospective relief be “the least restrictive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The Three-Judge Court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions and that no other relief would suffice. *See Brown v. Plata*, 624 F.3d 956, 969 (9th Cir. 2010) (en banc), *rev’d on other grounds*, 563 U.S. 493 (2011). This finding is entitled to deference. *See Swarthout v. Cooke*, 562 U.S. 216, 219 (2011) (“[T]he court’s finding that no other relief would be effective is entitled to deference.”). The Court also noted that California had failed to implement less restrictive measures, such as reducing parole revocations or increasing good-time credits. *Plata*, 624 F.3d at 969. Thus, the order complies with the PLRA’s mandate.

C. The PLRA’s Ten-Year Sunset Provision Does Not Apply.

The PLRA’s sunset provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(e), terminates prospective relief after ten years unless the court makes specific findings. However, this provision applies only to relief granted *after* the PLRA’s enactment in 1996. *See id.* § 3626(e)(1). The Three-Judge Court’s order is based on violations that predate the PLRA and were the subject of prior litigation. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 570 F.3d 1090, 1100 (9th Cir. 2009) (noting that the constitutional violations “have persisted for decades”). Thus, the sunset provision does not apply.

**II. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Is Consistent with This Court’s Precedent on Prison Reform Litigation.**

This Court has repeatedly upheld population caps as a permissible remedy for constitutional violations. In *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493 (2011), this Court affirmed the Three-Judge Court’s order reducing California’s prison population to 137.5% of design capacity. The Court held that the order was narrowly tailored and necessary to remedy Eighth Amendment violations. *Id.* at 521 (“The need for speedy relief was great, and the efficacy of the remedy was justified.”). The Court also rejected the argument that the PLRA barred such relief, noting that the PLRA’s provisions were satisfied. *Id.* at 518.

Similarly, in *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), this Court upheld a district court’s finding that double-celling prisoners violated the Eighth Amendment, though it remanded for further findings on causation. The Court recognized that overcrowding could constitute cruel and unusual punishment when it results in deprivations of basic human needs. *Id.* at 345-46. The Three-Judge Court’s order here is consistent with *Rhodes* in targeting overcrowding as the primary cause of unconstitutional conditions.

**III. The Three-Judge Court Properly Exercised Its Equitable Authority.**

The PLRA does not divest courts of their equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations. *See Swarthout*, 562 U.S. at 219 (“The PLRA does not strip courts of their inherent equitable powers.”). The Three-Judge Court’s order is a permissible exercise of that authority, as it is narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found.

A. The Order Is Narrowly Tailored.

The Three-Judge Court’s order does not mandate the release of specific inmates but instead reduces the prison population to a level that ensures constitutional compliance. *See Plata*, 563 U.S. at 521 (“The order before us is not a mandate to California to release any particular prisoner.”). This approach is consistent with this Court’s precedent, which permits courts to order population caps without dictating individual releases. *See id.* at 518-19.

B. The Order Is Necessary to Remedy Constitutional Violations.

The Three-Judge Court found that California’s prisons were operating at nearly double their design capacity, leading to severe shortages in medical and mental health care, as well as increased violence. *Plata*, 624 F.3d at 969. The Court concluded that no other relief would be effective, given California’s history of noncompliance with prior orders. *Id.* This finding is entitled to deference. *See Swarthout*, 562 U.S. at 219.

**Conclusion**

The Three-Judge Court’s population reduction order is lawful under the PLRA and this Court’s precedent. It is narrowly tailored, necessary to remedy constitutional violations, and consistent with the equitable authority of federal courts. This Court should affirm the order in full.
