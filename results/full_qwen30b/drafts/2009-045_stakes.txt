**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is the presumptively correct measure of a “reasonable” fee under statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897, 104 S. Ct. 1541, 1548 (1984). This framework is designed to ensure that fee awards are grounded in objective, record-based evidence, not judicial subjectivity or personal admiration for counsel.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209 (11th Cir. 2008), and the district court’s award of a $4.5 million enhancement—nearly 43% of the total fee—rest on a fundamental misunderstanding of *Blum* and its progeny. The Court in *Blum* explicitly rejected the notion that “the quality of representation” or “the results obtained” can serve as independent bases for increasing the lodestar. Id. at 899, 104 S. Ct. at 1549. The Court emphasized that “the lodestar figure is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” and that “upward adjustments are not warranted merely because the attorney performed well or achieved a favorable outcome.” Id. at 897, 104 S. Ct. at 1548. The Court further clarified that any enhancement must be supported by “specific evidence” demonstrating that the quality of service was “superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged,” and that the results were “exceptional.” Id. at 899, 104 S. Ct. at 1549.

In this case, the district court awarded a $4.5 million enhancement based almost entirely on the judge’s own personal observations and subjective impressions—his “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge,” his “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta,” and his “observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). These statements were not evidence in the record; they were not subject to cross-examination; they were not supported by affidavits, declarations, or any other form of documented proof. They were, in essence, the judge’s own testimony—delivered ex parte and without notice to the opposing party—about the quality of counsel’s work. This is not the kind of “specific evidence” required by *Blum*. To the contrary, it is precisely the kind of unreviewable, subjective assessment that *Blum* sought to prevent.

The dissenting opinion in the Eleventh Circuit, authored by Judge Tjoflat, correctly identifies the constitutional infirmity in this process. The district court’s reliance on its own untested, unchallenged, and unrecorded observations deprived the defendants of due process. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 438, 98 S. Ct. 2864, 2874 (1978) (holding that due process requires that a party be given notice and an opportunity to respond to evidence relied upon in a fee award). The defendants were not given the chance to challenge the judge’s personal evaluation of counsel’s performance. They were not allowed to present evidence of their own. They were not even informed that the judge was relying on his own experience and impressions as a basis for the enhancement. This is not a proper exercise of judicial discretion—it is a violation of fundamental fairness.

The majority’s reliance on *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 106 S. Ct. 3088 (1986), is misplaced. *Delaware Valley I* did not overrule *Blum*; it reaffirmed it. The Court in *Delaware Valley I* stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” Id. at 565, 106 S. Ct. at 3098 (emphasis added). The Court did not hold that a judge may enhance a fee based on his own subjective assessment of counsel’s performance. It did not hold that a judge may testify on behalf of a party in a fee determination. It did not hold that a fee enhancement may be justified simply because the judge “thought” the work was excellent.

The Eleventh Circuit’s assertion that “there is no circuit split” on this issue is false. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have all recognized that enhancements based on quality of performance and results are permissible only in rare and exceptional cases supported by specific evidence. But the *Eleventh Circuit’s* interpretation of *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), goes far beyond their holdings. In *NAACP*, the court vacated and remanded because the district court failed to address the plaintiffs’ argument for an enhancement based on exceptional success. 812 F.2d at 1336–37. In *Norman*, the court reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in evaluating the results. 836 F.2d at 1306. Neither case held that a district court must affirm an enhancement based on superior performance or results. Neither case held that a judge may rely on his own unrecorded observations as evidence.
