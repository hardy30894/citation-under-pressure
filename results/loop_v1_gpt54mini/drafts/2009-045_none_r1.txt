The Court should answer the question presented in the negative. A fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may not be enhanced solely because counsel performed well and achieved an excellent result. The lodestar method—reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—supplies the presumptively reasonable fee, and the very factors of skill, quality, and success are ordinarily already reflected in that calculation. Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 433-37 (1983); Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 897-901 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 565-68 (1986).

That rule follows from the basic function of fee shifting. A “reasonable” fee is compensation for necessary legal work, not a bonus for excellence. Ex parte Peterson, 253 U.S. 300, 312-13 (1920); Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161, 166-67 (1939). The Court has repeatedly cautioned that fee awards should not become windfalls. Hensley, 461 U.S. at 433-37; Blum, 465 U.S. at 897; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-66.

Blum is especially controlling. There, the Court rejected an upward adjustment based on “the quality of representation” and “the results obtained” because those considerations were already subsumed in the lodestar. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900. The Court explained that the lodestar “normally” yields a fully compensatory fee and that any enhancement would require something more than proof that counsel did a superior job in the ordinary sense. Id. at 897-901. Delaware Valley reaffirmed that point, stating that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee.” 478 U.S. at 566. The Court thus rejected the notion that a district court may add a premium simply because it believes the representation was unusually effective. Id. at 565-68.

Hensley likewise shows that “results obtained” matters in determining whether a party should recover a fee and in gauging the reasonableness of the hours claimed, not as a separate enhancement factor. 461 U.S. at 434-37. The Court made clear that the fee inquiry centers on the relationship between the relief obtained and the hours reasonably expended. Id. at 436-37. Once those components are accounted for in the lodestar, there is no further room to increase the award for the same reasons. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-68.

Older fee-award decisions point in the same direction. In Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc., 488 F.2d 714, 717-19 (5th Cir. 1974), the court identified “the skill requisite to perform the legal service properly” and “the amount involved and the results obtained” as factors bearing on a reasonable fee. But Johnson predated the lodestar framework and cannot be read to authorize double counting after the lodestar has already absorbed those considerations. Id. at 717-19; Hensley, 461 U.S. at 433-37. Likewise, King v. Greenblatt, 560 F.2d 1024, 1027-28 (1st Cir. 1977), and Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer, 427 U.S. 445, 456-57 (1976), treated fee awards as compensation for legal services, not as awards of success premiums. See also Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp., 487 F.2d 161, 167-69 (3d Cir. 1973); Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp., 540 F.2d 102, 117-18 (3d Cir. 1976).

The contrary rule would undo the lodestar’s central virtue: administrability. If superior performance and exceptional results alone can support enhancement, the fee inquiry becomes an open-ended judgment about how much praise counsel deserves. That is precisely what Blum and Delaware Valley rejected in favor of a presumptive, objective baseline. Blum, 465 U.S. at 897-901; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-68. It is also inconsistent with the Court’s insistence that fee-shifting statutes be construed as limited exceptions to the American Rule. Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society, 421 U.S. 240, 262-71 (1975); Arcambel v. Wiseman, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 306 (1796).

In short, the Court should hold that a federal fee-shifting award cannot be enhanced solely on the basis of quality of performance and results obtained. Those considerations may help establish the reasonableness of the lodestar, but they cannot, standing alone, justify increasing it. Hensley, 461 U.S. at 433-37; Blum, 465 U.S. at 897-901; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-68.
