Under Title VII, punitive damages do not turn on a free-floating requirement that the employer’s conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the state of mind Congress specified. Congress authorized punitive damages where the defendant acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That statutory text focuses on the employer’s mental state, not on an additional judicial gloss demanding exceptional cruelty, insult, or opprobrium.

This Court’s punitive-damages cases likewise center on culpable state of mind. In Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), the Court explained that exemplary damages may be awarded where the defendant acted “wantonly, or oppressively, or with such malice as implies a spirit of mischief or criminal indifference to civil obligations.” Id. at 107. The point was not that tortious conduct had to be independently outrageous; rather, punitive damages require the requisite culpable intent. Id.; see also Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris, 122 U.S. 597 (1887) (recognizing punitive damages where the defendant acted with “bad intent” and an unlawful purpose); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886) (describing punitive inquiries as governed by “fixed rules of law,” not unguided moral impressions).

The same principle appears in Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society, 320 U.S. 238 (1943), where the Court sustained punitive damages without imposing any separate requirement that the conduct be uniquely heinous. The Court rejected the notion that punitive awards must bear some “definite mathematical relationship” to compensatory damages and emphasized that punitive damages depend on “all the attendant circumstances.” Id. at 243. That is a far cry from an independent “egregiousness” threshold.

Likewise, the Court’s First Amendment malice cases show that “malice” and “reckless disregard” describe subjective fault, not a separate demand for shocking conduct. In New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), the Court held that liability for certain speech required proof of “actual malice” — “knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not.” Id. at 280. St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), reiterated that reckless disregard is shown by a subjective awareness of probable falsity, not by an extra layer of outrageousness. Id. at 731-32. Henry v. Collins, 380 U.S. 356 (1965), likewise treated “malice” as including “culpable recklessness or a wilful and wanton disregard.” Id. at 357. And Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), used the same framework, asking whether the defendant acted with actual malice rather than whether the publication was independently egregious. Id. at 155.

That distinction matters because Congress, in § 1981a, selected the same state-of-mind formulation. When Congress uses terms with an established legal meaning, courts ordinarily apply that meaning. See, e.g., Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893); Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society, 320 U.S. 238 (1943). Here, Congress authorized punitive damages upon proof of “malice or reckless indifference,” not upon proof of some additional qualitative measure of badness. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Requiring a separate showing that the conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous” would add language Congress did not enact.

The lower court therefore erred to the extent it treated punitive damages as available only in cases of extraordinary misconduct beyond the statutory mental-state requirement. Where the employer knowingly disregards the federally protected rights of an employee, punitive damages may be considered. That rule accords with the punitive-damages cases, which ask whether the defendant acted with the necessary blameworthy state of mind, not whether the conduct meets an extra-statutory indignity test. See Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893); Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris, 122 U.S. 597 (1887); Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society, 320 U.S. 238 (1943); New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964); Henry v. Collins, 380 U.S. 356 (1965); Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130 (1967); St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727 (1968); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886). 

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed insofar as it imposed an independent “egregious” or “outrageous” requirement as a prerequisite to punitive damages under Title VII, and the case remanded for application of the proper statutory standard.
