**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the "Navigability for Title" Test by Focusing on the Rivers' Status at Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly determined that the relevant inquiry under the "navigability for title" test is whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable *at the time Montana achieved statehood in 1889*. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) ("The test as to whether a river is navigable for title is whether it was navigable in fact at the time the State became a member of the Union."); *Bonelli Cattle Co. v. Arizona*, 414 U.S. 313, 329 (1973) ("The equal-footing doctrine hinges on whether the streams in question were navigable at the time of statehood."); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) ("The equal-footing doctrine provides that when a State is admitted into the Union, it takes title to all lands under navigable waters within its borders, in trust for all the people.").

PPL’s contention that the trial court should have considered post-statehood evidence of navigability is foreclosed by binding precedent. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the critical moment for determining navigability for title purposes is the date of statehood. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76; *Bonelli*, 414 U.S. at 329. This rule ensures stability in property rights and prevents States from losing title to submerged lands due to later changes in navigability. *Id.* The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied this precedent by examining historical evidence—including territorial surveys, military reports, and commercial records—to determine whether the rivers were capable of supporting trade or travel at the time of statehood. *PPL Montana, LLC v. State*, 2010 MT 248, ¶¶ 60-80 (Mont. 2010).

**II. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Admitting Historical Evidence to Prove Navigability at Statehood.**

PPL challenges the admission of historical documents—such as territorial surveys, military reports, and periodicals—used to establish navigability at statehood. However, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that such evidence is admissible under the Federal Rules of Evidence and this Court’s precedents. *Smith v. Four Rivers Serv. Corp.*, 2003 MT 213, ¶ 39 (Mont. 2003) (affidavits must be based on personal knowledge or admissible hearsay); *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79-81 (Mont. 2008) (public records exception to hearsay applies to historical documents). The Court also properly relied on Rule 902’s self-authentication provisions for public records and newspapers. *M. R. Evid.* 902(4), (5), (6), (8).

This Court has long recognized that historical evidence is essential to proving navigability at statehood, as direct testimony from the 19th century is unavailable. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76 (relying on historical records to determine navigability in 1896). The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting this evidence, which was properly authenticated and fell within recognized hearsay exceptions. *Smith*, ¶ 47; *Crumleys Inc.*, ¶¶ 79-81.

**III. The State’s Evidence Sufficiently Established That the Rivers Were Navigable at Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the State presented substantial evidence demonstrating that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at statehood. The Court relied on:
- **Territorial surveys** documenting steamboat traffic and trade;
- **Military reports** describing the rivers as "navigable" for log drives and transportation; and
- **Commercial records** showing active trade and commerce on the rivers before 1889.

*Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76 (navigability established by "actual use for purposes of trade and travel"); *Bonelli*, 414 U.S. at 329 (navigability determined by "whether the stream was used for commerce in the ordinary and usual way"). The trial court’s findings are supported by the record and entitled to deference. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985) (factual findings reviewed for clear error).

**IV. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Claims.**

PPL argues that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s ability to seek compensation for use of state-owned riverbeds. This contention is meritless. The Supreme Court has held that the FPA does not preempt state property law claims. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946) ("The Federal Power Act establishes a dual system of control, preserving state authority over proprietary rights."); *Public Utility Dist. No. 1 of Pend Oreille Cty. v. Seattle*, 382 F.2d 666, 671-72 (9th Cir. 1967) (FPA does not preempt state compensation claims).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this precedent, noting that the FPA explicitly contemplates state regulation of "beds and banks" and requires licensees to comply with state law. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 161 n.6; 16 U.S.C. § 802. The FPA’s licensing scheme does not divest States of their title to submerged lands under the equal-footing doctrine. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551.

**V. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar the State’s Claims.**

PPL’s reliance on the federal navigational servitude is misplaced. The servitude does not extinguish state title to submerged lands; it merely ensures that navigable waters remain open to interstate commerce. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941). The Supreme Court has held that the servitude does not preempt state property law unless Congress has clearly authorized such preemption. *Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954). The FPA contains no such authorization. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76.

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the "navigability for title" test by focusing on the rivers’ status at statehood, admitted proper historical evidence, and properly rejected PPL’s preemption arguments. The judgment below should be affirmed.
