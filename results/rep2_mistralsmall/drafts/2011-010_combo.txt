**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION PROVISION REQUIRES THAT THE INJURY OCCUR ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain text of this provision, read in light of its statutory context and purpose, demonstrates that Congress intended to limit coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory coverage under the LHWCA is geographically circumscribed. In *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), the Court held that the LHWCA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on “navigable waters of the United States.” *Id.* at 255. Similarly, in *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244 (1941), the Court reaffirmed that the LHWCA’s coverage is tied to the situs of the injury on navigable waters. *Id.* at 246. These decisions establish that the LHWCA’s coverage is inherently tied to the location of the injury.

The OCSLA incorporates the LHWCA’s coverage provisions but extends them to the outer Continental Shelf. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The OCSLA does not, however, eliminate the geographic limitation inherent in the LHWCA. In *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court explained that when Congress incorporates provisions of one statute into another, it does so with the understanding that the incorporated provisions retain their original meaning unless Congress explicitly states otherwise. *Id.* at 106. Here, Congress did not explicitly eliminate the geographic limitation of the LHWCA when it extended coverage to the outer Continental Shelf. Thus, the OCSLA’s coverage remains tied to the situs of the injury.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is consistent with this interpretation. The court held that the OCSLA requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself. *Id.* at 362. The court reasoned that the OCSLA’s coverage provision is tied to the “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” and thus the injury must occur in the same location as the operations. *Id.* This interpretation ensures that the OCSLA’s coverage is geographically limited, as Congress intended.

**II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS” TEST IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE OCSLA’S STATUTORY TEXT AND PURPOSE.**

The Ninth Circuit in this case adopted a “substantial nexus” test, holding that the OCSLA’s coverage extends to injuries occurring on land if there is a substantial nexus between the injury and operations on the outer Continental Shelf. *See Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore*, 6933 F.3d 6933 (9th Cir. 2010). This test is inconsistent with the OCSLA’s statutory text and purpose.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory coverage provisions must be interpreted according to their plain text. In *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470 (1917), the Court held that “the intent of Congress is to be gathered from the words used in the statute.” *Id.* at 485. Here, the OCSLA’s coverage provision explicitly requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain text of this provision does not support the Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which would extend coverage to injuries occurring far from the outer Continental Shelf.

The Ninth Circuit’s test also conflicts with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985). In *Herb’s Welding*, the Court held that the OCSLA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. *Id.* at 424. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA’s coverage is tied to the situs of the injury, not the location of the operations. *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test would eviscerate this geographic limitation, allowing coverage for injuries occurring far from the outer Continental Shelf.

**III. THE OCSLA’S COVERAGE PROVISION IS TIED TO THE SITUS OF THE INJURY, NOT THE LOCATION OF THE OPERATIONS.**

The OCSLA’s coverage provision requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The Supreme Court has held that the phrase “as the result of” does not eliminate the geographic limitation of the LHWCA. In *Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Ellenberger*, 288 U.S. 257 (1933), the Court held that the phrase “as the result of” does not expand the scope of coverage beyond the statutorily defined situs. *Id.* at 265. Similarly, in *New York Cent. R. Co. v. Winfield*, 244 U.S. 147 (1917), the Court held that the phrase “as the result of” does not eliminate the requirement that the injury occur on navigable waters. *Id.* at 150.

The OCSLA’s coverage provision is tied to the situs of the injury, not the location of the operations. In *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Supreme Court held that the OCSLA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. *Id.* at 478. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA’s coverage is tied to the situs of the injury, not the location of the operations. *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test would eviscerate this geographic limitation, allowing coverage for injuries occurring far from the outer Continental Shelf.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is inconsistent with the OCSLA’s statutory text and purpose. The Supreme Court’s decisions in *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244 (1941), *Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Ellenberger*, 288 U.S. 257 (1933), *New York Cent. R. Co. v. Winfield*, 244 U.S. 147 (1917), and *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), support this interpretation. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that the OCSLA’s coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself.
