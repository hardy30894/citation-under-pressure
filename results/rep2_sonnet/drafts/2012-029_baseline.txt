# ARGUMENT

The federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over every case in which a patent issue happens to arise, even where that issue is embedded within a state-law claim. Congress has granted federal district courts jurisdiction over cases "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), but that grant does not sweep in state legal-malpractice claims merely because they require a hypothetical, backward-looking assessment of how a patent case *would have* come out. Under this Court's decision in *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005), federal-question jurisdiction over state-law claims exists only where "a state-law claim necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Id.* at 314. Minton's malpractice claim fails that test.

## I. The Embedded Patent Question in a Legal-Malpractice Suit Is Not "Substantial" Within the Meaning of *Grable*.

This Court has long cautioned that federal jurisdiction does not attach to every claim in which federal law appears somewhere in the case. In *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court explained that federal courts must apply "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside," *id.* at 117–18, and in *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that a claim does not arise under federal law unless it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law." *Id.* at 569. *Grable* refined but did not abandon this exacting inquiry, requiring that the federal issue be "substantial" in the sense of implicating "the validity, construction, or effect" of federal law itself—not merely its application to a discrete set of historical facts. 545 U.S. at 313.

Minton's malpractice claim requires only that a Texas jury determine, as a matter of hypothetical "case-within-a-case" causation, whether the experimental-use exception would have saved the '643 patent from the on-sale bar had Gunn raised it in a timely fashion. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004) (malpractice plaintiff must prove that but for counsel's negligence, the underlying suit would have been won). That inquiry resolves nothing about patent law prospectively. No patent will issue, be invalidated, or be enforced differently as a result of the state court's resolution of this question. The '643 patent remains invalid regardless of what the Texas courts conclude about Gunn's malpractice liability. Because the outcome "will have no preclusive effect on future patent litigation by other parties" and does not "change the real-world result of the prior federal patent litigation," the patent question here is the sort of backward-looking, case-specific application of law to fact that this Court has repeatedly held insufficient to satisfy *Grable*'s substantiality requirement. *See Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 700–01 (2006) (federal issue not substantial where dispute was "fact-bound and situation-specific").

## II. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Judicial Responsibilities.

*Grable*'s fourth requirement asks whether federal jurisdiction may be exercised "without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. Legal malpractice is a quintessential state-law cause of action, traditionally and exclusively the province of state courts applying state tort and professional-responsibility law. States possess overwhelming interests in regulating the conduct of attorneys licensed within their borders and in providing a forum for their citizens' malpractice claims. Sweeping such claims into federal court simply because the underlying representation happened to involve a patent would work a substantial reallocation of judicial authority that Congress never intended when it enacted § 1338(a).

This Court has already recognized the danger of allowing embedded federal issues to override well-established state adjudicatory authority. In *Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804 (1986), the Court held that the mere presence of a federal standard within a state tort claim did not create federal jurisdiction, explaining that Congress's decision not to create a private federal cause of action was "tantamount to a congressional conclusion that the presence of a claimed violation of the [federal] statute as an element of a state cause of action is insufficiently 'substantial' to confer federal-question jurisdiction." *Id.* at 814. The same logic applies here: Congress created a federal cause of action for patent infringement, not for legal malpractice arising out of patent litigation. Recognizing federal jurisdiction over the latter would, in the Federal Circuit's own words, extend "arising under" jurisdiction well beyond anything *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), contemplated.

## III. The Federal Circuit's *Air Measurement* Line of Cases Rests on a Flawed Reading of *Christianson* and *Grable*.

The Federal Circuit's decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), assert exclusive federal jurisdiction over patent-related malpractice claims based on a generalized interest in "uniformity" of patent law, without conducting the federalism analysis *Grable* demands. As the Texas Supreme Court dissent correctly observed, later Federal Circuit decisions applying this rule "neither cited *Grable* nor mentioned its factors at all." This approach cannot be reconciled with *Christianson*'s admonition that jurisdiction depends on whether "the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law," 486 U.S. at 809 (emphasis added), nor with *Grable*'s insistence that federal jurisdiction not disturb the state-federal judicial balance.

For these reasons, the state-law malpractice claim at issue does not "arise under" federal patent law, and the Texas courts properly possessed subject-matter jurisdiction to adjudicate it.
