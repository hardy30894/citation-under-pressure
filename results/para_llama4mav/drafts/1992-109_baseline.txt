ARGUMENT

I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE A TRIAL COURT TO APPLY A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD TO DETERMINE THE COMPETENCY OF A DEFENDANT TO ENTER A PLEA OF GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL.

The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard to determine the competency of a defendant to waive constitutional rights, such as the right to counsel or the right to plead not guilty. The Supreme Court has established that the standard for determining competency to stand trial is whether the defendant has a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and is capable of assisting his counsel. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975). This Court has not recognized a separate, heightened standard for determining competency to waive constitutional rights.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and subsequent cases, is misplaced. While Sieling did suggest that a higher level of mental functioning is required for a defendant to waive constitutional rights, this Court has never endorsed such a standard. In fact, the Court has implied that the same standard for competency to stand trial applies to determinations of competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. See Godinez v. Moran, to be decided.

Furthermore, applying a heightened "reasoned choice" standard would create unnecessary complexity and confusion in the law. The competency standard for standing trial is well-established and easily administrable. Extending this standard to waivers of constitutional rights ensures consistency and predictability in the application of the law.

II. EVEN IF A HEIGHTENED STANDARD WERE APPLICABLE, THE STATE COURT'S FINDINGS WERE BASED ON THE WRONG LEGAL STANDARD, AND THE RECORD DOES NOT SUPPORT A FINDING THAT MORAN WAS COMPETENT TO WAIVE HIS CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS.

Even assuming, arguendo, that a heightened "reasoned choice" standard applies, the state court's findings were premised on the wrong legal standard. The state court relied on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, not his competency to waive constitutional rights. As the Ninth Circuit correctly noted, these evaluations did not address the specific issue of Moran's capacity for "reasoned choice" among alternatives.

Moreover, the record raises significant doubts about Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights. Moran had attempted suicide, was taking multiple medications, and gave monosyllabic responses to the court's questions. Under these circumstances, the state court should have entertained a good faith doubt about Moran's competency and held a hearing to evaluate his ability to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of his constitutional rights.

In conclusion, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to apply the same standard for competency to stand trial to determinations of competency to waive constitutional rights.
