ARGUMENT

I. THE ALASKA NATIONAL INTEREST LANDS CONSERVATION ACT OF 1980 AUTHORIZES THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES ON STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS.

The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) makes clear that the National Park Service (NPS) has regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within Conservation System Units (CSUs). Section 103(c) provides that "only those public lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of the unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The statute does not limit the applicability of NPS regulations to only federal lands within CSUs. Instead, it subjects all lands within CSUs, regardless of ownership, to NPS regulations.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon’s interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute. As the court noted, "the statute does not say that only federal lands within the boundaries of CSUs are subject to NPS regulations.” Sturgeon v. Masica, 768 F.3d 1066, 1075 (9th Cir. 2014). Rather, it indicates that all lands within CSUs are subject to NPS regulations, regardless of ownership.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc., 474 U.S. 121 (1985), where the Court held that the Corps of Engineers' regulatory jurisdiction under the Clean Water Act extends to non-federal wetlands adjacent to navigable waters. Similarly, in this case, NPS's regulatory authority under ANILCA extends to all lands within CSUs, including state-owned lands and waters.

II. STURGEON LACKS A VALID PRIVILEGES OR IMMUNITIES CLAUSE CLAIM.

Sturgeon's claim that the NPS hovercraft ban implicates his right under the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to navigate the Nation River is without merit. The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. 36 (1872), on which Sturgeon relies, do not support his claim. In Slaughter-House, the Court held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protects citizens from state infringement on their right to use navigable waters, but it does not preclude federal regulation of those waters.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the federal government has a dominant navigational servitude over navigable waters. See, e.g., United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121, 122-23 (1967). This servitude allows the federal government to regulate activities on navigable waters, including those within CSUs.

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT ALASKA LACKS STANDING.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS regulations. Alaska's alleged injuries, including increased staff time and expense in complying with NPS procedures, are not redressable by a favorable ruling. As the court noted, "a favorable decision would not remedy injuries relating to DFG's chum and sockeye salmon study in 2010, which have already been incurred and suffered.” Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1073.

Furthermore, Alaska's claim that the NPS regulations interfere with its sovereign right to control its lands and waters is too speculative to establish standing. Alaska failed to identify any actual conflict between NPS's regulations and its own statutes and regulations. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992) (requiring that injuries be "actual or imminent” to establish standing).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's decision holding that ANILCA authorizes NPS to regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs, and that Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS regulations.
