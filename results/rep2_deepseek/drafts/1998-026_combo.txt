## ARGUMENT

The District Court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal, in violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2), does not warrant habeas relief because petitioner knew of that right and elected not to appeal. This Court reviews non-constitutional, non-jurisdictional errors for harmlessness. When the purpose of the rule has been fully satisfied, no relief is required.

### I. The violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review.

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure expressly provide that “[a]ny error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). This Court has long applied harmless-error analysis to procedural errors. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946). Even constitutional errors may be harmless if they are not “structural” and do not “affect substantial rights.” *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967). A rule violation that does not affect a defendant’s actual knowledge of his appeal right—and thus does not prejudice him—is a classic candidate for harmless error.

### II. The District Court’s failure to advise was harmless because petitioner knew of his right.

The District Court expressly found that petitioner “was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing.” That finding is binding on this Court. When a defendant already knows of his right, the omission of the formal advisement cannot affect his substantial rights. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to ensure that defendants are informed of their appeal rights—is fully served. This Court has held that strict compliance with a procedural rule is not required when the purpose of the rule has been achieved. *United States v. Hark*, 320 U.S. 531, 534–35 (1944) (substantial compliance with statutory requirement sufficient). Here, “substantial compliance” occurred because petitioner’s actual knowledge satisfied the rule’s object.

### III. Habeas relief is not available for this technical violation under 28 U.S.C. § 2255.

Section 2255 provides relief only for errors that “jurisdictional” or “constitutional” or that result in a “complete miscarriage of justice.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). In *Hill*, this Court held that a district court’s failure to comply with Rule 32(a) (the right of allocution) was not a ground for habeas relief because the error was neither jurisdictional nor constitutional, and did not result in a miscarriage of justice. *Id.* at 427–28. The same reasoning applies here. The failure to advise is a non-jurisdictional, non-constitutional procedural error. And because petitioner knew of his right, no miscarriage of justice occurred. *See also Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 104 (1942) (habeas relief limited to “fundamental” defects).

### IV. Petitioner’s knowledge demonstrates a knowing and intelligent waiver of his appeal right.

This Court has long recognized that a defendant may waive procedural rights, including the right to appeal, so long as the waiver is knowing and intelligent. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). Here, petitioner was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it. That choice constitutes a valid waiver. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944) (knowing failure to object waives procedural right). A defendant who knows of his right and declines to appeal cannot later claim the right was denied.

### V. This Court’s habeas precedents confirm that technical rule violations do not warrant relief.

The writ of habeas corpus is not a vehicle for correcting every procedural misstep. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 510–11 (1954). Only errors that “infect” the entire proceeding or “deprive the defendant of a fair trial” justify habeas intervention. *See Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 22. Petitioner’s actual knowledge of his appeal right leaves him in precisely the same position he would have occupied had the court given the formal advisement. The error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 765.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
