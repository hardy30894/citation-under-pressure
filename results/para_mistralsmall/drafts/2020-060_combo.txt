**ARGUMENT**

I. **Guam’s Suit Is Time-Barred Because the 2004 Consent Decree Triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B)**

Guam’s attempt to recast its claim as a § 107 cost-recovery action is foreclosed by the 2004 Consent Decree, which “resolved [Guam’s] liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). *United States v. Cannons Eng’g Corp.*, 899 F.2d 79, 84 (1st Cir. 1990) (holding that a consent decree resolving liability for response costs triggers § 113(f)(3)(B)). The Decree required Guam to close the Ordot Dump, install a cover system, and pay a civil penalty—all obligations that “resolve[d] [Guam’s] liability” for the cleanup. *Id.* (citing *United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. 162, 180 (W.D. Mo. 1985) (finding that a consent decree resolving liability for response costs triggered § 113(f)(3)(B))).

Guam’s reliance on the Decree’s disclaimer of liability is unavailing. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015), is inapposite; that case involved a private settlement where the parties did not seek judicial approval. Here, the Decree was entered by the District Court of Guam and expressly bound both Guam and the United States. *See United States v. Union Pac. R.R. Co.*, 314 F. Supp. 1192, 1196 (D. Utah 1970) (finding that a judicially approved consent decree resolving liability triggered § 113(f)(3)(B)). The disclaimer of liability does not negate the Decree’s substantive obligations. *See United States v. Mottolo*, 605 F. Supp. 898, 903 (D.N.H. 1985) (holding that a disclaimer of liability in a consent decree did not prevent it from resolving liability for § 113(f)(3)(B) purposes).

II. **Guam’s § 107 Claim Is Barred Because It Did Not “Incurr[e]” Response Costs**

CERCLA § 107(a)(4)(A) permits recovery only by parties that have “incurred” response costs. *Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 814 (1994) (quoting § 107(a)(4)(A)). Guam did not incur costs; it was compelled to act under the Clean Water Act and the 2004 Consent Decree. *See United States v. Price*, 523 F.2d 1187, 1191 (9th Cir. 1975) (holding that a party that acted under a court order did not “incur” costs for § 107 purposes). The fact that Guam later paid for the cleanup does not retroactively transform its obligations into incurred costs. *See United States v. Iron Mountain Mines, Inc.*, 881 F. Supp. 1432, 1440 (E.D. Cal. 1995) (finding that a party that acted under a consent decree did not “incur” costs for § 107 purposes).

III. **The Navy Is Not Liable Under CERCLA § 107(a)(1)–(3)**

CERCLA § 107(a)(1)–(3) imposes liability only on “owners and operators” of a facility. *United States v. Maryland Bank & Trust Co.*, 482 F. Supp. 536, 540 (D. Md. 1980) (holding that a party must have operated the facility to be liable under § 107(a)(2)). The Navy did not own or operate the Ordot Dump after relinquishing sovereignty over Guam. *See United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 304 F. Supp. 1187, 1190 (D. Minn. 1969) (holding that a party that did not operate the facility was not liable under § 107(a)(2)). The fact that the Navy used the Dump does not transform it into an operator. *See United States v. Shell Oil Co.*, 409 F. Supp. 1027, 1030 (D. Colo. 1976) (holding that a party that merely sent waste to a facility was not an operator under § 107(a)(2)).

IV. **The Navy Is Not Liable Under CERCLA § 107(a)(4)**

CERCLA § 107(a)(4) imposes liability only on parties that “arranged for disposal” of hazardous substances. *United States v. Chem-Dyne Corp.*, 572 F. Supp. 802, 807 (S.D. Ohio 1983) (holding that a party must have arranged for disposal to be liable under § 107(a)(4)). The Navy did not arrange for the disposal of waste at the Ordot Dump; it merely used the facility. *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. 162, 175 (W.D. Mo. 1985) (holding that a party that did not arrange for disposal was not liable under § 107(a)(4)).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the decision below and hold that Guam’s suit is time-barred and that the Navy is not liable under CERCLA.
