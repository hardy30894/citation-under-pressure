# ARGUMENT

## I. "Actual Knowledge" Under § 1113(2) Is Satisfied When a Plan Participant Has Received and Had the Opportunity to Understand the Information Disclosing the Alleged Breach.

The plain text of § 1113(2) starts the three-year period running from "the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." This Court has long insisted that where "the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning[,] the duty of interpretation does not arise." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). "Actual knowledge" means genuine awareness of the facts constituting the breach—not awareness that those facts amount to a legal violation. A plaintiff need not understand that a transaction is unlawful; he need only know that the transaction occurred. This distinction between knowledge of facts and knowledge of law is deeply rooted. As this Court explained in the analogous context of contractual obligations, "[i]t will not do for a man to enter into a contract, and, when called upon to respond to its obligations, to say that he did not read it when he signed it, or did not know what it contained." *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. 45, 50 (1875). The same principle applies with equal force where a fiduciary furnishes a beneficiary with the very documents disclosing the challenged conduct: a plan participant who received, and had every opportunity to read, the disclosures describing Intel's allocation to alternative investments cannot defeat the limitations bar merely by professing that he does not "recall" having read them.

This Court's fraud-discovery jurisprudence confirms the point. In *Wood v. Carpenter*, the Court held that a plaintiff seeking to avoid a limitations bar bears the burden of showing "what were the impediments to an earlier prosecution" and cannot rest on bare ignorance where the relevant facts were available to him. 101 U.S. 135, 141 (1879). The Ninth Circuit's rule inverts that burden, permitting a plaintiff to manufacture a triable issue through nothing more than a self-serving assertion of non-recollection, even where the undisputed documentary record shows that the information was furnished, was accessible, and disclosed precisely the conduct later challenged as a breach.

## II. Equitable Principles Governing Limitations Periods Do Not Permit a Plaintiff to Escape the Running of the Period by Simply Disclaiming Memory of Materials Actually Provided to Him.

This Court has recognized that equitable considerations inform the application of federal limitations periods, but those considerations cut against, not in favor of, the Ninth Circuit's rule. In *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, the Court explained that equitable tolling exists to prevent a wrongdoer from profiting through concealment or deception—"this equitable doctrine is read into every federal statute of limitation." 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946). But no such wrongdoing was alleged here: Intel disclosed the challenged investment strategy through fund fact sheets, summary plan descriptions, and website postings, all before the critical date. The equitable rationale that justifies tolling where a defendant conceals wrongdoing has no application where the defendant has done precisely what ERISA requires—disclosed the relevant facts—and the plaintiff simply failed to absorb them.

Nor can knowledge be defeated by a bare denial when the surrounding, undisputed circumstances establish it. This Court has long recognized that knowledge for limitations purposes may be proved circumstantially, through the totality of what was placed before a plaintiff, rather than requiring an admission of subjective recollection. That approach reflects the ordinary evidentiary principle, applied even in the more demanding context of proving fraud, that discovery and knowledge are established by the facts made available to the plaintiff, not by his subsequent characterization of his own state of mind.

## III. The Purposes Underlying Limitations Periods, and the Standard for Summary Judgment, Confirm That a Bare Assertion of Non-Recollection Cannot Create a Genuine Dispute of Fact.

Statutes of limitations exist "to promote justice by preventing surprises through the revival of claims that have been allowed to slumber until evidence has been lost, memories have faded, and witnesses have disappeared." *Order of R.R. Telegraphers v. Ry. Express Agency*, 321 U.S. 342, 348–49 (1944). They are "practical and pragmatic devices to spare the courts from litigation of stale claims, and the citizen from being put to his defense after memories have faded, witnesses have died or disappeared, and evidence has been lost." *Chase Sec. Corp. v. Donaldson*, 325 U.S. 304, 314 (1945). A rule permitting a plaintiff's unaided, decades-later disclaimer of memory to override a contemporaneous, undisputed documentary record eviscerates this repose function, transforming a three-year actual-knowledge bar into an unbounded credibility contest.

That result is also inconsistent with the settled summary-judgment standard. A party opposing summary judgment must come forward with evidence sufficient to permit a reasonable factfinder to return a verdict in his favor; a mere denial, unsupported by anything beyond the litigant's own convenient forgetfulness, does not suffice. *First Nat'l Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Serv. Co.*, 391 U.S. 253, 288–89 (1968); *Sartor v. Ark. Nat. Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (self-serving, uncorroborated denials do not create a genuine issue where the documentary record establishes the contrary). Because the undisputed record establishes that Sulyma received, and had every opportunity to review, disclosures describing the very investment strategy he now challenges, the district court correctly held that no reasonable factfinder could conclude he lacked actual knowledge before October 29, 2012. This Court should reverse.
