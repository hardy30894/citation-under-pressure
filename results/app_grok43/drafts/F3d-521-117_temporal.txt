The Fourth Amendment forbids unreasonable searches of the person. Weeks v. United States, 232 U.S. 383, 390 (1914). A pat-frisk constitutes a search. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 16 (1968). Such a search is permissible only when an officer can “point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant” the conclusion that the person “may be armed and presently dangerous.” Id. at 21, 27.

Mere presence in an automobile stopped for a traffic infraction supplies no such justification. United States v. Di Re, 332 U.S. 581, 587 (1948) (“We are not convinced that a person, by mere presence in a suspected automobile, loses immunities from search of his person to which he would otherwise be entitled.”). The observed hand movements toward the console and door, without more, do not establish individualized suspicion that the passenger was armed. See Johnson v. United States, 333 U.S. 10, 16-17 (1948) (requiring particular facts rather than “a mere hunch”).

Soares’s subsequent agitation, rocking, and verbal complaints likewise fall short. The record contains no testimony that any officer observed a bulge, weapon, or threatening gesture directed at the officers. Compare Terry, 392 U.S. at 28 (frisk upheld where officer saw suspects conferring and one “patting” the other’s pocket in a manner suggesting a concealed weapon). Verbal abuse and nervous movement, without more, have never been held sufficient to authorize a frisk. See, e.g., Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89, 96-97 (1964).

Because the officers lacked the requisite particularized suspicion, the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment. The handgun should have been suppressed.
