**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT AN INHERITED IRA IS NOT “RETIREMENT FUNDS” UNDER § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12) OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE.**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Clark v. Rameker* (In re Clark), 714 F.3d 559 (7th Cir. 2013), conflicts with the plain text and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemption. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts from a debtor’s bankruptcy estate “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code, including § 408 (IRAs). The Seventh Circuit held that an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” because it must be distributed before the beneficiary’s retirement. This interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory text and the Code’s broad remedial purpose.

A. **The Bankruptcy Code’s Retirement-Fund Exemption Does Not Require That Funds Be Reserved for the Debtor’s Own Retirement.**

The Seventh Circuit erred in requiring that retirement funds be “held for the owner’s retirement” to qualify for exemption. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561. The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but it does not impose a temporal or ownership requirement. The exemption applies to funds “in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” § 408, regardless of who holds the account or when the funds are distributed. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); *id.* § 522(d)(12). The Seventh Circuit’s focus on the beneficiary’s inability to hold the funds until retirement conflates the *source* of the funds (an IRA) with their *current status* as retirement savings. This approach ignores the fact that inherited IRAs remain tax-deferred retirement accounts under the Internal Revenue Code. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (providing that inherited IRAs are exempt from taxation until distribution).

The Fifth Circuit correctly rejected the temporal approach in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012). The Fifth Circuit held that the exemption applies to funds that were “retirement funds” in the hands of the decedent, regardless of whether they remain retirement funds in the hands of the beneficiary. *Id.* at 490. This interpretation aligns with the statutory text, which does not condition the exemption on the debtor’s use of the funds. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); *id.* § 522(d)(12).

B. **The Seventh Circuit’s Interpretation Undermines the Purpose of the Retirement-Fund Exemption.**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision frustrates the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose of providing debtors with a “fresh start.” *See* *Hanover Nat’l Bank v. Moyses*, 186 U.S. 181, 186 (1902). By denying debtors the ability to exempt inherited IRAs, the decision forces beneficiaries to liquidate retirement savings to satisfy creditors, defeating the Code’s goal of preserving essential assets. *See* *Owen v. Owen*, 500 U.S. 305, 313 (1991) (noting that exemptions are intended to ensure debtors retain the means of “supporting themselves and their dependents”).

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a hypothetical withdrawal of funds from an IRA is unpersuasive. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. Unlike a voluntary withdrawal, an inheritance is not a choice; it is a statutory transfer that Congress has expressly treated as a continuation of the IRA’s tax-deferred status. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). The beneficiary does not gain unfettered access to the funds; instead, the funds remain subject to mandatory distribution rules designed to preserve their tax-deferred status. *See id.* § 401(a)(9)(B).

C. **The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Creates an Unjustified Circuit Split and Disregards Binding Precedent.**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Eighth Circuit Bankruptcy Appellate Panel’s decision in *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), and the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486. These courts correctly held that inherited IRAs qualify as “retirement funds” under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because they are tax-deferred accounts that were originally established for retirement. *Nessa*, 426 B.R. at 317; *Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 490.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522 (1987), is misplaced. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. *Rodriguez* does not support the proposition that exemptions must be narrowly construed to avoid “frustrat[ing] legislative intent.” *Rodriguez*, 480 U.S. at 525–26. To the contrary, exemptions are to be construed liberally in favor of debtors. *See* *Owen*, 500 U.S. at 313.

II. **THE COURT SHOULD REVERSE THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION AND HOLD THAT INHERITED IRAS ARE EXEMPT UNDER § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12).**

The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemption applies to inherited IRAs because they are tax-deferred accounts that were originally established for retirement. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary decision is inconsistent with the statutory text, conflicts with binding precedent, and undermines the Code’s remedial purpose. This Court should reverse and hold that inherited IRAs are exempt under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).
