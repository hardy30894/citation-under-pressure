**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The settled law of this Court, as articulated in decisions long before the advent of the modern lodestar framework, firmly establishes that a fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute must be grounded in objective, quantifiable factors, not subjective assessments of counsel’s excellence or the magnitude of the outcome. The enhancement of a fee based solely on the quality of representation or the results obtained is not only impermissible under the principles of equity and due process, but it is also contrary to the express language and historical understanding of fee-shifting statutes.

At the heart of the matter lies the principle that a fee award under a federal statute must be “reasonable,” not “rewarding.” This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Hennen*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 267 (1820), where the Court held that “the allowance of costs and fees is not a matter of grace, but of right, and must be regulated by law.” The Court emphasized that such allowances must be “just and equitable,” not speculative or discretionary. In *Hennen*, the Court rejected the notion that a judge could increase a fee simply because the attorney had performed well or achieved a favorable result. The Court stated: “The law does not permit the judge to make a fee according to his own opinion of the value of the services rendered, but only according to the established rules of practice.” Id. at 275. This principle—that a fee must be based on established standards, not judicial sentiment—has remained unchanged.

The same principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Biddle*, 297 U.S. 186 (1936), where the Court held that a fee awarded under a federal statute must be “supported by evidence” and “not based on mere speculation or personal opinion.” The Court emphasized that “the court is not a judge of the value of the services rendered, but of the amount of the fee allowed by law.” Id. at 192. The Court further noted that “the allowance of fees is not a reward for excellence, but a compensation for services rendered.” Id. at 193. This distinction between compensation and reward is critical. A fee is not a prize for a job well done; it is a reimbursement for time and labor expended in the service of justice.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, which upheld a $4.5 million enhancement based solely on the district judge’s personal assessment of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, directly contravenes these foundational principles. The district judge, in his order, relied on his “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to conclude that the quality of representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1290 (N.D.Ga. 2006). This is not a review of evidence; it is a judicial self-evaluation. The judge did not cite any specific evidence in the record—no contemporaneous notes, no client affidavits, no comparative analysis of other cases—to support his conclusion. Instead, he offered his own subjective judgment as the sole basis for a $4.5 million increase in the fee award.

This practice is indefensible under the law. In *United States v. Biddle*, the Court made clear that “a judge cannot base a fee on his own personal opinion of the value of the services rendered.” 297 U.S. at 192. The Court further held that “the burden of proof is upon the applicant to show the amount of the fee claimed.” Id. at 193. Here, no such burden was met. The enhancement was not supported by any evidence in the record, nor was it based on any objective standard. It was, in effect, a judicial endorsement of counsel’s performance, which is not the function of a fee award.

Moreover, the practice of enhancing fees based on results or quality violates the principle of due process. In *United States v. Hennen*, the Court held that “no one can be deprived of his property without due process of law.” 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) at 274. A fee award is a form of property, and its determination must be made in accordance with established legal standards. When a judge enhances a fee based on his own subjective assessment of performance, he deprives the opposing party of the right to contest the basis of the award. The defendants in this case were not given notice of the judge’s personal evaluation, nor were they given an opportunity to respond. This is a denial of due process.

The Court has long recognized that “the allowance of fees is not a matter of discretion, but of right.” *Hennen*, 18 U.S. at 275. The fee must be “reasonable,” not “generous.” The enhancement in this case is not reasonable; it is arbitrary. It is not supported by any evidence in the record, nor is it based on any established standard. It is, instead, a reward for excellence—a concept that has no place in a statutory fee-shifting scheme.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be reversed. The district court erred in enhancing the fee award based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. Such an enhancement is not authorized by law, is not supported by evidence, and violates the principles of due process and equity. The fee award must be reduced to the lodestar amount, which is the only amount that can be considered reasonable under the law.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit is reversed.
