The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum.

The government has the authority to create a limited public forum, opening its property to certain groups or for certain purposes, without being required to allow all speech. See Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators' Assn., 262 U.S. 37 (1923). In such a forum, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on speech, as long as they are viewpoint-neutral. See Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268 (1951).

Milford Central School's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on speech in a limited public forum. The policy prohibits use of school facilities for "religious purposes," a clear and objective standard. The school has allowed various groups to use its facilities, including the Boy Scouts, the Girl Scouts, and the 4-H Club, but has excluded the Good News Club because its activities are deemed "quintessentially religious." See Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 202 F.3d 502 (2d Cir. 2000). This exclusion is not viewpoint discrimination, but rather a reasonable application of the school's policy.

The Good News Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, constitute a form of religious worship. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-14 (1952). The school's exclusion of the Club is justified by its concern about avoiding Establishment Clause violations, a legitimate government interest. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947).

The Club's argument that its activities represent a viewpoint on a secular subject, such as morals and character, is unpersuasive. The Club's teaching of morals and character is inextricably linked with religious doctrine. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940). The school is not required to allow the Club to use its facilities simply because other groups discuss similar topics from a secular perspective.

The government must maintain a degree of separation between church and state. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948). By excluding the Good News Club, Milford Central School upholds this principle. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals, as the school's community use policy is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech.

The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School's facilities is consistent with the principles established in Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), and Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158 (1944), which recognize the government's authority to regulate certain activities in order to maintain public order and protect the rights of others.

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum, justified by its concern about avoiding Establishment Clause violations.
