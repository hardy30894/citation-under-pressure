**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and the Third Circuit correctly held that she may not. This Court has long recognized that the Tenth Amendment does not confer standing upon private individuals to litigate federalism violations. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939), established that private parties, even when injured by federal action, lack standing to assert the Tenth Amendment’s limitations on federal power absent a showing of injury to the state itself. The Court’s holding in *Tennessee Electric* was not dictum but a jurisdictional prerequisite to adjudicating the merits of a Tenth Amendment claim. See *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (holding that *Tennessee Electric*’s standing analysis was “an alternative ground” and thus binding).

Bond’s attempt to circumvent this settled principle by arguing that *Tennessee Electric* is “textbook dicta” is foreclosed by precedent. The Supreme Court in *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10, 98 S.Ct. 2985, 57 L.Ed.2d 1018 (1978), reaffirmed that when a court resolves a constitutional jurisdictional issue—such as standing—before addressing the merits, the holding on standing is not mere dicta but a necessary predicate to jurisdiction. Here, the Third Circuit properly exercised its duty under *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998), to determine whether it had jurisdiction before reaching the constitutional question. Bond’s claim fails at the threshold.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that private parties may assert the Tenth Amendment as a personal defense to criminal prosecution. In *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201, 124 S.Ct. 1628, 158 L.Ed.2d 420 (2004), the Court emphasized that the Tenth Amendment “protects the States’ sovereignty, not the rights of individuals.” The amendment “does not create rights in individuals,” nor does it “grant standing to private parties to challenge federal legislation on federalism grounds.” *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004), cert. denied, 543 U.S. 874, 125 S.Ct. 88, 160 L.Ed.2d 124 (2004). Bond’s conduct—repeated attempts to poison another person with toxic chemicals—falls squarely within the scope of federal criminal law, particularly when implemented pursuant to a valid treaty. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a binding international obligation, and Congress has the authority under the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause to enact legislation to fulfill it. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920), remains controlling precedent: “If a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”

Bond’s argument that § 229 is unconstitutional because it lacks a federal interest element or a Commerce Clause basis is misplaced. The Constitution does not require that every federal criminal statute be grounded in the Commerce Clause. The Treaty Power, as recognized in *Holland*, provides an independent and sufficient basis for federal legislation. The Court has repeatedly upheld treaty-implementing statutes even when they regulate conduct that is otherwise local in nature. See *Lara*, 541 U.S. at 201, 124 S.Ct. 1628 (“Congress may regulate conduct that is otherwise within the domain of state criminal law when the regulation is necessary to fulfill a treaty obligation.”). The fact that Bond’s actions were personal and localized does not render the federal statute invalid. The federal government has a legitimate interest in preventing the use of toxic chemicals for harmful purposes, especially when such conduct threatens public safety and undermines international commitments.

Finally, Bond’s claim that § 229 is unconstitutionally vague fails for the same reason: she lacks standing to raise it. Even if the statute were vague, the challenge must be brought by a party who has suffered a concrete injury. *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92, 96 S.Ct. 316, 46 L.Ed.2d 228 (1975). Bond’s conduct—stealing toxic chemicals, attempting to poison another person, and using the mail to further her scheme—was not protected by the First Amendment, and the statute clearly proscribes such conduct. The definition of “chemical weapon” and “toxic chemical” in § 229F is sufficiently precise to provide fair notice. See *United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998) (upholding constitutionality of § 229 under due process and fair notice principles).

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. The Court has long recognized that private individuals cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal criminal statutes. Bond’s claim is not only without merit but also lacks the standing required to bring it. The federal government’s authority to implement its treaty obligations under the Necessary and Proper Clause is well established, and the statute at issue is a valid exercise of that power. The judgment below is correct, and it must be affirmed.
