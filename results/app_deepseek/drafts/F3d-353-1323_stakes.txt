**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing this action for lack of subject matter jurisdiction because the Government of Punjab’s offer of an alternative parcel of land in the United States constitutes “commercial activity carried on in the United States” within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The court below improperly focused on the expropriation itself, which is a sovereign act, and ignored the subsequent commercial dealings that occurred on American soil.

**I. The Government of Punjab Engaged in Commercial Activity in the United States**

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) provides an exception to immunity when “the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the question is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions that a private party could engage in.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). A foreign state acts commercially “when it acts not as a regulator of a market, but in the manner of a private player within it.” *Id.* at 614–15.

Here, the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States to offer Beg an alternative parcel of land in exchange for the property that had been expropriated. This act—negotiating and proposing a land swap—is precisely the kind of transaction that a private landowner could undertake. It is not a sovereign act of eminent domain or regulation; it is a voluntary, consensual exchange. The Supreme Court has held that even a foreign government’s issuance of bonds is commercial because private parties also issue bonds. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 615. By the same logic, offering to transfer real property in settlement of a dispute is a commercial activity that private parties routinely perform.

The district court erroneously concluded that no commercial activity occurred because the expropriation itself was sovereign. But the FSIA exception does not require that the entire course of conduct be commercial; it requires that the “action” (the claim) be “based upon” commercial activity. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 357 (1993). Beg’s complaint is not solely about the expropriation; it also seeks compensation for the Government of Punjab’s broken promise to provide alternative land—a promise made and negotiated in the United States. That promise is a commercial act.

**II. The Commercial Activity Occurred in the United States**

The Government of Punjab’s representative physically came to the United States to make the offer. This is not a case where a foreign state acted abroad and caused an effect here; it is a case where the foreign state acted *in* the United States. The FSIA’s first clause under § 1605(a)(2) requires only that the commercial activity be “carried on in the United States.” No additional “direct effect” showing is necessary. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618 (distinguishing the third clause’s direct-effect requirement from the first clause’s focus on activity in the United States). Because the offer and negotiation occurred on American soil, jurisdiction is proper.

**III. The District Court’s Reliance on the Expropriation’s Sovereign Character Was Misplaced**

The district court reasoned that the expropriation was a sovereign act and therefore no commercial activity existed. But the FSIA’s commercial activity exception looks to the *nature* of the act, not its purpose. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. Even if the expropriation was sovereign, the subsequent offer of alternative land was commercial. The two are separable. Beg’s claim for breach of that offer does not require relitigating the validity of the expropriation. *Cf. Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 713 (9th Cir. 1992) (holding that expropriation is sovereign, but subsequent commercial dealings may create jurisdiction). Here, the Government of Punjab stepped out of its sovereign role and into the marketplace when it sent a representative to the United States to negotiate a land exchange.

**IV. Alternatively, the Offer Had a Direct Effect in the United States**

Even if this Court were to conclude that the commercial activity occurred abroad (e.g., the expropriation or the Supreme Court of Pakistan’s rejection), the third clause of § 1605(a)(2) applies because the act caused a “direct effect in the United States.” The offer was made to Beg, a resident of the United States, and his reliance on that offer caused him to abandon further litigation in Pakistan. A direct effect is one that “follows as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s activity.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the Government of Punjab’s representative traveled to the United States and made a promise that Beg accepted. The effect—Beg’s change of position and his loss of the opportunity to pursue his claim in Pakistan—occurred directly in the United States. This satisfies the direct-effect requirement.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred by focusing solely on the expropriation and ignoring the Government of Punjab’s commercial activity in the United States. Because that activity falls squarely within the FSIA’s commercial activity exception, this Court should reverse the dismissal and remand for further proceedings.
