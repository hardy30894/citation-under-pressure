**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BOARD ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE ’880 APPLICATION IS ENTITLED TO THE FILING DATE OF THE ’566 APPLICATION.

A. The Board Misapplied the Continuity Requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 120.

The Board concluded that the ’880 application was entitled to the filing date of the ’566 application under 35 U.S.C. § 120, which permits a later application to claim the benefit of an earlier filing date if it "names an inventor or joint inventor in the earlier application" and "was filed before or on the same day as the patenting or publication of a claimed invention." *See* 35 U.S.C. § 120. To satisfy § 120, an applicant must show that the later application is a "continuation, divisional, or continuation-in-part" of the earlier application and that the disclosure of the earlier application is "substantially identical" to that of the later application. *See* *In re Hogan*, 559 F.2d 595, 601 (C.C.P.A. 1977); *In re Wertheim*, 541 F.2d 257, 262 (C.C.P.A. 1976). The Board, however, improperly relaxed these requirements by focusing on whether the ’880 application "incorporated" the ’579 application by reference, rather than whether the intervening applications in the priority chain satisfied the continuity requirements of § 120.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that § 120’s continuity requirements are strict and must be strictly construed. *See* *In re Janz*, 579 F.2d 455, 459 (C.C.P.A. 1978) ("The statute requires that the disclosure of the earlier application be substantially identical to that of the later application."); *In re Chu*, 66 F.3d 292, 297 (Fed. Cir. 1995) ("The purpose of § 120 is to ensure that the later application is a continuation of the earlier one, not merely a continuation of the subject matter."). The Board’s failure to apply this standard was reversible error.

B. The Intervening Applications Did Not Satisfy § 120’s Continuity Requirements.

The Board erred in holding that the ’880 application was entitled to the filing date of the ’566 application because the intervening applications (’838, ’768, and ’708) did not satisfy § 120’s continuity requirements. The ’838 application, a divisional of the ’566 application, did not incorporate the ’579 application by reference in a manner sufficient to satisfy § 120. *See* *In re Scheiber*, 587 F.2d 59, 62 (C.C.P.A. 1978) ("A divisional application must contain a disclosure that is substantially identical to that of the parent application."). The ’768 and ’708 applications, as continuations of the ’838 application, likewise failed to satisfy § 120 because they did not provide a disclosure substantially identical to the ’566 application.

This Court has held that an applicant cannot rely on incorporation by reference to cure a deficiency in the disclosure of an intervening application. *See* *In re Lund*, 376 F.3d 1365, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2004) ("Incorporation by reference cannot be used to supply missing disclosure in a continuation application."). The Board’s reliance on the incorporation statement in the ’566 application to satisfy § 120’s continuity requirements was misplaced, as the intervening applications did not themselves contain a disclosure substantially identical to the ’566 application.

C. The Board’s Holding Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent.

The Board’s holding conflicts with this Court’s precedent, which requires strict compliance with § 120’s continuity requirements. *See* *In re Hay*, 534 F.2d 917, 920 (C.C.P.A. 1976) ("The statute is clear: the later application must be a continuation of the earlier one."). The Board’s decision effectively allowed Harari to rely on the ’566 application’s filing date without satisfying § 120’s continuity requirements, a result this Court has repeatedly rejected. *See* *In re Basell Poliolefine Italia S.P.A.*, 501 F.3d 1299, 1304 (Fed. Cir. 2007) ("The purpose of § 120 is to prevent applicants from obtaining the benefit of an earlier filing date without complying with the statute’s requirements.").

II. THE BOARD ERRED IN HOLDING THAT HARARI’S ’880 APPLICATION PRECEDED HOLLMER’S CONCEPTION DATE.

A. The Board Misapplied the Law of Conception and Reduction to Practice.

The Board held that Harari’s ’880 application preceded Hollmer’s conception date because Harari’s ’880 application was entitled to the filing date of the ’566 application. This holding was erroneous because it failed to account for the fact that Hollmer’s ’601 patent was filed before Harari’s ’880 application, and thus Hollmer’s conception date was irrelevant to the priority dispute. *See* *Oka v. Youssefyeh*, 849 F.2d 581, 584 (Fed. Cir. 1988) ("The first to file rule applies in an interference proceeding.").

B. The Board’s Holding Conflicts with the First-to-File Rule.

The Board’s holding conflicts with the first-to-file rule, which governs priority disputes in interference proceedings. *See* *35 U.S.C. § 102(g)*; *Mahurkar v. C.R. Bard, Inc.*, 79 F.3d 1572, 1577 (Fed. Cir. 1996) ("The first party to reduce an invention to practice is not entitled to priority unless it was the first to file a patent application."). The Board’s decision effectively allowed Harari to prevail in the interference despite Hollmer’s earlier filing date, a result that conflicts with the first-to-file rule.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Board’s decision should be reversed, and judgment should be entered in favor of Hollmer.
