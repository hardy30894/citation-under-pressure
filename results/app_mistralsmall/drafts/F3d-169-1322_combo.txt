**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Appellees Were Not Joint Employers Under the AWPA.**

The AWPA defines an “employer” to include “any person acting directly or indirectly in the interest of an employer in relation to an employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 1802(5). The Supreme Court has long recognized that joint employment relationships may exist where two or more entities exercise sufficient control over the terms and conditions of a worker’s employment. *See, e.g., United States v. Silk*, 331 U.S. 704, 716 (1947) (“[W]here the work done, in its essence, is the work of the employees of the [putative employer], it is of no consequence that certain employees formally cast their lot with another employer.”); *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 121 (1941) (“The motive and purpose of the present statute are plain enough. The interstate movement of goods is commerce; the regulation of the people who employ others to work on those goods is not forbidden by any constitutional limitation.”).

Here, the record demonstrates that appellees exercised significant control over the workers’ terms and conditions of employment. Appellees contracted with Luxama to supply labor for the snap bean harvest, directed the workers to specific fields, and observed their progress daily. *See Silk*, 331 U.S. at 716 (finding joint employment where putative employers exercised control over workers’ hours, wages, and working conditions). Moreover, appellees’ contractual arrangement with Little Rock—under which they shared profits and marketing responsibilities—further evidences their joint employer status. *See Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb*, 331 U.S. 722, 730 (1947) (“[T]he question of whether the particular work done, in the light of all the circumstances, is part of the integrated unit of production is decisive.”).

**II. The District Court Erred in Failing to Award Actual Damages for Appellees’ Violation of the AWPA’s Registration Provision.**

The AWPA requires farm labor contractors (FLCs) to register with the Department of Labor and maintain liability insurance. 29 U.S.C. §§ 1811, 1841. The Supreme Court has held that statutory violations may give rise to actual damages where the plaintiff demonstrates a causal connection between the violation and the harm suffered. *See Texas & Pac. Ry. v. Rigsby*, 241 U.S. 33, 39 (1916) (“[W]here a statute creates a liability and gives an action to enforce it, the action is not necessarily confined to the cases and the extent of liability recognized at common law.”).

Here, appellees’ failure to verify Luxama’s registration and insurance directly contributed to the tragic accident that injured and killed workers. The district court’s refusal to award actual damages for this violation was error. *See Rigsby*, 241 U.S. at 39 (holding that statutory damages are not exclusive where actual harm is proven).

**III. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Little Rock and Hall Did Not “Utilize” Luxama’s Services Under the AWPA.**

The AWPA imposes duties on entities that “utilize” the services of an FLC. 29 U.S.C. § 1842. The Supreme Court has broadly construed “utilize” to encompass any entity that benefits from or exercises control over the labor arrangement. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 548 (1940) (“[T]he meaning of a statute is to be derived from its context and the mischief it seeks to remedy.”).

Here, Little Rock and Hall contracted with appellees to share profits from the snap bean harvest and supplied the boxes and trailer used in the labor process. This arrangement clearly demonstrates that they “utilized” Luxama’s services. *See American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 548 (finding utilization where an entity derived benefit from the labor arrangement).

**IV. The District Court Erred in Limiting Appellants’ Recovery to Statutory Damages Under § 1854(c)(1).**

The AWPA permits recovery of actual damages where a violation proximately causes harm. *See Rigsby*, 241 U.S. at 39. The district court’s refusal to award actual damages for appellees’ registration violation was contrary to the statute’s remedial purpose. *See Texas & N.O.R. Co. v. Railway Employees’ Dept.*, 281 U.S. 548, 568 (1930) (“[S]tatutes enacted for the protection of employees are to be liberally construed to effectuate their remedial purposes.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
