**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt a nonspousal inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12) exempt only “retirement funds” held in a tax-exempt account. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That text requires more than a tax-favored wrapper. It requires funds set aside for retirement, and an inherited IRA is not such a fund in the hands of the debtor. Statutory language must be given its ordinary meaning in context. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress did not use the broader phrase “tax-exempt account”; it used “retirement funds,” which points to the account’s function, not merely its label.

That distinction controls here. Heidi Clark inherited her mother’s IRA, but as a nonspouse beneficiary she could make no additional contributions, could not roll the account over into her own IRA, and had to begin required distributions immediately. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 560–61 (7th Cir. 2013). In other words, the account could not be held until Heidi’s retirement; it had to be depleted long before then. A fund subject to mandatory present consumption is not a retirement fund. Courts do not construe exemptions by divorcing statutory words from the economic reality they describe. *Old Colony R. Co. v. Commissioner*, 284 U.S. 552, 560 (1932); *United States v. Pelzer*, 312 U.S. 399, 402–03 (1941).

The structure of the Bankruptcy Code confirms that Congress knows how to write an exemption keyed to the debtor’s status when it wants one. For example, the homestead exemption protects a debtor’s residence because of the debtor’s own use of the property. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). By contrast, § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not say “funds that were once someone’s retirement funds” or “accounts that remain tax deferred.” Courts must not add words Congress omitted. *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 61 (1930); *Sutherland v. United States*, 264 U.S. 87, 88–89 (1924). If Congress had wanted inherited IRAs included, it could have said so.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would collapse the statutory distinction between retirement savings and inherited wealth. The Court has repeatedly refused to stretch a statute’s coverage simply because the broader reading may better serve a general policy. *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 93 (1934); *Badaracco v. Commissioner*, 464 U.S. 386, 398 (1984). The Bankruptcy Code protects funds saved for retirement; it does not immunize assets that are available for current use merely because they originated in a retirement account. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562–63. As the Seventh Circuit explained, inherited IRAs are “an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings.” *Id.* at 564.

The tax rules governing inherited IRAs reinforce that conclusion. Congress specifically limits inherited IRAs by prohibiting new contributions and requiring distributions within a short period. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560–61. Those restrictions distinguish inherited IRAs from the owner’s own retirement account. Under established interpretive principles, courts consider the legal operation of the property, not merely its source or title. *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280, 287–88 (1946). Because an inherited IRA is not available to accumulate until the beneficiary’s retirement, it is not “retirement funds” within the meaning of § 522.

The cases favoring exemption are unpersuasive because they detach “retirement funds” from the debtor’s present use of the account. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561–64. That approach would make the exemption turn on whether funds once served a retirement purpose for someone else, even though bankruptcy exemptions ordinarily depend on the debtor’s actual interest when the petition is filed. *Sutherland v. United States*, 264 U.S. at 88–89; *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. at 61. The better rule—and the one the text compels—is that an inherited IRA, though tax deferred, is not a retirement fund in the debtor’s hands. The judgment should be affirmed.
