**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling serves a distinct and legitimate purpose: to provide employers with greater certainty before acting on their doubts. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). Yet the Board insists that an employer must already possess the same quantum of evidence required to withdraw recognition—a standard that renders polling largely superfluous. This approach is arbitrary and fails under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843-44 (1984), because it ignores the unique function of polling and the deference owed to reasonable employer interpretations of the Act. *See NLRB v. United Food & Commercial Workers Union, Local 23*, 485 U.S. 505, 513-14 (1988).

The Board’s position is further undermined by the fact that polling is less disruptive than an RM election and provides immediate, verifiable results. *See Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). The Board’s insistence on identical evidentiary thresholds for polling and withdrawal of recognition ignores this distinction and contravenes the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *See Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc. v. NLRB*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). This Court should reject the Board’s rigid standard and defer to the well-reasoned decisions of the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits, which permit polling upon a showing of “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.” *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1140, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984).

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board improperly discounted multiple statements by employees expressing dissatisfaction with the union, including remarks by Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack—all of whom were part of the bargaining unit at the time of the poll. *See NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (substantial evidence standard requires reviewing courts to uphold findings if supported by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate”). The Board also erred in disregarding Mike Ridgick’s and Dennis Marsh’s statements, which were probative of their—and by extension, their coworkers’—opposition to the union. *See Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB*, 882 F.2d 19, 22 (1st Cir. 1989) (employee dissatisfaction with union services does not equate to opposition to representation).

Moreover, the Board ignored Kermit Bloch’s and Ron Mohr’s reports that a majority of employees opposed the union. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements—without explanation—was arbitrary. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 490 (1951) (Board findings must be supported by “reasoning minds could reasonably conclude” that the evidence supported the finding). The record overwhelmingly demonstrates that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt, and the Board’s contrary conclusion is unsustainable. *See Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 786 (Board’s factual findings must be upheld only if supported by substantial evidence).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s polling standard is arbitrary and inconsistent with the Act, and its factual finding is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should grant the petition for review and vacate the Board’s order.
