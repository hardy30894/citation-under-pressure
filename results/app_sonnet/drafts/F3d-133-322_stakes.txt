ARGUMENT

**I. Mrs. Parker's Convictions Under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) Cannot Stand Because the Conduct on Which They Rest Was Not an "Official Act" Within Any Duty, Place of Trust, or Established Usage She Possessed.**

Section 201(b)(2)(C) reaches only acts done "in violation of the official duty of such official." 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). This Court has recognized that such duties need not be confined to written rules, because "duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery." United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). But Birdsall's "settled practice" doctrine presupposes an act the official could legitimately be called upon to perform as part of her office — not the wholesale fabrication of a federal judge's signature on a document she had no authority, express or customary, to issue.

Mrs. Parker's duties were limited to clerical support: assisting at hearings, taking notes, and typing and mailing the ALJ's decisions. No "settled practice" within the Office of Hearings and Appeals permitted a clerk to alter applicants' names on form letters, forge the ALJ's signature, and issue benefit determinations in his stead. That conduct was not a corrupted exercise of a duty she held; it was the impersonation of authority she never possessed. Birdsall's rationale — that bribery statutes must reach informally defined duties so that officials cannot escape liability by pointing to gaps in written job descriptions — does not extend to acts wholly outside any place of trust the official occupies. The Government's proof therefore established, at most, fraud and forgery, not the bribery of a "public official" acting "in such official's official capacity" or "place of trust or profit" as required by 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). This Court should hold that § 201(b)(2)(C) does not criminalize the purchase of a forged act that no employee of Mrs. Parker's grade could ever lawfully or customarily perform.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Sustain Mr. Parker's Conspiracy and Substantive Bribery Convictions.**

On review of sufficiency, the question is "whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). That standard is not satisfied by proof that a defendant was merely present when money changed hands. A conspiracy requires proof of a knowing and voluntary agreement to join the unlawful scheme; participation in a single transaction, without more, does not supply it. Direct Sales Co. v. United States, 319 U.S. 703, 711 (1943) (conspiracy requires that a party "in some sense promote their venture" and share the intent to further it); United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205, 210 (1940) (knowledge that goods will be put to unlawful use, without intent to further the unlawful enterprise, does not establish conspiracy). Likewise, this Court has cautioned that although the evidence must be viewed favorably to the verdict, "evidence connecting a particular defendant with the conspiracy" must still be substantial, not merely evidence of proximity or association. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942).

The record shows only that Mr. Parker was present at his wife's side when $500 was handed over at Simmons's home, an event captured on videotape. Nothing in that recording, nor in Mrs. Parker's confession, establishes that Mr. Parker knew the money was paid in exchange for the fraudulent approval and reinstatement of SSI benefits, that he understood the forged letters or the falsified signature, or that he agreed to further the broader scheme involving Henry, Lemon, Scott, and Johnson. Presence and receipt of currency, without proof of knowledge of the corrupt purpose behind it, is precisely the kind of evidence Falcone and Direct Sales hold insufficient to convict. Because the conspiracy count and three of the four substantive counts against Mr. Parker rest on nothing more than his presence at one transaction, his convictions cannot stand under Jackson.

**III. Alternatively, Mrs. Parker's Convictions on the Counts Involving Henry, Lemon, Scott, and Johnson Rest Impermissibly on Her Own Uncorroborated Confession.**

Even accepting Mrs. Parker's confession as voluntary, it cannot alone sustain convictions on counts for which the Government offered no independent corroboration. "It is agreed that in the federal jurisdiction a conviction must rest upon firmer ground than the uncorroborated admission or confession of the accused." Opper v. United States, 348 U.S. 84, 89 (1954); accord Smith v. United States, 348 U.S. 147, 152 (1954). While Simmons's testimony and the recorded and videotaped evidence independently corroborate the counts involving Kevin Simmons, the record contains no comparable independent proof — no testimony from Henry, Lemon, Scott, or Johnson, no recordings, and no financial records — corroborating Mrs. Parker's statement that she defrauded those four additional claimants. Absent such corroboration, Opper and Smith require reversal of the convictions resting solely on the confession's uncorroborated assertions.

For these reasons, Mrs. Parker's convictions under § 201(b)(2)(C) should be reversed, Mr. Parker's convictions should be reversed for insufficient evidence, and, in the alternative, the counts unsupported by independent corroboration should be vacated.
