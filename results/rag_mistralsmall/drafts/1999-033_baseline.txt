**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Recognizes a "Class-of-One" Theory of Discrimination Where a Plaintiff Alleges Arbitrary and Irrational Governmental Action Motivated by Ill Will or Malice.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause prohibits state action that is "invidious" or "arbitrary." *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886). This Court has long recognized that equal protection violations may occur even absent discrimination against a suspect class or a fundamental right, provided the plaintiff demonstrates that the government’s action was motivated by "a spiteful effort to 'get' [the plaintiff] for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 565 (2000) (quoting *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386, 390 (7th Cir. 1998)). This "class-of-one" theory is firmly rooted in equal protection jurisprudence, as it targets governmental actions that are "irrational" or "maliciously motivated." *Id.* at 564–65 (citing *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446–47 (1985)).

The Seventh Circuit correctly applied this principle in *Olech*, holding that a plaintiff need not allege membership in a protected class to state an equal protection claim where the government’s action was "motivated solely by a spiteful effort to 'get' [the plaintiff] for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Olech*, 160 F.3d at 390. This Court has repeatedly affirmed that equal protection encompasses claims of arbitrary or malicious governmental conduct, even where no traditional class-based discrimination is alleged. *See, e.g., Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 632 (1996) (invalidating a law motivated by "animus" toward a group); *U.S. Dept. of Agric. v. Moreno*, 413 U.S. 528, 534 (1973) (striking down a law "invidiously discriminatory" against a group of individuals).

The Village’s argument—that a plaintiff must allege membership in a protected class—misapprehends the scope of equal protection. The Clause prohibits *all* arbitrary state action, not merely discrimination against defined groups. *See Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923) ("[T]he inhibition of the amendment, if it covers any case not embraced within the guaranty of due process, is directed against class legislation which is unreasonable and arbitrary."). The "class-of-one" theory is thus a valid mechanism for challenging governmental actions that, while facially neutral, are applied with malicious intent or irrationality. *See Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564–65.

**II. The Village’s Demand for an 18-Foot Easement, Unlike Its Treatment of Similarly Situated Property Owners, Violates the Equal Protection Clause Under the "Class-of-One" Theory.**

Olech has alleged that the Village conditioned her water connection on an 18-foot easement while imposing only a 15-foot easement on similarly situated property owners. This differential treatment, if proven, constitutes an arbitrary and irrational application of municipal policy, precisely the type of conduct the Equal Protection Clause forbids. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74 (invalidating a facially neutral law applied discriminatorily); *see also Sioux City Bridge*, 260 U.S. at 445 (prohibiting "class legislation which is unreasonable and arbitrary").

The Village’s reliance on *City of Joplin v. Southwest Missouri Light Co.*, 191 U.S. 150 (1903), is misplaced. That case involved a contractual dispute over municipal franchises, not an equal protection challenge to arbitrary governmental action. *Id.* at 159–60. By contrast, Olech’s claim targets the Village’s *malicious* or *irrational* enforcement of its easement policy—a quintessential equal protection violation under the "class-of-one" theory. *See Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564–65.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Holding Comports with This Court’s Precedent Recognizing Equal Protection Claims Outside Traditional Class-Based Discrimination.**

This Court has never required plaintiffs to allege membership in a protected class to state an equal protection claim where the government’s action is arbitrary or motivated by malice. *See, e.g., Romer*, 517 U.S. at 632 (invalidating a law motivated by animus); *Moreno*, 413 U.S. at 534 (striking down invidious discrimination). The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Olech* aligns with this principle, recognizing that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits *all* arbitrary state action, regardless of whether it targets a defined class. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564–65.

The Village’s argument—that equal protection claims require class-based discrimination—would eviscerate the Clause’s protections. The Framers intended the Equal Protection Clause to guard against *all* arbitrary governmental conduct, not merely discrimination against suspect classes. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74. The Seventh Circuit’s "class-of-one" theory is a necessary extension of this principle, ensuring that maliciously motivated or irrational state action does not escape constitutional scrutiny.

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the Equal Protection Clause permits a "class-of-one" theory of discrimination where a plaintiff alleges arbitrary or malicious governmental action. The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement, unlike its treatment of similarly situated property owners, violates this principle. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
