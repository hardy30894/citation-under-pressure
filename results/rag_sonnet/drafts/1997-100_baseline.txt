# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE RETRIAL ON THE PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN THIS NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against "a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Enhancement proceedings under California's Three Strikes law do not implicate this protection because they do not adjudicate guilt of a separate "offense"; they merely inform the sentencing court's discretion regarding punishment for the underlying substantive crime. This Court's precedents confirm that sentencing determinations, including findings on recidivism, fall outside the Clause's protective ambit except in the narrow, sui generis context of capital sentencing.

### A. This Court's Recidivism-Enhancement Jurisprudence Confirms That Double Jeopardy Protections Do Not Attach to Ordinary Sentencing Determinations

In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), this Court confronted a materially identical scenario: a Missouri persistent-offender enhancement was vacated for insufficient proof, and the state sought to retry the enhancement allegation. This Court expressly recognized that "*Both Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.*" *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392. That observation was not incidental; it reflects this Court's consistent understanding that the capital-sentencing double jeopardy doctrine established in *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), rests on features unique to capital proceedings—namely, that such proceedings are, "*in all relevant respects*," "*like the immediately preceding trial on the issue of guilt or innocence*." *Sorola v. Texas*, 493 U.S. 1005, 1006 (1989) (Marshall, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (quoting *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438).

Noncapital sentencing enhancement proceedings possess none of these trial-like hallmarks. They do not determine "guilt or innocence" of a new offense; they merely establish a sentencing fact—the existence of a prior conviction—that triggers a statutorily prescribed sentencing range for the *already-adjudicated* substantive offense. The Missouri Court of Appeals' reasoning in the very case this Court reviewed in *Caspari* captures the point precisely: "*The question of double jeopardy was not involved because those provisions of the Fifth Amendment have been held not to apply to sentencing.*" *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 388 (quoting *State v. Bohlen*, 698 S.W.2d 577, 578 (Mo. App. 1985)).

### B. This Court's General Sentencing Jurisprudence Forecloses Extension of Double Jeopardy Protection to Noncapital Recidivism Findings

This Court's broader sentencing jurisprudence reinforces that ordinary sentencing proceedings lack the "finality" necessary to trigger double jeopardy concerns. In *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985), this Court rejected the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's double jeopardy-based limitation on resentencing, explaining that such reasoning is "*inconsistent with the rationale of the holding of this Court in [United States v.] DiFrancesco*," which established that "*a sentence [in a noncapital case] does not have the qualities of constitutional finality that attend an acquittal.*" *Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. at 30. If a final sentence itself lacks the finality necessary for double jeopardy protection, a fortiori a factual predicate underlying that sentence—here, the prior-conviction allegation—cannot possess greater constitutional weight.

*Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33 (1988), confirms this principle in the specific context of habitual-offender enhancements. There, this Court held that when a reviewing court vacates a habitual-offender sentence for evidentiary insufficiency, the State may retry the enhancement so long as the evidence actually admitted, "*whether erroneously or not*," "*would have been sufficient to sustain*" the enhancement. *Lockhart*, 488 U.S. at 34. This holding presupposes that no double jeopardy bar attaches to retrial of habitual-offender status determinations in the first instance; the Court's entire analysis proceeds on the understanding that such retrials are permissible absent the narrow trial-error/evidentiary-insufficiency distinction addressed in that case.

### C. Petitioner's Position Improperly Imports Capital-Sentencing Doctrine Into a Fundamentally Different Context

Respondent's reliance on capital sentencing analogies is misplaced. In the capital context, the sentencer's choice between life and death mirrors the guilt-determination process because the sentencing body must find specific aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, and a finding against death functions as an "acquittal" of the death penalty. *See Freddie Davis v. Georgia*, 459 U.S. 891 (1982) (Marshall, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (describing the "*analog[y]*" between capital aggravating-circumstance findings and "*the prosecution's effort to prove the crimes charged at the guilt-innocence phase*"). No such analogy exists here. The prior-conviction determination under California's Three Strikes law does not resolve any element of Monge's underlying drug offenses; it is a threshold sentencing fact—akin to a persistent-offender finding—that merely calibrates the sentencing range already established by the jury's guilty verdict.

Moreover, extending trial-like procedural protections is not indefinitely elastic. As this Court has emphasized, sentencing courts have historically enjoyed considerable procedural flexibility, and due process does not require freezing "*the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.*" *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949). California's voluntary provision of statutory procedural safeguards—a jury trial right, proof beyond a reasonable doubt—does not transform the enhancement proceeding into a "trial on the issue of guilt or innocence" for double jeopardy purposes. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be affirmed.
