ARGUMENT

Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. Criminal statutes incorporate the common-law mens rea requirement. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). "A statute which . . . forbids the doing of an act" without regard to intent "is inconsistent with our philosophy of criminal law." Id. at 250. The statute must therefore be read to demand subjective intent.

The First Amendment independently requires the same showing. Speech may be punished as a threat only when the speaker intends to produce imminent lawless action. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). "The question . . . is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger." Id. at 52. An objective standard would criminalize protected advocacy. Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925); Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 372 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring).

Punishment of speech without regard to the speaker's purpose chills expression the Amendment safeguards. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931); Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931). "The safeguarding of free speech . . . demands that the state show a clear and present danger" arising from the speaker's intended meaning. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 311 (1940). An objective test fails this requirement. Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941).

Even "fighting words" require that the speaker intend to provoke violence. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). The same principle governs threats under § 875(c). Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 503 (1951). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand.
