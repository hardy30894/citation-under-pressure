**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s ADA and MHRA Claims Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled.**

The district court held that Epps failed to establish that Pine Lawn *regarded* him as disabled under the ADA and MHRA. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C); Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010(17). This conclusion was erroneous.

Under the ADA, an individual is "regarded as" disabled if he establishes that the employer "believes that [he] has a physical impairment that substantially limits one or more of the major life activities of such individual." *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). The MHRA employs an identical standard. *See* *Hilton v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 143 S.W.3d 77, 81 (Mo. Ct. App. 2004). To prevail on a "regarded as" claim, the plaintiff must show that the employer’s belief was both *mistaken* and *based on an impairment that, if real, would substantially limit a major life activity*. *See id.* (citing *Murphy v. United Parcel Serv., Inc.*, 527 U.S. 516, 521 (1999)).

Here, Chief Hardy’s memoranda demonstrate that Pine Lawn terminated Epps based on its belief that his degenerative disk disease rendered him unable to perform the essential functions of a police officer. *See* Hardy’s Sept. 29, 1999, and Mar. 17, 2000, memoranda. However, Epps presented evidence that he was capable of performing light-duty assignments, such as desk work, which Pine Lawn failed to offer. *See* *Taylor v. Principal Fin. Group, Inc.*, 93 F.3d 155, 165 (5th Cir. 1996) (holding that an employer’s failure to consider reassignment to a vacant position may support a "regarded as" claim). Moreover, Dr. Mirkin’s initial assessment did not preclude Epps from performing *some* duties, and Pine Lawn’s own workers’ compensation carrier later approved benefits for Epps’s injuries. *See* *EEOC v. Rockwell Int’l Corp.*, 243 F.3d 1012, 1018 (7th Cir. 2001) (finding that an employer’s reliance on medical opinions without further inquiry may support a "regarded as" claim).

Thus, a reasonable jury could conclude that Pine Lawn’s belief that Epps was disabled was mistaken and that its decision to terminate him was based on an impairment that, if real, would not necessarily preclude him from performing major life activities. *See* *Hilton*, 143 S.W.3d at 81.

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s Workers’ Compensation Retaliation Claim Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Pine Lawn’s Proffered Reason for Termination Was Pretextual.**

Missouri’s workers’ compensation retaliation statute, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780, prohibits employers from discharging employees in retaliation for filing workers’ compensation claims. To establish a prima facie case, Epps must show that (1) he filed a workers’ compensation claim; (2) he was discharged; and (3) there was a causal connection between the two. *See* *Johnson v. Mo. Dep’t of Soc. Servs.*, 968 S.W.2d 287, 290 (Mo. Ct. App. 1998). Once the employer articulates a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for the discharge, the burden shifts to the employee to show that the proffered reason was pretextual. *See id.*

Here, Pine Lawn proffered two reasons for Epps’s termination: (1) his extensive absences and workers’ compensation claims; and (2) Dr. Mirkin’s opinion that Epps could not perform the duties of a patrol officer. However, Epps presented evidence that Pine Lawn failed to consider light-duty assignments and that Chief Hardy’s memoranda reflected animus toward Epps’s workers’ compensation claims. *See* *Holloway v. Potter*, 220 F.3d 1161, 1166 (8th Cir. 2000) (holding that temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action may support an inference of retaliation). A reasonable jury could conclude that Pine Lawn’s reasons were pretextual and that the termination was retaliatory.

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress Claim Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Pine Lawn’s Conduct Was Extreme and Outrageous.**

To prevail on an intentional infliction of emotional distress claim under Missouri law, Epps must show that Pine Lawn’s conduct was (1) intentional or reckless; (2) extreme and outrageous; and (3) caused severe emotional distress. *See* *Farrow v. St. Francis Med. Ctr.*, 41 S.W.3d 588, 596 (Mo. Ct. App. 2001). The district court held that sovereign immunity barred this claim, but Missouri’s sovereign immunity statute does not immunize municipalities from liability for intentional torts. *See* Mo. Rev. Stat. § 537.600; *Farrow*, 41 S.W.3d at 596.

Here, Epps presented evidence that Pine Lawn terminated him without considering reasonable accommodations and in retaliation for his workers’ compensation claims. A reasonable jury could conclude that this conduct was extreme and outrageous. *See* *Farrow*, 41 S.W.3d at 596.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s summary judgment should be reversed, and this matter should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
