**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by certifying a class under Rule 23(b)(3) without requiring that petitioners’ proposed damages model be tethered to the theory of antitrust liability—a model that failed to isolate damages attributable to the alleged anticompetitive conduct from other lawful market factors. The Third Circuit’s affirmance, deferring under a “clearly erroneous” standard to factual findings that lacked evidentiary support, compounded this error. This Court should reverse.

**I. Rule 23(b)(3) Requires a Rigorous Analysis of Whether Damages Can Be Measured Class-Wide Through Common Evidence**

Rule 23(b)(3) demands that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” This requirement extends to the element of damages. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 354-355 (2011). A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of whether the proffered evidence establishes that damages are susceptible to class-wide proof. *Id.* at 350-351. The court may not simply accept an expert’s say-so; it must resolve factual disputes relevant to certification and determine whether the evidence is “adequate” to show predominance. *General Telephone Co. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982).

Here, the district court failed that duty. It certified the class based on the theory that Comcast’s clustering deterred “overbuilder” entry, causing supra-competitive prices. Yet the damages model advanced by petitioners’ expert, Dr. James McClave, measured the price effects of *all* clustering—including lawful, pro-competitive swaps and acquisitions—not just the anticompetitive conduct that allegedly deterred overbuilders. The model thus conflated damages from the single theory of antitrust impact with damages from other, unchallenged conduct. That is not “common evidence” capable of proving class-wide damages; it is evidence that, if accepted, would overcompensate class members for injuries not caused by the alleged violation. *See, e.g., Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 35-36 (2013) (holding same in this very case).

The district court nevertheless found that McClave’s model provided a “common methodology” to measure damages class-wide. But this finding rested on no more than McClave’s *ipse dixit* that the model captured only antitrust injury. No admissible evidence—no regression isolating the overbuilder-deterrence effect, no comparison to non-clustered markets, no analysis of causation—supported the attribution of all price increases to the challenged conduct. A district court cannot “rigorously analyze” predominance by deferring to an expert’s unsupported conclusion. *See Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 366 (expert opinion “may be inadmissible” if not grounded in reliable methodology).

**II. The Third Circuit Applied an Impermissibly Deferential Standard of Review**

The Third Circuit reviewed the district court’s certification order for abuse of discretion, stating that factual findings could be overturned only if “clearly erroneous.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 655 F.3d 182, 193 (3d Cir. 2011). But the district court’s error was legal, not factual: it applied an incorrect standard by failing to require that the damages model correspond to the liability theory. This Court reviews de novo whether a district court “applied an incorrect legal standard.” *Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 312 (3d Cir. 2008) (cited approvingly in *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351). The Third Circuit should have reversed on that basis alone.

Moreover, even under clear-error review, the district court’s finding that the damages model measured class-wide injury was “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support.” *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972). McClave’s own testimony conceded that his model did not isolate the effect of overbuilder deterrence from other clustering effects. When a finding “bears no rational relationship to the supportive evidentiary data,” reversal is required. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-574 (1985). The Third Circuit’s refusal to upset this finding was itself an abuse of discretion.

**III. Permitting Class Certification Without Grounding the Damages Model in the Liability Theory Undermines Rule 23 and Antitrust Policy**

Rule 23(b)(3) exists to ensure that class actions are fair and efficient. Predominance is not satisfied when individual issues of causation and damages would overwhelm common ones. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 359-360. Here, because the damages model did not match the liability theory, each class member would need individualized proof of whether his or her higher prices resulted from the anticompetitive conduct. That is precisely the sort of individualized inquiry Rule 23(b)(3) forbids.

Moreover, allowing certification on such a flawed evidentiary basis invites meritless antitrust strike suits. If class plaintiffs can propose a damages model that charges defendants for all market conduct, lawful or not, and then bypass rigorous scrutiny at certification, the class-action device becomes a weapon for extracting settlements—not a tool for redressing genuine antitrust injury. This Court has long cautioned that Rule 23’s requirements must be “applied through reasoned analysis,” not rubber-stamped. *Falcon*, 457 U.S. at 161.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court certified a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured class-wide, and the Third Circuit erred in affirming that decision. The judgment below should be reversed.
