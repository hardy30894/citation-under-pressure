The equal footing doctrine vests a new state with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of its admission to the Union. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845); Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). Title therefore turns on the river's condition "at the time of the admission of the state." United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926). The constitutional test is satisfied only when the river "was used, or was susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce" at that historic moment. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870).

This Court has consistently required proof of navigability on the date of statehood rather than at any later time. Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). Evidence of present-day use or improvements made after statehood is irrelevant unless it demonstrates that the river was already navigable in its natural state when the state entered the Union. Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 118 (1921); United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co., 174 U.S. 690, 698 (1899). Segments must be examined individually; a finding that one reach was navigable does not establish title to another. Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. at 585.

The same rule governs the beds of non-tidal rivers. Barney v. Keokuk, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876). Because the inquiry is historical, the trial court must determine navigability as of the precise date Montana became a state. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. at 55. Any other approach would sever the constitutional link between equal footing and the beds that passed to the state upon admission. Pollard's Lessee, 44 U.S. at 223.
