## ARGUMENT

The Massachusetts buffer zone is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. It is content-neutral, serves a significant governmental interest, is narrowly tailored, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law under settled First Amendment principles that predate *Hill v. Colorado*.

### I. The Law Is Content-Neutral

The statute applies to all persons equally, regardless of viewpoint. It prohibits *anyone* from knowingly entering or remaining within a 35-foot zone adjacent to a reproductive health care facility, subject to specific exemptions for patients, employees, emergency personnel, and passersby. This neutrality satisfies the requirement that a regulation “be justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.” *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575 (1941) (upholding parade permit requirement that applied neutrally to all participants). Unlike the licensing scheme struck down in *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 305–07 (1940), which vested officials with discretion to deny permits based on religious content, the Massachusetts law contains no discretionary element. It is a fixed, geographic restriction that does not target any particular message.

### II. The Law Serves a Significant Governmental Interest

The Commonwealth has a compelling interest in ensuring safe and unimpeded access to medical facilities. This interest lies at the core of the State’s police power to protect public health and safety. *Jacobson v. Massachusetts*, 197 U.S. 11, 25 (1905) (upholding compulsory vaccination as a valid exercise of police power to protect public health). The buffer zone prevents physical obstruction, harassment, and congestion that can impede patients and staff from entering clinics. The State’s authority to regulate the use of public streets and sidewalks to promote safety and orderly movement has long been recognized. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515–16 (1939) (recognizing that streets and parks are public forums but subject to reasonable regulation). The buffer zone is a modest measure to protect the “right of the state to take reasonable steps to ensure the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities,” as the First Circuit noted.

### III. The Law Is Narrowly Tailored

The 35-foot zone is the minimum necessary to achieve the State’s interest. It is far smaller than the 100-foot zone at issue in *Hill v. Colorado*, and it applies only to entrances, exits, and driveways—not to entire sidewalks. The law does not ban speech; it merely relocates it a short distance. This is analogous to the sound-truck regulation upheld in *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 87–89 (1949), which prohibited “loud and raucous” noise but allowed other forms of amplification. The Court there held that a restriction on the *manner* of speech is permissible if it does not “unduly interfere with the normal use of the public streets for purposes of travel.” *Id.* at 87. Here, the buffer zone leaves the vast majority of the sidewalk open for speech and other activities. The State’s interest in preventing obstruction and ensuring access is at least as substantial as the interest in tranquility that supported the regulation in *Kovacs*.

### IV. Ample Alternative Channels Remain

Petitioners can still engage in all forms of expressive activity from outside the buffer zone. They may speak, pray, display signs, distribute literature, and use amplified sound. The record shows that petitioners have successfully counseled women and persuaded them to forgo abortions, even after the buffer zones were implemented. The Constitution does not guarantee the most effective means of communication, only that “adequate” alternatives exist. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939) (striking down a ban on handbilling but noting that the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of expression so long as alternative channels remain). Here, petitioners can still approach patients at conversational distance outside the 35-foot zone, and they can use signs and literature to convey their message. The law does not silence them; it merely moves them back a few steps.

### V. The Law Is Not Vague or Overbroad

The statute clearly defines the prohibited area and lists four specific exemptions. This provides fair notice of what conduct is forbidden. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 573–74 (1942) (upholding a statute against “fighting words” because it gave “sufficiently definite warning as to the proscribed conduct”). The Attorney General’s interpretive letter does not alter the plain text of the law. The law is not overbroad because it does not sweep in substantial protected speech; it only restricts entry into a narrow zone immediately adjacent to clinic entrances.

### VI. *Hill v. Colorado* Is Consistent with These Principles

Although *Hill* was decided after 1970, its reasoning aligns with the pre-1970 cases cited above. The Court in *Hill* upheld a similar buffer zone based on the same traditional time, place, and manner analysis. Because the Massachusetts law is constitutional under longstanding First Amendment doctrine, there is no need to limit or overrule *Hill*. The First Circuit correctly applied settled law.

### Conclusion

The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
