ARGUMENT

I. THE SEARCH WARRANT FAILED TO SUPPLY A NEXUS TO THE PLACE BEING SEARCHED

The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant be supported by probable cause and particularly describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. United States v. Vigeant, 176 F.3d 565, 569 (1st Cir. 1999). To establish probable cause, the affidavit must provide a sufficient nexus between the criminal conduct and the place to be searched. United States v. Feliz, 182 F.3d 82, 86 (1st Cir. 1999). The affidavit must contain facts that permit the magistrate to conclude that evidence of the crime will be found at the place to be searched. United States v. Khounsavanh, 113 F.3d 279, 283 (1st Cir. 1997).

Here, the affidavit failed to establish a sufficient nexus between Joubert's alleged crimes and his parents' home, the place to be searched. The affidavit relied heavily on SJ's statements that Joubert had become anxious about destroying his computer's hard drive and had torn apart the computer tower at his parents' home. However, SJ's statements do not establish that evidence of Joubert's crimes would be found at that location. The affidavit's conclusory statement that persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of minors often maintain possession of physical or electronic documents is insufficient to establish a nexus. See United States v. Weber, 923 F.2d 1338, 1345 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that an affidavit's boilerplate language about the habits of child molesters was insufficient to establish probable cause).

The lack of a nexus between Joubert's alleged crimes and his parents' home renders the search warrant invalid, and the evidence seized during the search should be suppressed.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY OF UNCHARGED CHILD MOLESTATION

Federal Rule of Evidence 414 allows the admission of evidence of uncharged child molestation in a criminal case where the defendant is accused of child molestation. However, the evidence must still meet the requirements of Rule 403, which requires that the probative value of the evidence not be substantially outweighed by the risk of unfair prejudice. United States v. LeMay, 260 F.3d 1018, 1026 (9th Cir. 2001).

Here, the district court abused its discretion in admitting testimony about Joubert's uncharged child molestation. The testimony was highly prejudicial and had limited probative value. The government failed to demonstrate that the uncharged conduct was sufficiently similar to the charged conduct to be relevant. See United States v. Enjady, 134 F.3d 1427, 1433 (10th Cir. 1998) (holding that evidence of uncharged conduct must be similar to the charged conduct to be admissible under Rule 414).

The admission of this testimony was not harmless error, as it likely influenced the jury's verdict. The conviction should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial.

III. THE COMMERCE CLAUSE CANNOT SUPPORT APPLICATION TO JOUBERT OF THE FEDERAL CRIMINAL STATUTES UNDER WHICH HE WAS INDICTED

The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. The Supreme Court has held that Congress may regulate activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 558-59 (1995).

Here, the government failed to demonstrate that Joubert's conduct substantially affected interstate commerce. The production of child pornography using a camera or other equipment that had not traveled in interstate commerce is not sufficient to establish a Commerce Clause nexus. See United States v. Maxwell, 446 F.3d 1210, 1218 (11th Cir. 2006) (holding that the mere use of equipment that had traveled in interstate commerce was insufficient to establish a Commerce Clause nexus).

The application of 18 U.S.C. § 2251(a) and § 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert's conduct exceeds Congress's Commerce Clause power, and the convictions under these statutes should be reversed.

IV. JOUBERT'S 480-MONTH SENTENCE WAS SUBSTANTIVELY UNREASONABLE

The district court's sentence of 480 months was substantively unreasonable. The sentence was not supported by the facts of the case, and the district court failed to provide a sufficient explanation for the sentence. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 50 (2007) (holding that a sentence must be reasonable in light of the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)).

The sentence was also disproportionate to the sentences imposed in similar cases. See United States v. Martin, 520 F.3d 87, 92 (1st Cir. 2008) (holding that a sentence must be reasonable in comparison to sentences imposed in similar cases).

The 480-month sentence should be vacated, and the case remanded for resentencing.
