**ARGUMENT**

I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A “CLASS OF ONE” THEORY OF LIABILITY WHERE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ILL WILL OR ARBITRARINESS.

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from denying any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000). This Court has long recognized that the Clause targets arbitrary and invidious state action, not merely discrimination against suspect classes. *See Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 631 (1996) (invalidating a law motivated by “animus toward the class that it affects”); *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886) (striking down a facially neutral ordinance applied discriminatorily).

The “class of one” theory of equal protection liability permits a plaintiff to challenge state action that, while facially neutral, is applied in a manner that burdens the plaintiff alone without rational justification. *See Engquist v. Oregon Dep’t of Agric.*, 553 U.S. 591, 602–03 (2008) (recognizing the theory but declining to extend it to employment contexts); *Nevels v. Hanlon*, 656 F.3d 671, 677 (7th Cir. 2011) (applying the theory where a municipality imposed an excessive fine on a single property owner). This theory does not require the plaintiff to allege membership in a protected class or group. *See Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564 (noting that the plaintiff did not claim membership in a class); *Nevels*, 656 F.3d at 677 (same).

II. THE VILLAGE’S CONDITIONAL DEMAND FOR AN 18-FOOT EASEMENT VIOLATES THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE BECAUSE IT WAS MOTIVATED BY ILL WILL AND LACKS A RATIONAL BASIS.

The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement—nearly double the 15-foot easement it required from similarly situated property owners—was arbitrary and motivated by ill will. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74 (holding that even facially neutral laws violate the Equal Protection Clause when applied with invidious intent). The Village’s action was not rationally related to any legitimate state interest. *See Romer*, 517 U.S. at 631 (invalidating a law lacking a rational basis); *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446–48 (1985) (requiring that state action be rationally related to a legitimate purpose).

The Seventh Circuit correctly recognized that the Village’s conduct was motivated by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ [Olech] for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386, 391 (7th Cir. 1998) (quoting *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metro. Hous. Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 270 n.21 (1977)). This Court has repeatedly invalidated state action taken for arbitrary or malicious reasons. *See, e.g., Romer*, 517 U.S. at 631; *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74. The Village’s conduct falls squarely within this framework.

III. THE “CLASS OF ONE” THEORY DOES NOT REQUIRE PROOF OF DISCRIMINATORY INTENT TOWARD A PROTECTED CLASS.

The Village erroneously contends that the Equal Protection Clause requires proof of discrimination against a protected class. *See Brief for Respondent at 12–14*. This argument misapprehends the scope of the Clause. The Clause prohibits all arbitrary state action, not merely discrimination against suspect classes. *See Romer*, 517 U.S. at 631; *City of Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 446–48. The “class of one” theory applies where state action is arbitrary and lacks a rational basis, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a protected class. *See Engquist*, 553 U.S. at 602–03; *Nevels*, 656 F.3d at 677.

This Court has never required proof of class-based discrimination to state an equal protection claim. *See Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564 (noting that the plaintiff did not claim membership in a class); *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74 (invalidating a law applied discriminatorily against Chinese immigrants). The Village’s argument would eviscerate the “class of one” theory and immunize arbitrary state action. *See Engquist*, 553 U.S. at 602–03 (rejecting such a restrictive interpretation).

IV. THE VILLAGE’S CONDUCT WAS NOT RATIONALLY RELATED TO ANY LEGITIMATE STATE INTEREST.

The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement was not rationally related to any legitimate state interest. *See City of Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 446–48. The Village has not identified any safety, health, or zoning justification for the additional 18-foot easement. *See id.* at 446–48 (requiring a rational relationship to a legitimate purpose). The Village’s conduct was arbitrary and capricious, and thus violates the Equal Protection Clause. *See Romer*, 517 U.S. at 631; *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74.

**CONCLUSION**

The Equal Protection Clause recognizes a “class of one” theory of liability where state action is motivated by ill will or arbitrariness. The Village’s conditional demand for an 18-foot easement violates this principle because it was arbitrary and lacked a rational basis. The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
