**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Circuit Correctly Upheld the Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Law as a Content-Neutral, Narrowly Tailored Time, Place, and Manner Regulation Consistent with the First Amendment.**

The Massachusetts law at issue here—Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½—imposes a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinic entrances, exits, and driveways. As the First Circuit correctly held, this law is a permissible content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation under *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989), and *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). The statute does not target speech based on viewpoint or subject matter, but rather regulates the *location* of speech to protect public safety and access to medical care. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791 ("[E]ven in a public forum the government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, or manner of protected speech, provided the restrictions are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.").

The First Circuit properly applied *Ward*’s three-part test: (1) the law is content-neutral; (2) it is narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest; and (3) it leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The law’s neutrality is evident because it applies equally to all speakers, regardless of their message. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-77 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014). The government’s interest in protecting patients and clinic staff from harassment, obstruction, and violence is undeniably significant. *See Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994) (upholding 36-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances as serving compelling state interests in public safety and medical access).

The law is narrowly tailored because it does not ban speech entirely but merely restricts it to a limited area. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 722-23 (upholding 100-foot buffer zone as narrowly tailored despite leaving 88 feet of public sidewalk open for speech). The 35-foot buffer here is even smaller and leaves ample space for petitioners to communicate—through signs, spoken words, and literature—at a respectful distance from clinic entrances. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82.

Finally, the law leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. Petitioners can still speak, hand out literature, and pray outside the buffer zone. *See id.* at 181. The First Circuit correctly found that the law does not prevent petitioners from engaging in effective advocacy, as they can still reach clinic patrons before or after they enter the buffer zone. *See id.* at 181-82.

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled.**

Petitioners argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled, but this Court should reject that invitation. *Hill* upheld a Colorado law prohibiting certain speech within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances, finding it a permissible time, place, and manner regulation. 530 U.S. at 722-23. The Massachusetts law is materially indistinguishable: it regulates speech in a public forum to prevent harassment and obstruction while leaving open ample alternatives for communication. *See id.* at 715-16 (recognizing sidewalks as public forums but upholding buffer zone as reasonable regulation).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is misplaced. Those cases involved content-based restrictions on speech, whereas the Massachusetts law is content-neutral. *See Sorrell*, 564 U.S. at 566-67 (striking down law targeting specific speakers based on content); *Citizens United*, 558 U.S. at 324 (invalidating law restricting corporate political speech). The First Amendment tolerates content-neutral time, place, and manner restrictions even when they incidentally burden speech. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

**III. The First Circuit Properly Rejected Petitioners’ As-Applied Challenges.**

The First Circuit correctly held that the law is constitutional as applied to the three clinics at issue. Petitioners failed to show that the buffer zone prevents them from effectively communicating their message. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen III)*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213-25 (D. Mass. 2012). They can still speak, hand out literature, and pray outside the buffer zone, and many patients pass through areas where speech is permitted. *See id.* at 213-15.

Petitioners’ claim that the law is unconstitutional as applied because it prevents "close personal contact" is unavailing. The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to intrude upon private property or harass individuals seeking medical care. *See Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 767-68. The buffer zone simply ensures that clinic entrances remain accessible and safe for patients and staff.

**IV. The First Circuit Did Not Err in Applying the Law of the Case Doctrine.**

The First Circuit properly applied the law of the case doctrine to preclude relitigation of the facial constitutionality of the law. *See United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). Petitioners’ attempt to revisit *McCullen I* fails because they did not demonstrate a dramatic change in controlling legal authority or a miscarriage of justice. *See id.* at 14. Their reliance on *Sorrell* and *Citizens United* is unpersuasive because those cases involved content-based restrictions, not the content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation at issue here.

**Conclusion**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer-zone law as a permissible content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation consistent with the First Amendment. *Hill* controls, and petitioners’ arguments for limiting or overruling it should be rejected. The law is narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests in public safety and medical access, and it leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. The judgment below should be affirmed.
