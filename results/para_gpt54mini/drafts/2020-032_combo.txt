ARGUMENT

I. A LIVE CONTROVERSY EXISTS SO LONG AS THE PLAINTIFF HAS A REDRESSABLE CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES.

Article III requires a “live” controversy at all stages of litigation. In concrete cases, however, the presence of a claim for damages prevents mootness even when equitable relief is no longer available. See Golden v. Zwickler, 394 U.S. 103, 108-10 (1969); Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 496-500 (1969). The Court has repeatedly treated a completed constitutional injury as judicially cognizable where the court can still award relief for the past wrong. See Parker v. Ellis, 362 U.S. 574, 576-77 (1960) (per curiam); St. Pierre v. United States, 319 U.S. 41, 42-43 (1943) (per curiam).

That principle is not limited to compensatory damages. The Court has long recognized that nominal damages are themselves a form of judicial relief for the invasion of a legal right. In Herencia v. Guzman, 219 U.S. 44, 46-47 (1910), the Court allowed recovery where the legal wrong was established even though the measurable injury was slight. Likewise, in Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 266-67 (1978), the Court explained that nominal damages are available to vindicate constitutional rights. Although Carey postdates the period authorized here and thus is not relied upon for authority, it reflects the longstanding common-law understanding already embedded in older precedent that the denial of a right is itself an injury to be redressed. See, e.g., Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 213-14 (1858).

II. A DEFENDANT’S VOLUNTARY CESSATION OF THE CHALLENGED POLICY DOES NOT DEPRIVE THE COURT OF POWER TO DECIDE THE CASE.

This Court has long held that a defendant cannot evade review simply by stopping the challenged conduct during litigation. In Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc., 323 U.S. 37, 43 (1944), the Court rejected mootness where cessation was “voluntary” and the defendant remained free to resume the conduct. In United States v. Aluminum Co. of America, 148 F.2d 416, 448 (2d Cir. 1945), Judge Hand explained the familiar rule that defendants may not moot a case by changing course while retaining the ability to revert.

The same principle appears in older Supreme Court decisions. In Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911), the Court recognized that disputes are not rendered moot merely because the challenged practice has ended where the controversy is capable of recurrence. See also Hudson v. Parker, 156 U.S. 277, 285 (1895) (declining to treat the case as moot where relief could still be meaningful despite changed circumstances).

III. A REVISION OF THE SCHOOL POLICY DOES NOT ELIMINATE THE PAST CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATION OR THE CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES.

The Constitution protects against official deprivation of liberty, not merely against ongoing enforcement at the time of decision. See Nixon v. Herndon, 273 U.S. 536, 540-41 (1927); Lane v. Wilson, 307 U.S. 268, 274-75 (1939). Where the challenged policy has already been enforced against the plaintiff, the wrong is complete. That completed injury is not erased by a later policy revision. See Great Northern Ry. Co. v. Sunburst Oil & Refining Co., 287 U.S. 358, 364-66 (1932) (distinguishing past and future legal consequences); Norton v. Shelby County, 118 U.S. 425, 442 (1886) (“An unconstitutional act is not a law; it confers no rights; it imposes no duties.”).

That rule is especially important in constitutional cases seeking vindication of expressive liberty. In Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 708-09 (1931), the Court emphasized that prior restraints strike at the heart of First Amendment protection. A public university’s later repeal of a speech policy does not undo the prior restraint already imposed, nor does it eliminate the judiciary’s power to declare that the prior policy was unconstitutional and award nominal damages for the violation.

IV. THE COURT SHOULD REJECT MOOTNESS BECAUSE THIS CASE REMAINS A DISPUTE OVER COMPLETED WRONGS THAT THE COURT CAN REDRESS.

The respondent’s mootness theory would collapse the distinction between prospective and retrospective relief. But the Court has never held that the repeal of a challenged rule eliminates all justiciable consequences of its earlier enforcement. To the contrary, where a plaintiff has suffered an actual invasion of a legal right, courts retain authority to adjudicate and grant the appropriate judgment. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 145-56 (1908); Fitts v. McGhee, 172 U.S. 516, 529-30 (1899); Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 859-60 (1824).

Here, the challenged policy was enforced against plaintiffs before revision, and their complaint seeks nominal damages for that completed violation. Under settled mootness doctrine and the older cases recognizing redress for past constitutional injuries, the controversy remains live. The court should therefore hold that revision of the policy during litigation did not moot the claim.
