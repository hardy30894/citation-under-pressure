**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether, when a design patent covers only a component of a multicomponent product, damages for infringement must be limited to the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to that component. This Court’s precedents compel an affirmative answer: a patentee may recover only those profits that are causally linked to the patented design, not the entire profit from the sale of the product.

**I. The Apportionment Requirement Is Longstanding and Applies to Design Patents**

For over a century, this Court has held that when a patented invention is only a part of a larger article, the patentee must apportion profits or show that the entire value of the article is attributable to the patented feature. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 444–45 (1885), the Court rejected an award of the entire profit from carpets containing a patented design, stating that “no rule has been sanctioned which will allow … the entire profit from the manufacture and sale of the carpet … unless it is shown that the entire value of the carpet as a marketable article is properly and legally attributable to the design.” *Accord Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 17 (1886) (same rule for design patents). The same principle governs utility patents. *Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614–15 (1912) (quoting *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884)): the patentee must “give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits … between the patented feature and the unpatented features,” or “show … that the entire value of the whole machine … is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” *See also Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480, 490–91 (1854) (for a patented improvement, damages cannot be measured as if the entire machine were infringed).

**II. The Patentee Bears the Burden of Proving Apportionment or Entire Value**

The burden rests on the patentee to provide a reliable basis for apportionment. *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 615. If the patentee fails to do so, only nominal damages are recoverable. *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet*, 114 U.S. at 446. This rule is not unique to patent law. In copyright, this Court held in *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390, 400–01 (1940), that an infringer’s profits must be apportioned between the copyrighted material and the infringer’s own contributions, and the copyright owner must prove the portion attributable to the infringement. The same logic applies here: Apple’s design patents cover only specific visual elements of a smartphone—such as a rounded-corner front face and a grid of icons—not the entire device. The smartphone contains countless unpatented features (e.g., internal hardware, software, camera, battery) that contribute to its market value. Apple presented no evidence apportioning Samsung’s profits to the patented designs or showing that those designs drove the entire value of the phones. The jury’s award of Samsung’s entire profits thus violated the apportionment rule.

**III. The Lower Court’s Decision Conflicts with These Precedents**

The Federal Circuit affirmed the jury’s award of Samsung’s entire profits from the sale of its smartphones. That decision cannot be squared with *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet* and *Dobson v. Dornan*, which expressly forbid awarding the entire profit from an article when the design is only a component. Nor can it be reconciled with *Westinghouse* and *Garretson*, which require the patentee to apportion or prove entire value. The court below effectively treated the design patents as covering the entire smartphone, ignoring that the patented designs are but a few of the many features that make a smartphone desirable. *Cf. McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co.*, 141 U.S. 459, 466 (1891) (in estimating profits, comparison must be between the patented invention and what was known and open to the public at the time of the patent). The patented designs here were incremental improvements over prior art; they did not create the entire market for smartphones.

**IV. The Rule of Apportionment Protects Against Overcompensation**

Allowing a patentee to recover the entire profit from a multicomponent product based on a design patent covering only a component would produce windfalls and discourage innovation. As this Court recognized in *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. at 490–91, a patentee of an improvement cannot recover damages as if the entire machine were his invention. The same principle applies to design patents. The design is not the product; it is a feature of the product. To award the entire profit is to ignore the value contributed by the infringer’s own unpatented additions. *See Lehnbeuter v. Holthaus*, 105 U.S. 94, 96 (1882) (design patent is prima facie evidence of novelty and utility, but that does not alter the measure of damages). The burden is on the patentee to separate the value of the design from the value of everything else. Apple did not meet that burden.

**Conclusion**

This Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that when a design patent covers only a component of a product, damages for infringement are limited to the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to that component. The patentee must either apportion profits or prove that the entire value of the product is attributable to the patented design. Because Apple failed to do so, the award of Samsung’s entire profits cannot stand.
