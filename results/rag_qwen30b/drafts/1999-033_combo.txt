**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment affords a cause of action to a plaintiff who alleges that a governmental entity has treated him differently from others in similar circumstances, even when the plaintiff does not belong to a protected class or claim membership in a defined group. This principle—known as the “class of one” doctrine—has long been recognized in our jurisprudence, and the facts of this case present a paradigmatic example of a violation of equal protection under that doctrine. The Village of Willowbrook’s imposition of a 33-foot easement on Grace Olech, while requiring only a 15-foot easement from other similarly situated property owners, constitutes arbitrary and irrational treatment that violates the fundamental guarantee of equal protection.

The foundation of this claim lies in the principle that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits the government from treating individuals differently without a rational basis. As this Court held in *Skaneatales Water Works Co. v. Skaneatales*, 184 U.S. 354, 363 (1902), “the property of the plaintiff remains wholly untouched. Its value has decreased because the village no longer takes water from it, and the inhabitants will probably also take their supply from the village works, but the plaintiff’s property has not been taken, as that term is understood in constitutional law.” This language underscores that the constitutional injury is not necessarily a physical taking, but rather the arbitrary deprivation of a benefit or the imposition of a burden without justification. Here, the Village’s refusal to connect Olech’s property to the water supply unless she grants an 18-foot wider easement—while granting lesser easements to others—constitutes such a deprivation. The Village’s action is not based on any legitimate distinction between Olech and other property owners; it is instead a unilateral, arbitrary demand that cannot be justified by any rational state interest.

The Court has long recognized that equal protection is not limited to cases involving classifications based on race, gender, or other suspect categories. In *City of Joplin v. Southwest Missouri Light Co.*, 191 U.S. 150, 157 (1903), this Court emphasized that “there are presumptions, we repeat, against the granting of exclusive rights and against limitations upon the powers of government.” The Village’s imposition of a uniquely burdensome condition on Olech, while allowing others to connect under less onerous terms, constitutes an impermissible exercise of governmental power. The Village’s action is not a neutral application of a general rule, but a selective and discriminatory enforcement of a condition that serves no legitimate public purpose. The fact that the Village has not applied the same standard to other property owners demonstrates that its conduct is not grounded in any rational policy, but rather in an arbitrary whim or personal animus.

This Court has also held that the Equal Protection Clause protects against governmental actions that are “wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” In *Rogers Park Water Co. v. Fergus*, 180 U.S. 624, 630 (1901), the Court stated that “the contract claimed concerned governmental functions, and such functions cannot be held to have been stipulated away by doubtful or ambiguous provisions.” The Village’s demand for a 33-foot easement is not a neutral, rule-based requirement; it is a discretionary imposition that cannot be justified by any legitimate public interest. The Village has not demonstrated that a 33-foot easement is necessary for the safe or efficient operation of the water system. Indeed, the fact that other property owners are required to provide only a 15-foot easement suggests that the Village’s demand is not based on engineering necessity, but on an arbitrary and capricious decision.

Moreover, the doctrine of equal protection does not require a plaintiff to belong to a class or group to assert a constitutional claim. In *Consumers' Co. v. Hatch*, 224 U.S. 148, 155 (1912), the Court held that “the Water Company was not required by its charter in express terms to make a service connection and the benefits of such connection would inure solely to the house owner, to compel the Water Company to bear the cost of the connection would amount to a confiscation of its property in violation of the due process clause.” While that case involved due process, the principle is equally applicable to equal protection: the government cannot impose a unique burden on one individual without a rational basis. The fact that Olech is not part of a defined class does not bar her claim. The Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary governmental action, regardless of whether they are part of a larger group.

The Village’s conduct also violates the principle that governmental action must be “reasonably related to a legitimate state interest.” In *Boise Artesian Hot & Cold Water Co. v. Boise City*, 230 U.S. 84, 90 (1913), the Court stated that “the city, both under its charter and the general law of the State, had power to obtain a water supply for both general and fire purposes.” But that power does not extend to imposing arbitrary conditions on individual property owners. The Village’s demand for a 33-foot easement is not reasonably related to any legitimate interest in water supply, public safety, or infrastructure. The fact that other property owners are not subject to the same condition further demonstrates the irrationality of the Village’s action.

This Court has consistently held that when a government treats one person differently from others in similar circumstances, without a rational basis, it violates the Equal Protection Clause. In *Norwood v. Baker*, 172 U.S. 269, 275 (1898), the Court stated that “the power conferred upon the commissioners was, not to make assessments upon abutting properties, nor to give notice to the property owners of such assessments, but to determine the question of the propriety and necessity of laying water mains and water pipes.” The Village’s decision to impose a unique easement requirement on Olech, without any such determination, is not a legitimate exercise of governmental authority. It is instead an arbitrary act that cannot withstand constitutional scrutiny.

Finally, the principle that equal protection applies to individualized treatment is reinforced by *Hetrick v. Village of Lindsey*, 265 U.S. 384, 387 (1924), where the Court held that “the Ohio statutes relating to special assessments for street improvements do not require a notice to, and a hearing on behalf of, the owner of the property-assessed.” The absence of procedural fairness in the Village’s decision-making process further underscores the arbitrariness of its action.
