# ARGUMENT

## THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN WHO CANNOT BE REPATRIATED

### I. Indefinite Civil Detention Without a Foreseeable Endpoint Violates the Due Process Clause

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects "all persons" within the territorial jurisdiction of the United States, alien and citizen alike. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896) (holding that "all persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection guaranteed by [the Fifth and Sixth] amendments"). This protection extends to aliens who have entered the country, even those subject to a final order of deportation. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886) (the Fourteenth Amendment's protections "are universal in their application, to all persons within the territorial jurisdiction, without regard to any differences of race, of color, or of nationality").

Freedom from bodily restraint lies at the heart of the liberty the Due Process Clause protects. This Court has recognized that "[l]iberty from bodily restraint always has been recognized as the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause from arbitrary governmental action." Civil detention, untethered from any criminal conviction and unbounded by any foreseeable release date, cannot be reconciled with this fundamental guarantee. Petitioner's detention is not temporary custody incident to an ongoing removal effort—it is permanent incarceration masquerading as a transitional measure, imposed because no nation will accept him.

This Court's decision in *Wong Wing* is controlling on the antecedent principle: even in the immigration context, where Congress possesses broad power to exclude and deport, that power does not extend to imposing punishment without the protections of criminal process. 163 U.S. at 237. Detention that has lost all connection to its stated civil purpose—securing removal—becomes indistinguishable from punishment. Where removal is not merely delayed but has become a practical impossibility, continued detention serves no purpose the government may legitimately advance in a civil proceeding.

### II. The Post-Removal Detention Statute Should Be Construed to Avoid This Serious Constitutional Question

It is a "cardinal principle" of statutory construction that when a statute is susceptible of two constructions, "one of which is constitutional and the other unconstitutional," courts must adopt the construction that avoids the constitutional infirmity. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932); *see also United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909) ("where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter").

Nothing in the text of the post-removal-period statute compels the Attorney General's reading that detention may continue indefinitely, without temporal limit, so long as removal remains theoretically conceivable. The statutory language authorizing detention "beyond the removal period" says nothing about detention *in perpetuity*. Where Congress has not spoken with the clarity required to sustain a construction raising grave constitutional doubts, this Court should not impute to Congress an intent to authorize what the Constitution forbids. *See Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. at 408.

The proper construction limits detention to that period reasonably necessary to accomplish the statute's evident purpose—securing the alien's presence for removal. Once removal has become no longer reasonably foreseeable, the statutory authority to detain, grounded as it must be in the practical objective of effectuating deportation, is exhausted. This construction gives the statute its full, lawful effect while avoiding the serious due process question that would arise from a reading permitting detention without end.

### III. This Case Does Not Involve an Alien Seeking Initial Entry, and *Mezei* Is Inapplicable

The government's reliance on immigration cases involving exclusion at the border is misplaced. Petitioner was lawfully admitted to the United States as a resident alien decades ago. He has resided here, married, and raised a family within this country's borders. His situation is categorically different from that of an alien stopped at the threshold who has never effected entry.

This Court's decision in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), addressed the detention of an excluded alien who was denied entry and left in limbo at Ellis Island, having never been legally admitted into the United States. *Id.* at 208–09. The Court's holding that Mezei's continued exclusion did not violate due process rested squarely on the settled principle that "an alien who seeks admission to this country may not do so under any claim of right." *Id.* at 212. Mezei stood, in the Court's words, "on the threshold of initial entry." *Id.* at 215.

Petitioner, by contrast, stands nowhere near that threshold. He was admitted, he resided here as of right subject to the conditions of his status, and only years later was ordered deported. The due process calculus for a resident alien who has effected entry and become part of the national community differs fundamentally from that governing an alien who has never gained admission. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 369 (protections extend to all persons "within the territorial jurisdiction"). *Mezei*'s narrow holding, confined to the exclusion context, provides no warrant for indefinite detention of a resident alien who has lived, worked, and formed family ties within the United States for decades.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
