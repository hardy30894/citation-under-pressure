**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998, 18 U.S.C. § 229, because private parties may not assert the rights of states under the Tenth Amendment. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) ("The [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]."). This rule is rooted in the fundamental principle that the Tenth Amendment protects the reserved powers of the states, not the rights of individuals. *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 63–64 (1936) ("The Tenth Amendment provides: 'The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.' The amendment states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered."). Thus, a private party may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal legislation on federalism grounds.

The Court has consistently adhered to this principle. In *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 348 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring), the Court recognized that the Tenth Amendment "is not a barrier to the exercise of federal power" when acting within enumerated powers, and that private parties may not challenge federal actions on Tenth Amendment grounds. Similarly, in *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis*, 301 U.S. 548, 589–90 (1937), the Court held that a taxpayer lacked standing to challenge the Social Security Act on Tenth Amendment grounds, emphasizing that the Amendment "is not concerned with the federal taxing power." These cases confirm that the Tenth Amendment is a structural protection of state sovereignty, not an individual right enforceable by private litigants.

Petitioner’s reliance on *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), is misplaced. *Holland* upheld the Migratory Bird Treaty Act against a Tenth Amendment challenge, but it did so in the context of a suit brought by a state, not a private party. *Id.* at 432–33 ("It is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well-being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could."). The Court in *Holland* did not address whether a private party could assert a Tenth Amendment claim, and subsequent cases have made clear that such standing is unavailable. *Tennessee Electric Power*, 306 U.S. at 143–44.

Moreover, the Necessary and Proper Clause provides ample authority for Congress to enact § 229 as a means of implementing the Chemical Weapons Convention. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421 (1819) ("Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."). The Convention itself was a valid exercise of the treaty power under Article II, § 2, and Congress acted within its authority in implementing it. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33 ("If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.").

Petitioner’s argument that § 229 violates the Tenth Amendment by intruding on state criminal jurisdiction also fails. The Court has long recognized that federal criminal laws may coexist with state laws, and that Congress may regulate conduct traditionally within state purview when acting pursuant to an enumerated power. *United States v. Lanza*, 268 U.S. 513, 523 (1925) ("We have here two sovereignties, deriving power from different sources, capable of dealing with the same subject-matter within the same territory. ... Each government, in determining what shall be an offense against its peace and dignity, is exercising its own sovereignty, not restrained by the other."). The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act does not displace state authority but supplements it, as Congress is empowered to do under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Finally, petitioner’s contention that § 229 is unconstitutionally vague or overbroad is meritless. The statute provides clear notice of the conduct it prohibits, and its terms are sufficiently definite to withstand constitutional scrutiny. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1875) ("The question whether a statute is void for uncertainty must be decided by the circumstances of the case and the particular law; and the statute must be examined in its application to the facts as they exist."). The statute’s definitions of "chemical weapon" and "toxic chemical" are precise and tailored to the treaty’s objectives, ensuring fair notice to individuals of the prohibited conduct.

In sum, petitioner lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229, and the statute is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty and necessary and proper powers. The judgment below should be affirmed.
