# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1692a(6) DEFINES A DEBT COLLECTOR BY REFERENCE TO WHETHER IT COLLECTS DEBT FOR ITSELF OR FOR ANOTHER—NOT BY THE DEBT'S DEFAULT STATUS AT ACQUISITION

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as any person "who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This Court has recognized that this definition turns on the relationship between the collector and the debt—specifically, whether the collector is gathering money owed to itself or to a third party. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (noting the Act's definition "includes a person 'who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another'"). Nothing in this text conditions debt-collector status on whether the debt happened to be in default when acquired.

Petitioners' theory—that default status at acquisition is the "determining factor" of debt-collector status—cannot be squared with the statute's structure. Section 1692a(6) first defines who *is* a debt collector, then separately carves out exclusions, including the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) for persons collecting non-defaulted debt owed to another. As the Eleventh Circuit correctly explained, "where a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. [Instead] . . . he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements." *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015). An exclusion cannot be bootstrapped into an affirmative inclusion. Petitioners' argument inverts the statute: it treats an exclusion for a subset of third-party collectors as though it were itself the operative inclusionary definition for creditors who purchase their own accounts.

The legislative history confirms that Congress intended the "regularly collects" definition to capture persons who collect "debts for others"—not creditors collecting their own accounts regardless of when those accounts were acquired. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), *reprinted in* 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698 ("The Committee intends the term 'debt collector' . . . to cover all third persons who regularly collect debts for others"). Congress's stated purpose was "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors," 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e)—a purpose aimed at the agency relationship inherent in third-party collection, not at the timing of a purchase.

## II. RESPONDENT ACTED AS A CREDITOR, NOT A DEBT COLLECTOR, BECAUSE IT COLLECTED ON ACCOUNTS IT OWNED FOR ITS OWN ACCOUNT

The phrase "owed or due another" must be assessed at the time of the challenged collection conduct, not by reference to the debt's original creditor. When Santander purchased petitioners' defaulted retail installment contracts from CitiFinancial Auto, the debts became "owed or due" Santander itself. Santander's subsequent collection efforts were therefore collection of its *own* debt, placing it squarely within the creditor category rather than the debt-collector category defined in § 1692a(6).

This reading follows the ordinary meaning of the statutory text. As the Ninth Circuit held, "[t]he statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another.'" *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013). Likewise, the Eleventh Circuit reasoned that "our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection." *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318. Petitioners' contrary theory—that a debt's history as "another's" obligation forever colors the purchaser's status—finds no support in the statutory language and would require this Court to insert words Congress did not write.

Nor does it matter that Santander may, with respect to other accounts or at other times, have served as a third-party servicer collecting debts owed to others. The FDCPA's coverage attaches to specific conduct: a defendant is liable under § 1692e only if it was "acting as a debt collector, as defined by § 1692a(6), when it engaged in misrepresentations in connection with the collection of debt from the plaintiff." A consumer finance company that both originates and purchases loans is not rendered a debt collector for *all* purposes simply because some of its activities might independently qualify as third-party debt collection. Petitioners' rule would sweep every creditor that also services debt for others into blanket FDCPA coverage for collection of its own accounts—a result Congress never intended, given its careful bifurcation of "creditor" and "debt collector" throughout the statute. *See* 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), 1692a(6).

## III. PETITIONERS' STATUTORY ARGUMENT MISREADS THE "CREDITOR" EXCLUSION AND THE (F)(iii) EXCLUSION

Petitioners argue that because § 1692a(4) excludes from the definition of "creditor" any person who receives a transfer of debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another," and because Santander does not fit within that narrow exclusion, Santander must therefore be a debt collector. This argument fails on its own terms: the § 1692a(4) exclusion applies only to persons who acquire debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection . . . for another"—a description that does not fit Santander, which purchased petitioners' loans as part of a $3.55 billion investment transaction for its own account.

Petitioners' reliance on § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) fares no better. That provision excludes from "debt collector" status "any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt . . . owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another to the extent such activity . . . concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). This exclusion protects entities such as mortgage servicers handling non-defaulted debt on behalf of creditors. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 4 ("[T]he committee does not intend the definition [of debt collector] to cover the activities of . . . mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing"). But this exclusion is irrelevant unless a defendant first satisfies the threshold definition of "debt collector" under the main text of § 1692a(6)—i.e., unless the defendant is collecting debt "owed or due . . . another." Santander does not need the (F)(iii) exclusion because it never satisfied the threshold definition in the first instance: it was collecting debt owed to itself.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
