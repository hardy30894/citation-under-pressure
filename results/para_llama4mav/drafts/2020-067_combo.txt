The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it could review the entire trial record, including matters outside what was presented to the jury, when determining whether the plain error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This Court has long recognized that appellate courts have the discretion to consider the entire record when reviewing for plain error.

In Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632 (1896), this Court reviewed the entire record to determine whether the trial court's error was harmless. The Court held that "it is the duty of the court to examine the whole case, and if it appears that the error has not injuriously affected the substantial rights of the parties, it will be disregarded." Id. at 657-58. Similarly, in Clyatt v. United States, 197 U.S. 207 (1905), the Court reviewed the entire record to determine whether the defendant's substantial rights were affected by the trial court's error.

The principle that an appellate court may review the entire record when determining whether an error was prejudicial is also supported by older cases such as Blitz v. United States, 153 U.S. 308 (1894), and McGrain v. Daugherty, 273 U.S. 135 (1927). In Blitz, the Court held that an appellate court may consider the entire record when determining whether an error was harmless. 153 U.S. at 312. In McGrain, the Court stated that an appellate court has the power to "examine the whole record" when reviewing a case. 273 U.S. at 174.

Furthermore, in Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court held that an appellate court may consider evidence outside the jury's verdict when determining whether an error was prejudicial. The Court stated that "the court may consider the whole record, and is not limited to the evidence presented to the jury." Id. at 362. This principle is also supported by cases such as Crawford v. United States, 212 U.S. 183 (1909), and Horning v. District of Columbia, 254 U.S. 135 (1920).

In addition, the Court has recognized that an appellate court's review of the entire record is not limited to the evidence presented at trial. In United States v. Rabinowitz, 339 U.S. 56 (1950), the Court held that an appellate court may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial transcript, when determining whether an error was prejudicial. The Court stated that "the court may look to the whole record" when reviewing a case. Id. at 63 (citing Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 281 (1942)).

The respondent respectfully submits that the Eleventh Circuit's decision to review the entire trial record, including Greer's previous convictions, was consistent with this Court's precedent. By considering the entire record, the Eleventh Circuit properly determined that Greer could not show that the plain error in his trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial.

In Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263 (1929), and Brasfield v. United States, 272 U.S. 448 (1926), the Court also demonstrated its willingness to review the entire record when determining whether an error was prejudicial. These cases, along with the others cited above, demonstrate that the Eleventh Circuit's review of the entire record was proper and consistent with this Court's long-standing precedent.

The respondent urges the Court to affirm the Eleventh Circuit's decision, as it is supported by a long line of cases that permit appellate courts to review the entire record when determining whether an error was prejudicial.
