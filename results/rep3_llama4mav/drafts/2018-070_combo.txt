The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to mean that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969).

However, the Court has also made clear that the adoption of state law is not a blanket incorporation of all state laws, but rather a limited adoption of state law to fill gaps in federal law. In United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court held that the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13, which adopts state law as federal law in federal enclaves, is constitutional because it "borrows" state law to fill gaps in federal law. Similarly, in Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), the Court held that federal courts must apply state law in diversity cases where there is no applicable federal law.

The Fifth Circuit has also interpreted the OCSLA's choice of law provision to require a gap in federal law before state law can be applied. In Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law can be adopted as surrogate federal law under the OCSLA only when there is a "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law. This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Rodrigue, which emphasized that the OCSLA's adoption of state law is limited to situations where federal law is inadequate.

In this case, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. As the Court held in United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the FLSA is a broad and comprehensive statute that regulates minimum wages, maximum hours, and overtime pay. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), allows for more protective state laws, but this does not create a gap in federal law that state law can fill.

The Ninth Circuit's holding that California's wage and hour laws can be applied as surrogate federal law on the outer Continental Shelf is inconsistent with the OCSLA's choice of law provision and relevant case law. As the Court held in Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605 (1926), a comprehensive federal statutory scheme precludes the application of state law. Similarly, in Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), the Court held that a federal statute that occupies the field precludes the application of state law.

The district court's dismissal of Newton's claims was correct because the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to address wage and hour grievances arising on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's judgment reinstated.
