# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests “all legislative powers” in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering a complex regulatory scheme. This Court settled the controlling test more than sixty years ago: “if Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) meets that test many times over.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). That is precisely the kind of policy and criterion to govern executive action whose *absence* doomed the statutes in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). Those decisions turned on the total absence of standards, not on the mere fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action; Section 811(h) supplies exactly the standards those statutes lacked.

The Court has consistently sustained delegations far less confined than this one when Congress faced a fast-moving regulatory problem. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix “fair and equitable” wartime prices, recognizing that the rapidity of changes in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same is true here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new “designer drugs” faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond, which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment proceedings. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), the Court held that the “public interest, convenience, or necessity” standard was an adequate guide for licensing broadcast stations, and in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), it approved the similarly open-ended “fair and equitable” standard for utility reorganizations. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

Nor is the absence of exhaustive technical detail fatal where Congress relies on a delegatee's expertise to administer a complex field. In *United States v. Rock Royal Co-operative, Inc.*, 307 U.S. 533, 574-77 (1939), the Court upheld the Secretary of Agriculture's authority to fix minimum milk prices under the Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act, notwithstanding the breadth of discretion Congress left to the Secretary, because the statute's purposes and the conditions warranting action were adequately defined. And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture “excessive profits” — a standard with no fixed numerical content — because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to fill in details Congress itself could not practically anticipate. The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here.

Finally, judicial review of the *constitutionality* of the delegation remains available, satisfying the concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952). Although § 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, it does not — and could not — foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself, as the court below correctly recognized. That structural check remains intact, and it preserves the underlying principle, traceable to this Court's recognition that Congress generally cannot delegate its legislative power to another branch, *see Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), while accommodating Congress's practical need to obtain executive assistance in administering an inherently technical and time-sensitive regulatory scheme.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a simpler reason: Congress expressly authorized it. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) empowers the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization for the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. Cf. *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (upholding delegated authority to an executive department to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted, and the delegation to the DEA was therefore proper.
