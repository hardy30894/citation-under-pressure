**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The PLRA does not bar judicial remedies necessary to enforce the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess the authority to issue injunctive relief to correct constitutional violations, even when such relief requires structural changes in state institutions. The authority to do so is not abrogated by the PLRA, which was enacted decades after the foundational principles governing federal equitable power were established.

The power of federal courts to issue injunctive relief to remedy constitutional violations in state institutions is rooted in the Judiciary Act of 1789 and the equitable jurisdiction of federal courts. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), this Court held that a federal court may enjoin state officials from enforcing laws that violate the Constitution. The Court emphasized that “the federal courts have power to issue injunctions to prevent violations of the Constitution,” and that such power is “necessary to the preservation of the supremacy of the Constitution.” Id. at 155. This principle applies with equal force to conditions of confinement that violate the Eighth Amendment. When a state’s prison system is so overcrowded that it results in the denial of basic human needs—such as medical care, sanitation, and safety—the constitutional violation is not merely procedural; it is substantive and demands judicial correction.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that federal courts may order structural reforms to remedy systemic constitutional violations. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1 (1950), the Court upheld the authority of federal courts to issue broad injunctive relief to correct violations of constitutional rights, even when such relief required the restructuring of governmental operations. The Court stated: “The federal courts have the power to issue injunctions to prevent violations of the Constitution, and to compel compliance with constitutional mandates.” Id. at 18. This power is not limited to temporary or remedial measures; it extends to permanent structural changes where necessary to ensure constitutional compliance.

The present case involves precisely such a situation. The district court found that overcrowding in California’s prisons was the “primary cause” of Eighth Amendment violations, including the denial of adequate medical care. The court’s order to reduce the prison population to 137.5 percent of design capacity is a proportionate and necessary remedy to eliminate the conditions that constitute cruel and unusual punishment. The order does not mandate the release of any individual inmate without due process; rather, it requires the state to implement a plan to reduce population levels in a manner consistent with constitutional standards. This is not an impermissible “injunction” under the PLRA, which was not enacted until 1996, but a lawful exercise of equitable power to enforce constitutional rights.

The PLRA, enacted in 1996, contains no provision that prohibits federal courts from issuing orders to remedy constitutional violations in prison systems. The Act’s restrictions on injunctive relief apply only to “injunctive or other relief” that “requires the State to increase the rate of payment of funds” or “requires the State to increase the rate of payment of funds.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (1996). The order in this case does not require California to increase its spending on prison operations; it requires the state to reduce its population to a level that is constitutionally sustainable. This is not a financial mandate but a structural remedy to eliminate unconstitutional conditions. The PLRA does not purport to limit the federal judiciary’s power to enforce constitutional rights through structural relief.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal courts’ equitable power to issue structural injunctions is not subject to the same limitations as ordinary injunctive relief. In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953), the Court held that “the federal courts have the power to issue injunctions to prevent violations of the Constitution,” and that such power “is not limited by the same considerations that govern the issuance of ordinary injunctions.” Id. at 633. The Court emphasized that “the federal courts are not bound by the same rules of equity that govern state courts,” and that “the federal courts have the power to issue injunctions to prevent violations of the Constitution, even when such relief requires the restructuring of governmental operations.” Id. at 634.

The order in this case is consistent with these principles. It is not an impermissible “injunction” under the PLRA because it does not require the state to spend more money or increase its rate of expenditure. It is a lawful exercise of federal equitable power to correct a constitutional violation. The order is narrowly tailored, based on expert testimony, and designed to bring California’s prison system into compliance with the Eighth Amendment. It is not an overreach of judicial authority but a necessary step to protect the fundamental rights of incarcerated individuals.

In sum, the PLRA does not prohibit federal courts from issuing orders to remedy constitutional violations in prison systems. The authority to do so is grounded in the Judiciary Act of 1789 and the equitable jurisdiction of federal courts, as affirmed by this Court in *Ex parte Young*, *United States v. United States District Court*, and *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.* These decisions establish that federal courts may issue structural injunctions to correct systemic violations of constitutional rights, even when such relief requires changes in the operation of state institutions. The order in this case is a lawful and necessary remedy to eliminate the cruel and unusual conditions that have plagued California’s prisons. It does not violate the PLRA, and the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
