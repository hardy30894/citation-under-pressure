## ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries that occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) demands this result; the Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test reads the statute’s geographic limitation out of existence.

### I. The Statutory Text Requires That the Injury Occur on the Outer Continental Shelf

Section 1333(b) provides benefits for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The word “occurring” modifies “injury,” not “operations.” An injury that “occurs” does so at a particular place. Congress could have written “injury resulting from operations” without specifying where the injury occurs, but it added “occurring” to link the injury itself to the OCS. The natural reading is that the injury must take place on the shelf.

The “plain meaning” of a statute controls unless the language is ambiguous. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *United States v. American Trucking Assns.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here the language is unambiguous: “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” ties the injury’s location to the shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation—that any injury with a “substantial nexus” to OCS operations qualifies, even if it occurs miles inland—abandons this plain meaning.

### II. OCSLA’s Structure Confirms a Geographic Limitation

OCSLA’s other provisions are uniformly situs-based. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) applies the law of the adjacent state “to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and to artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). This geographic anchor is the statute’s organizing principle. As this Court noted in *Rodrigue*, OCSLA “replaces state law with federal law for the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and for artificial islands and fixed structures.” *Id.* at 356. The workers’ compensation provision should not be read to sweep more broadly than the statute’s core coverage.

Moreover, the Longshore Act—which OCSLA incorporates—itself has a well-established situs requirement. The LHWCA covers injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). This Court has consistently enforced that geographic limitation. *Swanson v. Marra Brothers, Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1946) (holding that the Act’s coverage turns on “the place where the injury occurred”). *Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson*, 396 U.S. 212, 218-19 (1969) (requiring both maritime situs and maritime employment). *Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co.*, 370 U.S. 114, 127 (1962) (confirming that the LHWCA’s coverage “is keyed to the location of the injury”). By incorporating the LHWCA, OCSLA imported this same situs principle.

### III. Pre-1970 OCSLA Precedent Supports the Situs-of-Injury Test

The Fifth Circuit, which has the most OCSLA experience, has consistently required that the injury occur on the shelf. *Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes*, 293 F.2d 60, 64-65 (5th Cir. 1961) (denying OCSLA benefits for an injury on a platform in state waters because the platform was not on the OCS). *United States v. Ray*, 294 F.2d 10, 15 (5th Cir. 1961) (defining the OCS as “submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters”). In *Pure Oil*, the court emphasized that “the Act was intended to apply only to injuries occurring on the shelf,” rejecting an argument for coverage based on a connection to shelf operations. *Pure Oil*, 293 F.2d at 65. These early interpretations, contemporaneous with the 1953 enactment, are entitled to great weight.

### IV. Congressional Purpose Confirms a Narrow Geographic Scope

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision was designed to fill a gap: workers on the OCS had no state workers’ compensation coverage because the shelf is outside state territorial waters. Congress did not intend to duplicate state coverage for onshore injuries. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test would cover thousands of onshore workers whose injuries have only a remote connection to shelf operations—such as the forklift accident here—and would override state compensation schemes. Nothing in OCSLA’s legislative history suggests such a sweeping result. The statute’s “principal aim” was to provide federal law for “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, not for adjacent land areas.” *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 358.

Because the plain text, structure, contemporaneous judicial interpretation, and purpose all require that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
