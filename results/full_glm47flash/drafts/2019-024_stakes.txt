**Argument**

**I. The Statute of Limitations is Triggered by Knowledge of the Breach, Not Knowledge of the Law.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by requiring a plaintiff to have knowledge that an ERISA fiduciary’s act was *imprudent* to trigger the three-year statute of limitations. Under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), the limitations period begins to run from the "earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." The term "breach" refers to the act or transaction itself, not the legal conclusion that the act violated the fiduciary duty of care. This Court has consistently held that "actual knowledge" is triggered by knowledge of the transaction that constitutes the alleged violation, not by knowledge of the law.

In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, the Ninth Circuit held that a fiduciary’s counterclaim was barred by the statute of limitations because the fiduciaries "had actual knowledge of the transaction at the time it took place." The Court explained that the statute of limitations is "triggered by the [trustees'] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law." This rule was reaffirmed in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, where the Court held that a participant had actual knowledge of a breach every time he received a reduced pension check, even though he "may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA." The Court reiterated that "knowledge of the transaction... is sufficient to trigger the three-year limitations period."

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation effectively reads the "actual knowledge" requirement out of the statute. If a plaintiff must know that an act was unlawful or imprudent to trigger the limitations period, the statute functions as a constructive knowledge provision. Congress repealed the constructive knowledge provision in 1987, leaving only the actual knowledge requirement. The Court should not resurrect a provision Congress explicitly discarded.

**II. Knowledge of the Investment Strategy Constitutes Knowledge of the Breach.**

Applying the correct standard, Sulyma had actual knowledge of the breach more than three years before filing suit. Sulyma alleges that Intel breached its fiduciary duty by investing plan assets in alternative investments, such as hedge funds. The "breach" is the act of investing in these assets. Sulyma had access to the documents disclosing this strategy, including Fund Fact Sheets and the Summary Plan Description, which detailed the allocation to alternative investments and the associated risks. Under *Blanton* and *Meagher*, Sulyma’s knowledge of the investment strategy—knowing that Intel invested plan assets in hedge funds—constitutes actual knowledge of the breach.

The Ninth Circuit incorrectly required Sulyma to have known that the investments were *imprudent*. This requirement conflates the factual knowledge necessary to trigger the statute with the legal standard for liability. A plaintiff need only know that a fiduciary *acted* in a certain way to start the limitations clock; the plaintiff need not know at that moment whether the act met the "prudent man" standard of care. As the Supreme Court noted in *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, fiduciaries are required to act with the "care, skill, prudence, and diligence" of a prudent person. However, the statute of limitations is not a bar to liability for imprudence; it is a bar to liability for failing to sue within a reasonable time after learning of the act. Because Sulyma knew of the investment in alternative investments, the statute of limitations began to run at the time of the investment, not when he later learned of the poor performance. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to require knowledge of imprudence is inconsistent with controlling Ninth Circuit precedent and improperly expands the statute of limitations.
