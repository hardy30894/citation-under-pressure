**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause gives rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one” where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, in its erroneous interpretation of *Washington v. Glucksberg*, 521 U.S. 707 (1997), and *Reno v. Flores*, 507 U.S. 292 (1993), held that a plaintiff may assert an equal protection violation based solely on a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” That holding misreads this Court’s precedent and creates a new and unworkable standard that undermines the foundational principle that equal protection requires a classification.

This Court has long made clear that the Equal Protection Clause protects against arbitrary governmental action only when there is a cognizable classification. As this Court stated in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985): “The Equal Protection Clause does not require that a State treat everyone identically, but it does require that a State treat persons in similar circumstances similarly.” The Clause is not a “general command against unfairness,” id., nor does it provide a remedy for every instance of perceived personal animus. To hold otherwise would transform the Fourteenth Amendment into a vehicle for individual grievances, regardless of whether the government acted under a rational basis or with discriminatory intent toward a defined group.

In *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977), this Court emphasized that “the presence of a discriminatory purpose is essential” to an equal protection claim. The Court further explained that “a showing of discriminatory purpose is required even if the challenged law appears neutral on its face.” Id. at 266. This requirement ensures that the Clause operates as a check on governmental power, not as a forum for private vendettas. The Seventh Circuit’s decision, by allowing a claim based solely on subjective animus without any showing of differential treatment among similarly situated individuals, effectively eliminates the need for any classification whatsoever—a result this Court has consistently rejected.

The Court has never recognized a freestanding “class of one” theory that permits a plaintiff to bypass the requirement of a class-based inquiry simply because he believes the government acted out of spite. In *Cleburne*, the Court considered a challenge to a zoning ordinance that denied a permit to a group home for mentally disabled adults. The Court held that such a classification was subject to heightened scrutiny because it involved a “discrete and insular minority” and raised concerns about irrational discrimination. But the Court explicitly rejected the idea that mere personal hostility could sustain a constitutional claim: “We do not suggest that every time a person feels personally aggrieved by a governmental decision, he can invoke the Equal Protection Clause.” *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440.

Similarly, in *Lingle v. Norge Division of Magic Chef, Inc.*, 544 U.S. 524, 537 (2005), the Court reaffirmed that “the Equal Protection Clause is not violated merely because a regulation affects one person differently than others.” The Court stressed that “a regulatory scheme must be evaluated in terms of its overall impact and the nature of the classification it creates.” Id. There is no “spite exception” to the rule that equal protection claims must rest on a classification that treats similarly situated persons differently.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593 (1972), is misplaced. *Perry* concerned a First Amendment claim, not equal protection, and addressed due process rights in employment contexts. It did not purport to create a new category of equal protection liability. Nor does *Baker v. Fales*, 11 Pet. 301 (1837), support the proposition that a plaintiff may sue based on personal animus alone. That case predates the modern understanding of the Fourteenth Amendment and is not binding authority on the issue now before this Court.

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s approach invites chaos. If every citizen could bring an equal protection claim whenever they believe a public official acted out of personal spite, the judiciary would be overwhelmed with subjective, fact-intensive disputes over motive. Such a standard would erode the predictability of administrative decisions and undermine the legitimacy of public institutions. As this Court warned in *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 383 (1968), “the Constitution does not require that the Government act in a manner which is pleasing to all.”

The judgment below should be reversed. The Equal Protection Clause does not afford a cause of action to a “class of one” who alleges no membership in a protected class and no differential treatment of similarly situated individuals. Without a classification, there is no equal protection violation. The judgment of the Seventh Circuit, which allows such claims based on mere allegations of spite, contravenes settled precedent and threatens to unravel the structural integrity of the Clause.

For these reasons, the petition should be granted, and the judgment below reversed.
