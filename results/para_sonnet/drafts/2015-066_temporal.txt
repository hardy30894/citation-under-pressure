# ARGUMENT

## I. The Sixth Amendment guarantees the assistance of counsel because uncounseled proceedings are inherently unreliable, and this Court has repeatedly refused to let the fruits of such proceedings taint later prosecutions.

The right to counsel exists because "the average defendant does not have the professional legal skill to protect himself" and, without a lawyer, "though he be not guilty, he faces the danger of conviction because he does not know how to establish his innocence." *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 69 (1932). This Court has treated the right as so fundamental to a fair proceeding that a conviction obtained without it is void: "the writ of habeas corpus ought to issue" because a court "no longer has jurisdiction to proceed" once a defendant is deprived of counsel. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938). In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), this Court reaffirmed that "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours," and held that right binding on the States because "any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him." *Id.* at 344.

This Court has never treated the right to counsel as a mere technical formality confined to the proceeding in which it was denied. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), Texas sought to use a prior uncounseled felony conviction to prove an element of a recidivist enhancement statute. This Court held that "to permit a conviction obtained in violation of *Gideon v. Wainwright* to be used against a person either to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense... is to erode the principle of that case." *Id.* at 115. The Court explained that such use "would deprive [the defendant] of due process of law" because the constitutional infirmity of the prior conviction "is not erased by the actions of the State" in a later proceeding. *Id.* The vice identified in *Burgett* is precisely the vice here: the government seeks to use Bryant's tribal convictions—obtained without counsel and resulting in imprisonment—not merely as background, but as an essential element the prosecution must prove beyond doubt to convict him of a federal felony under § 117(a).

## II. The reliability concerns underlying the right to counsel apply with full force where an uncounseled conviction is offered to establish an element of a subsequent federal offense.

It is true that the Sixth Amendment, of its own force, does not govern tribal court proceedings, because tribal authority is "the outgrowth of the original tribal sovereignty" and is not an exercise of power delegated by the Federal Constitution. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896). Tribes retain "such powers of self-government as have never been extinguished," and their courts function as instruments of that retained sovereignty rather than organs of the federal government. *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 568-69 (1883); see also *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 381-82 (1886).

But that principle answers a different question than the one presented here. The question is not whether the tribal court violated the Sixth Amendment when it convicted Bryant without counsel; it did not, because the Amendment did not apply to that court. The question is whether the *federal government*, prosecuting Bryant in *federal court* under a *federal statute*, may use that uncounseled, custodial conviction as an element it must prove to convict him of a new federal felony. When a federal court is asked to give effect to a judgment rendered under a different sovereign's procedures, this Court has long recognized that principles of comity do not require blind deference where the foreign proceeding lacks the process this Court demands of its own courts. *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 202-03 (1895) (a judgment rendered abroad will be given effect only where "there has been opportunity for a full and fair trial abroad before a court of competent jurisdiction... under a system of jurisprudence likely to secure an impartial administration of justice"). Comity toward tribal adjudications is not, and has never been, a license for the federal sovereign to import into its own criminal process a conviction that would not satisfy the due process this Court demands when the federal government itself seeks to brand a person a felon.

The reliability rationale of *Powell*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, and *Gideon* does not evaporate merely because the underlying conviction happened to arise in a forum where the Sixth Amendment was inapplicable. *Burgett* did not ask whether the predicate conviction was itself unconstitutional in the abstract; it asked whether using an uncounseled, unreliable conviction to establish guilt in the present prosecution was consistent with due process. 389 U.S. at 115. The same unreliability that concerned this Court in *Burgett*—the risk that an unrepresented defendant, "though he be not guilty," *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 69, was nonetheless convicted—infects a tribal conviction obtained without counsel just as it would a state conviction obtained without counsel. The federal government cannot escape the constitutional command simply by pointing to the tribal forum in which the defect arose.

## III. Conclusion

Because Bryant's prior tribal convictions were obtained without the assistance of counsel and resulted in imprisonment, the government's use of those convictions as elements of the § 117(a) offense violates the due process guarantees this Court articulated in *Burgett*, rooted in the reliability principles of *Powell*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, and *Gideon*. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
