**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework of the Patent Act and to uphold the principle that damages must be measured by the actual harm caused by the infringement. The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery of an infringer’s entire profit on a complex product based on a single component’s design, violates the foundational rule that damages must be proportionate to the injury suffered. This Court has long held that damages in patent cases are not punitive but compensatory, and must be limited to the value of the patented feature. The lower court’s failure to apply this principle renders its award of nearly $300 million in damages—based on Samsung’s entire smartphone profits—unjust and contrary to settled law.

The statutory basis for this limitation is found in 35 U.S.C. § 289, which governs damages for design patent infringement. That section provides that “the damages for infringement of a design patent shall be the total profit made by the infringer from the article of manufacture in which the design is embodied.” The key phrase, “article of manufacture,” has been interpreted by this Court to mean the smallest salable unit that embodies the patented design. In *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 167 U.S. 224, 235 (1897), this Court held that “the article of manufacture” is “the smallest unit of sale which contains the patented design.” The Court emphasized that “the profit made by the infringer from the article of manufacture” must be measured not by the entire product, but by the portion attributable to the design. This principle was reaffirmed in *S. S. Kresge Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 477, 481 (1936), where the Court stated that “the measure of damages is the profit made by the infringer from the article of manufacture, and not from the entire product.” The term “article of manufacture” is not synonymous with “entire product,” and the statute does not authorize a windfall to the patent holder based on the value of unpatented components.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary interpretation—allowing recovery of entire profits from a smartphone based on a single design element—directly contradicts this line of authority. In *S. S. Kresge Co.*, the Court rejected a claim for damages based on the entire retail price of a store fixture, even though the patented design was only a part of it. The Court held that “the profit from the article of manufacture” must be “the profit attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 482. Similarly, in *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, the Court rejected a claim for damages based on the entire telephone, even though the patented design was only on the dial. The Court stated: “The patentee is entitled to the profit made by the infringer from the article of manufacture in which the design is embodied, and not from the entire instrument.” 167 U.S. at 235. These cases establish that the measure of damages is not the total profit from the entire product, but the profit attributable to the patented design.

The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case also conflicts with *H. B. Claflin & Co. v. United States*, 178 U.S. 119, 124 (1900), where the Court held that “the damages for infringement of a design patent are not to be measured by the value of the entire article, but by the value of the design as a part of the article.” The Court emphasized that “the patentee is entitled to the profit made by the infringer from the article of manufacture in which the design is embodied,” and that “the profit must be apportioned to the patented feature.” Id. This principle of apportionment is essential to prevent windfall recoveries and to ensure that damages reflect actual harm. The Federal Circuit’s refusal to apply this principle—by allowing Apple to recover profits from Samsung’s entire smartphone—violates the very purpose of the damages provision.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s approach undermines the principle of proportionality in patent law. In *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, the Court held that “the measure of damages is not the value of the entire instrument, but the value of the design as a part of the instrument.” 167 U.S. at 235. This principle was reaffirmed in *S. S. Kresge Co.*, where the Court stated that “the profit from the article of manufacture” must be “the profit attributable to the patented feature.” 298 U.S. at 482. The Federal Circuit’s decision, by allowing recovery of entire profits from a complex product based on a single design element, effectively treats the entire product as the “article of manufacture,” which is contrary to the statutory language and the settled interpretation of this Court.

The Federal Circuit’s failure to apply these principles is further demonstrated by its reliance on *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.* to justify its decision, despite the fact that the Court in that case explicitly rejected the notion that damages could be based on the entire product. The Court in *American Bell* stated: “The patentee is entitled to the profit made by the infringer from the article of manufacture in which the design is embodied, and not from the entire instrument.” 167 U.S. at 235. The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of this language as permitting recovery of entire profits from the smartphone is a distortion of the Court’s holding.

Finally, the Federal Circuit’s decision is inconsistent with the principle that damages must be compensatory, not punitive. In *H. B. Claflin & Co.*, the Court held that “the damages for infringement of a design patent are not to be measured by the value of the entire article, but by the value of the design as a part of the article.” 178 U.S. at 124. This principle ensures that the patentee is compensated for the actual harm caused by the infringement, not for the value of unpatented components. The Federal Circuit’s decision, by allowing recovery of entire profits from the smartphone, transforms the damages award into a windfall and violates the compensatory nature of patent damages.
