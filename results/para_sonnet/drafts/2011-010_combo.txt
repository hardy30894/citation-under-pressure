ARGUMENT

I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Ties the Situs Requirement to “Operations,” Not to “Injury.”

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act compensation to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies “operations”—the noun it immediately follows—not the more distant word “injury.” This Court has long recognized that “the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Here Congress did not write that benefits are available for injuries “occurring on the outer Continental Shelf”; it wrote that benefits follow injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted” there. The statute thus asks only whether the causal source of the injury—the extractive operations—was located on the Shelf, not where the employee happened to be standing when he was hurt. Petitioner’s situs-of-injury gloss requires this Court to add words Congress did not choose, a course this Court has consistently rejected. See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc., 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“Often these words are sufficient in and of themselves to determine the purpose of the legislation. In such cases we have followed their plain meaning.”).

II. The Longshore Act’s Remedial Purpose, Which Section 1333(b) Imports Wholesale, Forecloses a Rigid Situs-of-Injury Rule.

Even were the text less clear, the settled canon governing the Longshore Act—incorporated into OCSLA by its express cross-reference—confirms that coverage should not turn on the happenstance of geography. This Court has repeatedly instructed that the Longshore Act “is to be liberally construed in conformance with its purpose, and in a way which avoids harsh and incongruous results.” South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 259-60 (1940). In Cardillo v. Liberty Mutual Insurance Co., 330 U.S. 469 (1947), this Court refused to give the phrase “arising out of and in the course of employment” a technical, location-bound reading, holding instead that the statute must be read broadly so as not to defeat compensation for injuries causally tied to the employment relationship. And in O’Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc., 340 U.S. 504 (1951), the Court made clear that the touchstone under this family of compensation statutes is not the geographic locus of the harm but “whether the obligations or conditions of employment create[d] the zone of special danger out of which the injury arose.” Id. at 507. That causal, not spatial, inquiry is precisely the “substantial nexus” standard the Ninth Circuit adopted below, and it is precisely the inquiry Congress wrote into § 1333(b) when it conditioned coverage on injuries “resulting from” shelf operations.

Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328 (1953), reaffirmed that courts must not allow technical readings of the Longshore Act to produce gaps in coverage inconsistent with its humane purpose. A situs-of-injury rule creates exactly such a gap: it would deny compensation to a roustabout whose fatal injury was unquestionably caused by his platform employment merely because the fatal blow landed onshore rather than on a fixed structure three miles out to sea. Nothing in the remedial scheme Congress built tolerates that arbitrary result.

III. Calbeck and Rodrigue Confirm that Congress Intended Comprehensive, Not Fragmented, Coverage for Injuries Connected to Outer Continental Shelf Operations.

This Court’s decision in Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114 (1962), is instructive. There the Court rejected an artificially narrow reading of “navigable waters” under the Longshore Act that would have stranded workers in a jurisdictional “twilight zone” without any compensation remedy, holding that Congress intended a uniform national scheme unburdened by shifting geographic technicalities. Id. at 126-27. The same concern animates § 1333(b): Congress extended the Longshore Act to Shelf operations precisely because state compensation schemes and the Longshore Act’s ordinary navigable-waters situs left OCS workers exposed. A situs-of-injury requirement would recreate the very gap Calbeck condemned, denying coverage whenever an OCS employee’s injury—wholly attributable to platform operations—occurs at a shoreside facility integrated into those operations, as Juan Valladolid’s plainly was.

This Court’s later treatment of OCSLA in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), likewise confirms that Congress designed the Act to furnish a complete remedial scheme for injuries and deaths connected to Shelf operations, treating fixed structures and their associated activities as an integrated whole rather than as isolated points on a map. Id. at 355-57. That integrated understanding is incompatible with parsing coverage clause-by-clause according to which side of an arbitrary jurisdictional line an employee’s body came to rest.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
