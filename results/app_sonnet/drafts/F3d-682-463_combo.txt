ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred by Resolving Disputed Questions of Motive and Intent on Summary Judgment.

Whether Ottobre harassed Wasek "because of" his sex is a question of motive and intent that a jury, not a court on paper submissions, must resolve. The Supreme Court has long cautioned that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases where "motive and intent play leading roles" and the truth can only be tested through live testimony. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). That admonition applies with full force here: the record shows repeated sexual touching, sexually explicit remarks directed at Wasek's body ("you've got a pretty mouth," "boy you have pretty lips"), and a supervisory chain that dismissed the conduct as horseplay. A reasonable jury could conclude from this evidence that Ottobre's conduct was sexual in nature and directed at Wasek because he was male. On summary judgment, "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts . . . must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). The district court instead drew every inference against Wasek, crediting Arrow Energy's characterization of the conduct as personal animus untethered to sex, even though the conduct itself was explicitly sexual and physically invasive. Where, as here, the evidence permits conflicting inferences about a defendant's state of mind, the case is not suited to disposition by affidavit. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (recognizing that credibility and inference-drawing from conflicting evidence are functions committed to the trier of fact). The judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.

II. Sexually Explicit Conduct Directed at an Employee's Body Satisfies the "Because of Sex" Requirement of Title VII and the Elliott-Larsen Act.

Title VII forbids an employer from discriminating against an employee "because of such individual's . . . sex." 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). Courts must give that language its ordinary meaning, for "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress did not limit the statute's reach to conduct motivated by desire for the opposite sex, nor did it exempt conduct merely because it occurs between members of the same sex. Where a defendant's actions are themselves sexual in character—touching another man's buttocks, prodding him with tools in that same area, and commenting on his lips and mouth in sexually suggestive terms—the sex-specific nature of the conduct is itself evidence sufficient to satisfy the statutory causation element.

This reading is consistent with the Fifth and Seventh Circuits' early and generous construction of Title VII's sex provision. In Weeks v. Southern Bell Telephone & Telegraph Co., the court held that exceptions to Title VII's protections must be read narrowly, explaining that the bona fide occupational qualification defense "was in fact meant to be an extremely narrow exception to the general prohibition of discrimination on the basis of sex." 408 F.2d 228, 236 (5th Cir. 1969). Similarly, in Bowe v. Colgate-Palmolive Co., the Seventh Circuit refused to permit facially neutral policies to mask sex-based treatment, insisting that employment decisions be judged by the individual employee's actual capacities rather than generalized assumptions tied to sex. 416 F.2d 711 (7th Cir. 1969). Both decisions reflect the principle that Title VII's sex provision must be construed to reach the full range of conduct Congress intended to eliminate, not confined by narrow, pre-existing assumptions about what "sex discrimination" must look like. And as the court recognized in Quarles v. Philip Morris, Inc., Title VII was enacted to dismantle discriminatory patterns and practices, not merely isolated acts fitting a preconceived template. 279 F. Supp. 505 (E.D. Va. 1968). Because Ottobre's conduct was explicitly and repeatedly sexual, a jury could find it was directed at Wasek "because of" his sex within the meaning of Title VII, and, by extension, the Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act, which Michigan courts construe coextensively with federal law.

III. Arrow Energy Is Liable for Failing to Remedy Harassment of Which It Had Actual Knowledge.

An employer bears responsibility for workplace hazards it knows about and has the power to correct. In Ira S. Bushey & Sons, Inc. v. United States, Judge Friendly explained that liability properly attaches where the risk of harm is one arising out of and incidental to the very activity the employer has undertaken to control. 398 F.2d 167 (2d Cir. 1968). Here, three levels of supervision—Tripp, Smillie, and Strong—received direct, repeated reports of Ottobre's sexual touching and remarks. Rather than intervening, Tripp threatened Wasek with a "starvation schedule," Smillie suggested Wasek "kick [Ottobre's] ass," and Strong told the men to "duke it out." This is not a case of an employer unaware of misconduct; it is a case of an employer informed again and again, and choosing inaction. Under the standards articulated above, that failure created a jury question on Arrow Energy's liability that summary judgment improperly foreclosed.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed.
