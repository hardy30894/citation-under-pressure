**ARGUMENT**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt, including the statutory requirement that the conspiracy existed within the applicable limitations period. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); *United States v. Lozano*, 511 U.S. 100, 104 (1994). The D.C. Circuit erred in concluding that petitioners—who presented evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders separated before the limitations period—bore the burden of proving their withdrawal from the conspiracy, thereby relieving the government of its constitutional obligation to prove that each petitioner remained a member during the relevant period.

**I. The Government’s Burden Includes Proof That the Conspiracy Continued Into the Statute of Limitations Period**

A conspiracy conviction requires proof that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at some point within the statute of limitations. *Lozano*, 511 U.S. at 104. The government must prove this element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. Here, the government indicted petitioners for a conspiracy that allegedly spanned from 1995 to 2003. The statute of limitations for conspiracy is five years. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3282. Thus, the government was required to prove that the conspiracy—and each petitioner’s membership in it—existed after 1998.

Petitioners presented evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, severed their relationship before 1998, effectively ending the single conspiracy alleged in the indictment. *Cf. United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 462 (1978) (conspiracy terminates when its objective is abandoned or when no member continues to act in furtherance of the agreement). Once petitioners raised this evidence, the government bore the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that petitioners remained members. *See United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1236 (7th Cir. 1981) (government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the limitations period). The trial court’s contrary instruction—that petitioners had to prove withdrawal—impermissibly shifted the burden.

**II. Withdrawal Is a Defense That Relieves the Government of Proving Continued Membership, but Only If the Defendant Proves Withdrawal by a Preponderance of the Evidence**

This Court has long recognized that a defendant may avoid liability for conspiracy by proving withdrawal. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). Withdrawal is an affirmative defense; the defendant must show that he took affirmative steps to defeat or disavow the conspiracy. *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106, 113 (2013). However, withdrawal is not the only mechanism by which a defendant can challenge the government’s proof of the temporal element. When the defendant presents evidence that the conspiracy itself ended before the limitations period, the government must still prove that the defendant was a member during that period. The burden of persuasion on the elements of the offense never shifts to the defendant. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 215 (1977) (due process requires that the state prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt).

Here, petitioners did not merely claim personal withdrawal; they argued that the conspiracy alleged by the government had terminated before the limitations period. The government’s theory of a single conspiracy depended on the continued collaboration of Moore and Gray. When the evidence showed that Moore and Gray had split before 1998, the government was obligated to prove that the conspiracy continued—and that petitioners were still members—beyond a reasonable doubt. The D.C. Circuit’s imposition of a burden on petitioners to prove withdrawal conflated the distinct concepts of “withdrawal” and “termination of the conspiracy.” *See United States v. Lozano*, 511 U.S. at 104 (conspiracy ends when its objective is achieved or abandoned). The court’s error allowed the government to obtain a conviction without proving the essential element of timeliness.

**III. The Government’s Burden Is Not Satisfied by Inferring Continued Membership From the Mere Fact of Conspiracy Formation**

The government cannot rely on the defendant’s initial membership to infer continued membership years later. *See United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1236. The D.C. Circuit’s decision effectively permitted the jury to presume that petitioners remained members of the conspiracy unless they proved otherwise. This presumption violates due process. *See Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 524 (1979) (jury instruction that shifts burden of proof to defendant on an element of the offense is unconstitutional). The Constitution requires proof of each element, not a presumption that the element continues. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Conflicts With This Court’s Precedents and Will Chill the Exercise of the Right to Present a Defense**

If the government may rest on an inference of continued membership, defendants who challenge the existence of the conspiracy during the limitations period will be forced to prove a negative—that they did not remain members. This is precisely the kind of burden-shifting that the Constitution forbids. *See Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 215. The D.C. Circuit’s rule also discourages defendants from presenting evidence of a conspiracy’s termination, because doing so risks being saddled with a burden of proof they cannot satisfy. This Court should reverse and hold that the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member during that period.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the government’s constitutional burden of proof.
