**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s Denial of Access to the Good News Club Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because the Club Was Seeking to Engage in Religious Worship, Not Merely Religious or Moral Discussion.**

The First Amendment does not compel public schools to open their facilities to religious worship under the guise of free speech. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Supreme Court recognized that a university’s open forum policy could exclude religious worship services, even if such services included speech, because the university had a compelling interest in avoiding the appearance of state endorsement of religion. *Id.* at 271-72. The Court distinguished between religious *discussion* and religious *worship*, noting that the latter falls outside the protections of free speech when conducted in a public forum. *Id.* at 269 n.6 (quoting *Healy v. James*, 408 U.S. 169, 186 (1972), for the proposition that "the First Amendment does not require a state to finance speech it disfavors").

Here, the Good News Club’s activities—singing hymns, reciting scripture, praying, and inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior—constitute religious worship, not mere moral or character instruction. The Second Circuit correctly characterized these activities as "quintessentially religious," distinguishing them from secular moral development programs like the Boy Scouts or 4-H Club. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000) (citing *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (O’Connor, J., concurring) ("[R]eligious worship services . . . are not speech about religious topics but are speech consisting of religious worship.")).

Milford’s policy explicitly excludes religious worship, and the Club’s proposed activities fall squarely within that prohibition. The school’s denial was not viewpoint discrimination but a neutral exclusion of religious worship from a limited public forum. *See Healy*, 408 U.S. at 187 (holding that a state may regulate speech in a limited public forum "in a nondiscriminatory manner and without regard to the content of the speech").

**II. Milford’s Policy Was Justified by the Establishment Clause Because Permitting the Club’s Activities Would Have Conveyed State Endorsement of Religion.**

The Establishment Clause prohibits government from endorsing or advancing religion. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 222 (1963) ("[T]he State may not employ mechanisms which ‘aid or oppose’ any religion."). In *School Dist. of Abington Township v. Schempp*, the Court struck down mandatory Bible readings in public schools, emphasizing that such practices "place[] the student in the position of an involuntary captive audience" and "convey[] a message of state approval of religion." *Id.* at 224-25.

Permitting the Good News Club to conduct worship services in Milford’s facilities would similarly convey state endorsement of Christianity. The Club’s activities—prayer, scripture recitation, and evangelism—are indistinguishable from religious worship services. As the Second Circuit noted, the Club’s meetings were not merely discussions of morality from a religious perspective but included "singing songs, hearing [a] Bible lesson and memorizing scripture," followed by prayer and an invitation to accept Christ. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 506. Such activities, when conducted in a public school, would inevitably be perceived as state-sponsored religion, violating the Establishment Clause.

The Court has long held that the government may not "aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 103-04 (1968) (citing *Everson v. Bd. of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947)). Milford’s policy avoids this constitutional pitfall by excluding all religious worship, not just Christianity. The Club’s claim that it was merely discussing morality is unavailing; the activities it proposed were inherently religious in nature and would have impermissibly endorsed Christianity in a public school setting.

**III. Milford’s Policy Was Neutral and Generally Applicable, Not Targeted at the Club’s Religious Viewpoint.**

The First Amendment does not require the government to permit speech that conflicts with its own policies, even in a limited public forum. In *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966), the Court upheld a trespass conviction for protestors who refused to leave a jailhouse driveway, rejecting the argument that the state’s property could not be used to exclude speech. The Court held that the state "has the power to control the use of its property for its own lawful nondiscriminatory purpose," and that "[t]he United States Constitution does not forbid a State to control the use of its own property for such lawful purposes."

Milford’s policy is neutral and generally applicable: it excludes all religious worship, not just the Good News Club’s viewpoint. The Club’s attempt to recast its activities as moral instruction is unpersuasive; the record clearly shows that its meetings included prayer, scripture recitation, and evangelism—activities that fall outside the protections of free speech in a public forum. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6 (distinguishing between religious discussion and religious worship).

**Conclusion**

Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club did not violate the First Amendment because the Club sought to engage in religious worship, not merely religious or moral discussion. The school’s policy was justified by the Establishment Clause, as permitting the Club’s activities would have conveyed state endorsement of religion. Finally, Milford’s policy was neutral and generally applicable, not targeted at the Club’s viewpoint. The judgment below should be affirmed.
