ARGUMENT

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this action, and the judgment below must therefore be vacated. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 94–95 (1998) (jurisdiction is a threshold question that a federal court must resolve before reaching the merits).

I. The Pressls’ Complaint Does Not Arise Under Federal Law for Purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1331.

Federal removal jurisdiction is governed by the well-pleaded complaint rule: whether a case “arises under” federal law is determined by looking at what the plaintiff has actually pleaded, not at defenses the defendant may raise. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149 (1908). A case may not be removed on the basis of a federal defense, “even if the defense is anticipated in the plaintiff’s complaint, and even if both parties concede that the federal defense is the only question truly at issue.” Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386, 393 (1987). It is equally settled that “the plaintiff is the master of the complaint.” Id. at 392, 398–99 (plaintiff may “avoid federal jurisdiction by exclusive reliance on state law”).

Here, the Pressls’ complaint asks a Virginia court to construe a private instrument—an easement conveyed by their predecessors to APCO in 1960—and to declare the parties’ respective rights under it. Easements are creatures of state property and contract law, and their construction is a quintessential state-law question. See Franchise Tax Bd. of Cal. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust, 463 U.S. 1, 27–28 (1983) (federal jurisdiction depends on the nature of the right asserted, not on the possible relevance of federal regulation). Nothing on the face of the complaint invokes the Federal Power Act, FERC’s license, or any federal regulation as a source of the Pressls’ claimed right to build a dock; the complaint instead asks the court to interpret language granting APCO the right to “overflow and/or affect” the property and to remove structures “below the contour the elevation of which is 800 feet.” That the property happens to sit within the boundary of a FERC-licensed hydroelectric project, and that APCO’s defense to the suit may rest on the license, does not transform the Pressls’ state-law declaratory judgment claim into one arising under federal law. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804, 808–09 (1986) (federal question jurisdiction cannot rest on a defendant’s anticipated federal defense).

Nor does this case fall within the “special and small category” of state-law claims that nonetheless arise under federal law because they necessarily raise a disputed and substantial federal issue. Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh, 547 U.S. 677, 699 (2006); Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). Under Grable, federal jurisdiction over an embedded federal issue is proper only where the issue is (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution in federal court without disturbing the congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. 545 U.S. at 314. The dispositive question here—what the 1960 easement’s text permits APCO to do below the 800-foot contour—can be, and routinely is, resolved by state courts applying ordinary principles of easement construction. See id. at 318–19 (distinguishing the “rare” case implicating a substantial federal issue from “the mine run” of cases in which state law furnishes the rule of decision). Any reference to APCO’s FERC license would arise, if at all, only as a matter of defense or as background regarding the scope of APCO’s property interest—not as an essential element of the Pressls’ claim. That falls well short of the substantiality Grable requires, and permitting jurisdiction on this record would open the door to federal jurisdiction over any easement dispute involving federally licensed infrastructure, upsetting the state-federal balance Grable was careful to preserve. 545 U.S. at 318–20.

II. The District Court Lacked Jurisdiction Under 16 U.S.C. § 825p.

Section 825p confers exclusive federal jurisdiction over “violations” of the Federal Power Act and its implementing regulations and orders, and over “suits in equity and actions at law brought to enforce any liability or duty created by” the Act or its regulations and orders. 16 U.S.C. § 825p. That provision does not reach this case. The Pressls have not sued to enforce any duty or liability created by the Act, FERC’s regulations, or the Smith Mountain license; they have sued to enforce rights created by a private easement executed decades before FERC issued any order concerning the Pressls’ specific property. The mere existence of a FERC license authorizing APCO’s project does not convert every dispute about the scope of APCO’s privately granted easement into a suit “to enforce” a duty created by the Act. Cf. City of Tacoma v. Taxpayers of Tacoma, 357 U.S. 320, 336 (1958) (recognizing the limited scope of federal jurisdiction attaching to FERC licensing matters). The district court therefore erred in relying on § 825p, and in concluding that the Pressls were obligated to exhaust remedies before FERC before litigating the meaning of their own easement in state court.

III. The Case Must Be Vacated and Remanded to State Court.

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under both 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p, it had no authority to reach the merits of APCO’s motion to dismiss. See Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 94. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c), a case removed without jurisdiction “shall be remanded.” This Court should vacate the judgment below and remand with instructions to remand the case to the Circuit Court of Franklin County, Virginia.
