ARGUMENT

The Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” from a debtor’s estate. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The plain meaning of that term, confirmed by the structure and purpose of the exemption, limits its reach to funds that are set aside for the debtor’s own retirement. An inherited individual retirement account (“inherited IRA”) does not satisfy that standard. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that such an account is not exempt.

**I. The Text of § 522 Exempts Only Funds That Are the Debtor’s Retirement Funds.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. The operative term is “retirement funds.” That phrase unambiguously refers to funds held for the purpose of retirement. As the Seventh Circuit recognized, “[a]n inherited IRA does not have the economic attributes of a retirement vehicle, because the money cannot be held in the account until the current owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 563 (7th Cir. 2013). The statute does not exempt “funds that were once retirement funds” or “funds that originated in a retirement account,” but only “retirement funds” themselves. Id.

The debtor’s argument that the statute omits the word “debtor’s” and therefore covers any retirement funds regardless of ownership is unpersuasive. The term “retirement funds” carries an inherent possessive quality: it describes funds that belong to someone for retirement. When those funds pass to a non-spousal beneficiary, they cease to be “retirement funds” in the beneficiary’s hands. The Internal Revenue Code itself distinguishes between an IRA and an “inherited IRA,” 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C), and imposes rules that prevent the inherited account from functioning as a retirement vehicle. An inherited IRA must begin distributing its assets within a year of the original owner’s death, 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); no new contributions can be made, 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); and the balance cannot be rolled over into the beneficiary’s own IRA. Id. These features confirm that the inherited account is not the beneficiary’s “retirement funds” but rather a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle for current consumption.

**II. The Statutory Context Reinforces the Plain Meaning.**

Other exemptions in § 522 use the possessive “debtor’s,” but the absence of that word in the retirement-funds exemption does not support the broad reading the debtor advances. As the Seventh Circuit explained, the omission serves a different purpose: it ensures that new contributions to a retirement fund made during bankruptcy are also exempt, without a temporal restriction. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. That is a far cry from converting a decedent’s retirement funds into the debtor’s retirement funds. See *Patterson v. Shumate*, 504 U.S. 753, 758 (1992) (exemptions are to be applied according to their terms, not extended by implication).

The homestead exemption provides a useful analogy. Section 522(b)(3)(A) exempts a “domicile” in which the debtor resided for at least 730 days before filing. If a debtor inherits a house that was the decedent’s home, the debtor cannot claim the homestead exemption unless the house was her own home for the required period. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. The exemption depends on how the debtor uses the property, not how the decedent used it. So too with retirement funds: the exemption depends on whether the funds are the debtor’s retirement funds, not whether they were once the decedent’s.

**III. The Purpose of the Exemption Confirms That Inherited IRAs Are Not Exempt.**

Congress intended the retirement-funds exemption to protect assets that are “set aside for retirement” and thus not available for pre-retirement consumption. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561. An inherited IRA, by contrast, must be distributed to the beneficiary before the beneficiary’s retirement. The money can be “freely used for current consumption.” Id. at 562. Allowing debtors to shield such funds from creditors would undermine the central purpose of the bankruptcy exemption: to preserve assets that are genuinely needed for retirement, not to create a windfall for beneficiaries.

The debtor’s policy argument—that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage savings for bequests—proves too much. As the Seventh Circuit observed, “[o]ne could equally say that it would promote savings to hold that any asset acquired from one’s relatives by will, insurance, annuity, or survivorship designation is exempt from creditors’ claims. That is not remotely what § 522 provides.” *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564 (citing *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (statutes do not pursue their purposes at all costs)). Congress chose a specific exemption for retirement funds, not a general exemption for inherited wealth.

**IV. The Circuit Split Should Be Resolved in Favor of the Plain Meaning.**

The Fifth Circuit, in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), held that inherited IRAs are exempt because the statute does not require the funds to be the debtor’s retirement funds. That approach ignores the plain meaning of “retirement funds” and the fundamental economic reality that an inherited IRA is not a retirement vehicle for the beneficiary. The Eighth Circuit Bankruptcy Appellate Panel in *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), reached the same conclusion as the Fifth Circuit, but that reasoning has been rejected by the Seventh Circuit and should be rejected here.

The better-reasoned view is that an inherited IRA does not constitute “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands. The Seventh Circuit’s decision is correct and should be affirmed.
