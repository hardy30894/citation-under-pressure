The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The respondent argues that it can, and that the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar fee in this case was not an abuse of discretion.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that district courts have discretion to award attorneys' fees in excess of the lodestar amount in exceptional cases. In Missouri State Life Insurance v. Jones, 290 U.S. 199 (1933), the Court acknowledged that attorneys' fees can be awarded as part of the costs in certain cases. Similarly, in United States v. Waters, 133 U.S. 208 (1890), the Court noted that courts have discretion to allow counsel fees in certain cases.

The Court has also recognized that the quality of representation and results obtained are relevant factors in determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. In Savings Bank v. Ward, 100 U.S. 195 (1880), the Court emphasized the importance of exercising reasonable care and skill in the performance of an attorney's duties. In Walling v. Harnischfeger Corp., 325 U.S. 427 (1945), the Court considered the quality of representation in determining whether a wage agreement complied with the Fair Labor Standards Act.

Furthermore, in Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc., 390 U.S. 400 (1968), the Court held that a successful plaintiff in a Title II suit should ordinarily recover an attorney's fee unless special circumstances would render such an award unjust. This decision highlights the importance of considering the results obtained in determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

Other cases have also recognized the relevance of the quality of representation and results obtained in determining attorneys' fees. In Hilborn v. United States, 163 U.S. 342 (1896), the Court considered the district attorney's services in defending habeas corpus cases in determining his fees. In Bachman v. Lawson, 109 U.S. 659 (1884), the Court interpreted a statute allowing for the award of attorneys' fees in certain cases.

In 149 Madison Avenue Corp. v. Asselta, 331 U.S. 199 (1947), the Court considered the hourly rate derived from a weekly wage agreement in determining whether it complied with the Fair Labor Standards Act. In Hopkins v. Cohen, 390 U.S. 530 (1968), the Court interpreted the Social Security Act's provision for attorneys' fees, highlighting the importance of considering the results obtained in determining a reasonable fee.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court has discretion to enhance the lodestar fee based on the quality of performance and results obtained in exceptional cases. The respondent respectfully submits that the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar fee in this case was not an abuse of discretion, and that the Eleventh Circuit's affirmance of that decision was correct.

The petitioners' argument that an enhancement based on quality of performance and results obtained is never permissible is not supported by the case law. The authorities cited above demonstrate that the quality of representation and results obtained are relevant factors in determining a reasonable attorneys' fee, and that district courts have discretion to award fees in excess of the lodestar amount in exceptional cases.

Therefore, the respondent respectfully requests that the Court affirm the Eleventh Circuit's decision and hold that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained in exceptional cases.
