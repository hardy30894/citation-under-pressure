### Argument

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and its mirror provision, § 522(d)(12), protect only “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation” under the listed provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(12). An inherited IRA does not fit that description because, by definition, it is no longer a vehicle for the owner’s retirement.

This Court should follow the Seventh Circuit’s reasoning in **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d 559 (7th Cir. 2013), which correctly held that inherited IRAs are not exempt retirement funds. As that court explained, an inherited IRA differs in crucial ways from a traditional IRA. No additional contributions may be made. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). The account cannot be rolled over. Id. And, most importantly, the beneficiary must begin distributions soon after the original owner’s death, with payout completed in as little as five years. 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 560–61. Those rules show that an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 561.

The statutory text confirms that result. Section 522 protects “retirement funds,” not merely assets that once served as retirement funds in someone else’s hands. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C). That distinction matters. As the Seventh Circuit recognized, at the time of bankruptcy the inherited IRA no longer represented anyone’s retirement savings; it had been the decedent’s retirement fund, but after death it became a mandatory-distribution account available for current consumption. **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 562–63. The Code does not extend bankruptcy exemptions to assets that have lost their retirement character simply because they passed by inheritance.

Petitioners rely on decisions such as **In re Chilton**, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and **In re Nessa**, 426 B.R. 312 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010), but those cases read the statute too broadly. The fact that the Code does not say “the debtor’s retirement funds” does not mean any funds with a retirement-related origin are exempt forever. **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 562–64. Congress knew how to use debtor-specific or time-specific language in § 522. See, e.g., 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). The absence of those words in § 522(b)(3)(C) does not create a loophole for inherited assets that are no longer retirement savings. **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 564–65.

Nor does the tax treatment of inherited IRAs change the analysis. The Bankruptcy Code requires both tax-exempt status under the specified IRC provisions and “retirement funds.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(12). Congress’s decision to preserve tax deferral for inherited IRAs does not mean it intended to shield them from creditors in bankruptcy. **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 563. As this Court has recognized, a statute’s policy goals do not justify rewriting its text. **Rodriguez v. United States**, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987).

This Court should also reject Petitioners’ appeal to the broad remedial purposes of bankruptcy exemptions. While exemptions are construed to give debtors a fresh start, that principle cannot override statutory limits. See **Schwab v. Reilly**, 560 U.S. 770, 791–92 (2010). Here, Congress chose to protect retirement funds, not inherited accounts available for mandatory withdrawals and current spending. **In re Clark**, 714 F.3d at 566. Expanding the exemption would “shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption.” Id. at 563.

The judgment below should be reversed. Inherited IRAs are not exempt under § 522 because they are not retirement funds in the hands of the debtor.
