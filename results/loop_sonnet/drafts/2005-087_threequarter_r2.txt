**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, concluding that the formulation of a State's rule of criminal responsibility is a matter for the State to decide and that Oregon's choice of test and allocation of burden did not violate due process. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows a fortiori that a State may also determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, emphasizing that questions of personal accountability for criminal conduct have traditionally been left to development through the political process and state law rather than through a single constitutional formula imposed by this Court. *Id.* at 535–37. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation.

This Court has likewise recognized that the allocation of burdens surrounding an insanity defense, including the burden of proving mental incapacity, is a matter within the traditional competence of the courts developing federal common law and, by extension, the States. See *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895) (discussing the burden of proof on the issue of insanity). Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits mental-disease evidence to be considered fully on the question of insanity—is consistent with that traditional allocation.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). The Fourteenth Amendment leaves the States free to shape their own criminal procedures, subject only to the requirement of fundamental fairness, and does not itself supply a code of criminal procedure binding upon the States in every particular. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 110–11 (1908).

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence for a limited purpose despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, holding that such state evidentiary determinations do not violate due process absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), is whether the proceeding was conducted with such fundamental unfairness as to deprive the defendant of due process, the Court there instructing that the aim of the due process requirement is to prevent fundamental unfairness in the use of evidence, not merely to police the correctness of state evidentiary rulings. *Id.* at 236. Here, the trial court permitted Clark to introduce the full range of expert psychiatric testimony and to have it evaluated by the factfinder on the issue of insanity—the very evidence he now claims should also negate intent. Nothing in *Davis*, which addressed the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be simultaneously admissible, unconstrained by the burdens attending that defense, for an entirely separate purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people," *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, and this Court should affirm.
