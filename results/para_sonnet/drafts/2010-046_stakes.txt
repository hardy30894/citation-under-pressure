**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court's Population Reduction Order Satisfies the PLRA's Requirements for Prisoner Release Orders.**

Congress did not forbid population caps; it conditioned them. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3), a court may enter a "prisoner release order" only after a three-judge panel finds, by clear and convincing evidence, that crowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violation and that no other relief will remedy it, and only after the defendant has had a reasonable time to comply with prior, less intrusive orders. That is precisely the course followed below. The panel entered its order only after years of stipulated remedial decrees, the appointment of a Receiver to manage medical care, and repeated findings that the State remained unable to comply. Congress itself recognized that population limits may be the only adequate remedy for chronic, systemic Eighth Amendment violations in overcrowded prisons; this Court held as much in *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), affirming a district court's order capping the population of an isolation unit because "a court of equity has traditionally had the power to fashion a remedy that will assure a unitary system" free of unconstitutional conditions. The three-judge court's order here is a direct descendant of that traditional equitable authority, now expressly codified and constrained by the PLRA's procedural safeguards—safeguards the panel satisfied.

**II. The Underlying Constitutional Violation Is Well Established and Warrants Structural Relief.**

The Eighth Amendment forbids the "unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain," *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976), and prohibits prison officials from acting with deliberate indifference to inmates' serious medical needs or to conditions posing a substantial risk of serious harm, *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 828 (1994). Whether conditions violate the Amendment depends on "the totality of the circumstances," *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 362 (1981) (Brennan, J., concurring in judgment); *see id.* at 347 (majority opinion assessing conditions as a whole). The district court's extensive factual findings—entered after evidentiary hearings spanning years—established that overcrowding was the primary driver of inadequate medical and mental health care, resulting in preventable deaths and suffering. Those findings are entitled to deference under the clearly-erroneous standard that governs review of a trial court's factual determinations, *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573–74 (1985) ("[A] finding is 'clearly erroneous' when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed."). Petitioners identify no such mistake here; they instead ask this Court to substitute its own assessment of the record for that of the tribunal that heard the evidence firsthand.

**III. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn, Extends No Further Than Necessary, and Is the Least Intrusive Means to Correct the Violation.**

Section 3626(a)(1)(A) requires that prospective relief in prison cases be "narrowly drawn," "extend no further than necessary to correct the violation," and constitute "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation," while giving "substantial weight" to public safety. The panel's order satisfies each requirement. Rather than dictating the mechanism of population reduction, the order left California free to choose among a range of measures—parole reform, credit-earning programs, out-of-state transfers, or new construction—reserving the more coercive step of direct release only as a last resort. This flexibility mirrors the deference this Court has afforded to state officials implementing structural remedies in institutional-reform litigation. *See Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367, 383 (1992) (courts must accord "flexibility" to institutional defendants and take account of "the fact that a court has continuing supervisory authority" over ongoing constitutional violations); *cf. Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343, 362–63 (1996) (courts should give prison officials the first opportunity to formulate remedies before imposing more specific relief). The panel did not seize control of prison administration; it set an outer population ceiling calibrated to the record evidence of what capacity the prisons' health-care infrastructure could safely support, leaving the State's own officials to devise the means of compliance.

**IV. The Panel Gave Substantial Weight to Public Safety.**

The record demonstrates that the panel considered extensive expert testimony on recidivism, classification systems, and alternative-to-incarceration programs before concluding that a measured reduction, implemented over time, would not compromise public safety. This is the very inquiry the PLRA demands, and nothing in the statute requires a court to reach a particular substantive outcome on that question—only that it undertake the analysis in good faith, as this panel plainly did.

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge panel's order tracks precisely the statutory pathway Congress created in the PLRA for addressing intractable, unconstitutional prison conditions caused by overcrowding. It followed years of failed, less intrusive remedies; it rests on well-supported factual findings; and it affords California substantial flexibility in achieving compliance while safeguarding public safety. The judgment below should be affirmed.
