ARGUMENT

The district court’s finding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter Danelsy is clearly erroneous. This Court must reverse because the record shows only an ambiguous, conditional agreement that falls short of the unambiguous consent required to defeat a Hague Convention petition. Under the clearly erroneous standard, a finding must stand unless “the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); accord United States v. Yellow Cab Co., 338 U.S. 338, 342 (1949). Such is the case here.

I. THE DISTRICT COURT’S FINDING OF CONSENT IS UNSUPPORTED BY THE EVIDENCE

Consent to a child’s removal or retention must be “clear and unequivocal.” No pre-1970 case directly addresses this standard in the Hague Convention context, but the principle that consent must be voluntary, knowing, and unambiguous is fundamental to contract and tort law. See, e.g., Standard Oil Co. v. United States, 221 U.S. 1, 58 (1911) (requiring “intelligent and free” consent). The district court relied on Gonzalez-Caballero’s acts of giving Mena Danelsy’s documents and accompanying them to the airport. But those acts are fully consistent with a temporary arrangement—not with permanent relinquishment of custody.

Gonzalez-Caballero consistently testified that she allowed Danelsy to travel to the United States only for two weeks until she could obtain immigration papers and rejoin her daughter. Her testimony was “confused” only in the sense that she described her hopes for eventual reunification, not because she contradicted the temporary nature of the arrangement. The district court erred by treating her hope of future immigration as evidence of consent to indefinite retention. See Steele v. United States, 267 U.S. 505, 510 (1925) (evidence of intent must be evaluated in context of surrounding circumstances). The record shows no statement by Gonzalez-Caballero that Mena could keep Danelsy permanently. Such an extraordinary concession would require “clear and convincing proof,” which is absent here. Compare United States v. Oregon State Medical Society, 343 U.S. 326, 339 (1952) (requiring strong evidence to support a finding of anticompetitive intent).

II. GONZALEZ-CABALLERO’S POST-REMOVAL CONDUCT CONTRADICTS CONSENT

Immediately after Danelsy’s departure, Gonzalez-Caballero contacted Panamanian child custody authorities, police, the Judge Advocate General’s office, and even the Panamanian President. She sought help from multiple agencies and eventually traveled to the United States alone to recover her daughter. Such prompt and persistent efforts to regain custody are “inconsistent with the idea of consent.” Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Hill, 193 U.S. 551, 555 (1904) (subsequent conduct may explain prior ambiguous act). A person who freely consents to permanent separation does not immediately seek legal intervention. The district court discounted this evidence without adequate explanation, thereby committing clear error. United States Gypsum, 333 U.S. at 395.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT MISWEIGHED THE TESTIMONY

The district court credited Mena’s account that Gonzalez-Caballero said she “could no longer adequately care for Danelsy.” But even if that statement were made, it does not establish consent to permanent removal; it expresses a temporary difficulty. Moreover, Mena’s own testimony reveals that he promised to help Gonzalez-Caballero immigrate, implying that he understood the arrangement was conditional. The trial court’s failure to consider this condition and its undue reliance on Mena’s self-serving version resulted in a finding that “cannot be reconciled with the plain language of the record.” Commissioner v. Duberstein, 363 U.S. 278, 291 (1960) (reversing factual finding based on erroneous inference). 

In short, the evidence, even viewed in the light most favorable to Mena, shows at most an ambiguous agreement. The law requires more. Because the district court’s finding of consent is clearly erroneous, this Court should reverse and order Danelsy’s return to Panama.
