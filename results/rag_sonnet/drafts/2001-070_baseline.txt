# ARGUMENT

**THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CATEGORICALLY BAR EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS**

## I. This Court's Decision in *Penry v. Lynaugh* Controls and Forecloses Petitioner's Claim

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely considered "whether it is cruel and unusual punishment to execute a mentally retarded person" and held that it is not. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on *Penry* in rejecting Atkins' identical claim. Nothing has changed in the constitutional analysis that would justify overruling that precedent.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is measured against "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion), and this Court has consistently looked to objective indicia of societal consensus—primarily state legislation—to determine whether such a consensus exists. In *Penry*, the Court found no such consensus barring execution of the mentally retarded, and the same objective evidence supports that conclusion today. At the time of *Penry*, only two States and the federal government had enacted statutes categorically exempting mentally retarded offenders from capital punishment. The mere fact that additional States have since joined that minority does not establish the kind of overwhelming national consensus this Court has required before declaring a punishment categorically unconstitutional under the Eighth Amendment.

## II. Individualized Consideration of Mental Retardation as a Mitigating Factor—Not a Categorical Bar—Satisfies the Eighth Amendment

Respondent does not dispute that mental retardation is a significant mitigating circumstance that sentencers must be permitted to consider. Indeed, in *Penry v. Johnson*, 532 U.S. 782 (2001), this Court reaffirmed that a capital sentencing jury must be given a vehicle to give effect to a defendant's mitigating evidence, including evidence of mental retardation. That case involved Johnny Paul Penry's second death sentence, and the Court held that Texas's supplemental jury instruction failed to allow "adequate consideration" of his mitigating evidence.

But *Penry I* and *Penry II* together demonstrate that the constitutional remedy for inadequate consideration of mental retardation is *individualized sentencing procedure*, not a categorical exemption from the death penalty altogether. The jury in Atkins' own resentencing heard extensive testimony regarding his intellectual limitations and nonetheless returned a sentence of death after weighing that evidence against the aggravating circumstances of his crime. This is precisely the individualized determination that the Eighth Amendment requires and that *Penry* contemplated as the appropriate mechanism for addressing diminished culpability.

Mental retardation, unlike a bright-line categorical characteristic such as chronological age, exists on a spectrum. As the classification systems discussed in *Penry* recognize, individuals with IQ scores in the range historically associated with "mild" retardation exhibit widely varying levels of functioning, moral understanding, and capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of their conduct. See *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 308 n.1 (1989) (noting the range of scores associated with mild retardation). A categorical rule sweeping in this diverse population risks exempting from capital punishment individuals who nonetheless possess the requisite moral culpability the jury is uniquely positioned to assess.

## III. This Court Has Repeatedly Declined to Constitutionalize Categorical Exemptions Based on Diminished Capacity, Leaving Such Determinations to Case-by-Case Sentencing

The dissenting opinions cited by petitioner from this Court's certiorari denials confirm, rather than undermine, the correctness of the *Penry* framework. In case after case, individual Justices expressed the view that a particular defendant's mental retardation should have precluded a death sentence—but the Court as a whole declined to adopt a categorical constitutional rule to that effect. See, e.g., *Smith v. Francis*, 474 U.S. 925 (1985) (Marshall, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari, describing petitioner's IQ of 65); *Reed v. United States*, 479 U.S. 954 (1986) (Marshall, J., dissenting, describing petitioner's IQ "below 73"). These repeated denials of certiorari—despite squarely presented facts involving severe intellectual impairment—reflect the Court's considered judgment that the Eighth Amendment does not compel a categorical rule, and that individualized sentencing remains the constitutionally adequate vehicle for addressing diminished culpability due to mental retardation.

Similarly, in cases like *McCollum v. North Carolina*, 512 U.S. 1254 (1994), sentencing juries found mental retardation as a *mitigating circumstance* to be weighed against aggravating factors—precisely the individualized process the Eighth Amendment demands, rather than a categorical exclusion determined pretrial or as a matter of law.

## IV. Stare Decisis Counsels Strongly Against Overruling *Penry*

This Court should not lightly discard the reasoned holding of *Penry v. Lynaugh*. Atkins has identified no new objective evidence of a "national consensus" sufficient to overcome the presumption favoring adherence to precedent, particularly where—as here—the precedent rests on a considered assessment of state legislative practice that has not undergone the kind of dramatic transformation this Court has required in other Eighth Amendment contexts.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
