**Argument**

Section 1407(a) does not permit a transferee district court to assign a transferred case to itself for trial. Congress instructed that civil actions transferred for multidistrict proceedings are to be sent to a district “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That command is mandatory, not precatory. *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 35 (1998). The statute “obliges the Panel to remand any case not resolved in pretrial proceedings to the original district court.” *Id.* at 37.

The district court’s use of § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial cannot be squared with that text. Section 1404(a) authorizes a district court to transfer a civil action “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice.” But this Court has made clear that a specific statutory command controls over a general venue statute. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40-41. Here, § 1407(a) specifically addresses the lifecycle of multidistrict cases: transfer for pretrial proceedings, then remand. A transferee court therefore may not invoke § 1404(a) to do what § 1407(a) forbids. *Id.* at 37, 41-42.

The contrary approach would nullify the remand mandate. As the Court explained, allowing a transferee court to assign a case to itself would “contravene” the statutory instruction that the Panel “shall” remand the action after pretrial proceedings. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 34-35. Congress knew how to confer trial authority if it wished; it did not do so here. *Id.* at 37-40. The statute’s structure confirms the point: § 1407 was enacted to promote pretrial efficiency through centralized proceedings, not to permit the transferee court to become the trial court by unilateral venue transfer. *Id.* at 34-36.

The Court of Appeals erred in relying on prior cases and practice approving self-transfer. *Lexecon* rejected that line of authority, including *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and the judicially created rule that had allowed transferee courts to keep cases for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40-41. Whatever administrative convenience that practice may have served, it cannot override the statute’s plain command. *Id.* at 35, 42.

Nor can the result be defended as harmless or efficient. Congress’s policy choice was to centralize common pretrial matters while preserving remand to the originating district for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40. That balance reflects a deliberate limitation on multidistrict assignments, and courts are not free to revise it in the name of convenience. *Id.* at 41-42. As the Court stated, the statute “speaks with the clarity of an instruction, not a suggestion.” *Id.* at 35.

The judgment should be reversed. The transferee court lacked authority under § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial, and the case must be remanded to the transferor court as § 1407(a) requires. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 37, 40-42.
