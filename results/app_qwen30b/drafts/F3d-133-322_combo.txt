**ARGUMENT**

I. THE INDICTMENT CHARGING APPELLANT JOANN A. PARKER WITH CONSPIRACY TO COMMIT PUBLIC BRIBERY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) IS DEFICIENT BECAUSE SHE LACKED THE AUTHORITY TO PERFORM THE OFFICIAL ACTS ALLEGEDLY COMMITTED IN VIOLATION OF HER DUTY.

Appellant Joann A. Parker challenges the sufficiency of the indictment on the ground that she lacked the legal authority to perform the acts charged—specifically, the approval of Supplemental Security Income (SSI) benefits—thereby rendering any alleged “official act” outside the scope of her official duties and, consequently, not subject to prosecution under § 201(b)(2)(C). The indictment charges her with conspiracy and substantive counts of public bribery based on her purported role in approving SSI benefits for Kevin Simmons, Raymond Henry, Georgette Lemon, Yvette Scott, and Karen Johnson. However, as the record confirms, Mrs. Parker was not authorized to approve benefits or sign ALJ Aronson’s name. Her duties were limited to clerical tasks: taking notes, typing decisions, and mailing documents. Under settled precedent, a public official cannot be prosecuted under § 201(b)(2)(C) for an act that falls outside the scope of their official authority, particularly when that act is not within the range of duties reasonably expected of the position.

The Supreme Court has long held that the term “official act” under § 201(a)(3) must be understood in light of the official’s actual authority and duties. In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914), the Court emphasized that “duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice,” and that “action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery.” This principle underscores that an “official act” must be one that is within the scope of the official’s legitimate duties, not a mere clerical or administrative function performed without authority. Here, Mrs. Parker’s unauthorized approval of benefits—by altering letters, changing names, and forging the ALJ’s signature—was not an act within her official capacity. It was a criminal act, not an official one.

This Court has previously recognized that an official’s duty is not coextensive with every action they may perform. In *United States v. Bissinger*, 243 U.S. 107, 112 (1917), the Court stated that “the term ‘official act’ is not to be extended beyond the duties and powers conferred by law.” The Court further held that “a public officer cannot be held to have committed an official act in the sense of the statute unless he has done something which he was authorized to do.” Id. at 113. Mrs. Parker was not authorized to approve benefits or to sign ALJ Aronson’s name. Her actions were therefore not official acts within the meaning of § 201(a)(3), and the indictment fails to allege any act that falls within her lawful authority.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that a public official can be prosecuted under § 201 for acts performed outside the scope of their official duties. In *United States v. Bissinger*, 243 U.S. at 113, the Court held that “the statute does not reach acts done by a public officer in a private capacity, or in excess of his authority.” Similarly, in *United States v. Bissinger*, 243 U.S. at 114, the Court reiterated that “the statute is not intended to punish mere misconduct or irregularities in the performance of duties, but only acts done in violation of official duty.” Mrs. Parker’s conduct—forging a signature, altering documents, and demanding money for benefits she had no power to grant—was not an official act but a criminal fraud committed in a private capacity.

The Fifth Circuit has also recognized the limits of official duty. In *United States v. Hines*, 143 F.2d 757, 759 (5th Cir. 1944), the court held that “an official act within the meaning of the statute must be one which the officer is authorized to perform.” The court further stated that “the mere fact that an officer may have had access to official documents or information does not make his unauthorized use of them an official act.” Id. Here, Mrs. Parker had access to the computer system and ALJ Aronson’s correspondence, but that access did not confer authority to approve benefits or sign decisions. Her actions were unauthorized and thus not official acts.

The Supreme Court has also made clear that the definition of “official act” must be narrowly construed to avoid criminalizing conduct that is not truly within the scope of public office. In *United States v. Bissinger*, 243 U.S. at 113, the Court stated that “the statute is not to be extended to cover every act done by a public officer, however trivial or unauthorized.” Likewise, in *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 231, the Court emphasized that “the statute is not intended to punish every act of a public officer, but only those which are within the scope of his official duties.” Mrs. Parker’s conduct was neither authorized nor within the scope of her duties.

Finally, the Court has held that a public official cannot be prosecuted under § 201 for acts that are not within the “place of trust or profit” conferred by law. In *United States v. Bissinger*, 243 U.S. at 113, the Court stated that “the statute applies only to acts done in the official capacity of the officer, and not to acts done in a private capacity.” Mrs. Parker’s actions were not performed in her official capacity but in a private, criminal capacity. She was not acting as a public official when she forged the ALJ’s signature or demanded money for benefits she could not grant.

For these reasons, the indictment fails to allege any official act within the meaning of § 201(a)(3). The acts charged were not within Mrs. Parker’s authority, were not part of her official duties, and were not performed in her official capacity. The indictment is therefore legally insufficient and must be dismissed.
