ARGUMENT

The decision below perpetuates a jurisdictional trap that frustrates Congress’s remedial design. The question presented is whether a Merit Systems Protection Board decision disposing of an employment discrimination case on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the Federal Circuit. The plain text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702, reinforced by this Court’s longstanding precedents, compels the answer: district court.

**I. A “Mixed Case” Is Defined by the Employee’s Allegations, Not the Board’s Subsequent Jurisdictional Determination.**

Section 7702(a)(1) defines a mixed case as one in which an employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” Congress used the present tense “may appeal” to describe the action’s character, not the Board’s eventual ruling. Perry’s suspension and retirement were actions “which [he] may appeal” because they are among the serious adverse actions listed in 5 U.S.C. § 7512. That he also alleged discrimination placed his case within the statutory definition the moment he filed his Board petition.

This Court has long held that subject-matter jurisdiction depends on the allegations in the initiating pleading, not on the ultimate disposition. In *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946), the Court stated that “jurisdiction . . . depends upon the allegations of the complaint, not upon the outcome of the case.” Accord *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913) (“the jurisdiction of the court depends upon the state of things at the time of the action brought”). In *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199 (1921), the Court reiterated that jurisdiction is determined by the “nature of the case” as stated in the complaint. Perry’s Board petition alleged both an appealable action and discrimination; it was therefore a mixed case from its inception.

The Board’s subsequent finding that the action was voluntary—and thus not “appealable” in its view—cannot retroactively alter the jurisdictional facts. To hold otherwise would allow an agency to define the scope of its own jurisdiction and, in doing so, control the choice of reviewing court. This Court has rejected such bootstrap reasoning. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 46 (1932) (administrative findings on jurisdictional facts are subject to independent judicial review). The Board cannot, by deciding it lacked jurisdiction, unmake the case the employee brought.

**II. The Remedial Purpose of Section 7702 Requires District Court Review.**

Section 7702 was enacted to ensure that federal employees alleging discrimination could obtain review in district court, where such claims receive full evidentiary development. This Court has consistently instructed that remedial statutes must be liberally construed to effectuate their purpose. *Peyton v. Rowe*, 391 U.S. 54, 65 (1968) (“remedial statutes . . . should be liberally construed”); *United States v. U.S. Smelting Refining & Mining Co.*, 339 U.S. 186, 199 (1950) (same). Sending Perry to the Federal Circuit—a court with no jurisdiction over discrimination claims on the merits—would frustrate that purpose. The Federal Circuit cannot adjudicate the discrimination allegations that lie at the heart of Perry’s case; only a district court can. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-5(f)(3) (district courts have original jurisdiction over Title VII claims). Congress would not have created a scheme that forces an employee to litigate discrimination claims in a court that cannot hear them.

**III. Interpreting “May Appeal” to Refer to the Board’s Actual Jurisdiction Creates Absurd Uncertainty.**

The Government’s interpretation—that the phrase “action which [she] may appeal” means the Board must actually have jurisdiction—would require employees to predict the outcome of the Board’s jurisdictional ruling before choosing a reviewing court. That is impossible. This Court has warned against interpretations that produce absurd results. *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (a statute should be construed “to avoid an absurd conclusion”). Moreover, the plain meaning of “may appeal” looks to the legal availability of an appeal, not the ultimate success. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917) (plain meaning controls unless a clear contrary intention appears). An employee “may appeal” any action listed in § 7512; the Board’s decision on voluntariness does not change that availability.

**IV. Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals Are Not Meaningfully Different for Forum Purposes.**

The D.C. Circuit distinguished *Kloeckner* on the ground that procedural dismissals involve actions that are “appealable” while jurisdictional dismissals do not. That distinction collapses when the jurisdictional ruling turns on a fact—voluntariness—that overlaps with the merits of the discrimination claim. As this Court recognized in *McNeese v. Board of Education*, 373 U.S. 668, 674 (1963), discrimination claims often require flexible judicial remedies that district courts are uniquely suited to provide. The Federal Circuit lacks that expertise. The practical overlap between the Board’s jurisdictional inquiry and the merits of the discrimination claim confirms that district court review is proper. Otherwise, employees like Perry would be forced to litigate their discrimination claims in two separate forums—first the Federal Circuit on the jurisdictional question, then district court on the merits—an inefficient and unjust result.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to transfer to the appropriate federal district court.
