**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE "TRUE THREAT" EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN**

The First Amendment does not protect "true threats." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). But the Court has never defined a "true threat" as an objective matter. To the contrary, the Court has consistently required proof of the defendant’s *subjective* intent to threaten. *Sexton v. California*, 189 U.S. 319, 321 (1903) ("It was the *extorting* of the money by reason of *fear induced by the threat* that constituted the crime.") (emphasis added); *Brown v. Pierce*, 74 U.S. 205, 212 (1869) ("[T]he person threatened with slight injury to the person...ought to have sufficient resolution to resist such a threat.").

This Court’s precedents confirm that a threat is not punishable unless the speaker *intended* to threaten. In *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 104 (1942), the Court set aside a conviction where the defendant alleged that FBI agents had coerced his guilty plea by threats to "publish false statements and manufacture false evidence." The Court did not ask whether a reasonable person would have felt threatened; it asked whether the defendant *himself* had been threatened. *Id.* at 102. Similarly, in *Rogers v. Richmond*, 365 U.S. 534, 541 (1961), the Court suppressed a confession obtained by threats, not because a reasonable person would have felt coerced, but because the defendant *himself* had been overborne by fear.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a "true threat" exists whenever a reasonable speaker would foresee that a statement would be interpreted as a threat—finds no support in this Court’s decisions. *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991). This Court has never held that the First Amendment permits punishment of speech based solely on its *objective* effect. To the contrary, in *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948), the Court reversed a conviction where the defendant was tried for an offense not charged in the indictment, explaining that due process requires that a defendant be "informed of the charges against him." The same principle applies here: a defendant must be *informed*—by the statute’s terms—of the mental state required for guilt. The Third Circuit’s standard fails that requirement.

II. **THE STATUTE’S TEXT AND HISTORY CONFIRM THAT § 875(c) REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE INTENT**

Section 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of "any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another." 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). The word "threat" in this context has long been understood to require *intent*. In *Iron Mountain & Helena Railroad v. Johnson*, 119 U.S. 608, 614 (1887), the Court explained that a "threat" is an "expression of an intention to inflict injury." The Court did not ask whether a reasonable person would have felt threatened; it asked whether the speaker *intended* to inflict injury.

This understanding is consistent with the common law. In *Brown v. Pierce*, 74 U.S. at 212, the Court noted that duress requires threats of "loss of life," "loss of limb," or "imprisonment"—not threats that a reasonable person would find coercive. The same principle applies to § 875(c): the statute targets *intentional* threats, not speech that a reasonable person would interpret as threatening.

III. **THE COURT’S PRECEDENTS REJECT THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S OBJECTIVE STANDARD**

This Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to punish speech based on its *objective* effect. In *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 484 (1965), the Court held that a statute permitting the suppression of speech "on the ground that it is unconstitutional" could not be enforced unless the speaker *intended* to engage in protected activity. The Court did not ask whether a reasonable person would have found the speech threatening; it asked whether the speaker *intended* to engage in protected activity. *Id.* at 486.

Similarly, in *Lee v. Mississippi*, 332 U.S. 742, 748 (1948), the Court reversed a conviction where the defendant’s confession had been obtained by threats, explaining that the Due Process Clause requires that confessions be *voluntary*. The Court did not ask whether a reasonable person would have felt coerced; it asked whether the defendant *himself* had been overborne by fear. *Id.* at 747.

The Third Circuit’s rule—that a "true threat" exists whenever a reasonable speaker would foresee that a statement would be interpreted as a threat—finds no support in these decisions. The Court has never held that the First Amendment permits punishment of speech based solely on its *objective* effect. To the contrary, the Court has consistently required proof of the defendant’s *subjective* intent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The Court should hold that § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten, and that the Third Circuit’s objective standard violates the First Amendment.
