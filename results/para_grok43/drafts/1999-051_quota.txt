**ARGUMENT**

A federal habeas court may not entertain a claim that a state prisoner procedurally defaulted in state court unless the prisoner demonstrates cause for the default and actual prejudice. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause, but only if the prisoner first presented that claim to the state courts as an independent claim. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 489 (1986). The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding—that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may itself supply cause—contradicts this settled rule.

The exhaustion requirement demands that a prisoner “fairly present” each federal claim to the state courts before seeking federal relief. *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971); *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 518-19 (1982). A claim of appellate counsel’s ineffectiveness is no exception. *O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 845 (1999). When, as here, the state courts dismissed that claim as untimely, it is procedurally defaulted. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). Allowing the defaulted claim to excuse another default would permit prisoners to circumvent state procedural rules simply by labeling counsel ineffective—an end-run the exhaustion doctrine forbids.

Cause must be something external to the defense that cannot fairly be attributed to the prisoner. *Id.* at 753. An unexhausted ineffective-assistance claim fails this test. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488. The prisoner bears the risk of attorney error in post-conviction proceedings, *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-53, and must therefore present the ineffectiveness claim in the manner state law requires. *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 129 (1982). Only then may the federal court consider whether counsel’s performance was constitutionally deficient under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

The Sixth Circuit’s rule would also undermine federalism by forcing federal courts to adjudicate defaulted claims without the benefit of a state-court record. *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 88-90. Because Carpenter never properly presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio courts, that claim cannot serve as cause for the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
