**ARGUMENT**

The Court should hold that 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. In **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 689, 699, 701, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 2498, 2503, 2505, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001)**, this Court squarely construed the statute to avoid serious constitutional problems and held that post-removal-period detention is limited to a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. The statute “**does not permit indefinite detention**.” **Id. at 689, 121 S. Ct. at 2498.** It follows that when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued custody is not authorized. **Id. at 699, 121 S. Ct. at 2503.**

I. **The text of § 1231(a)(6), as construed in Zadvydas, contains a temporal limit.**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain aliens “**may be detained beyond the removal period**.” But in **Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 699, 121 S. Ct. at 2503**, the Court rejected the Government’s reading that this language permits detention for as long as removal remains merely possible. Instead, the Court interpreted the statute “**in light of the Constitution’s demands**” to mean that detention is limited to “**a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal**.” **Id. at 689, 121 S. Ct. at 2498.** That construction is controlling. See **United States v. Jin Fuey Moy, 241 U.S. 394, 401 (1916)** (courts avoid constructions raising grave constitutional doubts).

II. **Indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns.**

This Court construed § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit reasonable-time limitation because otherwise it would authorize potentially permanent confinement of aliens who have already been ordered removed. **Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 690, 121 S. Ct. at 2498.** The Court explained that the Government’s interpretation would raise “serious constitutional concerns,” including due process concerns. **Id. at 682, 121 S. Ct. at 2495.** The Due Process Clause does not permit punishment without the protections associated with criminal process, and civil detention must bear a sufficient relation to a legitimate regulatory purpose. See **Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80, 83 (1992)**; **Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 363 (1997)**.

Here, as in **Zadvydas**, the asserted justification is removal, not punishment. But when removal is not reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves that purpose. **Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 699, 121 S. Ct. at 2503.** At that point, confinement becomes constitutionally suspect because it is untethered to any realistic regulatory end. See **Foucha, 504 U.S. at 82-83**.

III. **The Court established a workable standard and burden-shifting framework.**

The Court made clear that post-removal detention is presumptively reasonable for six months, but not beyond that point absent a sufficient showing. **Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 701, 121 S. Ct. at 2505.** “**After this 6-month period, once the alien provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing.**” **Id.** That standard governs here.

The Fifth Circuit’s pre-Zadvydas approach—upholding detention so long as “good faith efforts” continued—was rejected because it would require the alien to prove that removal is impossible, “**which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear**.” **Id. at 702, 121 S. Ct. at 2505.** The statute does not authorize the Government to hold a person simply because deportation efforts are ongoing. It authorizes detention only so long as removal remains reasonably foreseeable. **Id. at 699-701, 121 S. Ct. at 2503-05.**

IV. **The Government cannot rely on general immigration authority to justify endless detention.**

The Government may argue that because Congress has broad power over immigration, detention may continue until removal occurs. But **Zadvydas** forecloses that contention. The Court expressly held that § 1231(a)(6) “**does not permit indefinite detention**,” **id. at 689, 121 S. Ct. at 2498**, and it rejected the idea that post-removal detention can continue merely because the alien has not yet been removed. Congress may authorize supervision and conditions of release, and the Government may reincarcerate an alien who violates those conditions, but that is a different regime from open-ended imprisonment. **Id. at 700, 121 S. Ct. at 2504; id. at 698, 121 S. Ct. at 2501.**

V. **Applied here, the statute does not authorize continued custody.**

Under **Zadvydas**, once the detainee shows no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, continued detention is unauthorized. **Id. at 701, 121 S. Ct. at 2505.** That is precisely the circumstance the statute addresses. The Attorney General may detain during the 90-day removal period, and may continue detention only within the bounds of reasonableness tied to removal. **Id. at 689, 699, 121 S. Ct. at 2498, 2503.** Beyond that point, the remedy is supervised release, not indefinite custody. **Id. at 698-700, 121 S. Ct. at 2501, 2504.**

For these reasons, the Court should reaffirm that § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention beyond the 90-day removal period and should grant relief consistent with **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001)**.
