ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit's affirmance of Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten undermines the First Amendment's protection of free speech. The "true threats" exception, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to necessitate a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

The Supreme Court has consistently emphasized the importance of intent in evaluating the constitutionality of statutes regulating speech. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court held that the speaker's intent is a critical factor in determining whether speech is protected, as it is essential to distinguish between speech that is intended to incite violence and speech that is merely provocative. The Court in Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), upheld a statute prohibiting "fighting words" because such words, by their very utterance, inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace, highlighting the significance of the speaker's intent.

Lower courts have also recognized the importance of intent in determining whether a statement constitutes a threat. The Seventh Circuit, in United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1 (7th Cir. 1955), and the Second Circuit, in United States v. Carrier, 168 F.2d 881 (2d Cir. 1948), have held that a threat requires an "intent to cause the apprehension" of harm or "intent to cause fear or apprehension." These decisions demonstrate that a speaker's intent is essential in assessing whether their words constitute a true threat.

Furthermore, the requirement of intent is not limited to threat cases. In statutes regulating speech, courts have consistently required a showing of intent to convey a prohibited message. The Court in Pierce v. United States, 314 U.S. 306 (1941), and Gorin v. United States, 312 U.S. 19 (1941), established that intent is a necessary element in such statutes. Additionally, Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1 (1949), and Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242 (1937), support the proposition that a statute regulating speech must be applied with regard to the defendant's intent.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which adopted an objective standard for determining true threats, is misplaced. An objective standard is inconsistent with the emphasis on intent in the aforementioned cases. A subjective intent standard is necessary to ensure that § 875(c) is applied in a manner consistent with the First Amendment.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard. This approach is necessary to protect the First Amendment rights of individuals like Elonis and to ensure that § 875(c) is applied in a constitutional manner.
