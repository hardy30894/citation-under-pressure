## ARGUMENT

The District Court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal violated Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2). That error, however, does not entitle petitioner to habeas relief because he undisputedly knew of his appellate rights at sentencing and voluntarily chose not to appeal. Under settled precedent, a defendant who receives actual notice of his appeal right suffers no prejudice from the court’s omission, and the error is harmless.

### I. The Purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) Was Fully Satisfied

Rule 32(a)(2) requires the sentencing court to “advise the defendant of any right to appeal the sentence.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2) (1992). This requirement exists not as an end in itself, but to ensure that defendants are aware of their appellate rights. As the Third Circuit correctly held, “the rule’s prophylactic purpose is to inform a defendant of his right to appeal, not to create a remedy for every technical omission.” *United States v. Peguero*, 145 F.3d 133, 134 (3d Cir. 1998).

Where a defendant already knows of his right to appeal, the rule’s purpose is “fully served, even if the court fails to advise him.” *United States v. Mills*, 597 F.2d 693, 694 (9th Cir. 1979). Every circuit to consider the question has held that a defendant who was actually aware of his appeal right cannot obtain relief simply because the court omitted the formal advisory. *See, e.g., United States v. Turcotte*, 558 F.2d 893, 894 (8th Cir. 1977); *United States v. Brizendine*, 659 F.2d 215, 219 (D.C. Cir. 1981). Here, the District Court expressly found that petitioner “was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing.” That finding is not clearly erroneous, and it conclusively establishes that the error caused no harm.

### II. Rule 32 Violations Are Subject to Harmless-Error Review

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure command that “[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). This harmless-error standard applies to violations of Rule 32, just as it applies to other procedural rules. *See United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783–84 (1979) (rejecting per se reversal for Rule 11 violations and holding that “collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements of the Rule”). In *Timmreck*, the Court held that a defendant who understood his rights despite the court’s incomplete advice could not obtain habeas relief. *Id.* at 784. The same logic governs here.

Moreover, when a defendant fails to object at sentencing, his claim is reviewed for plain error under Rule 52(b). *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Petitioner did not object, and he cannot show that the error affected his substantial rights. *Id.* at 734. He knew he could appeal. He chose not to. The outcome would have been identical had the court read the advisory aloud. Thus, any error was harmless beyond any reasonable doubt.

### III. Habeas Relief Requires a Showing of Prejudice

Petitioner seeks relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. That statute permits a prisoner to “move the court which imposed the sentence to vacate, set aside or correct the sentence” only on the ground that it was “imposed in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States.” § 2255. Even when a constitutional error occurs, habeas relief requires the petitioner to demonstrate that the error “had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury’s verdict.” *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993). A statutory violation that does not affect substantial rights cannot satisfy that standard.

Here, the error was purely statutory. No constitutional right is at stake. The defendant possessed actual knowledge of his appeal right. He cannot show that the court’s omission prejudiced him in any way. Without prejudice, § 2255 relief is unavailable. *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 166–68 (1982) (a § 2255 movant must show “actual prejudice” from the error). Petitioner’s claim is precisely the kind of “technical violation” that habeas corpus is not intended to remedy. *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784.

### IV. The Judgment Below Should Be Affirmed

The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review and concluded that the rule’s purpose had been served. Because petitioner knew of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue that remedy, he suffered no prejudice from the District Court’s inadvertent omission. The error was harmless, and habeas relief was properly denied.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
