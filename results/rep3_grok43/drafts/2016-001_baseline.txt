The text of 35 U.S.C. § 289 authorizes an infringer to “account for * * * all the profits” made from the “article of manufacture” to which the patented design “has been applied.” When the design patent covers only a component, the relevant “article of manufacture” is that component, not the entire finished product. Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc., 580 U.S. 53, 62 (2016). The statute therefore requires the patentee to prove the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to the component itself.

This limitation follows directly from the Court’s construction of the identical phrase in the predecessor statute. In Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885), the Court held that a design-patent plaintiff “must show what part of the defendant’s profit” was attributable to the patented design; an award of the defendant’s entire profit on the finished article was improper. Congress responded by enacting the 1887 statute (now § 289) to overrule Dobson only to the extent it had required apportionment when the design was applied to the entire article sold. See Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 222 F. 902, 904 (2d Cir. 1915). The statute left untouched the apportionment rule when the design covered only a component.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—that the patentee may recover the infringer’s entire profit on the finished product whenever the design is applied to any component—cannot be reconciled with the statutory text or with Dobson. That rule would permit recovery of profits caused by unpatented features, a result the Court has repeatedly rejected in analogous patent-damages contexts. See, e.g., Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1854); Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884). Because Apple presented no evidence apportioning Samsung’s profits to the patented components, the judgment awarding Samsung’s entire profits on the phones must be reversed.
