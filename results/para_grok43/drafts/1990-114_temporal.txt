The Equal Protection Clause does not forbid Missouri's mandatory retirement rule for its judges. A state may classify its officers by age so long as the distinction rests upon a rational basis. Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc., 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955). The Court will not overturn such a classification unless it is "palpably arbitrary." Id. Missouri could reasonably conclude that the exacting and continuous demands of judicial office make age a relevant proxy for sustained capacity. Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949). A fixed retirement age also spares the state the necessity of individualized removal proceedings whose delays and uncertainties would themselves impair the administration of justice. McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961) ("A statutory discrimination will not be set aside if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it.").

The same principle sustains the ADEA claim. Congress did not manifest a clear intention to displace the states' traditional authority over the tenure of their own judges. Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). The statute's exclusion of "an appointee on the policymaking level," 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (1967), confirms rather than contradicts that restraint. Because the Missouri provision rests on a permissible classification and because Congress did not unmistakably reach the state judiciary, the judgment below should be affirmed.
