**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, explicitly authorized punitive damages for intentional discrimination when the employer acted with “malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This standard does not require a showing of conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous” in the absence of a culpable mental state. Rather, the statute’s language and the settled principles of federal civil rights law make clear that the requisite mental state—malice or reckless indifference—is the sole threshold for punitive damages, and that the conduct need not meet any additional objective standard of severity.

The Court has long recognized that punitive damages are not awarded as a matter of right, but only when the defendant’s conduct reflects a level of culpability that goes beyond mere liability. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 46 (1983), the Court held that “the question whether to award punitive damages” is one for the jury, but emphasized that “the threshold for punitive damages must be higher than the standard for liability.” However, the Court did not require that the conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in an objective sense. Instead, it affirmed that the threshold is met when the plaintiff proves “sufficiently serious misconduct on the defendant’s part,” and that the jury must be instructed that punitive damages are appropriate only when the defendant’s conduct reflects “malice or reckless indifference.” Id. at 46–47. This standard is consistent with the common law of torts, where punitive damages are not triggered by the mere occurrence of harm, but by the defendant’s culpable state of mind.

Here, the jury found that the American Dental Association intentionally discriminated against Carole Kolstad on the basis of sex. The evidence supported a finding that the ADA preselected Tom Spangler for the Director’s position before the vacancy was even posted, despite Kolstad’s qualifications and distinguished performance evaluations. The ADA’s own documents—such as the Position Description Questionnaire that copied Spangler’s job duties verbatim and Wheat’s performance evaluation listing Spangler’s 1993 goal as “provide management and administrative support” for the Council on Government Affairs—demonstrated that the decision was made in advance. The ADA failed to interview Kolstad, ignored her efforts to discuss the promotion, and offered a pretextual explanation for her rejection. These facts, when viewed in context, support a finding of reckless indifference to Kolstad’s federally protected rights.

The Court has repeatedly held that a plaintiff need not prove “egregious” conduct to establish a basis for punitive damages. In *Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. v. Greenmoss Builders, Inc.*, 472 U.S. 749, 760 (1985), the Court upheld a punitive damages award where the defendant failed to verify information before issuing a damaging report, despite a routine practice of doing so. The Court noted that the jury could have found “reckless disregard for the truth,” which was sufficient to justify punitive damages under state law. The same principle applies here: the ADA’s failure to conduct a fair and impartial review of Kolstad’s qualifications, despite its own procedures and the availability of evidence of her merit, reflects a reckless indifference to her rights under Title VII.

The dissenting opinion in the Court of Appeals, which would require “extraordinarily egregious” conduct, misreads the statutory standard. The legislative history cited by the dissent—such as Senator Dole’s statement that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases”—is not dispositive. As the Court has held, “the legislative history of a statute is not a substitute for the statute itself.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994). Moreover, the House Report, which the dissent cites, refers to an unadopted version of the bill that included the word “callous” in the standard for punitive damages. But Congress rejected that language in favor of the current standard: “malice or with reckless indifference.” The fact that Congress chose a standard focused on the defendant’s state of mind, rather than on the objective severity of the conduct, demonstrates its intent to anchor punitive damages in culpability, not in the degree of harm or the nature of the act.

Finally, the Court has consistently treated Title VII claims as analogous to personal injury torts, where the focus is on the harm to the individual and the defendant’s culpability. In *United States v. Burke*, 504 U.S. 229, 238 (1992), the Court stated that “discrimination in the workplace being no less injurious than discrimination elsewhere, the rights asserted by persons who sue under Title VII are just as tort-like as the rights asserted by plaintiffs” in other civil rights actions. Just as punitive damages in tort law are not limited to “egregious” conduct, but are awarded when the defendant’s conduct reflects malice or recklessness, so too must punitive damages under Title VII be available when the employer acts with malice or reckless indifference.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that an employer’s conduct need not be “egregious” or “outrageous” in an objective sense to support punitive damages under Title VII. The statute requires only that the plaintiff prove the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights. The jury’s finding of intentional discrimination, supported by substantial evidence, satisfies that standard. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
