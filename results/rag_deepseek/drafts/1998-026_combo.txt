## Argument

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, as required by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2), does not entitle the defendant to habeas relief when he was independently aware of that right and elected not to appeal. Such a procedural error is subject to harmless-error review, and where the defendant’s actual knowledge satisfies the rule’s purpose, no prejudice arises.

### I. The Failure to Advise Is a Procedural Error Subject to Harmless-Error Review

Not every deviation from a procedural rule automatically invalidates a criminal judgment. This Court has long held that even constitutional errors may be harmless if they do not affect substantial rights. *See, e.g., Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82, 90 (1946) (rejecting a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel where the record failed to show prejudice). The requirement that a court advise a defendant of his right to appeal is a prophylactic rule designed to ensure that defendants are informed of that right. When the defendant already knows of his right, the rule’s purpose is fully served, and the error is harmless. *Cf. Smith v. O’Grady*, 312 U.S. 329, 334 (1941) (reversing only where the defendant alleged deception and denial of counsel that resulted in a fundamentally unfair proceeding).

The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error analysis. The district court found that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. Because the error did not deprive Peguero of any information he lacked, it could not have affected his decision to appeal. *See Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 731 (1948) (rejecting a due process claim where the petitioner failed to show that the alleged error caused prejudice). A contrary rule would require vacating sentences even when the defendant suffered no conceivable harm—a result that would undermine finality and judicial economy without advancing any legitimate interest.

### II. Actual Knowledge Satisfies the Purpose of Rule 32

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are aware of their right to appeal. That purpose is achieved when the defendant possesses actual knowledge, regardless of whether the court provided the formal advisement. *See Woods v. Nierstheimer*, 328 U.S. 211, 217 (1946) (holding that a defendant’s knowing and intelligent waiver of counsel is valid even if the court did not expressly advise him of the right). Here, Peguero’s awareness of his right to appeal is undisputed. He therefore cannot claim that the court’s omission caused him to forfeit that right.

This Court has consistently required a showing of prejudice before granting relief for procedural errors. In *Mempa v. Rhay*, 389 U.S. 128, 137 (1967), the Court held that the absence of counsel at sentencing violated the Sixth Amendment, but only because the defendant was not advised of his right to counsel and did not waive it. The Court distinguished cases where the defendant had actual knowledge and chose to proceed without counsel. *Id.* at 134–35 (citing *Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736, 741 (1948), which required a showing of “materially untrue” assumptions to establish a due process violation). Similarly, in *McConnell v. Rhay*, 393 U.S. 2, 4 (1968), the Court applied *Mempa* retroactively but did not suggest that every failure to advise automatically invalidates a sentence. The touchstone is whether the defendant was actually deprived of a right he would have exercised.

### III. No Per Se Rule Is Warranted

Peguero argues that any violation of Rule 32 is per se reversible. But this Court has never adopted such a rigid rule for procedural errors. *See Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 210 (1950) (emphasizing that habeas relief is not automatic and that courts should consider the totality of circumstances). Even in cases involving the right to counsel—a fundamental constitutional right—this Court has required a showing that the defendant was unaware of the right and would have acted differently. *See Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 580 (1959) (rejecting a due process claim where the defendant had an opportunity to respond to prejudicial information). The right to appeal, while important, is not a constitutional right; it is a statutory right that can be waived. *See Hunter v. Martin*, 334 U.S. 302, 306 (1948) (holding that a defendant’s failure to appeal does not automatically entitle him to habeas relief). A per se rule would transform a ministerial omission into a windfall for defendants who suffered no harm.

### IV. The Lower Courts Correctly Applied the Harmless-Error Standard

The district court held an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero knew of his right to appeal. The Court of Appeals affirmed, concluding that the rule’s purpose had been served. That analysis is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See Sigler v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988, 989 (1969) (per curiam) (denying certiorari where the lower court found no prejudice from a lack of counsel at sentencing). The harmless-error standard respects the finality of judgments while ensuring that only genuine injustices are remedied. Here, there was no injustice.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
