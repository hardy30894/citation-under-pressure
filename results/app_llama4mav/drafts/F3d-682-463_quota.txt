The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Arrow Energy Services, Inc. on Harold Wasek's claims of hostile work environment under Title VII and Michigan's Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act ("ELCRA"). Wasek presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue of material fact as to whether he was subjected to a hostile work environment because of his sex.

To establish a hostile work environment claim under Title VII, a plaintiff must show that (1) he is a member of a protected class, (2) he was subjected to unwelcome harassment, (3) the harassment was based on his sex, (4) the harassment unreasonably interfered with his work performance by creating a hostile, offensive, or intimidating work environment, and (5) the employer is liable. See Hafford v. Seidner, 183 F.3d 506, 512 (6th Cir. 1999). The ELCRA requires a similar showing. See Radtke v. Everett, 442 Mich. 368, 382-83 (1993).

Wasek's evidence demonstrates that he was subjected to unwelcome harassment by his coworker, Paul Ottobre. Ottobre's conduct, including touching Wasek in a sexual manner, making sexually explicit comments, and telling crude jokes and stories, was pervasive and severe. See Williams v. General Motors Corp., 187 F.3d 553, 562-63 (6th Cir. 1999) (holding that a plaintiff can establish a hostile work environment claim by showing that the harassment was "sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of [his] employment and create an abusive working environment").

The harassment was also based on Wasek's sex. While Ottobre's motivations may have been complex, his conduct was objectively sexual in nature, and Wasek perceived it as such. See Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 80-81 (1998) (holding that same-sex harassment can be actionable under Title VII if it is "because of sex"). The fact that Ottobre may have been motivated by a bisexual orientation does not negate the fact that his conduct was directed at Wasek because of his sex. See Rene v. MGM Grand Hotel, Inc., 305 F.3d 1061, 1067-68 (9th Cir. 2002) (en banc) (holding that same-sex harassment can be actionable even if the harasser is attracted to both men and women).

Moreover, Wasek's supervisors, Pat Tripp and Bill Strong, were aware of the harassment and failed to take adequate action to stop it. See Gallagher v. C.H. Robinson Worldwide, Inc., 567 F.3d 263, 275 (6th Cir. 2009) (holding that an employer can be liable for a hostile work environment if it "knew or should have known of the charged [harassment] and failed to implement prompt and appropriate corrective action"). Tripp's response to Wasek's complaints was to advise him not to "make waves" and to "handle" the problem himself, while Strong suggested that Wasek and Ottobre "duke it out" to resolve their differences. See id. (holding that an employer's response to harassment is inadequate if it "permits the harassment to continue").

The district court erred in concluding that Wasek's complaints to Smillie and Strong were insufficient to put Arrow Energy on notice of the harassment. See Hawkins v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 517 F.3d 321, 338-39 (6th Cir. 2008) (holding that an employer's knowledge of harassment can be imputed through a supervisor's knowledge). Wasek's complaints to Smillie and Strong were detailed and specific, and they were aware of the severity of the situation.

Furthermore, Arrow Energy's failure to take prompt and effective action to stop the harassment renders it liable for the hostile work environment. See Jackson v. Quanex Corp., 191 F.3d 647, 663-64 (6th Cir. 1999) (holding that an employer's failure to take prompt and effective action to stop harassment can render it liable for a hostile work environment).

In sum, Wasek presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue of material fact as to whether he was subjected to a hostile work environment because of his sex. The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Arrow Energy, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings. See EEOC v. Harbert-Yeargin, Inc., 266 F.3d 498, 510-11 (6th Cir. 2001) (reversing summary judgment on a hostile work environment claim where the plaintiff presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue of material fact).
