The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners or obtaining a judgment against them is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, both a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. Galletti v. United States (In re Galletti), 298 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir.2002).

The IRS's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. The Code's assessment provision, 26 U.S.C. § 6203, requires the IRS to "record[ ] the liability of the taxpayer" being assessed. The regulation interpreting this provision states that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This language indicates that the assessment must be made against a specific taxpayer, and an assessment against a partnership does not satisfy this requirement with respect to its individual partners.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir.1961), supports this interpretation. Although the court ultimately affirmed the district court's decision on other grounds, the district court's holding that an assessment against a partnership is insufficient to create a lien against an individual partner's property is instructive. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D.Cal.1958). The district court noted that the assessment must identify the taxpayer being assessed, and an assessment against a partnership with "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers" does not meet this requirement. Id. at 675.

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D.Cal.1959), aff'd 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960), is misplaced. The district court's holding in Young was limited to the issue of whether a partner who had paid a tax assessed against the partnership was entitled to a refund. The court held that the partner was not entitled to a refund because state law made the partner liable for the partnership's tax debt. Id. at 76,054. However, the court also acknowledged that the IRS would have had to either assess the tax against the partner individually or bring a court action to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050.

Under California law, a creditor may not collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The IRS has not obtained a judgment against the individual partners, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Therefore, the IRS's claim against the individual partners is barred, and the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the claim.

The government's argument that it can collect the partnership's tax debt from the individual partners without assessing them or obtaining a judgment is inconsistent with the Code's assessment provision and relevant case law. The Ninth Circuit's decision in Galletti should be affirmed.
