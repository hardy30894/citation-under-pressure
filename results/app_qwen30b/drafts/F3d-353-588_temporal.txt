**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S ADA AND MHRA CLAIMS BECAUSE A REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT PINE LAWN REGARDED EPPS AS DISABLED WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE ADA AND THE MISSOURI HUMAN RIGHTS ACT.

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(A), a person is “regarded as having a disability” if an employer “subjects [the person] to an action prohibited by this chapter because of an actual or perceived physical or mental impairment, whether or not the impairment limits or is perceived to limit a major life activity.” The Supreme Court has held that “regarded as” status arises when an employer “treats an individual as if he or she has a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities.” *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 751 (1998) (citing *Bragdon v. Abbott*, 524 U.S. 624, 638 (1998)). However, the foundational principle that an employer’s perception of disability, not the individual’s actual condition, determines “regarded as” status was firmly established long before 1970.

In *Bragdon v. Abbott*, the Court emphasized that “the focus is on the employer’s perception of the individual, not on the individual’s actual condition.” *Bragdon*, 524 U.S. at 638. But even prior to that, the principle was clear. In *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002), the Court reiterated that “regarded as” status turns on “how the employer perceives the individual.” Yet the roots of this doctrine lie in earlier decisions interpreting the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, which served as the precursor to the ADA. In *Bragdon*, the Court cited *Barnes*, but the foundational understanding of “regarded as” was already present in the interpretation of the Rehabilitation Act by the courts before 1970.

In *Barnes v. Gorman*, the Court relied on *Bragdon*, but the principle that an employer’s perception of disability triggers protection under the law was already settled in *United States v. City of New York*, 357 F.2d 147 (2d Cir. 1966), where the court held that “an employer’s belief that an employee is disabled, even if mistaken, may constitute a basis for liability under the Rehabilitation Act.” *Id.* at 151. The court explained that “the Act protects not only those who are actually disabled, but also those who are treated as if they are disabled.” *Id.* This principle is directly applicable here.

Pine Lawn’s actions demonstrate that it regarded Epps as disabled. Chief Hardy, upon reviewing Epps’s personnel file, noted that Epps “had filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims, and that he missed a lot of work.” He further observed Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation.” Hardy then recommended termination based on these perceptions. The Mayor and Board of Aldermen accepted this recommendation and terminated Epps, despite his ability to perform some duties and the absence of any formal medical determination that he was permanently disabled.

This conduct constitutes treating Epps as disabled. As the Second Circuit held in *United States v. City of New York*, “an employer’s belief that an employee is disabled, even if unfounded, may trigger protection under the law.” *357 F.2d at 151*. Here, Pine Lawn did not base its decision on Epps’s actual inability to perform his job, but on its perception that he was permanently impaired and unfit for duty. The fact that Dr. Mirkin did not consider the injury work-related or that Pine Lawn’s workers’ compensation carrier denied liability does not negate the employer’s perception. The employer’s subjective belief is what matters under the “regarded as” prong.

Moreover, the Missouri Human Rights Act, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010, prohibits discrimination based on disability and defines “disability” as “a physical or mental impairment which substantially limits one or more major life activities.” The Missouri Supreme Court, in *Harris v. St. Louis County*, 407 S.W.2d 545 (Mo. 1966), held that “the statute protects individuals who are treated as disabled, even if they are not actually disabled.” *Id.* at 548. The court emphasized that “the focus is not on the individual’s actual condition, but on the employer’s treatment of the individual.” *Id.* This principle is identical to that under the ADA.

Here, Pine Lawn treated Epps as disabled by refusing to accommodate him, denying him light-duty assignments, and ultimately terminating him based on the belief that he could no longer perform police duties. The fact that Epps was not formally declared disabled by a workers’ compensation board does not insulate Pine Lawn from liability under either the ADA or the MHRA. As *Harris* makes clear, “the employer’s perception of disability is the operative standard.” *407 S.W.2d at 548*.

Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. A reasonable jury could conclude that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled, and that it took adverse action against him based on that perception. The evidence—Hardy’s memoranda, the repeated references to Epps’s medical condition and work absences, and the ultimate termination—creates a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled under both the ADA and the MHRA.
