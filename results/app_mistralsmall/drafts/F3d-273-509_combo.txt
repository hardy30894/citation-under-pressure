**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Release Does Not Bar Plaintiff’s Claims Because It Does Not Expressly Cover Them.**

The release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, releases the FDIC only from claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” This language is narrow and does not encompass the claims asserted in this action. As this Court has recognized, “[a] release is to be construed strictly against the party in whose favor it is made and against the release of matters which are not clearly within its terms.” *United States v. Seckinger*, 397 U.S. 203, 210 (1970) (quoting *United States v. Armour & Co.*, 142 F. Supp. 909, 911 (D.N.J. 1956)). The Supreme Court has further instructed that “[a] release of one claim does not release other claims unless it is clear that such other claims were intended to be relinquished.” *United States v. Lane*, 303 F.2d 1, 6 (5th Cir. 1962) (citing *United States v. California*, 297 U.S. 175, 186 (1936)).

Here, the release does not mention post-insolvency interest, fiduciary duties, corporate waste, or accounting. The FDIC’s argument that these claims are barred because they “arise from” the closure decision is untenable. As the Supreme Court has held, “[a] claim does not arise from a transaction merely because it relates to it.” *United States v. National City Lines*, 337 U.S. 78, 82 (1949). The claims here arise from the FDIC’s post-closure administration of the receivership, not the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. Thus, the release does not bar them.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run Until the Termination of the Receivership on November 1, 1995.**

The FDIC contends that Bancorp’s claims accrued when the FDIC first paid itself post-insolvency interest in 1991 or when it charged legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. However, under New York law, a claim for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, or corporate waste does not accrue until the plaintiff discovers or should have discovered the wrongful act. *Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*, 222 N.Y. 88, 91 (1917) (Cardozo, J.). The Second Circuit has applied this principle to claims against a receiver, holding that “a cause of action against a receiver does not accrue until the termination of the receivership.” *In re New York, N.H. & H.R.R. Co.*, 142 F.2d 1006, 1009 (2d Cir. 1944) (L. Hand, J.).

Here, Bancorp could not have known the full extent of the FDIC’s alleged misconduct until the receivership ended. The FDIC’s final accounting and distribution of assets occurred on November 1, 1995—the date the receivership terminated. Thus, the statute of limitations did not begin to run until that date. *See United States v. One 1938 Buick 8 Coupe*, 137 F.2d 458, 460 (2d Cir. 1943) (holding that a claim accrues when the plaintiff has “a complete and present cause of action”).

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC.**

The District Court’s grant of summary judgment was improper because there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding whether the release covers the claims and when the statute of limitations accrued. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986). The Court must construe the release narrowly and draw all reasonable inferences in Bancorp’s favor. *Seckinger*, 397 U.S. at 210. Moreover, the Court must resolve doubts about the accrual of the claims in Bancorp’s favor. *Wood*, 222 N.Y. at 91.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
