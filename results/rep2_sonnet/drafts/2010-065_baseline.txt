**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tenth Amendment Protects the Retained Sovereignty of the States, Not the Personal Rights of Individuals, and Only a State or Its Officers May Invoke It in Litigation.**

The Third Circuit correctly held that a private individual convicted under a federal criminal statute lacks standing to challenge that statute as an intrusion on the reserved powers of the States. That holding follows directly from this Court's decision in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, private utility companies argued that a federal agency's sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment by regulating a local matter. This Court rejected the argument on the ground that the companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. That rule is not incidental to the case; it reflects the structural understanding, confirmed decades later in *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), that the Tenth Amendment "is not a font of individual rights," *id.* at 181, but instead "confirms that the power of the Federal Government is subject to limits that may, in a given instance, reserve power to the States," *id.* at 157. Because the Amendment protects States as sovereign entities rather than individuals as rights-holders, the party entitled to invoke it is the sovereign whose prerogatives are allegedly usurped—not a private litigant seeking to escape a federal conviction.

This understanding is reinforced by *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), in which this Court explained that a State's interest in the maintenance of its sovereign prerogatives against federal encroachment is fundamentally different from a private party's interest in her own affairs, and that private citizens cannot vindicate that sovereign interest through litigation of their own. The logic of *Mellon* and *Tennessee Electric* has been followed by a majority of the courts of appeals to consider the question. *See, e.g., United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004). Those courts have recognized what the Third Circuit likewise recognized below: a private defendant's Tenth Amendment claim, when unaccompanied by the participation of the State whose authority is supposedly threatened, amounts to nothing more than an assertion "that the State's exercise of political power ... has been improperly displaced," an interest that belongs to the State itself.

**II. Bond's Asserted Injury Is a Generalized Grievance About the Allocation of Governmental Power, Not a Judicially Cognizable Injury Sufficient for Standing.**

This Court's standing doctrine requires that a plaintiff's injury be "particularized," not a grievance "shared in substantially equal measure by all or a large class of citizens." *Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, 499 (1975); *see also Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 573–74 (1992). Bond's claim—that Congress lacked authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229 because the subject matter belongs to the States—is precisely the sort of claim about the structural allocation of authority between two levels of government that this Court has repeatedly treated as a "generalized grievance" unsuited to adjudication at the instance of a private litigant. *See Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church & State, Inc.*, 454 U.S. 464, 483 (1982). Bond does not contend that Pennsylvania has been deprived of any power it wishes to exercise, or that the Commonwealth objects to her federal prosecution; she asserts only her own preference that she be tried in state rather than federal court. That is a preference about forum and severity of punishment, not a legally protected interest in the constitutional structure. Under *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945), "the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress ... has created offenses against the United States"—a balance that the States themselves, not federal defendants, are positioned to police.

**III. Even If Bond Possessed Standing, Section 229 Is a Valid Exercise of Congress's Authority Under the Necessary and Proper Clause to Implement a Valid Treaty.**

Should the Court reach the merits, § 229 stands on firm constitutional footing. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), this Court upheld federal legislation implementing the Migratory Bird Treaty against a Tenth Amendment challenge, holding that "[i]f the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government." *Id.* at 432. This Court further observed that "there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could." *Id.* at 433. The Chemical Weapons Convention is such a treaty, and § 229 is the necessary and proper legislation implementing it. *Holland* forecloses Bond's argument that treaty-implementing legislation must independently satisfy an enumerated-power test apart from the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
