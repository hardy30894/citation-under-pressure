**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right, independent of his insanity defense, to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent. Arizona’s rule—limiting the use of such evidence solely to the insanity defense—complies with constitutional due process and is consistent with settled precedent. The defendant’s right to present a defense is not absolute, and the State may impose reasonable limits on the manner and purpose for which evidence may be introduced, so long as those limits do not deprive the defendant of a fair opportunity to present a complete defense.

This Court has long recognized that the burden of proving insanity rests upon the defendant, and that the jury must be instructed that the prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the crime, including criminal intent, beyond a reasonable doubt. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 795 (1952), the Court affirmed that “the prosecution was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged, including, in the case of first degree murder, premeditation, deliberation, malice and intent.” The Court further emphasized that “the evidence adduced during this trial to prove defendant’s insanity shall be considered and weighed by you, with all other evidence, whether or not you find defendant insane, in regard to the ability of the defendant to premeditate, form a purpose, to deliberate, act wilfully, and act maliciously.” This language confirms that expert evidence of mental condition may be considered by the jury in assessing criminal intent—even if the defendant fails to meet the burden of proving insanity—without violating due process.

The Arizona trial court did not bar Clark from introducing expert testimony about his mental state. It merely ruled that such evidence could not be used to negate criminal intent unless the defendant first established insanity by a preponderance of the evidence. This is not a constitutional violation. In *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373, 381 (1897), this Court held that “the burden of proof thereof rests with defendant” in an insanity defense, and that “the fact of sanity, as any other essential fact in the case, must be established to the satisfaction of the jury beyond a reasonable doubt.” Thus, the State may require a defendant to meet the burden of proving insanity before the jury may consider mental condition evidence in connection with criminal intent.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly upheld the principle that a defendant may not use expert mental health testimony to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof unless he first satisfies the burden of establishing insanity. In *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 107 (1954), the Court held that a defendant “is entitled to a hearing on the question whether he was insane at the time of the trial,” but only after exhausting state remedies. The Court did not hold that a defendant may use such evidence to attack criminal intent without first proving insanity. The same principle applies here: the State may condition the use of mental health evidence on the defendant’s ability to meet the burden of proof on the insanity defense.

The Court has also rejected the notion that a defendant may introduce expert testimony to negate criminal intent without first proving insanity. In *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966), the Court held that a defendant’s right to due process includes the right to a fair opportunity to present a defense, but only where the evidence is relevant and the defendant has made a proper showing. The Court emphasized that “the trial court’s rejection of these contentions deprived Robinson of due process of law under the Fourteenth Amendment” only because the trial court failed to conduct a hearing on mental capacity to stand trial. The Court did not hold that a defendant may introduce expert testimony on mental state to negate intent without first proving insanity.

Similarly, in *Blackburn v. Alabama*, 361 U.S. 199, 205 (1960), the Court affirmed that a defendant may be committed to a mental hospital if there is “reasonable ground to believe that the defendant was insane either at the time of the commission of [the] offense or at the present time.” The Court did not hold that such evidence could be used to negate criminal intent unless the defendant first proved insanity. The Court’s focus was on the procedural safeguards required when a defendant’s mental condition is at issue, not on expanding the use of such evidence beyond the insanity defense.

The Court has also recognized that the jury may consider mental condition evidence in assessing criminal intent, but only after the defendant has met the burden of proving insanity. In *Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413, 420 (1902), the Court upheld a jury instruction that allowed the jury to acquit if there was a “reasonable doubt” as to the defendant’s mental condition. The instruction made clear that the jury could acquit if it found the defendant was laboring under an insane delusion, but only if such a condition was proven. The Court did not hold that the defendant could introduce such evidence to negate intent without first proving insanity.

In *United States Ex Rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 565 (1953), the Court held that a plea of guilty does not constitute an admission of sanity, but that “if the evidence taken as to the defendant’s mental condition for the purpose of enabling the court to assess the proper punishment, raised a substantial doubt as to Smith’s sanity, it would have been the duty of his counsel to have moved to withdraw the plea of guilty so that a plea of 'not guilty because of insanity’ could be entered.” This confirms that the defendant must affirmatively pursue the insanity defense before the jury may consider mental condition evidence in connection with criminal intent.

Finally, in *Panico v. United States*, 375 U.S. 29, 31 (1963), the Court held that a defendant found guilty of criminal contempt must be given a plenary hearing to determine his criminal responsibility if he was suffering from a mental illness at the time of the conduct. The Court did not hold that such evidence could be introduced to negate criminal intent without first proving insanity. Rather, the Court emphasized that a hearing was required only because the defendant had raised a claim of mental illness that could affect criminal responsibility.
