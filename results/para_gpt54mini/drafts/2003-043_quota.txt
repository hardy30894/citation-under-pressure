**Argument**

The government’s position fails because the Internal Revenue Code extends the collection period only when the IRS has timely assessed **the taxpayer from whom it seeks to collect**. Congress did not authorize the IRS to obtain a 10-year extension by assessing only a distinct entity and then later suing different persons who were never assessed. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s assessment of the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations as to the individual partners. *United States v. Galletti*, 310 F.3d 551, 556-60 (9th Cir. 2002), rev’d, 541 U.S. 114 (2004).

Section 6502(a) permits collection by levy or suit only after “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the applicable limitations period. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Assessment is the formal recording of “the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203; 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The Code separately defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual” and “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Thus, a partnership and its partners are separate taxpayers for assessment purposes. *Galletti*, 310 F.3d at 557-58.

This Court has long recognized that tax collection against an individual requires a legally sufficient basis as to that individual, not merely as to some related entity. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458-63 (9th Cir. 1961), the Court sustained the government’s action only after addressing the defective lien process that depended on an underlying assessment; the decision did not authorize collection from an unassessed person merely because the partnership had been assessed. Likewise, *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960), affirmed a refund denial where the taxpayer had actually paid; it did not hold that the government could bypass assessment against the individual and proceed directly against personal assets. *Galletti*, 310 F.3d at 559-60.

The government’s reliance on partnership liability principles also fails. California law provides that a partnership judgment is not, by itself, a judgment against a partner, and partnership assets cannot be collected from a partner’s assets absent a judgment against that partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The Ninth Circuit correctly relied on that rule in concluding that a partnership assessment does not automatically mature into an enforceable claim against the partners. *Galletti*, 310 F.3d at 561-62. Other courts have likewise recognized that when the government seeks to reach a partner individually, it must proceed on a legally sufficient basis against that partner. See *United States v. Henco Holding Corp.*, 985 F. Supp. 2d 1099, 1107-10 (N.D. Cal. 2013) (discussing the distinction between entity liability and personal collection remedies).

The government’s cited authorities do not support its theory. In *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489, 495-97 (1930), the Court held that assessment triggered the collection period for the assessed taxpayer; it did not hold that an assessment against one taxpayer tolls limitations as to another. In *Russell v. United States*, 278 U.S. 181, 188-89 (1929), the Court emphasized that the government’s collection rights are constrained by the statute of limitations Congress enacted. And in *Donnelly v. Commissioner*, 262 F.2d 411, 413-14 (2d Cir. 1959), the court treated assessment as a taxpayer-specific step necessary to support collection.

The cases on which the government relies from other circuits are distinguishable because they involved either assessments against the individual partners as well as the partnership, or direct actions within an unexpired limitations period. In *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562-63 (7th Cir. 1995), the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the partners; the only issue was tolling during bankruptcy. *Galletti*, 310 F.3d at 560. Similarly, *United States v. Galletti* below correctly distinguished *Wright* on that ground. *Galletti*, 310 F.3d at 560-61. And in *United States v. Galletti* was consistent with *United States v. Gann*, 718 F.2d 1505, 1507-09 (10th Cir. 1983), which recognized that collection must rest on a timely and proper assessment framework.

The government’s reading would collapse the distinction between a partnership’s liability and the partners’ procedural protections, contrary to the Code’s text and structure. The assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period only for the partnership, not for the individual respondents. Because the IRS never assessed the partners within three years, its claims are time-barred. The judgment should be affirmed.
