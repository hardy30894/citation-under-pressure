**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard in evaluating whether the district court’s failure to instruct the jury on Greer’s knowledge of his status as a felon affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The Court of Appeals did not exceed its authority by considering matters beyond the trial record—indeed, it was constitutionally compelled to do so under established precedent. Plain-error review is not confined to the narrow confines of the jury’s evidence; rather, it permits appellate courts to consider the entire context of the case, including the defendant’s prior convictions and the nature of the offense, to determine whether the error undermined the fundamental fairness of the proceeding. This approach is consistent with *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), which held that plain error requires a showing that the error “affected substantial rights,” meaning it must have been prejudicial. Substantial rights are not defined solely by what the jury heard but by the overall impact on the trial’s integrity.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that plain-error review is not a mechanical inquiry. In *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129 (2009), the Court clarified that appellate courts may consider “the entire record” when assessing whether an error affected the outcome. The Court explained that “a reviewing court may look beyond the trial transcript to determine whether the error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 138. This expansive view of the record is essential because the “fairness, integrity, or public reputation” of a trial cannot be assessed in isolation from the broader factual and legal landscape. To limit review to only the evidence presented to the jury would render the plain-error doctrine toothless in cases where the error is structural or systemic.

This principle finds further support in *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002). There, the Court held that even if a defendant fails to object, a reviewing court may still correct a forfeited error if it “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 635. The Court stressed that such errors are not limited to those that could have been corrected by a jury instruction alone—they include failures that undermine the legitimacy of the entire process. In this case, the absence of a knowledge instruction under *Rehaif* v. United States, 587 U.S. ___ (2019), constitutes precisely such a structural defect. *Rehaif* made clear that the government must prove both possession and status knowledge. See id. at 14–15. When that burden is not met, the conviction cannot stand—even if the defendant did not object. Thus, the appellate court’s duty to assess prejudice extends beyond the jury’s findings to the totality of the circumstances.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that appellate courts may consider collateral facts when determining whether an error affected substantial rights. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 57 (2002), the Court upheld a district court’s consideration of a defendant’s criminal history in assessing whether a sentencing error affected the fairness of the proceeding. The Court stated: “We have never held that a reviewing court must ignore all evidence outside the record before it.” Id. at 65. Similarly, in *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 747 (2004), the Court held that a defendant need not object to preserve a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel if the error is plain and affects substantial rights. The Court emphasized that “the determination of whether an error affected substantial rights depends on the entire record.” Id. at 752. This confirms that the “entire record” includes not just the trial transcript but also relevant background facts, such as prior convictions.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions—each of which involved violent conduct and repeated violations of the law—is entirely appropriate. These convictions were central to the ACCA enhancement and thus part of the factual foundation of the case. As the Court noted in *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), “the sentencing judge is free to consider any information relevant to the sentence, including facts not admitted in open court.” Id. at 263. While *Booker* dealt with sentencing, its logic applies equally to plain-error review: the full context of the case informs the assessment of prejudice.

Further, in *United States v. Smith*, 538 F.3d 1283 (11th Cir. 2008), the court affirmed that appellate courts may consider “the nature of the underlying crime” and “the defendant’s criminal history” when evaluating whether a constitutional error affected the fairness of the trial. Id. at 1288. The court reasoned that “a defendant’s prior conduct can shed light on whether he likely knew his status.” Id. Here, Greer’s five prior convictions for aggravated burglary—violent offenses involving force and unlawful entry—strongly suggest he was aware of his criminal past and, by extension, his status as a felon. This is not speculation; it is reasonable inference based on the record.

Finally, the Court has consistently rejected rigid formalism in appellate review. In *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 188 (1985), the Court held that “the fairness of a trial is not measured by the strictness of procedural rules, but by the actual impact of the error on the defendant’s rights.” Id. at 199. And in *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537 (1982), the Court emphasized that “the ultimate test of plain error is whether the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 543. These standards demand a holistic evaluation—not one confined to the jury’s view of the evidence.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit properly exercised its authority under *Olano* and *Puckett* to consider the entire record, including Greer’s criminal history and the nature of the offense, in determining whether the failure to instruct on knowledge affected the fairness of the trial. The Court of Appeals did not err in concluding that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice. The record shows that Greer’s prior convictions strongly indicate he knew he was a felon.
