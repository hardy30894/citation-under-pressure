**ARGUMENT**

The United States respectfully submits that the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard, and that a reviewing court may—indeed, must—consider the entire record to determine whether an unpreserved error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness of the proceeding. The judgment below should be affirmed.

**I. The plain-error standard requires the defendant to demonstrate prejudice.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits an appellate court to notice forfeited errors only when they are plain and affect “substantial rights.” This principle is rooted in the venerable rule of *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936), which held that appellate courts may consider waived objections only if the error is “obvious” and “substantial.” The burden is on the defendant. As this Court stated in *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), a reviewing court will not reverse for an unobjected-to error unless it appears that the defendant “was prejudiced” by the ruling.

The standard for assessing prejudice derives from *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946). There, the Court held that a non-constitutional error warrants reversal only if it “probably” influenced the jury’s verdict or had a “substantial influence” on the outcome. *Id.* at 764-65. Even for constitutional errors, the Court in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), required the defendant to show that the error was not “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.” In the plain-error context, the defendant must make that showing. Greer cannot do so here.

**II. An appellate court may consider matters outside the trial record to assess prejudice.**

The question presented asks whether a reviewing court may examine the full record—including evidence of the defendant’s prior convictions—to determine whether an error affected substantial rights. The answer is yes. An appellate court’s function is “to search the entire record” for prejudice, not to blind itself to facts that bear directly on the question of innocence. *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 613 (1946).

In *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 83-84 (1942), the Court emphasized that the “record” properly includes all proceedings in the trial court, and that a reviewing court must evaluate the “whole record” to determine whether an error was prejudicial. That principle applies with full force here. Greer’s prior convictions for aggravated burglary were the very basis for the § 922(g) charge and his enhanced sentence. They are not extrinsic or impermissible; they are matters of public record that indisputably established his felon status. *Cf. United States v. Mascuch*, 111 F.2d 602, 604 (2d Cir. 1940) (L. Hand, J.) (considering the record as a whole to determine the effect of an error).

Nor is this a novel proposition. In *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 91-92 (1963), the Court held that a reviewing court must determine whether an error contributed to the verdict by examining “the entire record.” Had the Court intended to limit such review to the evidence admitted at trial, it would have said so. The plain-error inquiry would be nonsensical if a court were forced to disregard uncontroverted facts establishing guilt beyond any doubt.

**III. Here, the record conclusively establishes that Greer knew he was a felon.**

Greer pleaded guilty in 2007 to being a felon in possession. His prior convictions for aggravated burglary were the predicate for that plea. Aggravated burglary is a serious felony involving entry into the dwelling of another with the intent to commit a criminal offense. The defendant’s prior convictions necessarily involved a conviction, a sentence of imprisonment, and all the attendant consequences that would have made his felon status known to him. In *United States v. Burroughs*, 24 F. Cas. 727 (C.C.D. Md. 1850), a case involving analogous issues, the Court recognized that a person’s status as a convicted felon is a matter of judicial record that cannot be collaterally denied.

The Rehaif error—the failure to instruct the jury on knowledge of status—could not have affected Greer’s substantial rights because the evidence of his status was overwhelming and uncontradicted. As this Court stated in *Glasser*, a defendant is not entitled to reversal where the record demonstrates that the result would have been the same even absent the error. 315 U.S. at 83. That is precisely the situation here. Greer has never disputed that he knew he was a felon. Nor could he, given his five prior aggravated burglary convictions.

**IV. Considering the prior convictions does not undermine the fairness or integrity of the proceedings.**

Finally, the Eleventh Circuit properly concluded that the error did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings. The touchstone of this inquiry is whether the proceedings were fundamentally unfair to the defendant. *See Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. Greer received a trial that, aside from the instructional error, was fair and accurate. The omission of the knowledge-of-status element did not permit a guilty person to escape conviction or an innocent person to be erroneously punished. The record shows that Greer was a felon, that he knew it, and that he possessed a firearm.

To vacate his conviction on such facts would mock the integrity of the judicial process. It would reward a defendant who suffered no prejudice and who seeks to benefit from a technical flaw that could not have altered the outcome. This Court has long held that the plain-error rule is “to be invoked only in exceptional circumstances” to avoid a miscarriage of justice. *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658. Those circumstances are not present here.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
