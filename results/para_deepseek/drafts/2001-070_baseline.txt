## ARGUMENT

**I. The Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment.**

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). However, this Court has consistently held that "evolving standards of decency" are measured primarily by objective indicia of contemporary values—namely, state legislation and jury determinations—not by the independent judgment of the judiciary. *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 377-78 (1989). Under this standard, petitioner's claim must fail.

**A. There Is No National Consensus Against the Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders.**

At the time of petitioner's conviction, only two states—Georgia and Maryland—had enacted statutes categorically banning the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *See* Ga. Code Ann. § 17-7-131(j) (Supp. 2001); Md. Code Ann., Crim. Law § 2-202(b)(2) (2002). The federal government and the overwhelming majority of states that permit capital punishment continued to allow juries to consider mental retardation as a mitigating circumstance without imposing a categorical bar. This Court has held that when only a handful of states have enacted a particular prohibition, there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus. *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 370-73 (declining to find consensus where only 15 states prohibited juvenile executions).

Moreover, the jury in petitioner's case—and juries across the country—are fully capable of weighing evidence of mental retardation in determining appropriate punishment. The jury heard expert testimony regarding petitioner's intellectual functioning and nonetheless determined that death was the appropriate sentence. This individualized sentencing determination is precisely what the Eighth Amendment contemplates. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (holding that capital sentencing must allow "particularized consideration of relevant aspects of the character and record of the individual offender").

**B. Penry v. Lynaugh Controls and Should Not Be Overruled.**

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely held that mental retardation is a mitigating circumstance that must be considered by the sentencer, but that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Id.* at 340. The Court reasoned that because mental retardation is not an absolute bar to culpability—unlike insanity, which negates the capacity for criminal responsibility—it does not warrant a categorical exemption from the death penalty. *Id.* at 333-35; *see also Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 405 (1986) (prohibiting execution of the insane because such punishment serves no retributive or deterrent purpose).

Virginia has fully complied with *Penry*'s mandate. Under Virginia law, juries are instructed that mental retardation may constitute a mitigating circumstance warranting a sentence less than death. The jury in petitioner's case received such an instruction and, after considering the evidence, nonetheless imposed a death sentence. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly held that *Penry* forecloses petitioner's claim.

Petitioner asks this Court to overrule *Penry* based on the same evidence this Court considered and rejected in 1989. Stare decisis counsels against such a result, particularly where, as here, the "evolving standards of decency" have not evolved in the direction petitioner suggests. *See Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000) (noting that stare decisis is "the preferred course because it promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles"). The states that have considered this issue since *Penry* have, by a wide margin, declined to adopt categorical prohibitions.

**C. Mental Retardation Does Not Categorically Diminish Culpability.**

Petitioner's argument rests on the flawed premise that all mentally retarded individuals, by virtue of their condition, possess diminished culpability. But mental retardation exists on a spectrum, and its severity varies widely among individuals. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 338. Unlike the categorical exclusions this Court has recognized for juveniles under the age of 16, *see Thompson v. Oklahoma*, 487 U.S. 815, 838 (1988), mental retardation does not define a discrete class of offenders whose culpability is uniformly diminished. Rather, it requires precisely the individualized assessment that the jury conducted here.

The jury heard testimony from Dr. Evan Nelson, a forensic psychologist, who testified that petitioner was mildly mentally retarded with an IQ of 59. The jury also heard testimony from Dr. Stanton Samenow, who testified that petitioner possessed average intellectual functioning and was competent to stand trial. This conflicting evidence presented a classic credibility determination for the jury. Having weighed the evidence, the jury found that petitioner's alleged mental retardation did not warrant leniency.

**II. The Judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court Should Be Affirmed.**

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in affirming petitioner's death sentence. Because there is no national consensus against executing mentally retarded offenders, and because juries can and do consider mental retardation as a mitigating circumstance, the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit petitioner's sentence. The judgment below should be affirmed.
