**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause gives rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one”—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit answered in the affirmative, holding that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause when the government’s action is motivated solely by “a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 213 F.3d 958, 962 (7th Cir. 2000). This holding, while superficially appealing, is a radical departure from settled constitutional doctrine and creates a new and unbounded form of equal protection liability that the Fourteenth Amendment does not permit.

The Constitution does not guarantee equal treatment in every individualized decision. *See, e.g., Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 261 (1977) (“The Equal Protection Clause does not require that a State treat everyone identically, but only that it treat all persons similarly situated in a similar manner.”). The Clause is designed to prevent invidious discrimination against identifiable classes—racial, ethnic, religious, or otherwise—whose members are subject to systemic or categorical disadvantage. *See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954) (“Separate but equal has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.”). The “class of one” theory, by contrast, seeks to transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general right to fair treatment in individual administrative decisions—a right that is neither rooted in the text nor in precedent.

The Court has long recognized that a plaintiff must allege membership in a protected class to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. *See, e.g., Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985) (“The Equal Protection Clause protects persons, not classes, but only when the classification is impermissible.”). In *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1, 12 (1967), the Court struck down a law that prohibited interracial marriage, not because the law treated one individual unfairly, but because it created a racial classification that denied fundamental rights. Similarly, in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, the Court invalidated a zoning ordinance that denied a permit to a group home for the mentally disabled, not because the city had singled out one person, but because it had imposed a burden on a discrete and insular group based on a stigma. *Id.* at 440–41.

The Seventh Circuit’s “class of one” doctrine, however, collapses this essential distinction. It permits a plaintiff to claim that he was treated differently from others not because of a protected class, but because of personal animus. But the Constitution does not provide a remedy for personal grievances. *See, e.g., United States v. Armstrong*, 501 U.S. 432, 446 (1991) (“The Equal Protection Clause is not a general guarantee of fair treatment.”). To hold otherwise would open the door to an endless array of litigation based on subjective perceptions of unfairness. A landowner who dislikes a neighbor could claim that the government’s refusal to grant a permit was motivated by spite. A taxpayer could claim that a denial of a tax exemption was arbitrary and capricious. The result would be a constitutionalized version of administrative law, with courts second-guessing every discretionary decision.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the idea that a plaintiff may challenge a government decision simply because it was arbitrary or capricious. *See, e.g., Village of Arlington Heights*, 429 U.S. at 261 (“The Equal Protection Clause does not require that a State treat everyone identically, but only that it treat all persons similarly situated in a similar manner.”). The Clause is not a substitute for due process or the Administrative Procedure Act. *See, e.g., Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976) (“The Due Process Clause requires that the procedures by which laws are applied must be evenhanded, so that individuals are not subjected to the arbitrary exercise of government power.”). The remedy for arbitrary or capricious action lies in procedural safeguards, not in constitutional equal protection.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision also fails to recognize the practical dangers of such a doctrine. If a plaintiff can allege that a government official acted out of spite, then the mere assertion of personal animus—without proof of a discriminatory classification—becomes sufficient to trigger a constitutional claim. This would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a vehicle for personal vendettas, undermining the integrity of public administration. *See, e.g., O’Connor v. Donaldson*, 422 U.S. 563, 579 (1975) (“The Constitution does not require that government be fair or just in every respect, but only that it not act in a manner that violates fundamental rights.”).

The Court has never recognized a “class of one” claim under the Equal Protection Clause. *See, e.g., Heller v. Doe*, 509 U.S. 312, 320 (1993) (rejecting a claim that a law violated equal protection because it applied to a single individual). The Court has consistently required that a plaintiff show that he was treated differently from others similarly situated, and that the difference in treatment was based on a suspect classification or a rational basis. *See, e.g., Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 631 (1996) (“The Equal Protection Clause does not permit the State to make distinctions based on irrational or arbitrary grounds.”).

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed. The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one.” The Constitution protects against invidious discrimination, not personal grievances. To hold otherwise would undermine the structural integrity of the Clause and open the door to an unmanageable flood of litigation based on subjective perceptions of unfairness.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is reversed.
