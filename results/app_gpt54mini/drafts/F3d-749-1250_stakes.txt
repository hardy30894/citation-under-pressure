**Argument**

The district court plainly erred by allowing FBI Agent Charles Jones to tell the jury, in substance, that Stanley was lying and that his invocation of God showed deception. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, expert testimony must help the trier of fact and rest on “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge.” But expert testimony may not tell the jury what result to reach on credibility, because “credibility determinations are generally beyond the scope of expert opinion testimony.” *United States v. Toledo*, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993). This Court has likewise held that an expert may not “usurp the province of the jury” by expressing an opinion on a witness’s truthfulness. *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267 (10th Cir. 1999).

Agent Jones crossed that line repeatedly. He testified that Stanley’s answers “were not worthy of credence” and “did not make sense,” that Stanley displayed evasive behavior “common among the criminal element,” that he had “never” seen a statement like Stanley’s “with an innocent person,” and that references to faith were “deceptive statements.” Those remarks were not expert analysis of any legitimate forensic technique. They were direct credibility opinions, the functional equivalent of telling the jury Stanley lied. That is precisely what *Toledo* and *Charley* prohibit. See *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1470; *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267; see also *United States v. Hill*, 749 F.3d 1250, 1258–60 (10th Cir. 2014) (condemning testimony that impermissibly vouched for or attacked credibility).

The error was especially serious because the testimony came clothed with the authority of the FBI. The Supreme Court has recognized that expert testimony carries “a special aura of reliability” and can “usurp the jury’s function.” *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 313 (1998). This Court has warned that when an expert presents credibility opinions, the jury may give them undue weight because they come from an ostensibly specialized source. *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267. Here, the prosecutor used Jones’s credentials to amplify an improper message: Stanley was lying, and his religious statements were proof of deceit. That use of expert authority to adjudicate veracity was improper.

Because Stanley did not object contemporaneously, review is for plain error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732–36 (1993). Plain error exists when there is (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) affecting substantial rights, and (4) seriously affecting the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* at 732–36. Each requirement is met.

First, the admission of Agent Jones’s credibility opinions was error for the reasons above. Second, the error was plain. An error is plain when it is “clear” or “obvious” under existing law. *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266, 279 (2013). Long before this trial, *Toledo* and *Charley* clearly prohibited expert testimony bearing directly on credibility. See *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1470; *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267. Third, the error affected Stanley’s substantial rights. To do so, there must be a reasonable probability of a different result absent the error. *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81–82 (2004). That standard is satisfied because credibility was central to the government’s theory, and Jones’s testimony invited the jury to reject Stanley’s account for reasons unrelated to any lawful evidentiary basis. The case turned in substantial part on whether the jury believed the government’s identification and participation evidence; an FBI agent’s repeated assertion that Stanley was deceptive likely influenced that determination.

Fourth, the error seriously affected the fairness and integrity of the trial. The Supreme Court has explained that appellate courts should correct particularly prejudicial errors that “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736 (internal quotation marks omitted). Allowing an expert to brand a defendant a liar, and to suggest that religious references are signs of deception, undermines the jury’s exclusive role and the public legitimacy of the verdict. See *Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 313; *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267.

The government may argue harmlessness because the evidence was substantial. But plain-error review asks whether the error affected the outcome, not whether the government presented enough evidence in the abstract. *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 81–82. Where an FBI agent effectively testifies that a defendant is deceptive, the danger of prejudice is acute. The jury was entitled to decide credibility for itself; it was not entitled to receive an expert’s imprimatur on the answer.

For these reasons, Stanley’s conviction should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
