**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Case Is Moot Because the Challenged Policies Were Repealed and Plaintiffs No Longer Suffer Any Ongoing Injury.**

Article III of the Constitution limits federal jurisdiction to “Cases” and “Controversies.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 90 (2013). A case becomes moot “when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Id.* at 91. Here, Georgia Gwinnett College repealed the challenged speech-zone and code-of-conduct policies before the district court ruled. Plaintiffs do not contest that the policy change—combined with Uzuegbunam’s graduation—rendered their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot. The only remaining request is for nominal damages.

But a request for nominal damages, standing alone, cannot revive a moot case. This Court has never held that nominal damages provide an independent basis for Article III jurisdiction when the underlying controversy has evaporated. To the contrary, “past exposure to illegal conduct does not in itself show a present case or controversy regarding injunctive relief . . . if unaccompanied by any continuing, present adverse effects.” *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 102 (1983). The same principle applies to damages: a plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete, ongoing injury—not merely a historical wrong—to maintain a live case. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 109 (1998) (holding that a plaintiff cannot “rely on a completed past violation of [a] statute to establish standing” for damages). Because the policies were repealed and Uzuegbunam graduated, there is no continuing violation. The only injury alleged is the abstract fact that the policies once existed. That is insufficient.

**II. Nominal Damages Do Not Create a Live Controversy When the Underlying Claim Is Moot.**

Plaintiffs argue that their prayer for nominal damages preserves a live controversy. But this Court has consistently required that a plaintiff seeking any form of relief—including nominal damages—must have standing at the outset and maintain a live controversy throughout the litigation. *See Lewis v. Cont’l Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 477–78 (1990) (mootness occurs when “it is impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever”). Nominal damages are a remedy for a proven constitutional violation, not a jurisdictional hook. *See Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978) (nominal damages are available “if a plaintiff proves a violation of his constitutional rights” but cannot show actual injury). But if the underlying violation is no longer actionable because the challenged policy has been repealed and no ongoing harm exists, there is no “violation” to remedy. The claim for nominal damages becomes a request for an advisory opinion on a dead letter.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this principle in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), holding that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” *Id.* at 1255. That holding aligns with this Court’s teaching that federal courts “are not in the business of pronouncing that past actions which have no demonstrable continuing effect were right or wrong.” *Spencer v. Kemna*, 523 U.S. 1, 18 (1998). Allowing nominal damages to salvage a moot case would transform federal courts into forums for historical grievances, contrary to Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement.

**III. Plaintiffs Failed to Plead Any Compensatory Damages or Concrete Injury.**

Plaintiffs’ complaint sought only “nominal damages” and “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence.” They did not allege any actual injury—no lost time, no out-of-pocket expenses, no emotional distress. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that such vague references cannot be construed as a request for compensatory damages. *See Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (compensatory damages under § 1983 require proof of actual injury). Without any allegation of concrete harm, plaintiffs cannot rely on the possibility of compensatory damages to avoid mootness. *See Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins*, 578 U.S. 330, 339 (2016) (injury must be “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical”).

**IV. The Voluntary Cessation Exception Does Not Apply.**

Plaintiffs invoke the voluntary cessation doctrine, but that exception requires a “reasonable expectation” that the challenged conduct will recur. *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91. Here, GGC repealed the policies and has no intention of reinstating them. The district court found “no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” That finding is unchallenged. The voluntary cessation doctrine does not permit a plaintiff to keep a case alive solely to obtain nominal damages for a past policy that has been permanently abandoned.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
