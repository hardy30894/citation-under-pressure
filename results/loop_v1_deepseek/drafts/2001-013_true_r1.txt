**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously held that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lacks jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected oil and gas exploration barge. The plain text of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, coupled with the limited regulatory reach of the Coast Guard over uninspected vessels, compels the conclusion that OSHA’s citation was lawful.

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

The Act applies broadly to employment performed in a workplace in a State. 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). A drilling barge engaged in extracting oil from the seabed is not a “vessel in navigation” within the traditional maritime meaning. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 571 (1944) (a floating workshop “permanently affixed to the shore or riverbed” is not a vessel). *Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 370, 371 (1957) (a dredge moored during operations is not a “vessel in navigation” for Jones Act purposes). *Gahagan Construction Corp. v. Armao*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam) (same). Like those structures, Rig 52 was stationary while drilling—its sole purpose—and thus was not a vessel used for transportation. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870) (a vessel is “used, or capable of being used, as a means of transportation on water”). The fact that it could be moved does not transform it into a navigational vessel. *Cf. Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 191 (1952) (ferry that actively navigated was a vessel). Consequently, Rig 52 is a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Mere jurisdiction is insufficient; the agency must *actually* regulate the same condition. This Court has long held that preemption requires an actual conflict, not speculative overlap. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (preemption necessary only when “the matter is so fully covered by federal law as to leave no room for state action”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 grants it power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. It has no authority to prescribe comprehensive safety standards for drilling operations. *See The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17, 35 (1903) (inspection laws apply only to vessels “navigating” and carrying “passengers or freight”). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate the working conditions that gave rise to this explosion—well control, blowout prevention, and general workplace safety—means it has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over those conditions. *United States v. American Trucking Assns.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (exemptions in remedial statutes are construed narrowly).

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on Post-1970 Precedents Is Misplaced**

The court below rested on its own decisions in *Clary* and *Donovan*, both decided after the OSH Act’s 1970 enactment and thus inapplicable to this pre-1970 analysis. But even under pre-1970 law, workers on uninspected drilling barges are not “seamen” entitled to an industry-wide exemption. *Norton*, 321 U.S. at 571 (“The jurisdiction of admiralty does not extend to structures like this barge.”). Rig 52’s employees performed industrial oil well work, not traditional maritime duties. Their working conditions are analogous to those on a fixed platform—areas indisputably covered by OSHA. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365 (1969) (workers on fixed platforms are not seamen for LHWCA purposes). Overlapping regulation is not the anomaly the lower court feared; it is the norm when maritime safety touches landside operations. *The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903) (seamen’s remedies limited to those specified in maritime law; no preemption of state safety statutes).

In sum, Rig 52 is a workplace, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over its working conditions, and OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment below should be reversed.
