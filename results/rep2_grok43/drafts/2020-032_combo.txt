ARGUMENT

A federal court may adjudicate only an actual controversy. Muskrat v. United States, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911). When subsequent events make it impossible to grant effectual relief, the case ceases to be justiciable. Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895); California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co., 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893). Here the challenged policies were superseded in February 2017, rendering any claim for declaratory or injunctive relief impossible. Ex parte McCardle, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1868).

A bare request for nominal damages does not preserve jurisdiction once the underlying conduct has ended. The Constitution requires a real dispute affecting the rights of the parties, not an abstract question of law. Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126, 129 (1922); Ashwander v. TVA, 297 U.S. 288, 324 (1936). Where no actual injury is pleaded and the only remaining relief is a symbolic sum, the controversy is extinguished. Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, 240-241 (1937); Maryland Casualty Co. v. Pacific Coal & Oil Co., 312 U.S. 270, 273 (1941).

The complaint alleges no concrete loss of time, money, or reputation. Without such allegations, nominal damages rest solely on the “abstract value” of the constitutional right, which supplies no continuing case or controversy. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886); Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58, 62 (1897). Once the policies were withdrawn and one plaintiff graduated, nothing remained for judicial determination. United States v. Hamburg-Amerikanische Packetfahrt-Actien Gesellschaft, 239 U.S. 466, 475 (1915).

The capable-of-repetition exception does not apply. The revised policies contain no reasonable prospect of reversion, and the individual plaintiffs face no continuing threat. Southern Pac. Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911). Courts decline to decide moot questions merely to award a nominal sum. See, e.g., United States v. Alaska S.S. Co., 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920).

The district court therefore correctly dismissed the action.
