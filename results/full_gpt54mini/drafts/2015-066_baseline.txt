**Argument**

Section 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment when it relies on prior tribal-court convictions, even if those convictions were uncounseled. The Constitution requires counsel in state and federal prosecutions only when a sentence of imprisonment is imposed. Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979). Tribal courts, however, are not bound by the Sixth Amendment. United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 322-23 (1978); see also Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (“the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes”). Congress has long structured tribal criminal procedure through the Indian Civil Rights Act, not the Sixth Amendment. 25 U.S.C. § 1302; Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 56-59 (1978).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994). Nichols held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction valid under Scott may be used to enhance punishment for a later offense. 511 U.S. at 746-47. The Court explained that recidivist statutes “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Id. at 747. That principle controls here. Section 117(a) does not punish Bryant again for his tribal convictions; it punishes his new domestic assault, committed after two prior domestic-violence convictions. See Almendarez-Torres v. United States, 523 U.S. 224, 230-35 (1998); United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008).

Nothing in the Sixth Amendment forbids Congress from defining a recidivist offense by reference to prior convictions that were valid when entered. The Court has repeatedly held that recidivism is a traditional basis for increased punishment. Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 451 (1962); Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389, 400 (1995). And the Court has rejected the argument that a later sentencing or prosecution becomes unconstitutional merely because it considers an earlier conviction that did not itself require counsel. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-48. If that is true for sentencing enhancements, it is no less true for a recidivist element that Congress has chosen to make part of the offense definition. See Almendarez-Torres, 523 U.S. at 243-47.

The Ninth Circuit’s distinction between “sentencing” and “element” collapses on inspection. The Sixth Amendment concerns the validity of the prior conviction, not the label attached to the later proceeding. The Court has never held that a prior conviction constitutionally valid when entered becomes unusable simply because Congress places it in the offense definition rather than the sentencing rubric. To the contrary, the Court has treated prior convictions as facts that legislatures may rely on in defining recidivist crimes and in increasing punishment. See Oyler, 368 U.S. at 451; Almendarez-Torres, 523 U.S. at 243-47; Rodriquez, 553 U.S. at 386.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also disregards federalism and tribal sovereignty. Tribal convictions are valid because Congress has authorized tribal adjudication under the Indian Civil Rights Act, which does not impose the Sixth Amendment’s appointed-counsel guarantee. Santa Clara Pueblo, 436 U.S. at 56-57. A federal court may not retroactively invalidate tribal convictions by importing a constitutional rule that does not govern those tribunals. Wheeler, 435 U.S. at 322-23; Plains Commerce Bank, 554 U.S. at 337.

The better view, adopted by the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, is that valid tribal convictions may be used to prove recidivism under § 117(a). United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 604 (8th Cir. 2011); United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 999-1001 (10th Cir. 2011). Those decisions correctly recognized that Nichols governs and that the Constitution does not disable Congress from relying on prior tribal convictions that were valid under the governing law at the time. Id.; Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-48.

Bryant’s contrary position would produce untenable results. It would mean that Congress may not use valid tribal convictions to target serial domestic violence offenders, even though those convictions reflect conduct already adjudicated in a sovereign tribunal. Nothing in the Sixth Amendment compels that outcome. The judgment should be reversed.
