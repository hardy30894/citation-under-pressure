### Argument

The indictment should have been dismissed because the Government charged Mrs. Parker and Mr. Parker under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) on a theory that stretches “official act” beyond the statute’s limits. Section 201 reaches only a public official’s decision or action on a “question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy,” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3), and the Supreme Court has cautioned that bribery statutes must be applied to identifiable official matters, not generalized influence. See United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers of California, 526 U.S. 398, 406-08 (1999); McDonnell v. United States, 579 U.S. 550, 566-67 (2016).

Mrs. Parker was a clerk, not an ALJ. Her assigned tasks were ministerial: assisting at hearings, recording notes, and typing and mailing decisions. The Government’s theory was that she violated § 201 because she improperly altered benefit letters and forged ALJ Aronson’s signature. But the statute does not criminalize every dishonest act by a government employee. It criminalizes bribery tied to an official act. See McDonnell, 579 U.S. at 574-75; United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 230-31 (1914). Where the alleged conduct is not itself an exercise of official decisionmaking, the charge fails. See United States v. Lopez-Camacho, 132 F.3d 43, 45-46 (D.C. Cir. 1997); United States v. Dixon, 658 F.2d 181, 188-89 (3d Cir. 1981).

Birdsall does not save the indictment. Birdsall held that official duty may be informed by “established usage,” 233 U.S. at 231, but it still required conduct that is part of the officer’s duty. Later cases have recognized the same limitation: the bribe must seek action within the governmental function, not merely personal assistance or unauthorized meddling. See United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983); United States v. Biaggi, 853 F.2d 89, 99-100 (2d Cir. 1988). Here, the indictment alleged no official authority in Mrs. Parker to approve benefits, reinstate claims, or bind the SSA. At most, it alleged private fraud using access to agency forms and a forged signature. That is not the “decision or action” Congress defined. 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3); McDonnell, 579 U.S. at 570-71.

The Fifth Circuit should also reject the Government’s effort to sweep Mr. Parker into a conspiracy conviction without proof that he agreed to bribe a public official as the statute defines that term. Conspiracy requires proof of an agreement to accomplish the unlawful object, not merely association with a spouse who engaged in misconduct. See United States v. Nelson, 165 F.3d 1180, 1182-83 (8th Cir. 1999); United States v. Feola, 420 U.S. 671, 686-88 (1975). Where the substantive offense is not shown, the conspiracy falls with it. See United States v. Rosen, 716 F.2d 151, 154-55 (2d Cir. 1983); United States v. Waymer, 55 F.3d 564, 568-69 (11th Cir. 1995).

Nor can the convictions stand on the premise that Mrs. Parker was somehow a “public official” acting in an official capacity whenever she touched SSA files. Section 201 is limited to officials or persons acting “for or on behalf of the United States.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(1). Courts have refused to extend federal bribery statutes to employees whose conduct is outside the narrow scope of their delegated authority. See United States v. Quinn, 359 F.3d 666, 673-74 (4th Cir. 2004); United States v. Arroyo, 581 F.2d 649, 654-55 (7th Cir. 1978). The Government’s reading would collapse the distinction between bribery and employee theft or falsification.

Finally, the indictment was deficient because it did not allege with specificity the official acts supposedly corrupted by the alleged payments. An indictment must set forth the elements of the offense and fairly inform the defendants of the charge. See Hamling v. United States, 418 U.S. 87, 117-18 (1974); Russell v. United States, 369 U.S. 749, 763-64 (1962). Here, the Government’s broad theory obscured whether the alleged “official acts” were the approval of claims, the reinstatement of benefits, or simply the transmission of forged papers. That ambiguity is fatal under the Supreme Court’s insistence that the jury be instructed and the charge framed around a concrete “question or matter.” See McDonnell, 579 U.S. at 574-75; Sun-Diamond, 526 U.S. at 406-07.

Because the alleged conduct fell outside § 201(b)(2)(C), the convictions for conspiracy and substantive bribery cannot stand. The Court should reverse.
