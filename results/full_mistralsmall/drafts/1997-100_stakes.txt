**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings because such proceedings share the hallmarks of a criminal trial for purposes of double jeopardy protection.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Clause protects against not only multiple prosecutions but also multiple punishments for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they resemble the trial on guilt or innocence in critical respects. *Id.* at 438-39. Specifically, the Court noted that in a capital sentencing proceeding, the state must prove the existence of statutorily defined aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt, the jury makes specific findings, and the proceeding is conducted before a jury with full procedural protections. *Id.* at 439-40.

The Court reaffirmed this principle in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), where it held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial of an aggravating factor found insufficient by an appellate court. *Id.* at 210. The Court explained that the sentencing proceeding in *Rumsey* shared the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” because it required the state to prove the existence of an aggravating factor beyond a reasonable doubt and resulted in a finding that exposed the defendant to a greater punishment. *Id.* at 209-10.

The reasoning of *Bullington* and *Rumsey* is not limited to capital cases. As the Supreme Court has explained, the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protections turn on the nature of the proceeding, not the potential punishment. *See United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 134-35 (1980) (applying double jeopardy protections to a sentencing proceeding where the defendant was exposed to an increased sentence). The Court has emphasized that the Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense, regardless of whether the proceeding is characterized as part of the trial or sentencing. *See id.* at 129-30.

Here, the prior conviction allegation proceeding shares the hallmarks of a criminal trial for purposes of double jeopardy protection. Under California law, the state must prove the existence of a prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993), and the proceeding is conducted before a jury with full procedural protections, including the right to confrontation and cross-examination. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224 (1996). The proceeding results in a finding that exposes the defendant to a greater punishment, including a doubled sentence under California’s Three Strikes law. *See* Cal. Penal Code § 667(e)(1). Thus, the prior conviction allegation proceeding is indistinguishable from the trial on guilt or innocence for purposes of double jeopardy protection.

II. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Monge v. California*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), does not foreclose petitioner’s double jeopardy claim.

In *Monge*, the Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. *Id.* at 753. However, the Court’s decision in *Monge* was based on the unique circumstances of that case and does not control the outcome here. Specifically, the Court in *Monge* noted that the prior conviction allegation proceeding in that case was not a jury trial and did not require proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 752. In contrast, the prior conviction allegation proceeding in this case was a jury trial with full procedural protections, including proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Thus, *Monge* is distinguishable and does not foreclose petitioner’s double jeopardy claim.

III. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this case because the evidence was insufficient to support the finding.

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial where the evidence was insufficient to support the finding. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 18 (1978). Here, the California Court of Appeal held that the evidence was insufficient to support the finding that petitioner had suffered a prior serious felony conviction. *Monge v. California*, 68 Cal. App. 4th 1119, 1124 (1998). Thus, retrial of the prior conviction allegation would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this case. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
