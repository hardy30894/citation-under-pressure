**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of mental disease or defect to negate *mens rea* when the State has provided a separate insanity defense. Arizona’s evidentiary rule, which channels such evidence exclusively to the insanity determination, comports with due process because it preserves the State’s legitimate interest in maintaining a coherent and administrable framework for adjudicating criminal responsibility.

**I. Due Process Does Not Mandate That States Allow Expert Mental-State Evidence To Negate Mens Rea**

This Court has long recognized that “the Due Process Clause guarantees a defendant a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s accusations.” *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973). But that guarantee does not entitle a defendant to present any evidence he wishes, in any manner he chooses. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42 (1996) (plurality opinion). Rather, “the accused, as is required of the State, must comply with established rules of procedure and evidence designed to assure both fairness and reliability in the ascertainment of guilt and innocence.” *Chambers*, 410 U.S. at 302.

In *Egelhoff*, this Court upheld a Montana statute that prohibited a defendant from introducing voluntary intoxication evidence to negate *mens rea*. The plurality reasoned that the rule did not violate due process because it reflected a “value judgment” that “a person who voluntarily becomes intoxicated should be held to the same standard of conduct as a sober person.” *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 49-50. Similarly, Arizona’s rule reflects a judgment that mental disease or defect, when not rising to the level of legal insanity, should not negate criminal intent. The State may “define the precise mental state that is required for a particular crime” and may “choose to treat certain mental states as irrelevant.” *Id.* at 50 n.4.

**II. Arizona’s Insanity Defense Provides an Adequate Alternative Avenue for Considering Mental Disease**

Arizona’s rule does not bar all consideration of mental illness; it merely confines such evidence to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof. *See* Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502. This Court has never held that a defendant has a constitutional right to present mental-disease evidence for two distinct purposes—both to negate *mens rea* and to prove insanity. To the contrary, the Court has recognized that “the insanity defense is a separate affirmative defense that is not inconsistent with a finding that the defendant possessed the requisite *mens rea*.” *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 770 (2006) (citing *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 45-46).

The distinction between *mens rea* and insanity is not merely semantic. *Mens rea* focuses on whether the defendant intended the act; insanity focuses on whether the defendant lacked the capacity to understand the wrongfulness of the act. *See, e.g., United States v. Pohlot*, 827 F.2d 889, 900 (3d Cir. 1987). Arizona’s rule prevents confusion and preserves the jury’s ability to apply the law as the State has defined it. Due process does not require the State to blur these distinct inquiries.

**III. The Rule Does Not Violate the Principle of *In re Winship***

Petitioner argues that Arizona’s rule violates the principle that the State must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *See In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). But *Winship* does not require the State to admit any particular type of evidence. The State may “exclude evidence that is not relevant to the *mens rea* inquiry as the State has defined it.” *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 56. Because Arizona has defined *mens rea* without reference to mental disease or defect, evidence of such disease is simply irrelevant to that element. The State remains obligated to prove intent, but it may do so without requiring the jury to consider evidence that the legislature has deemed irrelevant to that question.

**IV. The Rule Is Consistent with Historical Practice and the Views of Other Jurisdictions**

At common law, evidence of mental disease was admissible only on the issue of insanity, not to negate *mens rea*. *See, e.g., State v. Mott*, 931 P.2d 1046, 1050 (Ariz. 1997) (en banc). Many States continue to follow this approach. *See, e.g., State v. Wilcox*, 700 N.E.2d 913, 918 (Ohio Ct. App. 1997); *State v. Herrera*, 993 P.2d 854, 860 (Utah 1999). This Court has consistently deferred to state legislative judgments in this area. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 536 (1968) (plurality opinion) (noting that the States have “wide latitude” in defining criminal responsibility).

**CONCLUSION**

Arizona’s rule does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
