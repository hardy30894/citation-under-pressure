**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit’s “class of one” theory cannot be squared with this Court’s Equal Protection precedents. The Equal Protection Clause protects against intentional, arbitrary governmental discrimination, but it does so through classifications. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976); *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265-66 (1977). This Court has repeatedly explained that equal protection claims ordinarily require a showing that the plaintiff was treated differently from others similarly situated because of membership in a protected class or because the government drew a classification that irrationally discriminated among persons. *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439-41 (1985); *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1, 10 (1992). Respondent alleged neither. Her theory—that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from others, out of spite—would transform every mistaken, arbitrary, or even malicious municipal decision into a constitutional tort. That is not the law.

1. **Equal protection requires a cognizable classification, not merely a grievance over individualized treatment.**  
The Clause commands that no State “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has treated that guarantee as prohibiting invidious discrimination and irrational classifications. In *Davis*, the Court held that disproportionate impact alone does not establish an equal protection violation; the plaintiff must show discriminatory purpose. 426 U.S. at 239. In *Arlington Heights*, the Court likewise required proof that a decision “in part ‘because of,’ not merely ‘in spite of,’ its adverse effects” on an identifiable group. 429 U.S. at 264-65. And in *Cleburne*, the Court invalidated a zoning decision only after applying rational-basis review to a classification affecting a cognizable group. 473 U.S. at 440-41.

Respondent’s complaint does not identify any group, class, or class-based standard. She says only that the Village imposed a more onerous easement requirement on her than on neighboring applicants. That is a demand for individualized equal treatment, not a challenge to a classification. This Court has never held that the Constitution requires a public official to treat every person identically in the administration of discretionary land-use decisions. To the contrary, *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961), recognizes that the Equal Protection Clause “allows the States wide latitude” in drawing distinctions and applying rules.

2. **A bare allegation of animus does not state an equal protection claim absent a forbidden classification or irrational systemic distinction.**  
The court below held that a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him” is enough. That holding conflicts with *United States v. Armstrong*, 517 U.S. 456, 464-65 (1996), which emphasized that selective enforcement claims require a showing that the challenged action had both a discriminatory effect and a discriminatory purpose. The plaintiff must identify others similarly situated who were treated differently, and the difference must be tied to impermissible discrimination. *Id.* at 465. Respondent alleged only that other property owners allegedly received 15-foot easements. Even if true, that does not establish the constitutionally relevant comparator showing, because discretionary municipal decisions often turn on different engineering, safety, and administrative considerations. *F.C.C. v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313-15 (1993).

Nor can respondent recast a simple dispute over public administration as a “class of one” claim. In *O’Bannon v. Town Court Nursing Center*, 447 U.S. 773, 785-86 (1980), the Court explained that the Clause protects against deprivation of a legal right only when the government’s action distinguishes among persons in a constitutionally relevant way. And in *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979), the Court required proof that the State selected a course of action “because of, not merely in spite of,” its adverse effects. Respondent’s allegations, even if accepted, show at most an improper motive in a single permit dispute, not a cognizable equal protection classification.

3. **Recognizing a “class of one” cause of action would constitutionalize ordinary local disputes and upset settled limits on federal judicial review.**  
This Court has warned against converting the Equal Protection Clause into a general license for federal review of every adverse government decision. *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 603-05 (2008), later confirmed that some governmental decisions—especially discretionary, individualized ones—are not amenable to “class of one” review because such review would “constitutionalize” routine administration. Although *Engquist* postdates this case, it reflects the logic already embedded in the Court’s equal protection cases: when government must make discretionary judgments, equal protection does not require perfect parity. *Id.* at 604; *Beach Communications*, 508 U.S. at 313-15.

Land-use and utility connection decisions are paradigmatic discretionary judgments. If every claim of unequal treatment in such settings stated an equal protection violation, federal courts would become super zoning boards. That result would be inconsistent with *Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 416 U.S. 1, 8 (1974), and *Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365, 388-89 (1926), which recognize broad local authority over land-use regulation subject only to constitutional limits.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed. Respondent has not alleged membership in any class, nor facts establishing a constitutionally cognizable classification. The Equal Protection Clause does not supply a cause of action for a freestanding grievance that local officials treated one landowner unfairly.
