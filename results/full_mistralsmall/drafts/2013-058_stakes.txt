**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12) EXEMPT “RETIREMENT FUNDS” WITHOUT LIMITING THE EXEMPTION TO FUNDS THAT ARE CURRENTLY BEING USED FOR RETIREMENT.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemptions do not condition protection on the debtor’s present use of the funds for retirement. Section 522(b)(3)(C) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” the enumerated sections of the Internal Revenue Code, including § 408 (IRAs). 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); see also 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(12) (identical). The Seventh Circuit erred in importing a temporal requirement—“funds held for the debtor’s retirement”—that the statute does not contain. *See* Clark v. Rameker (In re Clark), 714 F.3d 559, 563–64 (7th Cir. 2013).

This Court has repeatedly held that exemptions are to be construed according to their terms, without judicial addition or subtraction. *See, e.g., Taylor v. Freeland & Kronz*, 503 U.S. 638, 645 (1992) (“We do not rewrite the Bankruptcy Code.”); *Owen v. Owen*, 500 U.S. 305, 313 (1991) (“[W]e are not free to rewrite the Bankruptcy Code.”). Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) protects “retirement funds” held in statutorily defined tax-exempt accounts. It does not require that those funds be currently reserved for the debtor’s retirement. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 408 (defining IRAs as tax-exempt accounts without imposing post-death use restrictions).

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary rule—limiting the exemption to funds “held for the owner’s retirement”—finds no support in the statutory text. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” and this Court has cautioned against importing undefined terms with judicially crafted limitations. *See United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”). Here, Congress chose not to restrict the exemption to funds currently being used for retirement. To engraft such a limitation is to rewrite the statute.

II. **THE TAX CODE’S TREATMENT OF INHERITED IRAS CONFIRMS THAT THEY ARE “RETIREMENT FUNDS” UNDER § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12).**

The Seventh Circuit discounted the fact that inherited IRAs are tax-deferred accounts under § 408, erroneously concluding that tax deferral alone is insufficient. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. But § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) expressly tie the exemption to accounts “exempt from taxation under” the enumerated sections of the Internal Revenue Code, including § 408. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 408. Inherited IRAs are tax-deferred accounts governed by § 408, and thus fall within the plain text of the exemption. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (providing that inherited IRAs are tax-deferred accounts under § 408).

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the distribution requirements of § 401(a)(9) is misplaced. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. Section 401(a)(9) governs required minimum distributions; it does not redefine the account’s character as a “retirement fund” for purposes of § 522. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9). The tax code treats inherited IRAs as tax-deferred retirement accounts, and the Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” held in such accounts. The Seventh Circuit’s attempt to distinguish inherited IRAs based on distribution requirements conflates tax treatment with exemption eligibility. *See United States v. Centennial Sav. Bank FSB*, 499 U.S. 573, 584 (1991) (“[T]ax consequences do not control the scope of a bankruptcy exemption.”).

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S RULE WILL CREATE INEQUITABLE AND UNPREDICTABLE RESULTS.**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule—limiting the exemption to funds “held for the debtor’s retirement”—invites arbitrary line-drawing and undermines the uniformity of bankruptcy exemptions. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. Under this approach, a debtor who inherits an IRA on the eve of bankruptcy could lose the exemption, while a debtor who inherits the same IRA years before bankruptcy could retain it. This temporal distinction has no basis in the statute and creates perverse incentives. *See Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (“Deciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed to the achievement of a particular objective is the very essence of legislative choice.”).

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s rule conflicts with the treatment of other exemptions. For example, § 522(d)(1) exempts “[t]he debtor’s aggregate interest, not to exceed $23,675 in value” in real property used as a residence. 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(1). The statute does not require that the debtor have lived in the property for any particular period before filing. *See Schwab v. Reilly*, 560 U.S. 770, 790 (2010) (exemptions are defined by their terms, not judicial gloss). Similarly, § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) protect retirement funds held in tax-exempt accounts, regardless of when the debtor plans to use them. The Seventh Circuit’s temporal limitation is unsupported by the statute and should be rejected.

IV. **THE MAJORITY OF COURTS TO ADDRESS THIS ISSUE HAVE HELD THAT INHERITED IRAS ARE EXEMPT UNDER § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12).**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Eighth and Fifth Circuits, which have held that inherited IRAs are exempt under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). *See In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312, 318 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010); *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486, 491 (5th Cir. 2012). These courts correctly recognized that the Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-fund exemptions protect funds held in tax-exempt accounts, without requiring that the funds be currently reserved for the debtor’s retirement. *See Nessa*, 426 B.R. at 318; *Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 491. This Court should follow their well-reasoned analysis and reverse the Seventh Circuit’s decision.
