**Argument**

Section 17(a)(2) of the Bankruptcy Act, the predecessor to 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A), excepted from discharge liabilities “for obtaining property by false pretenses or false representations.” *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), explained that bankruptcy’s fresh start is for the “honest but unfortunate debtor,” not one who has procured property by fraud. The text and history of the discharge exception therefore support a narrow but real rule: a debtor may not erase debts that arose from his fraudulent acquisition of money or property. *Zerbe v. Baker*, 171 F.2d 748, 749 (2d Cir. 1948), held that a debt for money obtained by false representation was not dischargeable because the liability “arose out of” the fraud.

But the question here is not whether Cohen may discharge the amount he obtained by fraud. It is whether he must also pay consequential and statutory damages, including treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs, that were imposed after the fraud was established. Under the Bankruptcy Act, the answer was no unless the additional liability itself was “obtained by” the fraud. *In re Mogle*, 188 F.2d 20, 22 (3d Cir. 1951), recognized that discharge exceptions are construed with reference to the source of the debt, not merely the existence of a fraudulent act somewhere in the background. A liability that is imposed after the fact as a separate incident of litigation is not the same thing as the property obtained by fraud. See *In re Vickers*, 176 F.2d 285, 286 (10th Cir. 1949) (discharge exception focuses on the debt “obtained by” the proscribed conduct).

The statute’s phrase “to the extent obtained by” is limiting language. It confines nondischargeability to the amount actually procured through fraud. *Schwireck v. Schulte*, 213 F.2d 184, 186 (8th Cir. 1954), applied that principle and treated the exception as reaching only the debt traceable to the fraudulent acquisition itself. Likewise, *In re Ransford*, 194 F.2d 655, 657 (2d Cir. 1952), rejected an expansive reading that would sweep in liabilities beyond the money or property fraudulently obtained. Those cases reflect the ordinary meaning of the discharge exception: the bankruptcy court may preserve the victim’s claim to the fraudulently obtained amount, but it may not enlarge that exception into a general anti-debtor penalty provision.

That distinction is dispositive here. Cohen’s liability for the rent overcharges may be excepted from discharge to the extent those overcharges were obtained by false pretenses or actual fraud. But the treble-damages multiplier, attorney’s fees, and costs were not “obtained by” Cohen at all; they were imposed later by operation of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. The Bankruptcy Act did not except from discharge every obligation that a fraudulent debtor might later incur in court. *In re Mogle*, 188 F.2d at 22; *In re Ransford*, 194 F.2d at 657. The fact that a claim is related to fraud does not mean the entire judgment is a “debt for obtaining property by false pretenses.” See *Zerbe*, 171 F.2d at 749.

Nor can the punitive character of the treble-damages award save it from discharge. Under the pre-Code cases, punitive or exemplary damages were not automatically nondischargeable merely because they accompanied a fraud claim. *McIntyre v. Kavanaugh*, 242 U.S. 138, 141 (1916), held that discharge exceptions are governed by the statutory description of the debt, not by the moral indignation inspired by the debtor’s conduct. Likewise, *Cohen v. Marshall*, 140 F.2d 43, 45 (1st Cir. 1944), treated dischargeability as a question of whether the particular liability fit within the exception Congress enacted, not whether punishment would be desirable. The same principle applies here. Congress did not say “any debt arising out of fraud”; it said debt “to the extent obtained by” fraud. The multiplier and fees are not property obtained by fraud.

The contrary view would erase the statutory limitation. If every fraudulent debtor must pay not only the victim’s loss but also every civil penalty and fee award that follows, then “to the extent obtained by” does no work. Courts are not free to read that phrase out of the statute. *United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co.*, 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929), teaches that each statutory word must be given effect if possible. The narrower construction preserves both the exception and the fresh-start policy recognized in *Local Loan*, 292 U.S. at 244.

This Court should therefore reverse the judgment below insofar as it holds the treble-damages, attorney’s-fee, and cost awards nondischargeable. At most, Cohen’s discharge may be denied as to the actual amount unlawfully obtained through fraud. Everything beyond that is a separate post-fraud liability and is dischargeable.
