**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN NATIONAL PARK SYSTEM UNITS IN ALASKA IS UNAMBIGUOUSLY AUTHORIZED BY ANILCA § 103(C).**

The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands or waters from the National Park Service’s (NPS) regulatory authority. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (2012) (“[T]he Secretary [of the Interior] may continue to exercise regulatory authority in units of the National Park System in Alaska in accordance with the laws governing the administration of such units.”). This Court has consistently deferred to the Secretary’s interpretation of ANILCA where, as here, the statute’s language is unambiguous. *See, e.g., United States v. Alaska, 521 U.S. 1, 5-6 (1997) (holding that ANILCA § 103(c) preserves federal regulatory authority over Alaska lands within national parks); see also Alaska v. Babbitt, 38 F.3d 1068, 1073 (9th Cir. 1994) (same). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary interpretation in *Katie John v. United States*, 247 F.3d 1032 (9th Cir. 2001), was explicitly rejected by the Supreme Court in *Alaska*, which reaffirmed that ANILCA § 103(c) does not carve out an exception for state-owned lands. *Alaska*, 521 U.S. at 5-6.

Sturgeon’s argument that ANILCA § 103(c) limits NPS authority to federally owned lands is foreclosed by the statute’s text and legislative history. The statute provides that the Secretary “may continue to exercise regulatory authority in units of the National Park System in Alaska in accordance with the laws governing the administration of such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The phrase “in accordance with the laws governing the administration of such units” refers to the Organic Act and other federal laws governing national parks, not to state ownership of underlying lands. *See Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 544-45 (1976) (holding that the federal government’s power over public lands is plenary and not diminished by state ownership of underlying lands). The legislative history of ANILCA confirms that Congress intended to preserve federal regulatory authority over all lands and waters within park boundaries, regardless of ownership. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 96-1344, at 27 (1980) (“The Secretary’s authority to regulate activities within park boundaries is not diminished by state or private ownership of underlying lands.”).

**II. THE NPS’S HOVERCRAFT BAN APPLIES TO STATE-OWNED WATERS WITHIN YUKON-CHARLEY RIVERS NATIONAL PRESERVE.**

The NPS’s hovercraft regulation, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), prohibits the operation of hovercraft “within the boundaries of any unit of the National Park System.” This regulation is valid under the Secretary’s authority to regulate activities within park boundaries, regardless of the ownership of the underlying land or water. *See United States v. Lindsey, 595 F.3d 519, 524 (5th Cir. 2010) (upholding NPS regulation prohibiting hovercraft on state-owned waters within a national park); see also Sierra Club v. Babbitt, 65 F.3d 1502, 1508 (9th Cir. 1995) (holding that NPS regulations apply to all activities within park boundaries, irrespective of land ownership).

Sturgeon’s reliance on *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 872 F.3d 986 (9th Cir. 2017), is misplaced. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in that case was vacated by the Supreme Court, which granted certiorari and remanded for further consideration in light of *Alaska v. United States*, 521 U.S. 1 (1997). *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 138 S. Ct. 42 (2017). The Supreme Court’s decision in *Alaska* makes clear that ANILCA § 103(c) preserves federal regulatory authority over all lands and waters within park boundaries, including state-owned waters. *Alaska*, 521 U.S. at 5-6.

**III. STURGEON LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE NPS’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS.**

Sturgeon’s claim that the NPS’s hovercraft ban violates his rights under the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is meritless. The Clause does not create a right to operate a hovercraft on state-owned waters within a national park. *See Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. 36, 79 (1872) (holding that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only those rights “which owe their existence to the Federal government, its National character, its Constitution, or its laws”). Moreover, Sturgeon’s alleged injury—his inability to use a hovercraft on state-owned waters—is not fairly traceable to the NPS’s regulation, as the regulation applies to all waters within the park boundary, regardless of ownership. *See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992) (holding that a plaintiff must show that the injury is “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct).

Even if Sturgeon could establish an injury-in-fact, his claim fails because the NPS’s regulation is a valid exercise of federal authority under ANILCA § 103(c). *See Massachusetts v. EPA*, 549 U.S. 497, 518 (2007) (holding that a plaintiff must show that the injury is likely to be redressed by a favorable decision).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The NPS’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within national park system units in Alaska is authorized by ANILCA § 103(c), and the hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of that authority. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the regulation, and his claims are without merit.
