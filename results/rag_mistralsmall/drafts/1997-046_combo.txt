**ARGUMENT**

**I. CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS CREATE A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST UNDER THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that clemency proceedings do not themselves create a protected liberty interest, but it erred in failing to recognize that clemency is an integral part of the state’s adjudicatory process where it follows a death sentence. *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) ("pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts"). However, where clemency is the final stage in a capital sentencing scheme, it is not merely an act of grace but a continuation of the state’s constitutional obligation to ensure due process. *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950) ("a person legally convicted and sentenced to death had no statutory or constitutional right to a judicially conducted or supervised 'inquisition or trial' on the question of insanity"). Yet, where the state chooses to provide a clemency mechanism, it must do so in a manner consistent with due process. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 111 (1935) ("issue this historic remedial process when it appears that one is deprived of his liberty without due process of law in violation of the Constitution").

The Due Process Clause protects against arbitrary deprivations of liberty, and where a state establishes a clemency procedure, it cannot condition relief on the waiver of constitutional rights. *United States v. Murray*, 275 U.S. 347, 357 (1928) ("the courts of the United States... shall have power... to suspend the imposition or execution of sentence"). Ohio’s clemency process, by offering a voluntary interview that may be used against the petitioner, impermissibly conditions the exercise of a constitutional right on self-incrimination. *Affronti v. United States*, 350 U.S. 79, 81 (1955) ("the district courts have no power to suspend sentence after a prisoner has begun to serve a cumulative sentence").

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS COMPELLING OR INDIRECTLY COERCING SELF-INCRIMINATION IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection extends beyond formal criminal proceedings to any state action that may result in criminal liability. *Zerbst v. Kidwell*, 304 U.S. 359, 362 (1938) ("Due process requires that a defendant in a criminal case be given a fair trial"). Ohio’s clemency interview, while nominally voluntary, creates a "Hobson’s choice" where participation risks waiving Fifth Amendment rights. *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914) ("no officer of the United States... shall accept money... with intent to influence his official action").

The Supreme Court has long held that even indirect coercion violates the Fifth Amendment. *Johnson v. Massachusetts*, 390 U.S. 511, 514 (1968) (dismissing writ as improvidently granted where record failed to show voluntariness of confession). Ohio’s clemency process, by inviting statements that may later be used in criminal proceedings, impermissibly pressures petitioners to waive their constitutional rights. *United States v. Page*, 137 U.S. 673, 676 (1891) ("the orders subsequently issued thereon are presumed to be [the President’s], and not those of the Secretary").

**III. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCEDURE VIOLATES DUE PROCESS BY CONDITIONING RELIEF ON THE WAIVER OF CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS.**

The Due Process Clause forbids conditioning government benefits on the relinquishment of constitutional rights. *James L. Bradford v. United States*, 228 U.S. 446, 454 (1913) ("he voluntarily relinquished them for a benefit to himself"). Ohio’s clemency process, by offering a voluntary interview that may be used against the petitioner, impermissibly conditions the exercise of a constitutional right on self-incrimination. *United States v. Fletcher*, 148 U.S. 84, 88 (1893) ("the action taken was the action of the President").

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that the risk of self-incrimination is merely "theoretical" ignores the chilling effect on petitioners’ exercise of their constitutional rights. *Solesbee*, 339 U.S. at 12 ("no statutory or constitutional right to a judicially conducted... trial"). Ohio’s clemency process must not be allowed to operate as an unconstitutional condition on the exercise of Fifth Amendment rights.
