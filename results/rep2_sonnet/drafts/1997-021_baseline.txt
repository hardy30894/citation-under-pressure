# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD, AS APPLIED BY THE BOARD, IS ARBITRARY AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE ACT

This Court reviews the Board's interpretation of the National Labor Relations Act under the framework of *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), but that deference has limits. An agency's interpretation must be "rational and consistent with the statute," *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 787 (1990), and the Board cannot escape judicial scrutiny simply by invoking its expertise. As this Court has recognized, an incumbent union is entitled to a presumption of continuing majority support, but that presumption is *rebuttable*. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-38 (1987). The Board's implementation of this rebuttable presumption—requiring the same "reasonable doubt" showing for polling as for outright withdrawal of recognition—renders the presumption *irrebuttable* in practice, contrary to this Court's teaching in *Fall River*.

The Board has candidly acknowledged that Board-conducted RM elections are the "preferred" method for testing union support, and are procedurally superior to employer polls. Yet the Board applies the *identical* evidentiary threshold to both. This is internally incoherent. If polling and RM elections serve the same evidentiary function of testing—rather than presuming—union support, an employer should not need to already possess evidence sufficient to justify outright withdrawal before it may conduct the less drastic, more information-generating step of polling. As the Fifth Circuit correctly held in *NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1141 (5th Cir. 1981), polling is a "useful and legitimate tool for the employer who is in sincere doubt of the union's majority status," and should be available upon "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support," even where "that evidence is not sufficient by itself to justify withdrawal." *Id.* at 1145 (quoting *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)). The Ninth Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), and the Sixth Circuit did likewise in *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982).

The Board's collapse of these distinct standards forces employers into an intolerable dilemma. Under §§ 8(a)(1) and 8(a)(2) of the Act, an employer who continues bargaining with a union that has actually lost majority support commits an unfair labor practice. *See* 29 U.S.C. §§ 158(a)(1), (a)(2). Yet under the Board's regime, an employer possessing genuine, good-faith uncertainty—but not yet "sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal," *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1297—has no lawful mechanism to resolve that uncertainty short of unilaterally guessing. The poll, precisely the tool designed to replace speculation with objective information, is foreclosed until the employer no longer needs it. This is not a "rational" application of the Act's presumption; it renders polling "only marginally useful," a result the courts of appeals have unanimously rejected as inconsistent with the balance Congress struck between employee free choice and stable bargaining relationships.

## II. THE BOARD'S FACTUAL FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

Even applying the Board's own stringent standard, its finding fails under the substantial-evidence test of *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), which requires that the reviewing court consider the record "as a whole," including evidence that "fairly detracts" from the Board's conclusion. *Id.* at 488.

The Board arbitrarily discounted reliable, probative evidence of union disaffection. First, the Board disregarded statements by employees Ridgick and Marsh expressing dissatisfaction with the union, relying on a per se rule that job-interview statements and dues-complaints are categorically unreliable. But no such categorical rule finds support in the Act itself; the Board must weigh evidence, not exclude entire categories of relevant statements by fiat. Where, as here, employees volunteered candid assessments unprompted by coercion, the ALJ and Board were obligated to weigh—not summarily discard—that evidence.

Second, the Board rejected the reports of employees Bloch and Mohr—including Mohr, the union's own steward—that the night shift and broader unit did not support the union, on the ground that these were secondhand or "off-the-cuff" reports. Yet this Court has recognized that Board precedent otherwise permits employers to rely on objective indicia including employee statements reflecting group sentiment. *See Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 778. A steward's assessment of his own unit's sentiment is powerful, not negligible, evidence; the Board's discounting of it as merely "unverified" ignores the practical reality that employers rarely possess notarized affidavits of coworkers' political sentiments.

Finally, the Board's atomized, employee-by-employee refutation exercise loses sight of the aggregate picture: an emergent 32-employee unit, newly assembled from a larger predecessor unit, exhibiting numerous independent, unsolicited statements of union disaffection converging on the same conclusion—Local 724 had lost majority support. Considered "as a whole," *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488, this record does not permit the Board's finding that Allentown lacked good-faith reasonable doubt.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
