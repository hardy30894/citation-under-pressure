**ARGUMENT**

I.  
The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment, and its failure to afford due process violates petitioner Eugene Woodard’s fundamental rights.

Petitioner Woodard does not seek a right to clemency; he seeks only the right not to be deprived of his liberty without due process. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a person may have a ‘liberty’ interest in avoiding a particular punishment even if the State has not created that interest by statute.” *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 480 (1995). In *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), the Court held that prisoners possess a liberty interest in the absence of punitive conditions that are “so severe as to constitute a meaningful restriction on the freedom to live their lives in prison.” Id. at 557. That principle applies with equal force here: Ohio’s clemency procedure is not merely a discretionary act of mercy—it is an integral component of the state’s capital punishment system. As the Sixth Circuit acknowledged, “the clemency review is conducted in connection with a scheduled execution date,” and the state’s own regulations mandate it 45 days prior to execution. This procedural inevitability transforms what might otherwise be a mere administrative formality into a critical stage of the death penalty process.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that when a state establishes a procedure through which life or liberty is determined, due process attaches—even if the outcome is discretionary. In *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court rejected the notion that a prisoner lacks a liberty interest in commutation proceedings simply because the decision rests with a board. Justice Stevens dissented, correctly observing that “the Due Process Clause applies to each step” in the state’s decisionmaking process, including clemency. *Id.* at 471, 101 S.Ct. at 2468. The majority’s contrary holding rested on tradition, not constitutional principle. But tradition cannot override the Constitution’s guarantee of due process. As the Court stated in *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956): “The right to a fair trial is not a mere matter of form.” When the state creates a mechanism for reviewing a death sentence—especially one that operates with mechanical regularity and near-inevitability—the individual’s liberty interest in that process becomes cognizable under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Ohio’s clemency procedure is not a mere “act of grace.” It is a structured, mandatory inquiry into the propriety of executing a condemned inmate. The state’s regulation requires the parole board to schedule a review 45 days before execution unless stayed—a timeline that leaves no room for discretion. This procedural rigidity confirms that the clemency process is not incidental but central to the finality of the death sentence. To deny due process at this stage is to allow the state to impose irreversible consequences without affording the most basic safeguards. The Court has never countenanced such a result. In *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), the Court held that a state must provide counsel at the first appeal as of right because that stage is “an integral part of the adjudicatory process.” *Id.* at 393, 105 S.Ct. at 834. If due process applies to appeals, it must apply with greater force to clemency, which occurs *after* all judicial remedies have been exhausted and is the last opportunity for relief.

II.  
The option to participate in a clemency interview constitutes an unconstitutional condition, compelling a waiver of the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

The Ohio regulation permits the parole board to consider statements made during a voluntary interview in determining whether to recommend clemency. While the regulation does not explicitly compel testimony, the practical effect is coercive. A prisoner who refuses to speak risks being deemed uncooperative, thereby forfeiting any chance of clemency. This creates a “Hobson’s choice”: either waive constitutional rights or face the risk of losing life itself. Such a choice is precisely what the Fifth Amendment was designed to prevent.

In *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), the Court struck down a prison policy that threatened disciplinary action for refusing to answer questions during an internal investigation. The Court held that “the threat of punishment for asserting a constitutional right is impermissible.” *Id.* at 324, 96 S.Ct. at 1560–61. The same logic applies here. Ohio’s clemency process functions as a de facto interrogation, and the threat of adverse consideration based on silence—or worse, on incriminating statements—creates a direct incentive to waive the Fifth Amendment. The state cannot condition the possibility of clemency on the surrender of a fundamental constitutional right.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the idea that a “voluntary” choice can be truly free when the alternative is death. In *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 51 (2004), the Court emphasized that “the right to remain silent is not a mere procedural formality.” It is a substantive protection against governmental compulsion. Here, the state offers a “choice” between silence and self-incrimination—but silence carries the cost of death. That is not a choice; it is coercion.

The dissenting opinion in the Sixth Circuit correctly identifies the danger: “Once the camel’s nose is under the tent, I suspect that it may prove difficult to keep the rest of the animal out.” *Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 110 F.3d 1197, 1210 (6th Cir. 1997) (Nelson, J., concurring in part, dissenting in part). By allowing the state to use the threat of clemency denial to extract statements, the court opens the door to a system where the condemned are forced to testify against themselves—not in a criminal trial, but in a political process that determines whether they live or die.

III.  
The district court’s refusal to address these issues was error. The case should not be remanded for speculative inquiry.
