**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously holds that a Clean Water Act consent decree triggers CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s timely § 107 cost-recovery claim. That holding conflicts with the plain language of CERCLA and fundamental principles of statutory construction, sovereign immunity, and due process.

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) applies only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action.” The 2004 consent decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations—discharge of leachate without a permit. It did not mention CERCLA, hazardous substances, or response costs. This Court has long held that statutes must be interpreted according to their terms. *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad*, 118 U.S. 394, 396 (1886) (courts must “give effect to the intention of the legislature”). The decree’s silence on CERCLA liability cannot constitute a resolution of such liability. *See Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845) (federal jurisdiction is strictly construed; nothing passes by implication).

Moreover, the decree expressly disclaimed any “finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3. This disclaimer is dispositive. *Vilas v. City of Manila*, 220 U.S. 345, 357 (1911) (municipal laws protecting private rights continue until abrogated). A party cannot “resolve” liability it has not admitted. *Gonzales v. Williams*, 192 U.S. 1, 12 (1904) (territorial inhabitants retain rights unless clearly surrendered). The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on substantive obligations ignores the decree’s plain language.

**II. Guam Was Not a “Potentially Responsible Party” Under CERCLA**

CERCLA liability attaches only to “potentially responsible parties.” The Navy—not Guam—constructed and operated the Ordot Dump for decades, disposing of munitions and chemicals. Guam assumed ownership only after the Navy relinquished sovereignty. The United States, as sovereign, cannot be held liable under CERCLA without a clear waiver. *Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock*, 347 U.S. 110, 122 (1954) (sovereign immunity is strictly construed). The Navy’s historical control over Guam—treated as a “US Naval ship” until 1950—underscores that the United States, not Guam, was the operator. *Duncan v. Kahanamoku*, 327 U.S. 304, 324 (1946) (military authority is limited; civilian rights are paramount). Guam cannot be deemed a PRP for contamination it did not cause.

**III. The Decision Extinguishes Guam’s Property Interest Without Due Process**

A right to contribution under CERCLA is a property interest. The D.C. Circuit’s ruling effectively extinguishes that interest by deeming the consent decree a § 113(f)(3)(B) settlement, thereby triggering a three-year statute of limitations that expired in 2007. This deprives Guam of its property without due process. *Burns v. Wilson*, 346 U.S. 137, 142 (1953) (basic rights guaranteed by the Constitution cannot be denied). The decree gave no notice to Guam that it was resolving CERCLA liability. *Mora v. McNamara*, 389 U.S. 934, 935 (1967) (Douglas, J., dissenting) (courts must not lightly deprive parties of rights). The United States cannot silently extinguish Guam’s claim through a settlement that never mentioned CERCLA.

**IV. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Bar Guam’s § 107 Claim**

Because the consent decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s § 107 claim remains timely. The six-year statute of limitations for remedial actions under § 113(g)(2)(B) applies. *Pearcy v. Stranahan*, 205 U.S. 257, 263 (1907) (courts take judicial notice of territorial status and applicable law). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion rewrites the statute. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 331 U.S. 416, 429 (1947) (courts should not substitute their views for the statutory scheme). Congress deliberately provided different limitations periods for § 107 and § 113; the court’s decision collapses that distinction.

**Conclusion**

The judgment below should be reversed. Guam is entitled to pursue its § 107 cost-recovery claim against the United States for the Navy’s decades-long contamination of the Ordot Dump.
