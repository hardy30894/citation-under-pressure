I. The Reinstatement Provision Does Not Apply to Pre-Effective-Date Reentries.

Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), authorizes reinstatement of a prior removal order against an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally.” The provision took effect on April 1, 1997. See Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 309(a), 110 Stat. 3009-546, 3009-625. Petitioner reentered in 1982 and remained continuously thereafter. Because the statute speaks only to reentries occurring after its effective date, it does not reach his 1982 reentry.

The presumption against retroactive legislation “is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence.” Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). “Elementary considerations of fairness dictate that individuals should have an opportunity to know what the law is and to conform their conduct accordingly.” Id. at 265. Nothing in the text of § 241(a)(5) supplies a “clear indication” that Congress intended the provision to attach new consequences to conduct completed before April 1, 1997. See INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001). Absent such indication, the statute must be construed to apply only to post-enactment reentries.

II. Application to Pre-Effective-Date Reentries Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive.

Even if the text were ambiguous, attaching the statute’s severe disabilities to a pre-1997 reentry would operate retroactively. A statute has retroactive effect when it “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting Soc’y for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)). Section 241(a)(5) imposes precisely such a new disability: it strips an alien who reentered before 1997 of any opportunity to seek adjustment of status or other relief that was available at the time of reentry and for the ensuing fifteen years.

Petitioner’s long-term residence and subsequent marriage to a United States citizen created settled expectations that the new statute destroyed. Because Congress did not clearly authorize that destruction, the statute cannot be applied to him. See St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321-22. The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that petitioner lacked a “protectable expectation”—misapplies the Landgraf inquiry, which focuses on whether the statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment,” not on whether the alien subjectively anticipated the precise form of relief later foreclosed. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270.

The petition for review should be granted and the reinstatement order vacated.
