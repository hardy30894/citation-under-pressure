I.

A criminal conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting a threat cannot stand absent proof that the defendant subjectively intended his words to threaten. Criminal statutes are construed to require mens rea as to every element that makes conduct criminal. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (statutes silent on intent are read against “injustice” and in light of “the common-law tradition” requiring “some mental element”). The same rule applies to speech offenses. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (“The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger”).

The First Amendment supplies an independent requirement of subjective intent. Speech is protected unless the speaker intends to produce the forbidden consequence. Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616, 627 (1919) (Holmes, J., dissenting) (punishment requires “intent” to bring about the evil); Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 673 (1925) (Holmes, J., dissenting) (“every idea is an incitement” only when uttered with that purpose). Statutes punishing threats must therefore be limited to expressions the speaker meant to be taken as threats. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931) (statute invalid when it “does not distinguish” between innocent and culpable intent).

II.

Objective standards alone are insufficient. A speaker’s words may alarm yet remain protected if not uttered with threatening purpose. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309 (1940) (“Resort to epithets or personal abuse is not in any proper sense communication of information or opinion safeguarded by the Constitution” only when the speaker intends the abuse to threaten). Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942) (fighting words are those “which by their very utterance inflict injury” when spoken with the requisite intent). Absent that intent, the communication falls within the “marketplace of ideas” protected by the First Amendment. Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 375 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (“the path of safety lies in the opportunity to discuss freely”).

III.

The statute’s text and history confirm the requirement. Section 875(c) uses the word “threat,” a term that at common law connoted an intentional communication of harm. Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 318 (1957) (statutes employing common-law terms incorporate the common-law mental element). An objective test would criminalize protected speech uttered without intent to threaten, violating the principle that “the Constitution protects expression” unless the speaker intends to produce imminent lawless action. Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“speech . . . may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest”). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand.
