**ARGUMENT**

A Board refusal to reopen a prior benefits determination is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under the Railroad Retirement Act and Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act. Section 355(f) authorizes review of a “final decision” of the Board, and nothing in the statutory text excludes reopening determinations from that term. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when Congress authorizes review of “final” agency action, courts should not create a categorical jurisdictional bar absent clear statutory language. See **Sackett v. Environmental Protection Agency, 566 U.S. 120, 127–28 (2012)**; **Mach Mining, LLC v. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, 575 U.S. 480, 486–87 (2015)**.

The better reading of § 355(f) is the one adopted by the D.C. Circuit in **Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board, 826 F.3d 500, 502–05 (D.C. Cir. 2016)**. There, the court held that the Railroad Retirement Act “grants the [court] jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations.” Id. at 502. That conclusion follows naturally from the statutory structure. A reopening denial is not a mere internal housekeeping act; it is the Board’s conclusive word that the claimant will not receive the benefits determination he seeks. See **Stovic, 826 F.3d at 504–05**. When the Board refuses to reopen, there is nothing left for the agency to decide on that claim. That is the hallmark of finality. See **Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997)**.

The contrary rule adopted by the Fifth Circuit below rests on an overreading of **Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99 (1977)**. Sanders held only that the Administrative Procedure Act does not itself provide jurisdiction to review a refusal to reopen a Social Security claim. Id. at 107–09. The Court emphasized that its holding was tied to the APA and to the Social Security Act’s own review scheme. Id. at 108–09. Sanders did not announce a universal rule that reopening denials are never reviewable under a separate statute that expressly authorizes review of final Board action. See **Stovic, 826 F.3d at 503–04**; **Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966)**.

Indeed, the Court has repeatedly distinguished jurisdictional statutes that permit review of “final” agency action from statutes that do not. See **Darby v. Cisneros, 509 U.S. 137, 144–47 (1993)**; **FTC v. Standard Oil Co. of California, 449 U.S. 232, 239–45 (1980)**. Under those decisions, finality turns on whether the agency has reached the consummation of its decisionmaking process and whether legal consequences flow from the action. **Bennett, 520 U.S. at 177–78**. A refusal to reopen satisfies both requirements. It consummates the Board’s consideration of the reopening request, and it leaves the prior denial in force as the operative benefits determination. See **Stovic, 826 F.3d at 504–05**.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on its prior precedent does not answer the statutory question; it only explains why the panel felt bound. But precedent cannot foreclose review when it misconstrues the governing statute. The Court has “the responsibility of deciding what a statute means,” **Neal v. United States, 516 U.S. 284, 295 (1996)**, and must apply the text Congress enacted. Section 355(f) says what it says: final decisions of the Board are reviewable. A refusal to reopen is final as to the reopening request. See **Stovic, 826 F.3d at 504–05**.

This Court should therefore hold that the Board’s denial of reopening is a final decision subject to judicial review, vacate the dismissal, and remand for consideration of Salinas’s petition on the merits.
