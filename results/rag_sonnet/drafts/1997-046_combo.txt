ARGUMENT

I. WOODARD HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.

Due process protections attach only where the State works a deprivation of life or liberty through the judicial or adjudicative process. See Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103 (1935) (due process is violated only where a defendant "is deprived of his liberty without due process of law" through the operation of the courts). Executive clemency, by contrast, has never been understood as part of that adjudicative apparatus. It is, and has always been, an act of grace lying outside the judgment of guilt and sentence that the courts alone pronounce.

This Court's own precedents confirm that clemency is categorically different from trial or appeal. In Bradford v. United States, 228 U.S. 446 (1913), the Court explained that a pardon obtained through negotiated terms represented "the Government clemency," secured "not by wresting from him a right, either directly or through the coercion of circumstances, but by his voluntary reparation." Clemency there was treated as a benefit conferred by executive grace, not a legal entitlement flowing from any process due the petitioner. The same understanding pervades United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), where the Court treated recommendations bearing on "leniency" and executive clemency as matters of official discretion committed to executive officers, not judicial adjudication subject to procedural constitutional oversight.

This Court has likewise recognized that clemency and parole function as extrajudicial safety valves standing apart from the sentencing process itself. In United States v. Murray, 275 U.S. 347 (1928), the Court observed that Congress structured federal sentencing with awareness of "the existence of provisions for parole and executive clemency" as separate mechanisms outside the district courts' own sentencing authority. Affronti v. United States, 350 U.S. 79 (1955), reaffirmed that same understanding, again invoking "the existence of provisions for parole and executive clemency" as distinct from, and unconstrained by, the procedures that govern judicial sentencing. If clemency were itself part of the adjudicative process to which due process protections attach, this structural separation would make no sense.

Zerbst v. Kidwell, 304 U.S. 359 (1938), further illustrates that discretionary executive determinations of this kind do not generate liberty interests enforceable through due process litigation. There the Court held that a parole violator's status reverted to one "analogous to those of an escaped convict," and that "[t]he Parole Board and its members have been granted sole authority" over such determinations. The prisoner possessed no cognizable liberty interest in the Board's exercise of discretion. The same logic controls here: the Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency review is committed to its own discretion, and Woodard has no liberty interest in how, or whether, that discretion is exercised.

Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9 (1950), is directly on point. There a death-sentenced prisoner argued that due process required a judicial hearing before the Governor could determine, on the report of appointed physicians, whether the prisoner had become insane after sentencing. This Court upheld Georgia's procedure, agreeing that "determination of petitioner's sanity by the Governor supported by the report of physicians had met the standards of due process." If the Constitution tolerates final, non-judicial executive resolution of a life-or-death post-sentence question without a hearing, evidentiary confrontation, or counsel, it follows a fortiori that the distinct executive act of clemency review—traditionally even less judicialized than the insanity determination in Solesbee—likewise generates no independent due process entitlement.

II. THE OPTION TO PARTICIPATE VOLUNTARILY IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The Fifth Amendment forbids compulsion; it does not forbid opportunity. Woodard was "notified that he could have a pre-hearing interview," an option, not a mandate. Nothing in the Ohio procedure requires him to speak, and nothing penalizes his silence. Under Mooney, 294 U.S. 103, constitutional injury requires an actual deprivation worked by the State's process; here there is none, because Woodard remains free to decline the interview altogether.

This Court's decisions confirm that voluntary participation in clemency proceedings, including statements addressing the very offense of conviction, has long been an accepted feature of the clemency tradition without constitutional objection. In Johnson v. Massachusetts, 390 U.S. 511 (1968), the record reflected a capital defendant's own unsworn appeal to the jury: "I put my life into your hands. Please recommend clemency, life imprisonment." Such voluntary, self-referential pleas addressed to a sentencing or clemency-granting body have never been thought to implicate compelled self-incrimination, precisely because they are freely offered by the prisoner in pursuit of mercy, not extracted by threat of penalty.

Nor can Woodard manufacture compulsion out of speculation regarding future prosecutions. Where, as here, the risk of self-incrimination rests on proceedings not yet initiated and perhaps never to be initiated, any Fifth Amendment claim is unripe. Cf. Solesbee, 339 U.S. 9 (permitting executive resolution of sensitive post-sentence issues without constitutionalizing every contingent risk). Because the interview is optional, uncoerced, and unaccompanied by penalty for silence, Ohio's clemency procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed insofar as it recognizes any due process interest in clemency review, and the Fifth Amendment claim should be rejected as premature and without merit.
