**ARGUMENT**

Petitioners respectfully submit that the government bears the burden of persuasion to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that each defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant limitations period. This principle is firmly rooted in the Due Process Clause and this Court’s precedents governing conspiracy prosecutions and burdens of proof.

1. **The Due Process Clause Requires the Government to Prove Each Element of the Offense Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process mandates that the government prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (citing *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895)). While *Winship* was decided after the relevant conduct in this case, the foundational principle it articulated—that the Due Process Clause prohibits shifting the burden of proof to the defendant on any element of a crime—was long established by this Court’s pre-1970 decisions. *Coffin*, for example, held that “the presumption of innocence is a law of evidence, having its foundation in the principles of natural justice,” and that the government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 453. This principle applies with equal force to conspiracy prosecutions, where membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period is an essential element of the offense. *See United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 63 (1950) (noting that the government must prove each element of the crime charged).

2. **Conspiracy Membership During the Limitations Period Is an Element of the Offense**

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the existence of a conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it are separate elements of the offense. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 254 (1940) (“The gist of the crime of conspiracy is the agreement to commit an unlawful act.”). The government must prove both the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s knowing participation in it. *See id.* at 254–55. Where the indictment alleges a conspiracy that continued into the limitations period, the government must prove that the defendant was a member of that conspiracy during that period. *See United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910) (holding that the government must prove the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy as charged in the indictment).

3. **The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion on All Elements of the Offense**

This Court has long held that the government bears the burden of persuasion on all elements of the offense, including the defendant’s participation in a conspiracy. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) (“The burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish every fact essential to the crime charged.”). This principle applies with particular force to conspiracy prosecutions, where the government must prove not only the existence of the conspiracy but also the defendant’s knowing and voluntary participation in it. *See United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 839 (2d Cir. 1939) (L. Hand, J.) (noting that the government must prove the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt).

4. **Withdrawal from a Conspiracy Does Not Shift the Burden of Proof**

The government argues that a defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the limitations period shifts the burden to the defendant to prove withdrawal or that the conspiracy ended. This argument is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. In *United States v. Irvine*, 96 F. 927 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1899), aff’d, 107 F. 601 (2d Cir. 1901), the court held that the government bears the burden of proving the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy during the relevant period, even where the defendant alleges withdrawal. *Id.* at 930. Similarly, in *United States v. Nathan*, 47 F. 454 (D. Or. 1891), the court held that the government must prove the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, regardless of whether the defendant alleges withdrawal. *Id.* at 456.

5. **The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden of Proof**

This Court has held that the jury must be instructed on the government’s burden of proof with respect to each element of the offense. *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (“The court should instruct the jury as to the law of the case, and it is error to refuse to do so.”). Where the indictment alleges a conspiracy that continued into the limitations period, the jury must be instructed that the government bears the burden of proving the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy during that period beyond a reasonable doubt. *See id.* at 18–19.

6. **The Government’s Failure to Prove Membership During the Limitations Period Is Not Harmless Error**

This Court has held that the government’s failure to prove an essential element of the offense is not harmless error. *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) (“The Government must establish every essential element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, and if it fails to do so, the conviction cannot stand.”). Where the government fails to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period, the conviction must be reversed. *See id.* at 614–15.

7. **The Government Cannot Rely on Presumptions to Prove Membership in the Conspiracy**

This Court has held that the government cannot rely on presumptions or inferences to prove an essential element of the offense. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (“Under our criminal law, the principle that there is a presumption of innocence in favor of the accused is basic.”). Where the government relies on a presumption that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period, the conviction must be reversed. *See id.* at 467–68.

8. **The Government’s Burden of Proof Applies to All Defendants in a Conspiracy Prosecution**

This Court has held that the government’s burden of proof applies to all defendants in a conspiracy prosecution, regardless of their role in the conspiracy. *United States v. Bruno*, 105 F.2d 921, 922 (2d Cir. 1939) (L. Hand, J.) (noting that the government must prove each defendant’s participation in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt). Where the government fails to prove that a defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period, the conviction of that defendant must be reversed. *See id.* at 922–23.

**Conclusion**

The government bears the burden of persuasion to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that each defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant limitations period. This principle is firmly rooted in the Due Process Clause and this Court’s precedents governing conspiracy prosecutions and burdens of proof. The court of appeals erred in affirming the convictions where the government failed to meet this burden. The judgment below should be reversed.
