**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A “GAP” IN FEDERAL COVERAGE BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ADOPTED AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations ... the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. This Court has long recognized that the OCSLA is a “gap-filling statute,” designed to supply state law only where federal law is silent or insufficient. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (explaining that the OCSLA “fill[s] the statutory void” where federal law is inadequate); *see also United States v. Louisiana*, 363 U.S. 1, 13 (1960) (noting that the OCSLA was enacted to “fill the void” in federal law governing submerged lands).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this “gap-filling” requirement. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law may be adopted as federal law under the OCSLA only “to fill a significant void or gap” in federal coverage. This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA’s purpose: to ensure that state law supplements federal law where federal law is incomplete, not to allow state law to override or expand upon federal standards. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 359.

Here, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive federal statute governing wage and hour disputes. *See Mitchell v. Lublin, McGaughy & Assocs.*, 358 U.S. 207, 211 (1959) (describing the FLSA as a “comprehensive federal statute” regulating wages and hours). The FLSA includes a savings clause that permits state laws to provide greater protections than federal law, 29 U.S.C. § 218 (1964), but this clause does not create a “gap” in federal coverage. Rather, it preserves state authority where federal law is silent on a particular issue. *See generally Dole v. Vincent, 358 U.S. 227 (1959)* (upholding state wage laws under the FLSA’s savings clause).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law—ignores the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement. This misreading would allow state wage and hour laws to displace federal standards even where federal law is complete, a result inconsistent with the OCSLA’s text and purpose. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 359; *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036.

II. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE FLSA’S COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME.

Even if the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement were satisfied, California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA’s comprehensive regulatory framework. The OCSLA permits state law to apply only if it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Supreme Court has held that state laws are inconsistent with federal law if they “stand[] as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941).

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, explicitly preserves state wage and hour laws, but this clause does not authorize state laws that conflict with the FLSA’s core objectives. *See Dole v. Vincent*, 358 U.S. 227 (1959) (upholding state wage laws under the savings clause where they did not conflict with federal standards). Here, California’s meal period requirement conflicts with the FLSA’s flexible approach to meal breaks. The FLSA does not mandate meal periods, and its regulations permit employers to adopt reasonable break policies. *See 29 C.F.R. § 785.19 (1968)* (permitting meal breaks during working hours). California’s rigid meal period requirement—mandating a 30-minute break for every five hours worked—imposes obligations that are inconsistent with the FLSA’s framework. *See Mitchell v. Lublin, McGaughy & Assocs.*, 358 U.S. at 211 (federal wage standards preempt conflicting state laws).

III. THE OCSLA’S HISTORY CONFIRMS THAT STATE LAW APPLIES ONLY WHERE FEDERAL LAW IS SILENT.

The OCSLA’s legislative history demonstrates that Congress intended state law to apply only where federal law is incomplete. In 1953, Congress enacted the OCSLA to define a body of law governing the outer Continental Shelf. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. Congress recognized that federal law alone would be inadequate to govern the diverse activities on the OCS and thus incorporated state law to “fill the void.” *Id.* at 359. This gap-filling function was reaffirmed in 1975, when Congress amended the OCSLA to allow for the assimilation of future state laws, but only where federal law is silent or insufficient. *See S. Rep. No. 93-627, at 10 (1975)* (explaining that state law applies “to the extent necessary to fill gaps in federal coverage”).

The Ninth Circuit’s holding—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—would render the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement meaningless. This misreading conflicts with the OCSLA’s text, purpose, and legislative history. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 359; *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA permits the application of state law only where federal law is silent or insufficient. The FLSA is a comprehensive federal statute that leaves no gap for state wage and hour laws to fill. California’s meal period requirement is inconsistent with the FLSA’s framework. The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
