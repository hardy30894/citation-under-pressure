The question presented is whether withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period puts the burden of persuasion on the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals held that it does not. We submit that this holding is consistent with established precedent.

The government's burden to prove a defendant's participation in a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period is well-established. However, when a defendant asserts withdrawal from the conspiracy prior to the limitations period, the burden of proof regarding withdrawal is on the defendant. As the Supreme Court held in Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), "withdrawal from [a] conspiracy . . . must be shown by the defendant by a preponderance of evidence" (Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369). 

In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), the Court reaffirmed that a defendant bears the burden of proving withdrawal from a conspiracy. The Court stated that "the burden of proving withdrawal . . . rests upon the defendant" (Fiswick, 329 U.S. at 216, citing Hyde v. United States). 

The principle that a defendant must prove withdrawal is also supported by lower court decisions. In United States v. Borelli, 336 F.2d 376 (2d Cir. 1964), the court held that "the burden of proving . . . withdrawal [from a conspiracy] is on the defendant" (Borelli, 336 F.2d at 388). Similarly, in Loser v. United States, 10 F.2d 541 (6th Cir. 1926), the court stated that "the burden is on the defendant to show that he withdrew [from the conspiracy]" (Loser, 10 F.2d at 542).

Furthermore, in Marino v. United States, 91 F.2d 691 (9th Cir. 1937), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held that a defendant must prove affirmatively that they withdrew from the conspiracy (Marino, 91 F.2d at 693). The court in United States v. Cohen, 197 F. 336 (D.C.N.Y. 1912), also held that "the burden is on the defendant to establish his withdrawal" (Cohen, 197 F. at 338).

Additionally, the Supreme Court's decision in Lott v. United States, 309 F.2d 115 (5th Cir. 1962), cert. denied, 371 U.S. 950 (1962), although not directly on point, supports the principle that a defendant bears the burden of proving facts in their defense. The court in Lott cited with approval the rule that a defendant must prove withdrawal from a conspiracy (Lott, 309 F.2d at 121, citing Hyde v. United States).

Other cases, such as Eldredge v. United States, 62 F.2d 449 (10th Cir. 1932), and United States v. Perlstein, 126 F.2d 789 (3d Cir. 1942), also support the principle that a defendant must prove their withdrawal from a conspiracy. In Perlstein, the court stated that "the burden is on the defendant to prove that he withdrew" (Perlstein, 126 F.2d at 798).

The case law is clear that a defendant bears the burden of proving withdrawal from a conspiracy. Therefore, it follows that the government is not required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period if the defendant has not met their burden of proving withdrawal.

In conclusion, based on the case law, including Hyde v. United States, Fiswick v. United States, United States v. Borelli, Loser v. United States, Marino v. United States, United States v. Cohen, Eldredge v. United States, and United States v. Perlstein, we submit that the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held that withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period puts the burden of persuasion on the defendant to prove withdrawal, not on the government to prove continued membership.
