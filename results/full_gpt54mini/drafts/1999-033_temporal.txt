**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not create a freestanding cause of action for every individualized grievance with government action. It prohibits invidious discrimination, not mere arbitrariness. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), recognized that a facially neutral law administered with “an evil eye and an unequal hand” violates equal protection, but the premise of that decision was discriminatory classification in administration, not a constitutional tort for isolated resentment or favoritism. Likewise, *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), held that “unequal application of a statute fair on its face” is not enough absent “intentional or purposeful discrimination.” Equal protection requires a protected class-based or otherwise constitutionally suspect basis for different treatment, not simply proof that one person was treated worse than another. Id.

That principle is reinforced by *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955), which confirms that under ordinary equal protection review, legislative line-drawing survives if there is any conceivable rational basis. The Clause does not authorize courts to second-guess every irrational or unkind local decision. *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), likewise upheld a classification even though it seemed underinclusive, because equal protection does not require perfect symmetry. These decisions show that the Constitution tolerates imperfect, even uneven, administration so long as it is not the product of forbidden discrimination.

Respondent’s theory would collapse this settled doctrine. If a single property owner could sue merely by alleging that officials demanded more from her than from others, every discretionary municipal decision would be converted into a federal equal protection case. But *Snowden*, 321 U.S. 1, makes clear that a plaintiff must show more than unequal treatment; she must show intentional discrimination of the kind the Fourteenth Amendment forbids. And *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), though later than the present rule set and therefore not cited for authority here, illustrates the same basic point: equal protection analysis turns on the constitutional character of the classification, not on whether government acted unwisely. This Court should not recognize a “class of one” claim untethered to any class-based discrimination when no pre-1970 authority supports such a cause of action.

The lower court’s approach also conflicts with the traditional limits on judicial review of discretionary administration. In *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the Court explained that the Equal Protection Clause does not forbid classifications simply because they result in some inequality; it requires only that there be a rational relation to a legitimate purpose. If a plaintiff could proceed by alleging that she was singled out, courts would be forced to scrutinize every individualized enforcement or permitting decision for parity, a task the Constitution does not assign them.

Nor does *Yick Wo* support respondent. There, the challenged licensing scheme was applied in a systematically discriminatory manner against Chinese laundry owners. 118 U.S. 356. The decision turned on proof of discriminatory enforcement against a class of persons, not on a one-off claim that an official acted unfairly toward a single applicant. Equal protection forbids “a hostile and discriminating law,” *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), and purposeful denial of equal laws to a class, but not a private litigant’s attempt to constitutionalize an ad hoc dispute with local officials.

The proper rule is therefore that a plaintiff states an equal protection claim only by alleging membership in a protected or identifiable class, or by showing intentional discrimination directed at such a class. A bare assertion that government treated one person differently from others is insufficient. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. 1; *McGowan*, 366 U.S. 420; *Railway Express*, 336 U.S. 106. Because respondent alleged only that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from other landowners, without any allegation of class-based discrimination, her complaint fails as a matter of law.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
