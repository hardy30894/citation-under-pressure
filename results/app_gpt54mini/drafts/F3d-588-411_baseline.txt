### ARGUMENT

#### I. The district court erred by defining “shielding from detection” in a way that expanded § 1324 beyond its text and invited conviction on an overbroad theory.

This Court reviews de novo whether a jury instruction correctly stated the law, considering the instructions as a whole to determine whether they misled the jury or prejudiced the defendant. *United States v. Griesbach*, 540 F.3d 654, 657 (7th Cir. 2008); *United States v. Hach*, 162 F.3d 937, 945 (7th Cir. 1998). A conviction may not stand where the instruction permits guilt on conduct Congress did not criminalize. *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008).

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) punishes anyone who “conceals, harbors, or shields from detection” an alien known to be unlawfully present. Congress used three distinct verbs, and each must be given independent meaning. *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001). “Shielding” therefore cannot be read so broadly that it swallows the separate statutory terms or criminalizes ordinary employment-related conduct. See *United States v. Aguilar*, 515 U.S. 593, 605 (1995) (courts must avoid constructions that “leave[] no limiting principle”).

Ye objected when the jury asked for definitions of “concealing” and “shielding.” The district court nevertheless supplied a definition drawn from nonstatutory formulations used in other circuits. That was error because the statute itself supplies the operative standard, and courts should not add gloss that enlarges criminal liability. *Burrage v. United States*, 571 U.S. 204, 218 (2014); *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. at 514. Where a statute uses common words, those words should be given their ordinary meaning, not a judicially created expansion. *Taniguchi v. Kan Pacific Saipan, Ltd.*, 566 U.S. 560, 566 (2012).

The danger of the court’s instruction is apparent here. The government’s evidence showed that Ye hired undocumented workers, paid some in cash, failed to maintain forms, and provided housing and transportation. But § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) is not a general employment-verification statute. Congress separately criminalized hiring unauthorized aliens in § 1324a. A construction of “shielding” that covers the mere failure to keep records, the provision of employment, or routine assistance to employees would collapse the distinction between the felony and misdemeanor provisions. Courts must interpret criminal statutes to preserve the line Congress drew. *United States v. Kozminski*, 487 U.S. 931, 952 (1988); *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. at 515.

This Court should require proof that “shielding” means conduct intended to place an alien beyond the government’s ready detection, not mere concealment-by-omission or assistance ancillary to employment. Other circuits have cautioned that § 1324 does not reach ordinary employment decisions or all conduct that may make detection more difficult. See *United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567, 573-74 (2d Cir. 1999); *United States v. Rubio-Gonzalez*, 674 F.2d 1067, 1071 (5th Cir. 1982); *United States v. Cantu*, 557 F.2d 1173, 1181 (5th Cir. 1977). To the extent the district court’s instruction suggested otherwise, it was too broad.

The instructional error was not harmless. *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 9 (1999). The jury asked specifically for the meaning of the challenged term, demonstrating genuine uncertainty on the dispositive issue. The government’s proof of “shielding” depended on conduct equally consistent with hiring and retention of undocumented workers, not on a distinct effort to hide anyone from immigration authorities. Because the instruction allowed the jury to convict without finding the narrow intent Congress required, reversal is warranted. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 509-10 (1995).

#### II. The evidence was insufficient to prove Ye acted with the purpose of preventing government detection.

A conviction must be reversed if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, no rational juror could find each element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979); *United States v. Jones*, 635 F.3d 909, 914 (7th Cir. 2011). Intent may be proved circumstantially, but suspicion is not enough. *United States v. Garcia*, 919 F.3d 489, 493 (7th Cir. 2019).

Here, the evidence showed that Ye hired undocumented workers, paid them in cash, failed to keep employment records for some workers, and provided housing and transportation. But those facts, even if unlawful under other provisions, do not establish beyond a reasonable doubt that Ye acted for the purpose of preventing government detection. The government offered no evidence that Ye used false identities, secret compartments, coded communications, false addresses, or other classic concealment tactics. Compare *United States v. Tipton*, 518 F.3d 591, 602-03 (8th Cir. 2008), with *United States v. Costello*, 666 F.3d 1040, 1047-48 (7th Cir. 2012). Ye’s conduct is just as consistent with knowingly employing undocumented workers and minimizing paper trails to evade employment rules as it is with “shielding” them from detection under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii).

That distinction matters. Congress made it a separate offense to hire unauthorized aliens, and the jury convicted Ye on that count as well. The felony conviction cannot rest on evidence that proves only the misdemeanor hiring offense. *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. at 515; *United States v. Aguilar*, 515 U.S. at 605. Because the record does not support a finding that Ye specifically intended to prevent the government from detecting the workers, the § 1324 conviction must be vacated. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. at 319.

For these reasons, Ye respectfully requests that the Court reverse his conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and remand for further proceedings.
