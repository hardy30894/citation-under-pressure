**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s ADA and MHRA Claims Because Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled.**

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”) and the Missouri Human Rights Act (“MHRA”), an employer discriminates against a qualified individual with a disability when it takes adverse employment action based on a perception that the individual is disabled. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a) (1994); *Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.055* (1999). The ADA and MHRA incorporate the “regarded as” prong of disability discrimination, which prohibits employers from acting on the basis of a belief that an individual has a substantially limiting impairment, regardless of whether the individual is actually disabled. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C) (1994); *Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010(16)(c)* (1999). To prevail on a “regarded as” claim, a plaintiff must show that the employer perceived him as having an impairment that substantially limits a major life activity. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 1630.9 (1999).

Pine Lawn terminated Epps based on Chief Hardy’s belief that Epps’s degenerative disk disease rendered him unable to perform the essential functions of a police officer. Hardy’s March 17, 2000 memorandum explicitly stated that Epps “would never be able to function as a regular officer” due to his condition. This admission demonstrates that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. *See* *School Bd. of Nassau Cty. v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 284 (1987) (holding that an employer’s belief that an employee’s contagious disease substantially limits a major life activity satisfies the “regarded as” prong).

Pine Lawn’s reliance on *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471 (1999), is misplaced. *Sutton* does not alter the “regarded as” analysis; it merely clarifies that mitigating measures must be considered when determining whether an individual is actually disabled. *Id.* at 489. The “regarded as” prong remains unaffected. *See* *Murphy v. United Parcel Serv., Inc.*, 527 U.S. 516, 521 (1999) (distinguishing the “regarded as” prong from the actual disability inquiry).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s ADA and MHRA claims. The record contains sufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled.

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s Workers’ Compensation Retaliation Claim Because Missouri Law Prohibits Retaliation for the Exercise of Workers’ Compensation Rights.**

Missouri law protects employees from retaliation for exercising their rights under the workers’ compensation system. *See* Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780 (1999). This statute provides that no employer shall discharge or discriminate against an employee for filing a workers’ compensation claim. *Id.* The statute’s plain language prohibits adverse employment actions taken in retaliation for the assertion of workers’ compensation rights. *See* *Frohlich v. State ex rel. Mo. Hwy. & Transp. Comm’n*, 957 S.W.2d 506, 510 (Mo. Ct. App. 1997) (construing § 287.780 to prohibit retaliatory discharge).

Pine Lawn terminated Epps shortly after he underwent neck surgery for a work-related injury. The timing of his termination, coupled with Chief Hardy’s admission that he recommended termination due to Epps’s extensive workers’ compensation claims and absences, creates a prima facie case of retaliation. *See* *Holloway v. City of Erlanger*, 878 S.W.2d 842, 845 (Ky. Ct. App. 1994) (holding that temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action may establish a prima facie case of retaliation).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s workers’ compensation retaliation claim. The record contains sufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn retaliated against Epps for exercising his workers’ compensation rights.

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress Claim Because Pine Lawn’s Conduct Was Extreme and Outrageous.**

Under Missouri law, a plaintiff may recover for intentional infliction of emotional distress if the defendant’s conduct was “so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency, and to be regarded as atrocious, and utterly intolerable in a civilized community.” *State ex rel. Cardinal Glennon Mem’l Hosp. v. Gaertner*, 583 S.W.2d 107, 111 (Mo. 1979) (en banc) (quoting *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 46 cmt. d (1965)).

Epps’s termination followed a pattern of conduct by Pine Lawn that, if proven, could be deemed extreme and outrageous. Chief Hardy’s memoranda explicitly relied on Epps’s workers’ compensation history and medical condition to justify termination. The timing of the termination, coupled with the lack of any legitimate, non-retaliatory justification, raises a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn’s conduct was extreme and outrageous. *See* *Frohlich*, 957 S.W.2d at 512 (holding that a plaintiff may establish a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress by showing that the defendant’s conduct was extreme and outrageous).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s intentional infliction of emotional distress claim. The record contains sufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn’s conduct was extreme and outrageous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s summary judgment should be reversed, and this matter should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
