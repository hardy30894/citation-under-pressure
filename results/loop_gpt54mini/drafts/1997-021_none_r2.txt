Petitioner is entitled to relief for two independent reasons. The Board’s polling rule is not rationally consistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and the Board’s contrary factual finding is unsupported by substantial evidence.

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling standard is irrational and inconsistent with the Act.

The Act protects both employee free choice and stable bargaining relationships. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). But it does not authorize the Board to eliminate a limited, neutral procedure for testing whether an incumbent union still has support. The Board’s rule allows polling only when the employer already has objective evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition. That collapses two distinct inquiries into one and makes polling largely pointless. A poll is meant to confirm or dispel doubt before the employer takes the far more consequential step of withdrawing recognition. Nothing in the Act requires the Board to forbid that intermediate step. NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940); NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944).

The Board’s rule is especially unsound because it converts a rebuttable presumption into a practical conclusive one. The presumption of continuing majority support is an administrative device to promote stability, not a command that the employer remain unable to verify objective evidence of changed employee sentiment. See Radio Officers’ Union v. NLRB, 347 U.S. 17, 49-50 (1954). An employer facing credible indications of lost support should not be forced either to bargain blindly or to risk an unfair-labor-practice charge for seeking the truth through a safeguarded poll. This Court has repeatedly recognized that labor doctrine must be applied in light of practical realities, not formal abstraction. NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1938); Southern Steamship Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47 (1942).

Nor does the Act specifically regulate employer polling. In the absence of a statutory command, the Board may choose among permissible policies, but its choice must remain reasonable and faithful to the Act’s structure. NLRB v. Truck Drivers Local Union No. 449, 353 U.S. 87, 96-97 (1957); NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963). The Board’s rule is not reasonable because it strips polling of its core function. A standard that demands withdrawal-level proof before an employer may merely inquire into union support is internally inconsistent and practically self-defeating.

II. The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.

Even if the Board’s standard were accepted, its application fails under substantial-evidence review. Substantial evidence requires more than suspicion or selective reliance on isolated facts; the Board must account for the record as a whole. Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938); Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951).

The record here showed objective indications that the union’s majority support had eroded. Employees made statements to Allentown Mack suggesting that Local 724 no longer enjoyed broad backing. The company then conducted a secret-ballot poll supervised by a priest, and the result was 19 to 13 against the union. That evidence did not compel the Board to find good-faith doubt; it made the Board’s contrary conclusion untenable. The Board rejected much of this evidence by parsing comments too narrowly and by discounting statements that were plainly probative of employee sentiment.

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that the Board must evaluate such evidence realistically. In NLRB v. Century Cement Mfg. Co., 208 F.2d 84 (2d Cir. 1953), employee expressions of dissatisfaction were relevant to the employer’s doubt. In NLRB v. R. H. Oswald Co., 217 F.2d 193 (7th Cir. 1954), the court recognized that an employer may rely on objective signs of lost support without waiting for formal decertification. In NLRB v. Somerville Packing Co., 211 F.2d 574 (1st Cir. 1954), the court required contextual evaluation of employee statements rather than mechanical dismissal. And in Columbia Envelope Co. v. NLRB, 151 F.2d 571, 575 (7th Cir. 1945), the court explained that the Board must give reasons grounded in the record when it rejects probative evidence.

The Board did not do that here. It treated the company’s evidence as if the question were whether Allentown Mack had already proved actual loss of majority support, rather than whether it had objective grounds for reasonable doubt. That is a materially different and more demanding inquiry. Consolidated Edison Co., 305 U.S. at 229-30; Universal Camera Corp., 340 U.S. at 488-91. By imposing the wrong practical standard, the Board ensured the wrong result.

The Court should therefore hold that the Board’s polling restriction is inconsistent with the Act and that the finding against Allentown Mack is unsupported by substantial evidence. Allen-Bradley Co. v. NLRB, 286 U.S. 254, 260-61 (1932); Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 798-99 (1945).
