The district court correctly determined that appellees were not joint employers of appellants under the AWPA. Control over the manner and means of work remains the decisive inquiry. Singer Mfg. Co. v. Rahn, 132 U.S. 518, 523 (1889). Here, Luxama alone selected, directed, supervised, and paid the crew, while appellees merely designated fields and observed progress two or three times daily. Such limited oversight does not establish employment. Standard Oil Co. v. Anderson, 212 U.S. 215, 221-22 (1909); Chicago, R.I. & P. Ry. Co. v. Bond, 240 U.S. 449, 456 (1916).

The economic-realities test likewise confirms the absence of joint employment. The workers' compensation derived solely from Luxama, who furnished transportation, tools, and supervision. United States v. Silk, 331 U.S. 704, 716-18 (1947); Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb, 331 U.S. 722, 730 (1947). Appellees supplied neither the instrumentalities of labor nor the opportunity for profit or loss that defines an employer. Bartels v. Birmingham, 332 U.S. 126, 130 (1947). Little Rock's provision of seeds and boxes constituted only advance financing, not the assumption of employer status. Walling v. Portland Terminal Co., 330 U.S. 148, 152-53 (1947).

Nor did appellees "utilize" Luxama's services within the meaning of the registration provisions. The statute presupposes a direct employment relationship; independent contractors fall outside its reach. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 120-21 (1944). Because Luxama operated as an autonomous contractor whose registration had lapsed without appellees' knowledge, no duty to verify arose. Gray v. Powell, 314 U.S. 402, 411 (1941). The district court's grant of summary judgment therefore rests on settled principles governing the employer-employee distinction.
