**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the FDCPA regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors collecting debts for their own account. 15 U.S.C. § 1692e; 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Santander’s alleged conduct occurred after it purchased the loans, so at the time of collection it was collecting debts owed to itself, not “debts owed or due another.” That is outside the Act’s ordinary coverage. Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995); Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 135-39 (4th Cir. 2016).

The FDCPA’s text draws a basic distinction between creditors and debt collectors. A “creditor” is a person “to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). A “debt collector,” by contrast, is generally a person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). As this Court has explained, the Act’s purpose is “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” not to regulate ordinary creditors collecting their own debts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e); Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d at 136-37; see also Obduskey v. McCarthy & Holthus LLP, 139 S. Ct. 1029, 1035-36 (2019).

Petitioners’ theory collapses the statutory distinction by making default status dispositive. But the statute does not say that every buyer of defaulted debt is a debt collector. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that “the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition set forth in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).” Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d at 136. That conclusion follows from the text of § 1692a(6): the principal definition asks whether the entity “regularly collects” debts “owed or due another,” not whether it acquired them before or after default. Id. at 136-38; Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-18 (11th Cir. 2015); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1208-09 (9th Cir. 2013).

Petitioners rely heavily on the statutory exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes collection of debts “not in default at the time it was obtained.” But that exclusion presupposes that the person already fits the threshold definition of “debt collector.” Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d at 136-37; Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1314-15. Reading the exclusion as an independent definition would invert the statute’s structure. The better reading is the one the Fourth Circuit adopted: a person who acquires a debt and then collects it for itself is a creditor, unless it satisfies one of the Act’s separate definitions of “debt collector.” Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d at 136-39.

Nor does the fact that Santander may previously have serviced the loans for another entity change the analysis. FDCPA status is assessed at the time of the challenged conduct. Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318; Schlegel, 720 F.3d at 1209. Once Santander purchased the receivables, the alleged communications at issue were made on its own behalf, not “on behalf of another.” Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d at 138-39. A company does not remain a debt collector for all purposes merely because it also engages in some third-party collection activity. Id. at 139; see also Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 137 S. Ct. 1718, 1721-22 (2017).

The Supreme Court’s decision in Henson confirms the point. The Court held that entities who “regularly purchase debts originated by someone else and then seek to collect those debts for their own account” do not fall within the second prong of § 1692a(6). Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 137 S. Ct. at 1721-22. That is exactly this case. Petitioners’ claims therefore fail as a matter of law.

Because Santander collected debts it owned, the FDCPA did not apply to the challenged conduct. The judgment should be affirmed.
