**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Amendment Requires Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

The true-threat exception to First Amendment protection must be narrowly confined to speech that the speaker intends as a threat. This Court has consistently required subjective intent for other categories of unprotected speech, and the same principle governs here.

In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the Court held that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 871(a) for threatening the President required the government to prove a “true threat,” which must be distinguished from “political hyperbole” and “constitutionally protected speech.” The Court examined the context and the speaker’s apparent intent, concluding that the statement was not a threat because it was “a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition.” *Id.* at 708. *Watts* thus implicitly requires consideration of the speaker’s subjective purpose.

This Court’s incitement jurisprudence confirms that subjective intent is essential. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that the government may not punish advocacy of violence unless it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” The requirement that speech be “directed to” inciting lawless action demands proof of the speaker’s specific intent. Similarly, in *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 318 (1957), the Court construed the Smith Act to require “specific intent” to overthrow the government, distinguishing advocacy of abstract doctrine from incitement. *Accord Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 229 (1961) (membership in Communist Party requires “specific intent” to further unlawful aims); *Noto v. United States*, 367 U.S. 290, 297-98 (1961) (same).

The same principle applies to other categories of unprotected speech. In *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 152-53 (1959), the Court held that a strict-liability obscenity ordinance violated the First Amendment because it would “tend to restrict the dissemination of books which are not obscene” by punishing booksellers who lacked knowledge of the contents. The Court emphasized that “the constitutional guarantees of the freedom of speech and of the press stand in the way of imposing” strict liability. *Id.* at 153. *See also Roth v. United States*, 354 U.S. 476, 492 (1957) (obscenity requires scienter). And in *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court reversed a breach-of-peace conviction because the jury instruction allowed punishment for speech that “stirs the public to anger” without requiring a clear and present danger of serious evil.

The objective standard adopted by the Third Circuit—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as a threat—is irreconcilable with these precedents. That standard punishes speech that the speaker never intended to be threatening, such as rap lyrics, emotional outbursts, or hyperbolic statements. As *Watts* recognized, such speech is protected unless the speaker subjectively intends to communicate a threat. The objective standard chills protected speech because speakers cannot know whether a reasonable person might later deem their words threatening. *See Smith*, 361 U.S. at 152-53.

**II. Section 875(c) Requires Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

The statute criminalizes “any communication containing any threat.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). The term “threat” at common law required an intent to communicate a threat. *See United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 399 (1930) (statutory words are presumed to have their common-law meaning). This Court has repeatedly held that criminal statutes carry a presumption of mens rea, especially when First Amendment rights are at stake. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-51 (1952) (presumption that criminal intent is required); *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-52 (1922) (public-welfare offenses are the exception, not the rule). The public-welfare exception does not apply here because § 875(c) implicates core First Amendment freedoms. *See Smith*, 361 U.S. at 152-53.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard effectively eliminates the mens rea requirement. A speaker may be convicted even if he did not intend his words as a threat, so long as a reasonable person would have foreseen the threatening interpretation. That standard is inconsistent with *Morissette* and with the First Amendment.

**III. The Objective Standard Is Overbroad and Unconstitutional.**

The objective standard sweeps within its reach speech that is protected under the First Amendment. For example, a person who posts rap lyrics or engages in political hyperbole may be convicted even though he had no intent to threaten. *Watts* itself involved a statement that could reasonably be interpreted as a threat, but the Court reversed because the context and the speaker’s apparent intent showed it was not a true threat. 394 U.S. at 708. The objective standard would have allowed conviction in *Watts*.

This Court has consistently required that statutes restricting speech be narrowly drawn. *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931) (statute void for overbreadth). The objective standard is not narrowly tailored; it punishes speech that is not intended to threaten and thus fails to distinguish between protected and unprotected expression.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
