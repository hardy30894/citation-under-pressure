**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Held That Ford’s In-State Contacts Give Rise to Specific Jurisdiction Because the Claims “Arise Out of or Relate to” Those Contacts.**

This Court has long held that specific jurisdiction exists when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). The “relate to” prong is not a causation requirement; it is satisfied when there is “an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy, principally, an activity or an occurrence that takes place in the forum State.” *Id.* at 264 (quoting *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011)).

Ford’s contacts with Montana are extensive and directly related to the claims. Ford designs, markets, and sells vehicles nationwide, including in Montana. It maintains a network of authorized dealerships in the State, advertises extensively to Montana consumers, and sells replacement parts and provides repair services for its vehicles in Montana. These contacts are not random or isolated; they are deliberate efforts to serve the Montana market. As the Montana Supreme Court found, Ford “purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities in Montana” by “advertising, selling, and servicing its vehicles in the state.” *Ford Motor Co. v. Mont. Eighth Judicial Dist. Ct.*, 443 P.3d 407, 414 (Mont. 2019).

The claims in this case—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—arise directly from Ford’s decision to place its vehicles into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they would be driven in Montana. The Ford Explorer that killed Markkaya Gullett was designed and manufactured by Ford, and Ford’s failure to warn Montana consumers about the tire-separation defect is a central allegation. The connection between Ford’s Montana contacts and the claims is not attenuated; it is direct and substantial. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (specific jurisdiction exists when a defendant “delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State”).

**II. Ford’s Argument That the Claims Must Be “Caused By” Its Forum Contacts Misreads This Court’s Precedent.**

Ford contends that specific jurisdiction requires a causal link between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s claims. But this Court has never imposed such a rigid requirement. In *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, the Court explained that specific jurisdiction requires “a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” 582 U.S. at 265. The Court did not say that the connection must be causal. To the contrary, the Court recognized that the “arise out of or relate to” standard is “not a mechanical test” and that “the relationship must arise out of contacts that the defendant itself creates with the forum State.” *Id.* at 262 (quoting *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985)).

Ford’s contacts with Montana are precisely the kind of “contacts that the defendant itself creates.” Ford chose to sell its vehicles in Montana, to advertise there, and to maintain a network of dealerships and service centers. These contacts are not unrelated to the claims; they are the very means by which Ford’s defective vehicles entered the State and caused harm. As this Court has recognized, “when a defendant purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, it has clear notice that it is subject to suit there.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 474 (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)).

**III. The Exercise of Jurisdiction Comports with Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice.**

Even if Ford’s contacts with Montana are sufficient to satisfy the “arise out of or relate to” standard, the exercise of jurisdiction must also be reasonable. *Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987). Here, Montana has a strong interest in adjudicating claims arising from injuries that occur within its borders. *See McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (a State has a “manifest interest in providing effective means of redress for its residents”). Ford is a global corporation with the resources to defend itself in Montana, and the burden of litigating in Montana is minimal given Ford’s extensive presence in the State. *See Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 477 (modern transportation and communication have reduced the burden of defending in a distant forum).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
