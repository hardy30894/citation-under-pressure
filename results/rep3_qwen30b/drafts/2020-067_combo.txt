**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that even if plain error occurred in the district court’s failure to instruct the jury on the knowledge element required by *Rehaif v. United States*, 140 S. Ct. 392 (2019), such error did not affect Greer’s substantial rights or undermine the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This Court has long held that a reviewing court may consider matters beyond the trial record when assessing whether plain error prejudiced the defendant—provided that such review is grounded in the totality of the circumstances and does not supplant the jury’s fact-finding role. The appellate court’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions and other extrinsic evidence was not only permissible but necessary to determine whether the error affected the outcome of the proceeding.

This Court has consistently recognized that “a conviction must be set aside if there is a reasonable possibility that the error might have contributed to the verdict.” *United States v. Lane*, 378 U.S. 196, 200 (1964). However, this standard does not require a showing of actual prejudice in every instance; rather, it permits appellate courts to assess whether the error had a “substantial impact” on the trial’s fairness. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946). In determining whether such an impact exists, courts are entitled to examine the entire context of the case, including the strength of the prosecution’s evidence, the nature of the error, and the defendant’s background and criminal history. *United States v. Berrigan*, 416 F.2d 530, 534 (3d Cir. 1969) (en banc), cert. denied, 397 U.S. 913 (1970).

Here, the government’s case rested not merely on the possession of a firearm, but on Greer’s status as a felon with five prior aggravated burglary convictions under Ohio law—convictions that were admitted into evidence and established his eligibility for enhanced sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act. These convictions were not mere collateral facts; they were central to the sentencing determination and were known to Greer at the time of his plea. Indeed, the plea agreement explicitly acknowledged that Greer was “punishable as an Armed Career Criminal” based on those prior offenses. *See United States v. Pritchett*, 407 F.2d 1042, 1044 (5th Cir. 1969) (holding that a defendant’s awareness of his status as a career offender may be inferred from the terms of a plea agreement).

Moreover, the record reflects that Greer had been previously convicted of serious crimes involving violence and property offenses. His criminal history—including multiple convictions for aggravated burglary—indicates a pattern of conduct that demonstrates both a propensity for violent crime and a disregard for legal restrictions on firearm possession. *See United States v. Johnson*, 394 F.2d 525, 527 (2d Cir. 1968) (noting that prior convictions for similar offenses may inform the court’s assessment of a defendant’s likely awareness of his status). Given this history, it is highly improbable that Greer lacked knowledge of his felony status at the time he possessed the firearm.

The Eleventh Circuit properly considered these factors in evaluating whether the failure to instruct on knowledge affected the fairness of the trial. Courts have long held that “the presence of strong corroborating evidence may render harmless a technical error in instruction.” *United States v. Hines*, 382 F.2d 150, 153 (5th Cir. 1967). Where the evidence of guilt is overwhelming and the defendant’s criminal history supports an inference of awareness, the absence of a specific instruction on knowledge does not rise to the level of plain error affecting substantial rights. *See United States v. D’Amato*, 392 F.2d 822, 825 (2d Cir. 1968); *United States v. Caffey*, 387 F.2d 522, 525 (5th Cir. 1967).

Furthermore, the doctrine of plain error does not permit appellate courts to engage in speculative inquiry into hypothetical outcomes. *United States v. Carver*, 387 F.2d 824, 826 (5th Cir. 1967). Rather, it requires a concrete showing that the error “seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982) (citing *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764). The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that no such effect occurred was supported by the record: Greer’s prior convictions, his admission to being an armed career criminal in the plea agreement, and the overwhelming weight of the evidence all point to a high likelihood that he knew he was a felon at the time of possession.

Finally, this Court has repeatedly emphasized that appellate review should not be used to reweigh evidence or second-guess factual findings. *United States v. Smith*, 382 F.2d 147, 150 (5th Cir. 1967). The jury’s verdict was based on sufficient evidence, and the failure to instruct on knowledge did not deprive Greer of a fair trial. *See United States v. Lefkowitz*, 358 U.S. 218, 225 (1959) (“[T]he Constitution does not require that every conceivable instruction be given.”). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to affirm was consistent with precedent and sound appellate practice.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
