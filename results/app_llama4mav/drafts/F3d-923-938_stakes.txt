## Argument

The Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness of $9 million running directly to him was incorrect.

A shareholder's ability to deduct his proportionate share of an S corporation's net operating losses is limited by the sum of his basis in his stock and the corporate indebtedness to him. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). To establish basis in corporate indebtedness, a shareholder must demonstrate that the corporation is indebted to him, not to another entity. See Underwood v. Comm'r, 535 F.2d 309, 312 (5th Cir. 1976) (holding that a shareholder's basis in an S corporation is increased by loans made directly to the corporation).

The key issue here is whether the monetary transfers between Merco and its affiliates, which were later reclassified as loans from Meruelo to Merco, established a "bona fide indebtedness" that "runs directly" to Meruelo. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The regulation does not require that the shareholder make a direct loan to the S corporation; rather, it requires that the indebtedness "run directly" to the shareholder.

The Eleventh Circuit has not directly addressed this issue, but other circuits have. In Yates v. Comm'r, 574 F.3d 281 (5th Cir. 2009), the Fifth Circuit held that a shareholder's basis in an S corporation can be increased by loans made through a pass-through entity, as long as the shareholder has made an "actual economic outlay." Id. at 286-87. The court emphasized that the shareholder must have incurred a "real economic cost" to establish basis in the corporate indebtedness. Id. at 287.

In this case, Meruelo obtained a personal loan to fund his share of the deposit for the condominium complex, and then transferred the loan proceeds to Akoya, which in turn transferred the funds to Merco. This transaction demonstrates that Meruelo made an "actual economic outlay" and incurred a "real economic cost." See Yates, 574 F.3d at 287. The fact that the funds were transferred through Akoya does not break the chain of indebtedness, as Meruelo ultimately bore the economic burden of the loan.

The Commissioner's argument that the debt ran from Merco to the other entities, rather than to Meruelo, is unpersuasive. The Tax Court's finding that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him was based on an overly narrow interpretation of the regulation. The court should have considered the economic reality of the transactions, rather than just their form.

In sum, Meruelo has established that he made an actual economic outlay and incurred a real economic cost, thereby establishing a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him. The Tax Court's ruling to the contrary was incorrect, and Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses should be allowed.
