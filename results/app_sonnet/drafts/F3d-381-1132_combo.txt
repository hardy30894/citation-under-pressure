ARGUMENT

I. USE OF AN EASEMENT BEYOND ITS GRANTED PURPOSE MAY EFFECT A PHYSICAL TAKING

The Court of Federal Claims held that the "scope of the easement is not relevant" to a physical-takings analysis because Tuthill "has not identified a property interest that could have been taken." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. That holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's and the Supreme Court's long-standing recognition that an owner's property interest in land burdened by an easement consists precisely of everything not conveyed by the easement's terms, and that governmental appropriation of anything beyond those terms is a taking of the servient owner's remaining interest.

This principle traces to Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co., 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 166 (1872), where the Supreme Court held that a physical invasion of land effecting a substantial impairment of the owner's use is a taking within the meaning of the Constitution even absent a formal exercise of eminent domain. The Court has since reaffirmed that a taking may occur through direct governmental interference with the use and enjoyment of land, without any transfer of title or a wholesale ouster of the owner. United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261 (1946) (holding that low-altitude military overflights effected a taking of an easement in the airspace above private land because the government had, "in a practical sense," appropriated the owner's beneficial use). Likewise, in Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 327 (1922), the Court held that repeated governmental incursions upon land, even where the government held a limited right of use, could ripen into a compensable taking once the invasions exceeded that right. And in United States v. General Motors Corp., 323 U.S. 373 (1945), the Court explained that a taking encompasses any exercise of dominion over property that goes beyond the rights the government actually possesses, whether by physical occupation or by depriving the owner of the incidents of ownership.

These authorities establish that the "scope of the easement" is not, as the Court of Federal Claims believed, irrelevant to the takings inquiry — it is the taking inquiry. Tuthill's remaining property interest is defined by whatever the 1953 easement did not convey. If the BPA's fiber-optic installation and its lease of that installation to third parties exceed the easement's terms, the excess constitutes a new physical occupation of Tuthill's land for which the 1953 grant provided no compensation.

II. THE BPA'S INSTALLATION AND LEASING OF EXCESS FIBER CAPACITY EXCEEDS THE SCOPE OF THE 1953 EASEMENT

The 1953 instrument conveyed to the BPA a right to erect "appurtenant signal lines ... necessary in connection" with its power-transmission lines. An easement granted for a specific purpose may not be enlarged to serve purposes foreign to that grant without imposing an additional burden on the servient estate — a burden that must be separately compensated.

This rule is settled by Western Union Telegraph Co. v. Pennsylvania R.R. Co., 195 U.S. 540 (1904), in which the Supreme Court held that use of a railroad right of way to carry telegraph service having no necessary connection to railroad operations imposed a new burden on the underlying fee, distinct from the burden contemplated by the original grant. The same principle governs easements generally: a right of way limited by its terms to a particular use cannot be converted, without compensation, into a vehicle for an unrelated commercial enterprise. See Missouri Pacific Ry. Co. v. Nebraska, 217 U.S. 196, 207 (1910) (holding that compelling a railroad to devote its right of way to a use beyond the purpose for which the easement was acquired works a taking of an additional property interest). And in United States v. Welch, 217 U.S. 333, 339 (1910), the Court confirmed that an easement's extent "must be measured by the terms of the grant," not by whatever use the grantee later finds convenient.

Here, it is undisputed that BPA installed cable capacity vastly exceeding its own operational needs — capacity that BPA itself concedes it will not require until, at the earliest, 2018 or 2025 — and that BPA leases the unused fibers to a telecommunications company wholly unconnected to power transmission. That arrangement is not a "signal line ... necessary in connection" with the power easement; it is a commercial telecommunications facility occupying Tuthill's land under color of an easement that never authorized it. Under Western Union and Missouri Pacific, that additional, unauthorized use burdens the servient estate independently of the burden the 1953 grantor accepted, and therefore constitutes a further taking of Tuthill's property.

III. THE GOVERNMENT'S CONTINUING OCCUPATION AND COMMERCIAL EXPLOITATION OF EXCESS CAPACITY ENTITLES TUTHILL TO JUST COMPENSATION

Where the government appropriates a property interest for public use — including use through a private lessee benefiting from the government's own installation — just compensation is due regardless of whether the appropriation was direct or accomplished through a third party's occupancy. Jacobs v. United States, 290 U.S. 13, 16 (1933) (just compensation is required whenever private property is taken for public use, and the measure is the owner's loss). The taking need not be a single, discrete event; it may be a continuing physical occupation, assessed as such once the character of the intrusion is fixed. United States v. Dickinson, 331 U.S. 745, 749 (1947). And where repeated or ongoing physical invasions of land exceed any right the government previously held, the owner's cause of action for a taking accrues from the excess use itself. Peabody v. United States, 231 U.S. 530, 538 (1913).

Applying these principles, Tuthill's land is presently occupied by cable capacity — and a commercial telecommunications arrangement — that the 1953 easement never authorized. That occupation is continuing, physical, and exclusive of Tuthill's own use of the burdened strip. Under Pumpelly, Causby, General Motors, Western Union, Missouri Pacific, Welch, Jacobs, Dickinson, and Peabody, that excess occupation is a physical taking of Tuthill's remaining property interest, compensable under the Fifth Amendment. The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding otherwise, and its judgment should be reversed.
