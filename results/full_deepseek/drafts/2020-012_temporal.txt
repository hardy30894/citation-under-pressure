**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s Refusal to Reopen a Prior Benefits Claim Is a “Final Decision” Subject to Judicial Review Under the Railroad Retirement Act.**

The Railroad Retirement Act provides that a party aggrieved by a “final decision” of the Board may obtain review in the courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim is a “final decision” because it conclusively determines the rights of the parties and leaves nothing for further administrative action. *See Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967) (defining finality as an action that “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and is not “merely tentative or interlocutory”).

The Board’s reopening regulations, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, establish a structured process that culminates in a definitive agency determination. When the Board denies a reopening request, that denial is the agency’s last word on the matter; no further administrative appeal is available. *Cf. Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949) (finality turns on whether the decision “finally dispose[s] of the claimed rights”). The Board’s decision here—that Salinas’s 2006 application was beyond the four-year reopening period—was a final, conclusive ruling that barred any further consideration of his prior claim.

**II. The Text and Structure of the Railroad Retirement Act Confirm That Reopening Decisions Are Reviewable.**

Section 355(f) authorizes review of “any final decision of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The statute does not carve out reopening decisions from that broad grant of jurisdiction. When Congress intended to limit review of agency actions, it did so expressly. *See, e.g., Switchmen’s Union of North America v. National Mediation Board*, 320 U.S. 297, 305 (1943) (holding that judicial review is available unless Congress “plainly and unmistakably” precludes it). No such preclusion appears in the Railroad Retirement Act.

The Board’s own regulations treat reopening decisions as final. Under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, a reopening decision is binding unless challenged within a specified period. *Cf. United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 36 (1952) (agency regulations have the force of law). By creating a definitive administrative process for reopening, the Board has itself recognized that these decisions are final and consequential.

**III. The Majority Rule That Reopening Decisions Are Unreviewable Conflicts with Fundamental Principles of Administrative Law.**

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), is misplaced. *Sanders* addressed the scope of judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act, not the Railroad Retirement Act. The APA’s limitation on review of “agency action committed to agency discretion by law,” 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2), does not apply to the Railroad Retirement Act, which provides its own independent review provision. *See Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167, 171 (1936) (statutory review provisions are to be construed liberally to effectuate Congress’s remedial purposes).

Moreover, the rule that reopening decisions are unreviewable creates an arbitrary gap in judicial oversight. An applicant who is denied benefits on the merits may obtain review, but an applicant whose reopening request is denied—even if the denial is arbitrary or capricious—is left without any remedy. *Cf. Harmon v. Brucker*, 355 U.S. 579, 581 (1958) (per curiam) (judicial review is available to correct agency action that is “arbitrary, capricious, or an abuse of discretion”). Such a result is inconsistent with the “strong presumption” in favor of judicial review of administrative action. *See Rusk v. Cort*, 369 U.S. 367, 379 (1962).

**IV. The Second Circuit’s Reasoning in *Szostak* Correctly Recognizes That Reopening Decisions Are Reviewable.**

In *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), the Second Circuit held that a Board decision denying a reopening request is reviewable because it is a “final decision” under the Act. *Id.* at 254-55. The court reasoned that the Board’s refusal to reopen “finally determined” the applicant’s rights and that the statute’s broad language encompassed such decisions. *Id.* at 255. This approach respects the statutory text and ensures that the Board does not insulate its errors from judicial scrutiny by labeling them as “reopening” decisions.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule, adopted in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is not binding on this Court and should be rejected. *Roberts* relied on a flawed analogy to Social Security reopening decisions, which are governed by a different statutory scheme. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 405(g) (limiting review of Social Security reopening decisions). The Railroad Retirement Act contains no such limitation.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Board’s decision denying Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 benefits claim is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The petition for review should be reinstated and the case remanded for consideration on the merits.
