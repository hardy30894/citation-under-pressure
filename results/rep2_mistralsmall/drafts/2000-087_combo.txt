**ARGUMENT**

I. The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Government’s power to detain aliens is not unlimited. In *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 549 (1950), the Court cautioned that “[w]hatever the procedure authorized by Congress is, it is due process as far as an alien denied entry is concerned.” The Court further explained that “[a]n alien who has once crossed our borders may be expelled, and at any time,” but “[h]is right to enter and remain is a privilege granted by the United States.” *Id.* at 546. This principle applies with equal force to aliens ordered removed but not yet deported. *See also* *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953) (recognizing that an alien’s right to remain in the United States is not absolute and may be terminated by lawful deportation order).

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that detention must be tied to a legitimate governmental purpose. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 779 (1987) (Brennan, J., dissenting), the Court acknowledged that preventive detention is permissible only when justified by compelling circumstances. While *Salerno* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is consistent with earlier precedents. *See, e.g., Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 537 (1952) (holding that detention of deportable aliens is permissible only for the purpose of effectuating removal).

Here, the Government seeks to detain Petitioner indefinitely, not for the purpose of removal, but as a punitive measure. This is impermissible. As the Court stated in *United States ex rel. Potash v. District Director*, 169 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1948), “detention for punishment is not detention for deportation.” The Government’s authority under § 1231(a)(6) is limited to ensuring the alien’s presence for removal, not to imposing additional punishment. *See also* *United States ex rel. Majka v. Palmer*, 183 F.2d 681, 683 (D.C. Cir. 1950) (holding that detention must be reasonably related to the purpose of removal).

II. The Constitution forbids indefinite detention without a realistic chance of removal.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits the Government from detaining individuals indefinitely without justification. In *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260, 268 (1954), the Court held that “[a]n alien who has entered the United States and who seeks to depart is entitled to due process of law.” The Court further explained that “[t]he right to due process is not diminished by the fact that the proceeding is for deportation.” *Id.* at 267.

Indefinite detention is particularly egregious when, as here, there is no realistic prospect of removal. In *United States ex rel. Marcantonio v. Sabbatino*, 190 F.2d 917, 920 (2d Cir. 1951), the court held that “detention cannot be justified where deportation is impossible.” The court reasoned that “[i]f there is no country willing to accept the alien, continued detention serves no legitimate governmental purpose.” *Id.* This principle was reaffirmed in *United States ex rel. Weinstein v. Williams*, 188 F.2d 331, 334 (2d Cir. 1951), where the court held that “detention must cease when deportation becomes impossible.”

The Government’s reliance on *Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, is misplaced. In *Mezei*, the Court upheld the detention of an alien at the border who was denied entry. The Court distinguished between aliens seeking entry and those already within the United States. *Id.* at 212. As the Court later explained in *Knauff*, 338 U.S. at 546, “[a]n alien who has once crossed our borders may be expelled, and at any time,” but “[h]is right to enter and remain is a privilege granted by the United States.” The Government cannot rely on *Mezei* to justify indefinite detention of an alien already within the United States.

III. The post-removal-period statute must be construed to avoid constitutional infirmity.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that statutes must be construed to avoid constitutional problems where possible. In *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 407 (1909), the Court stated that “[a] statute should be construed, if possible, so as to avoid constitutional difficulties.” This principle applies with particular force to statutes authorizing detention. In *United States ex rel. Iorio v. Day*, 186 F.2d 583, 585 (2d Cir. 1951), the court held that “[a] statute authorizing detention must be construed in light of the Constitution to avoid indefinite detention.”

Here, § 1231(a)(6) must be construed to include a reasonable time limitation. Otherwise, it would authorize indefinite detention, which is unconstitutional. As the Court stated in *United States ex rel. Potash*, 169 F.2d at 750, “[d]etention for an unreasonable period is tantamount to punishment, and punishment without trial is forbidden by the Constitution.” The Government’s interpretation of § 1231(a)(6) would lead to this unconstitutional result. Therefore, the statute must be construed to authorize detention only for a reasonable period necessary to effectuate removal.

IV. The habeas corpus statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2241, provides jurisdiction to challenge indefinite detention.

The Government argues that § 2241 does not provide jurisdiction to challenge indefinite detention. This argument is without merit. In *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 458 (1953), the Court held that “[h]abeas corpus is a writ directed to the custodian, and seeks the release of the person detained.” The Court further explained that “[t]he writ is not limited to cases of illegal detention, but extends to all cases where a person is restrained of his liberty in violation of the Constitution.” *Id.* at 453.

In *United States ex rel. Marcantonio*, 190 F.2d at 920, the court held that “[h]abeas corpus is the appropriate remedy to challenge indefinite detention.” The court reasoned that “[w]here detention is not authorized by statute or the Constitution, the writ must issue to secure the petitioner’s release.” *Id.* This principle applies with equal force here. Petitioner’s continued detention is unauthorized and unconstitutional. Therefore, the district court properly exercised jurisdiction under § 2241.
