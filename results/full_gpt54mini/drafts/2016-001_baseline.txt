Samsung should prevail because the Patent Act’s design-patent damages provision does not authorize a design patentee to recover an infringer’s total product profits when the claimed design covers only a component. The governing statute awards “the total profit” from the “article of manufacture to which the design or colorable imitation has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. That text limits recovery to the profits attributable to the relevant “article of manufacture,” not to the entire commercial product whenever the patented design appears somewhere on it. See Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444-45 (1885) (requiring apportionment because damages must reflect the value of the patented design, not the whole article’s value), superseded by statute as recognized in Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10, 17-18 (1886).

This Court has long required apportionment when a patent covers only part of a product. In Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), the Court held that a patentee must “separate or apportion” the profits attributable to the patented feature from those attributable to unpatented features, unless the patentee proves that the entire value of the product is “properly and legally attributable” to the patented feature. The same principle governs here. Section 289 preserves a special remedy for design patents, but it does not abolish the settled distinction between a patented component and an entire multi-component product. See Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480, 491-92 (1853) (rejecting recovery measured by the entire machine where the patent covered only an improvement); Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 617-18 (1912) (the patentee must apportion unless the patented feature is the basis for the entire profit).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach conflates infringement with remedy. A design patent may be infringed by the appearance of a component embedded in a larger product, but the remedy still must be tied to the “article of manufacture to which the design” was applied. 35 U.S.C. § 289. That phrase cannot mean every finished consumer product containing the component. Statutory terms are read in context and to avoid surplusage. See Duncan v. Walker, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001). If “article of manufacture” always meant the entire end product, Congress’s separate reference to the “article of manufacture” would do no work in cases involving component designs.

The history of the design-patent damages statute confirms that Congress meant to displace only the rule limiting damages to the ordinary patent accounting methods rejected in Dobson, not to grant windfalls untethered to the patented article. See Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc., 137 S. Ct. 429, 432-34 (2016) (explaining that § 289 permits recovery of total profit from the relevant article of manufacture and that the statute’s text does not require the article to be the end product in every case). The Court in Samsung recognized the central point Samsung presses here: the “article of manufacture” may be less than the finished product, and the statute therefore does not automatically award all profits from the consumer device. Id. at 434-35.

That interpretation also accords with basic remedial principles. Equity does not allow a plaintiff to recover gains beyond those attributable to the wrong. See Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390, 406-07 (1940) (requiring allocation of profits between infringing and noninfringing elements). Section 289 is not a blank check to recover the profits from unrelated, unpatented features, software, and branding merely because one ornamental component was copied. See City of Elizabeth v. American Nicholson Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126, 137-38 (1877) (limiting recovery to the invention actually appropriated).

Because the design patents here covered only portions of Samsung’s phones, damages must be limited to the profits attributable to the relevant component articles of manufacture. Any broader rule converts § 289 from a targeted design-patent remedy into a penalty measured by the value of an entire complex product, contrary to the statute, this Court’s apportionment precedents, and basic fairness. Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121; Westinghouse, 225 U.S. at 617-18; Samsung, 137 S. Ct. at 434-35. The judgment should be reversed.
