# ARGUMENT

The Fourteenth Amendment permits a State to exercise specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant whenever there is "an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy, principally, activity or an occurrence that takes place in the forum State." *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011). Ford's own conduct in Montana—marketing, selling, and servicing thousands of Explorers, including the model that killed Ms. Gullett—supplies exactly that affiliation. Due process does not additionally require that the specific unit involved in the accident be first sold in the forum.

## I. Specific Jurisdiction Requires a Relationship Between the Forum, the Defendant's Contacts, and the Litigation—Not Strict Causation.

Since *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), the touchstone of personal jurisdiction has been whether maintaining suit offends "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. This Court has long recognized that specific jurisdiction may rest on contacts that "relate to," and need not strictly "arise out of," the litigation. In *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984), the Court described specific jurisdiction as applying "when a controversy is related to or 'arises out of' a defendant's contacts with the forum"—a disjunctive formulation the Court has repeated ever since. *See Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017) (specific jurisdiction exists when "the suit" "arise[s] out of or relate[s] to the defendant's contacts with the forum" (quoting *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014))).

The "relate to" prong exists precisely because a rigid causation requirement would sever jurisdiction from the realities of modern commerce, in which a manufacturer's product may travel through multiple States before reaching the site of injury. Ford's contacts with Montana—the deliberate cultivation of a market for Explorers through dealership networks, advertising, and parts and repair services—are not "random, fortuitous, or attenuated" contacts of the kind this Court condemned in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). They are the product of Ford's own purposeful choice to "avail[] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within" Montana, "thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." *Id.* at 475 (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)).

## II. Ford's Continuous and Deliberate Exploitation of the Montana Market Relates Directly to Gullett's Claims.

This case is a paradigm of the "stream of commerce" principle articulated in *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286 (1980). There, the Court explained that when the sale of a product "arises from the efforts of the manufacturer or distributor to serve directly or indirectly, the market for its product in other States, it is not unreasonable to subject it to suit in one of those States if its allegedly defective merchandise has there been the source of injury to its owner or to others." *Id.* at 297. Ford's conduct falls squarely within that principle: Ford does not passively await Montana buyers, as the New York retailer in *World-Wide Volkswagen* did; Ford has established Montana dealerships, distributes Explorers there, advertises there, and services and supplies parts for Explorers there. Ford should therefore "reasonably anticipate being haled into court" in Montana over Explorer-related claims. *Id.*

This Court's decision in *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770 (1984), confirms that a defendant who "continuously and deliberately exploit[s]" a forum market subjects itself to jurisdiction there for controversies connected to that market activity, even without a one-to-one causal link between the specific transaction and the forum. *Id.* at 781. Ford's decades-long, deliberate cultivation of Montana as a marketplace for Explorers is precisely the kind of exploitation that generates a reciprocal obligation to answer for the risks that activity creates—including the risk that a defective Explorer, wherever first sold, will end up on Montana roads.

## III. Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice Confirm the Reasonableness of Jurisdiction Here.

Even where minimum contacts exist, this Court considers "the burden on the defendant, the interests of the forum State, and the plaintiff's interest in obtaining convenient and effective relief." *Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987). All three factors favor jurisdiction. Ford is a multinational corporation that litigates nationwide; defending a single suit in Montana imposes no unconstitutional burden. Montana has a powerful interest in providing a forum for a resident killed on its own highways by an allegedly defective product marketed within its borders. And Gullett's estate has an obvious interest in litigating where the accident occurred, where evidence and witnesses are located, and where Gullett lived and died.

## IV. Bristol-Myers Squibb Is Not to the Contrary.

In *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, the Court held that California lacked jurisdiction over claims by nonresident plaintiffs who neither purchased, ingested, nor were injured by the drug in California. 137 S. Ct. at 1781–82. The Court emphasized that "[w]hat is needed... is a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue." *Id.* at 1781. Here, that connection exists: Gullett was a Montana resident, purchased her vehicle's tire service and repairs in Montana, drove the vehicle on Montana roads, and suffered her injury and death in Montana. Unlike the nonresident plaintiffs in *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, the plaintiff here is not "the only link between the defendant and the forum." *Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 289 (2014). Ford's own extensive activities in Montana supply that link.

The Montana Supreme Court's judgment should be affirmed.
