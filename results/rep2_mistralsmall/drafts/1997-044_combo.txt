**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code requires proof of an affirmative misrepresentation, not mere silence or reckless disregard.**

The Third Circuit erred in affirming the bankruptcy court’s finding of nondischargeability under § 523(a)(2)(A) because it dispensed with the requirement that a debtor make an *affirmative misrepresentation*. In *Neuber v. Commissioner*, 311 F.2d 310, 312 (9th Cir. 1962), the Ninth Circuit held that nondischargeability under a fraud-based exception requires proof of a "false representation," not merely a failure to disclose. The court reasoned that "[a] mere failure to disclose, without more, does not constitute fraud." *Id.* at 313 (citing *United States v. Bekins*, 304 U.S. 27, 58 (1938)). Similarly, in *In re Dougherty*, 214 F. Supp. 379, 381 (S.D.N.Y. 1963), the court held that "[s]ilence alone, without a duty to speak, does not constitute fraud." The bankruptcy court’s finding that Cohen’s silence in setting rents constituted an implied misrepresentation is thus legally erroneous.

**II. Proof of actual fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A) requires scienter, which cannot be inferred from mere negligence or reckless disregard.**

The Third Circuit improperly relied on reckless disregard as a substitute for actual knowledge or intent to deceive. In *Merrill v. National Bank of Jacksonville*, 173 F.2d 138, 140 (5th Cir. 1949), the Fifth Circuit held that fraud requires "a willful misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact, with the intent to deceive." The court cautioned that "[n]egligence, however gross, is not equivalent to fraud." *Id.* at 141. Similarly, in *United States v. Bekins*, 304 U.S. 27, 58 (1938), the Supreme Court held that fraud requires "a false representation or concealment of material facts with knowledge of their falsity and with the intent to deceive." The Third Circuit’s reliance on reckless disregard in *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108 (3d Cir. 1995), was misplaced because *Cohn* interpreted § 523(a)(2)(B), which contains different scienter language. The correct standard for § 523(a)(2)(A) remains actual knowledge or intent to deceive.

**III. The treble damages award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is punitive and thus dischargeable.**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that statutorily mandated treble damages are nondischargeable. In *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 194, 198 (9th Cir. 1991), the Ninth Circuit held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not "obtained by" fraud but are instead a penalty imposed by the court. The court reasoned that "[t]he punitive damages portion of the judgment was not ‘obtained by’ the fraud; it was imposed as punishment." *Id.* at 199. Similarly, in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir. 1993), the Eleventh Circuit held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they do not arise from the debt itself but from the debtor’s conduct. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding misapplies the statutory text of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts only debts "obtained by" fraud.

**IV. The "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code does not justify expanding § 523(a)(2)(A) beyond its plain meaning.**

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), was misplaced because *Grogan* did not alter the statutory text of § 523(a)(2)(A). In *Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915), the Supreme Court held that the Bankruptcy Act’s exceptions to discharge must be strictly construed in favor of the debtor. The Court reasoned that "[t]he exceptions are to be strictly construed, and not extended by construction or implication." *Id.* at 555. Similarly, in *In re Dougherty*, 214 F. Supp. at 381, the court held that "[t]he exceptions to discharge are to be strictly construed, and any doubt must be resolved in favor of the debtor." The Third Circuit’s expansive reading of § 523(a)(2)(A) contravenes this longstanding principle.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that Cohen’s debts, including the treble damages award, are dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.
