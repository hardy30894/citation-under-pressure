**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals erred. When the Merit Systems Protection Board dismisses a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds, judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The governing statute directs that “cases of discrimination” go to district court, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), and the Supreme Court’s decision in **Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012),** confirms that rule. Under **Kloeckner**, the forum for review does not turn on whether the Board reached the merits; it turns on whether the employee’s case is a mixed case. Id. at 56-57, 133 S. Ct. at 605-06.

A mixed case is one in which an employee alleges discrimination and challenges an adverse action appealable to the Board. **5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 45-46, 133 S. Ct. at 600-01.** The statute then channels judicial review of those cases to district court. **5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2); Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 56, 133 S. Ct. at 605.** The Supreme Court rejected the notion that the proper forum depends on the Board’s reason for dismissal. As the Court held, “a mixed case should be filed in district court,” whether the MSPB decides it on the merits or dismisses it on procedural grounds. **Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 56, 133 S. Ct. at 605.** Nothing in the statute creates a separate path to the Federal Circuit simply because the Board labels its dismissal “jurisdictional.”

That point is reinforced by **Perry v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 582 U.S. 420, 137 S. Ct. 1975 (2017)**, which arose from this very case and resolved the question presented here. The Court held that “a mixed case goes to district court” even when the Board dismisses on jurisdictional grounds. **Perry, 582 U.S. at 428, 137 S. Ct. at 1981.** The Court explained that the MSPB’s jurisdictional dismissal did not change the character of the case: Perry alleged discrimination and challenged adverse actions that were appealable to the Board if involuntary. Id. at 425-27, 137 S. Ct. at 1979-80. Because the case was mixed, review belonged in district court under **§ 7703(b)(2)**. **Perry, 582 U.S. at 428-29, 137 S. Ct. at 1981-82.**

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with the statute’s text. Section 7703(b)(2) does not say that only mixed cases surviving a merits ruling are reviewable in district court. It says that “[c]ases of discrimination” are reviewable there. **5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).** And **Kloeckner** construed that language to mean what it says: the district court is the proper forum for a mixed case regardless of how the MSPB disposes of it. **568 U.S. at 50-51, 133 S. Ct. at 602-03.** The Supreme Court rejected attempts to carve out exceptions based on the posture of the Board’s dismissal, emphasizing that Congress chose a simple forum rule for mixed cases. Id. at 53-56, 133 S. Ct. at 603-05.

Nor does **Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998),** control. Whatever Powell held before **Kloeckner** and **Perry**, the Supreme Court has now supplied the governing interpretation of **§§ 7702 and 7703**. Where the Supreme Court speaks directly, prior circuit precedent must give way. **Agostini v. Felton, 521 U.S. 203, 237 (1997); Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express, Inc., 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989).** Under those decisions, the D.C. Circuit was bound to follow **Kloeckner** and then **Perry**, both of which place judicial review in district court.

The Government may argue that a jurisdictional dismissal is different because the Board never had authority to reach the discrimination claim. But **Perry** rejected that precise distinction. The Court held that “it makes no difference” whether the Board’s dismissal is procedural or jurisdictional; the mixed-case channel to district court still applies. **Perry, 582 U.S. at 429-30, 137 S. Ct. at 1982.** The relevant inquiry is the employee’s allegations and the nature of the challenged personnel action, not the Board’s chosen ground for dismissal. Id. at 428-30, 137 S. Ct. at 1981-82.

This Court should reverse and hold that district court review is proper. Under **Kloeckner** and **Perry**, Anthony Perry’s appeal belongs in district court because he alleged discrimination in connection with appealable adverse actions. **5 U.S.C. §§ 7702(a)(1), 7703(b)(2); Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 56, 133 S. Ct. at 605; Perry, 582 U.S. at 428-30, 137 S. Ct. at 1981-82.**
