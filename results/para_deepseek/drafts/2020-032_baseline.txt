**ARGUMENT**

The case is moot. Georgia Gwinnett College revised its challenged policies before judgment, eliminating any ongoing violation. Petitioners’ sole remaining claim for nominal damages cannot revive an otherwise moot controversy because nominal damages do not redress a continuing injury and thus fail to satisfy Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement.

**I. The Policy Revision Rendered the Case Moot**

A case becomes moot “when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013) (quoting *Murphy v. Hunt*, 455 U.S. 478, 481 (1982)). Here, GGC’s revised policies permit expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged code-of-conduct provision was removed. Petitioners do not contest that their claims for injunctive and declaratory relief are moot. *See* Eleventh Circuit opinion at 7 n.1. No ongoing violation exists, and there is no reasonable expectation that the old policies will be reinstated. *See Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000).

**II. Nominal Damages Alone Cannot Save a Moot Case**

A plaintiff must maintain a “personal stake” in the outcome throughout the litigation. *Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 477-78 (1990). When the challenged policy is repealed, the plaintiff’s interest in prospective relief evaporates. A claim for nominal damages—which seeks only a symbolic vindication of a past constitutional violation—does not create a continuing controversy. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 107-08 (1998) (holding that a request for “declaratory relief, civil penalties, and attorney’s fees” did not save a moot case because “the only remedy [plaintiff] seeks is for past violations”); *see also City of Erie v. Pap’s A.M.*, 529 U.S. 277, 288 (2000) (plurality opinion) (mootness when challenged ordinance was repealed, even though plaintiff sought nominal damages).

The Court has never held that nominal damages alone satisfy Article III. In *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), the Court recognized that nominal damages are available for procedural due process violations, but that case involved a live controversy at the time of judgment. *Carey* did not address mootness. Here, the controversy died when the policies changed. A plaintiff cannot “revive a dispute that has become moot” by adding a claim for nominal damages. *Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1263 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied that principle.

Petitioners rely on cases from other circuits holding that nominal damages can prevent mootness. *E.g., Van Wie v. Pataki*, 267 F.3d 109, 114 (2d Cir. 2001). Those decisions are inconsistent with this Court’s precedent. A claim for nominal damages does not “seek to remedy any ongoing injury” and therefore cannot “satisfy the case-or-controversy requirement.” *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1263. The proper inquiry is whether the plaintiff has a “legally cognizable interest in the outcome” of the litigation. *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91. Once the policy is gone, the only interest is in a symbolic award for a past wrong—which is insufficient to maintain federal jurisdiction. *See Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 107.

**III. Petitioners Did Not Plead Compensatory Damages**

The district court correctly found that the First Amended Complaint sought only nominal damages. The prayer for relief requested “nominal damages” and “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence,” but the complaint alleged no actual injury—no monetary loss, physical harm, or emotional distress. *See* Eleventh Circuit opinion at 9-10. Under *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000), compensatory damages under § 1983 require proof of actual injury. Petitioners’ failure to plead any such injury confirms that they sought only nominal damages. Their belated attempt to characterize the request as compensatory is an impermissible “after-the-fact” recharacterization. *Id.* at 10.

**IV. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend**

Petitioners sought leave to amend only in a response to a motion to dismiss, which is procedurally improper. *See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). They did not file a proper motion or specify the substance of the proposed amendment. The district court acted within its discretion. Moreover, even if they had properly sought to add compensatory damages, the case would still be moot because the policy change eliminated any ongoing violation, and compensatory damages for past conduct do not create a live controversy. *See Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 107.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
