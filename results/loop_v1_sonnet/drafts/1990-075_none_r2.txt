# ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act's temporary-scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), reflects exactly the kind of delegation this Court has approved for decades: Congress articulated the goal, specified the criteria, cabined the duration, and preserved a role for expert consultation, leaving the Attorney General only the task of applying settled standards to particular substances. And because Congress separately authorized the Attorney General to delegate his statutory functions to Department of Justice personnel, his assignment of that authority to the DEA was lawful as well. The judgment below should be affirmed.

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but that provision has never been understood to prevent Congress from enlisting executive assistance to administer a complex regulatory scheme. The controlling test, settled more than sixty years ago, asks only whether Congress has laid "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) satisfies that standard.

**A. Congress supplied concrete, judicially administrable standards.** Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). This is precisely the kind of guiding criterion whose *absence* doomed the statute in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), where the Court found that Congress had not established "any criterion to govern the President's course." The same defect proved fatal in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935), where Congress left the President free to approve virtually any industry-drafted code without meaningful confining principles. Petitioners' reliance on those decisions is misplaced: both turned on the *total absence* of standards, not on the mere fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action. Section 811(h) supplies exactly the standards those statutes lacked.

**B. This Court has consistently sustained comparably broad delegations in fast-moving regulatory fields.** In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix "fair and equitable" wartime prices, recognizing that the "rapidity of changes" in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same logic governs here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new "designer drugs" faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond — which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment rulemaking. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), the Court approved the open-ended "public interest, convenience, or necessity" standard for broadcast licensing, and in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), it sustained the similarly general "fair and equitable" standard governing utility reorganizations. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either of the standards this Court has already approved.

**C. The absence of exhaustive technical detail is not fatal where the regulated field possesses established standards.** In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a bank-conservatorship statute with scant express criteria because the regulated field possessed "well-known and generally acceptable standards" on which the delegatee could draw — just as Congress here relied on established pharmacological and law-enforcement criteria codified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture "excessive profits," an inherently flexible standard, because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to supply details Congress itself could not practically anticipate. The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here: the specialized, rapidly evolving nature of clandestine drug chemistry is not a subject Congress could regulate substance-by-substance through ordinary legislation.

**D. Judicial review of the delegation's constitutionality remains available.** The concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) — that meaningful review must remain open when Congress delegates authority — is fully satisfied here. Section 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, but it does not foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself, consistent with the principle underlying *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), that the non-delegation doctrine safeguards "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution." That structural safeguard remains fully intact in this prosecution, as the Third Circuit correctly recognized in reaching the merits of petitioners' constitutional claim.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a more straightforward reason: Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) empowers the Attorney General to "delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization permitting the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to the agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. *Cf. United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (upholding delegated executive authority to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted in § 871(a), and the subdelegation was therefore proper.

## CONCLUSION

Because Section 811(h) supplies an intelligible principle confining the Attorney General's temporary-scheduling authority, and because Congress separately authorized subdelegation of that authority to the DEA, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
