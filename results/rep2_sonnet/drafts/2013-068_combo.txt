# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. The Buffer Zone Is a Valid, Content-Neutral Regulation of the Use of Public Streets and Sidewalks.

The First Amendment has never been understood to confer an absolute right to speak at any place, at any time, in any manner the speaker chooses. Even in the "quintessential" public forum of the streets, the government retains substantial authority to regulate access in the interest of public order. As this Court explained in *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), streets "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" for expressive purposes, but that privilege "is not absolute, but relative, and must be exercised in subordination to the general comfort and convenience, and in consonance with peace and good order." 307 U.S. at 515–16. Massachusetts's thirty-five-foot buffer zone does nothing more than subordinate the location of speech to those enduring public concerns.

This Court has repeatedly sustained laws regulating the time, place, and manner of expressive activity on public ways without regard to the content of the message conveyed. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), the Court upheld a licensing requirement for parades on public streets, observing that "[c]ivil liberties, as guaranteed by the Constitution, imply the existence of an organized society maintaining public order, without which liberty itself would be lost in the excesses of unrestrained abuses." 312 U.S. at 574. So too here: Massachusetts has not silenced petitioners' message; it has organized the space around clinic entrances so that speech and public safety can coexist.

The same principle animated *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), where the Court recognized that a State may enact "general and non-discriminatory" regulations governing the manner of solicitation on its streets, even where religious expression is implicated, so long as the regulation does not vest unbridled discretion in a licensing official. The Act challenged here contains no such discretion; it draws an objective, geographically fixed line applicable to every speaker regardless of viewpoint. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), likewise sustained a licensing ordinance governing use of a public park for religious meetings, confirming that reasonable, evenly applied restrictions on the location of speech survive constitutional scrutiny.

## II. The Commonwealth's Interests in Unobstructed Access to Healthcare Facilities and in Public Safety Justify the Buffer Zone.

The Act serves interests of the highest order — patient safety, unimpeded ingress and egress, and the prevention of congestion and confrontation at clinic entrances. This Court has consistently held that such interests justify reasonable restrictions on the location of speech. In *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315 (1951), the Court affirmed that although "the police cannot be used as an instrument for the suppression of unpopular views," the State may act "when . . . the speaker passes the bounds of argument or persuasion" and threatens a breach of the peace. 340 U.S. at 320–21. Massachusetts need not wait for confrontations between escorts, patients, and counselors to escalate into disorder before acting; it may, as in *Feiner*, regulate prophylactically to preserve order at a known point of friction.

Similarly, in *Breard v. City of Alexandria*, 341 U.S. 622 (1951), the Court upheld an ordinance restricting door-to-door solicitation, balancing the solicitor's expressive interest against the resident's countervailing interest in peace and privacy. Patients approaching a reproductive health facility possess an analogous interest in safe, unobstructed passage, and the Commonwealth may protect it. And in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), the Court confirmed that "the State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." 385 U.S. at 47. A public sidewalk abutting a medical facility may likewise be regulated to preserve its function as a route of access for patients seeking care.

Finally, in *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536 (1965), the Court upheld a statute restricting picketing "near" a courthouse, recognizing that conduct intertwined with expression remains subject to reasonable regulation where public order interests are implicated. The buffer zone here — fixed, viewpoint-neutral, and confined to a narrow strip of pavement — is a still more modest measure than the restriction sustained in *Cox*.

## III. Ample Alternative Channels of Communication Remain Open.

The Act leaves petitioners free to speak, to pray, to display signs, and to distribute literature from a location just beyond the buffer's edge. As *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), teaches, the First Amendment tolerates content-neutral restrictions on the manner of communication where, as here, the speaker retains meaningful means of "reach[ing] the minds of willing listeners." The record confirms that petitioners have persuaded women against abortion even under the current regime — proof that the statute burdens location, not persuasion.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
