**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle that satisfies the nondelegation doctrine. Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Temporary Scheduling Provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), easily meets this standard.

**I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Provides an Intelligible Principle Channeling the Attorney General’s Discretion.**

The Act requires the Attorney General to find that temporary scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is more specific than delegations this Court has repeatedly upheld. In *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948), this Court sustained a delegation to renegotiate war contracts to eliminate “excessive profits,” a standard no more precise than “imminent hazard.” In *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), this Court upheld a delegation to the Federal Communications Commission to regulate broadcasting in the “public interest,” a standard this Court described as “a criterion to be applied as a guide.” If “excessive profits” and “public interest” satisfy the intelligible principle test, the stricter “imminent hazard to the public safety” standard a *fortiori* satisfies it.

The Act further confines the Attorney General by mandating consideration of specified factors: “(4) Its history and current pattern of abuse; (5) The scope, duration, and significance of abuse; (6) What, if any, risk there is to the public health.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6). This Court has held that requiring consideration of enumerated factors is a hallmark of a lawful delegation. See *Lichter*, 334 U.S. at 778-79 (sustaining delegation where Congress directed consideration of “such factors as he may deem advisable”); cf. *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105-06 (1946) (upholding delegation where Congress specified factors for the SEC to consider in regulating holding companies).

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is also narrowly limited in duration—one year, with a possible extension of six months. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limit is a “defining boundary,” and far from the open-ended delegations that this Court has found problematic. This Court has long required that Congress provide an intelligible principle to guide the delegatee. *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409. Where Congress provides no standard, the delegation is invalid. Here, by contrast, Congress defined the hazard, prescribed factors, and imposed a sunset. No such infirmity exists.

**II. This Delegation Involves Criminal Sanctions, but the Intelligible Principle Standard Applies.**

The Toubys argue that a stricter test applies because temporary scheduling creates criminal prohibitions. But this Court has never held that delegating authority to define crimes requires greater specificity than delegating economic or regulatory authority. To the contrary, in *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), this Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to set standards for imported tea, violations of which were criminal, because Congress had “prescribed a rule” and “defined the subject” to guide the Secretary. So too here.

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is far more concrete than the delegation upheld in *Buttfield*. There, the Secretary had authority to determine “what is tea” and “what is unfit for consumption.” 192 U.S. at 496. Here, the Attorney General must make specific findings of “imminent hazard to the public safety” and consider three enumerated factors. This is not an uncontrolled grant to define crime.

**III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated to the DEA.**

The Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to “promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that officers to whom executive power is delegated may subdelegate that power unless Congress expressly prohibits it. *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887) (recognizing implied authority to subdelegate); *United States v. Eaton*, 144 U.S. 677, 686-87 (1892) (upholding subdelegation of authority by the Secretary of the Treasury to subordinate officers). Here, Congress vested the temporary scheduling power in the “Attorney General,” not in a specific subordinate or bureau. The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator, is a routine administrative arrangement.

The Toubys’ challenge would require this Court to hold that every executive officer must personally exercise every power Congress confers. That proposition has no support in precedent. *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557; *Eaton*, 144 U.S. at 686-87. It would paralyze law enforcement.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Temporary Scheduling Provision provides an intelligible principle—imminent hazard to public safety—coupled with specific factors and a fixed duration. This easily satisfies the nondelegation doctrine as applied in *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Lichter*, 334 U.S. at 778-79; *National Broadcasting Co.*, 319 U.S. at 225-26; *American Power & Light Co.*, 329 U.S. at 105-06; and *Buttfield*, 192 U.S. at 496. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is well within the authority recognized in *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557, and *Eaton*, 144 U.S. at 686-87. The judgment below should be affirmed.
