**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—is one of first impression in this Court and of profound consequence to federal employees seeking redress for unlawful discrimination. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Perry v. MSPB*, 875 F.3d 1085 (D.C. Cir. 2017), wrongly interprets the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L. Ed. 2d 433 (2012), as having overruled *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), thereby depriving federal employees of the right to judicial review in district court when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds. This Court should reverse, for *Kloeckner* did not and could not eviscerate *Powell*, and the statutory text, legislative history, and precedent compel the conclusion that judicial review of jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases belongs in district court.

**I. The D.C. Circuit’s Interpretation of *Kloeckner* Is Contrary to the Supreme Court’s Holding and Misreads the Statutory Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Grounds**

The D.C. Circuit’s central error lies in its assertion that *Kloeckner* “effectively overruled” *Powell* by collapsing the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. That conclusion is flatly inconsistent with *Kloeckner*’s own reasoning and the Court’s repeated emphasis on the procedural nature of the dismissal at issue. In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a procedural ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court explicitly framed the issue as “whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or in a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds.” Id. at 603 (emphasis added). The Court cited decisions from the Second and Tenth Circuits that had drawn a clear line between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, and it expressly limited its holding to the former. Id. at 603 n.3.

The Court’s decision was grounded in the statutory text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), which defines a “mixed case” as one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board.” The Court emphasized that Kloeckner had been “affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB,” and that the Board’s dismissal was based on untimeliness, not lack of jurisdiction. Id. at 604. This distinction is critical: the action was appealable, but the appeal was procedurally defective. The Court did not address whether a case could be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction—such as where the employee voluntarily accepted the action—because such a case would not satisfy the statutory threshold of being “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” Id. at 604.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to equate procedural and jurisdictional dismissals ignores this foundational distinction. As the Federal Circuit has correctly observed, “the Board has the authority to entertain appeals that are procedurally defective under its own regulations, but it may not hear a case over which it lacks jurisdiction.” *Conforto v. MSPB*, 713 F.3d 1113, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). This principle is rooted in *Matter of D’Amico*, 10 M.S.P.B. 155, 158 (1981), which held that jurisdictional bars are “not subject to waiver or excuse,” unlike procedural defects. Thus, a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is not a mere “procedural” error; it is a determination that the Board lacks authority to hear the case at all. *Kloeckner* did not and could not alter that fundamental distinction.

**II. The Statutory Framework and Legislative Intent Support Review in District Court for Jurisdictional Dismissals in Mixed Cases**

The text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) makes clear that the “mixed case” exception applies when the employee is “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” The D.C. Circuit’s interpretation would render this language meaningless in the context of jurisdictional dismissals, because it would allow the Board to dismiss a case on jurisdictional grounds and then deny the employee the right to seek review in district court—precisely the forum Congress intended for mixed cases. This result is contrary to the principle that “a statute should be construed to give effect to all its provisions.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538 (1955).

Congress enacted the mixed-case exception to ensure that employees alleging discrimination could obtain a full and fair hearing in a court with general jurisdiction, rather than being confined to the specialized Federal Circuit. *See* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (noting that the Federal Circuit’s “specialized jurisdiction” may not be “the most appropriate forum for adjudicating claims of discrimination”). The D.C. Circuit’s rule—requiring review in the Federal Circuit even when the Board has dismissed a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds—undermines this purpose. It allows the Board to avoid the scrutiny of a district court by simply labeling a jurisdictional issue as “procedural,” thereby depriving the employee of the right to a jury trial and the full range of remedies available in district court.
