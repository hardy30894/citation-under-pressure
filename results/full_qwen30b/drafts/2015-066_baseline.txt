**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—requires this Court to resolve a critical tension between federal criminal law, tribal sovereignty, and constitutional due process. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that such convictions cannot be used to establish an essential element of a federal felony offense, rests on a flawed interpretation of *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), and misapplies the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel to a context where it does not apply. The Court should reverse.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplies *Nichols* by Extending Its Sentencing Holding to the Guilt Phase

The Ninth Circuit’s central error lies in treating *Nichols* as a broad constitutional license to use uncounseled misdemeanor convictions in any context where they are relevant to criminal liability. That is not what *Nichols* held. The Court in *Nichols* addressed only the use of prior uncounseled convictions in the *sentencing* phase of a criminal prosecution, where the government need only prove a sentencing enhancement by a *preponderance of the evidence*, not *beyond a reasonable doubt*. See *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748, 114 S.Ct. 1921. The Court emphasized that the sentencing scheme in *Nichols* “accommodated” reliability concerns by allowing defendants to challenge the validity of prior convictions and by applying a lower evidentiary standard. Id. at 747–48, 114 S.Ct. 1921.

The Court in *Nichols* explicitly declined to address whether such convictions could be used to establish an *element of the offense itself*. Id. at 746, 114 S.Ct. 1921. To do so would conflict with the fundamental principle that “the government must prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068, 25 L.Ed.2d 368 (1970). The Ninth Circuit’s decision, by contrast, permits the use of tribal court convictions to establish a *necessary element* of a federal felony—two prior domestic violence offenses—without requiring the government to meet the *Winship* standard. This is a constitutional violation.

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Nichols* on the ground that it “leaves open the question” of whether an uncounseled conviction can support a conviction at the guilt phase is disingenuous. The Court in *Nichols* did not leave the question open—it declined to decide it. The Court’s silence on the guilt phase is not a license to expand *Nichols* to that context. Indeed, the Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is triggered when the government seeks to prove an essential element of a crime. *Middleton v. McNeil*, 541 U.S. 433, 437, 124 S.Ct. 1830, 158 L.Ed.2d 701 (2004). The Ninth Circuit’s decision, by allowing the use of potentially unreliable, uncounseled tribal court convictions to establish a felony offense, violates that principle.

### II. Tribal Court Convictions Are Not Subject to the Sixth Amendment, and the Government May Rely on Them When Congress So Provides

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they lack a Sixth Amendment right to counsel is legally untenable. The Sixth Amendment applies only to federal and state courts. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24, 98 S.Ct. 1079, 55 L.Ed.2d 303 (1978). Tribal courts are neither federal nor state courts; they are sovereign entities exercising inherent authority over their members. *Id.* at 323, 98 S.Ct. 1079. The Bill of Rights does not apply to tribal courts. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709, 171 L.Ed.2d 457 (2008).

Congress has recognized this distinction and, through the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1301 et seq., established a framework for due process in tribal courts that is *not* coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. The ICRA provides certain rights, including the right to counsel in cases where the penalty includes imprisonment, but it does so in a manner tailored to tribal self-governance. See 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)–(c). The Ninth Circuit’s demand that tribal court convictions be “coextensive” with the Sixth Amendment right to counsel imposes a federal constitutional standard that Congress never required and that the Supreme Court has never mandated.

Moreover, Congress has expressly authorized the use of tribal court convictions in federal recidivist statutes. Section 117(a) explicitly includes “Indian tribal court proceedings” as a source of predicate offenses. Congress did not condition this authorization on the presence of a Sixth Amendment right to counsel. To the contrary, Congress recognized that tribal courts operate under different legal traditions and procedural norms. The Court should not read a constitutional requirement into § 117(a) that Congress did not impose.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Undermines Federal Efforts to Combat Domestic Violence on Tribal Lands

The Ninth Circuit’s decision has profound real-world consequences. It creates a circuit split with the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *United States v.
