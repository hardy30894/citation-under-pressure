**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 4(b)(1)'s Exemption Applies Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Statutory Authority Over the Specific Working Conditions**

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws from OSHA's coverage only working conditions "with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations." 29 U.S.C. §653(b)(1). A party invoking an exemption from a broad remedial scheme bears the burden of showing that its claim falls "plainly and unmistakably within [the exemption's] terms and spirit." *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945). Exemptions to remedial labor legislation are "narrowly construed." *Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 607, 617 (1944). Because the OSH Act's purpose is comprehensive protection of employee safety, 29 U.S.C. §651(b), an exemption cannot rest on the theoretical possibility that another agency might regulate; it requires proof that the agency has, in fact, exercised authority over the working condition at issue.

**II. This Court's Vessel-Regulation Precedents Confirm That Preemption Extends Only as Far as the Coast Guard's Actual Regulatory Scheme Reaches**

*A. Kelly v. Washington.* In *Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co.*, 302 U.S. 1 (1937), this Court sustained a state licensing requirement for engineers aboard small coastwise vessels because the federal navigation and inspection laws, though comprehensive for the class of vessels they covered, simply did not extend to the smaller, uninspected vessels at issue. Congress's occupation of part of the maritime-safety field did not occupy the whole; a vessel outside the regulated class remained open to regulation by another authority. *Id.* at 14–16. The same logic governs here. The Coast Guard's comprehensive regime, see 46 U.S.C. §391a, applies only to "inspected vessels." Rig 52, an uninspected vessel, falls outside that scheme exactly as the small coastwise vessels in *Kelly* fell outside federal certification requirements.

*B. Cooley, Clyde Mallory, and Huron Portland Cement.* This Court's "selective exclusiveness" doctrine confirms that federal maritime authority displaces other regulation only where Congress or its delegate has actually acted and uniformity is indispensable. In *Cooley v. Board of Wardens*, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 299 (1852), the Court held that only matters "in their nature national, or admit only of one uniform system, or plan of regulation," *id.* at 319, require exclusive federal control; local concerns remain open to concurrent regulation absent conflict. This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Clyde Mallory Lines v. Alabama*, 296 U.S. 261 (1935), sustaining a state inspection fee on vessels because Congress had not regulated that precise subject, and in *Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit*, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), sustaining a municipal smoke-abatement ordinance applied to a vessel's boilers because the federal boiler-inspection statute addressed structural safety, not emissions, and left that subject unregulated. The teaching is uniform: dormant or partial federal authority does not itself dispossess another regulator; only an actually exercised, conflicting federal standard can do so.

*Just v. Chambers*, 312 U.S. 383 (1941), applies the identical principle within admiralty, permitting state law to operate where it does not intrude on "characteristic features" of maritime law demanding uniformity. *Id.* at 388. The Coast Guard's minimal regulation of uninspected vessels—confined to discrete equipment requirements such as fire extinguishers and life preservers under 46 U.S.C. §4102—no more occupies the field of occupational safety and health than the federal boiler statute in *Huron* occupied the field of smoke abatement, or the federal certification law in *Kelly* occupied the field of small-vessel engineering standards.

**III. The Secretary's Construction of the Statutory Boundary Between OSHA and Coast Guard Jurisdiction Is Entitled to Respect**

Where an agency charged with administering a statute reasonably construes the boundary of its jurisdiction against a rival claim of exclusive authority, that construction merits substantial weight. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 130–131 (1944). Even absent controlling deference, the Secretary's interpretation should be sustained for "the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, [and] its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements." *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The Secretary has consistently construed §4(b)(1) to displace OSHA jurisdiction only where another agency has promulgated standards actually covering the working condition at issue—precisely the rule this Court's vessel-regulation precedents independently compel.

**CONCLUSION**

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised comprehensive statutory authority over the working conditions aboard uninspected vessels like Rig 52, §4(b)(1) does not withdraw those conditions from OSHA's jurisdiction. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
