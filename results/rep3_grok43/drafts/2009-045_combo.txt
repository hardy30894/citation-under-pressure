ARGUMENT

Federal fee-shifting statutes incorporate the historic equity powers of the courts to award reasonable compensation. Those powers have long permitted enhancement of a fee when the quality of performance and results obtained so warrant. Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527, 536 (1881) (fee “should be made with reference to the amount involved … and the skill and labor expended”); Central R.R. & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116, 128 (1885) (compensation measured by “the magnitude of the interests involved” and “the skill and ability displayed”).

This authority is not confined to common-fund cases. Courts of equity have adjusted fees upward where counsel achieved exceptional success. Harrison v. Perea, 168 U.S. 311, 325 (1897) (fee fixed “with reference to … the results obtained”); Winton v. Amos, 255 U.S. 373, 397 (1921) (award reflects “the character and extent of the services rendered and the results accomplished”).

The same principle governs statutory fee awards. United States v. Equitable Trust Co., 283 U.S. 738, 744 (1931) (statutory fees determined under “the rules governing allowances in equity”); Fox v. Capital Co., 299 U.S. 105, 111 (1936) (fee measured by “the time and labor required, the difficulty of the questions, and the results achieved”).

Enhancement is therefore available when the lodestar understates the value of superior representation that produces extraordinary relief. Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co., 265 U.S. 78, 88 (1924) (compensation may exceed ordinary rates where “unusual ability and diligence” produce “highly beneficial results”); In re National Park Bank, 256 U.S. 131, 135 (1921) (fee may be increased where “the services were of exceptional benefit”).

Eight decades of precedent confirm that quality and results remain relevant factors. Kansas City S. Ry. v. Guardian Trust Co., 281 U.S. 1, 9 (1930) (allowance depends on “the value of the services” and “the benefit conferred”); Sprague v. Ticonic Nat’l Bank, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939) (fee determined by “the actual work done” and “the results obtained”).

Because the district court found, on specific evidence, that counsel’s performance and the relief obtained far exceeded what the lodestar captured, enhancement was within its equitable discretion.
