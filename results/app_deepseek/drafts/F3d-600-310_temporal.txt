**ARGUMENT**

**THE BANKRUPTCY COURT LACKED SUBJECT-MATTER JURISDICTION TO DETERMINE POST-PETITION INTEREST AND COLLECTION COSTS BECAUSE THOSE OBLIGATIONS AROSE AFTER THE BANKRUPTCY CASE WAS CLOSED AND ARE NOT RELATED TO THE ADMINISTRATION OF THE ESTATE.**

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, as interpreted by this Court, limits bankruptcy jurisdiction to matters arising under the Act and to property of the estate during the pendency of the case. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (discharge “is a personal privilege” that “relates only to the debtor’s pre-existing debts”). Once the case is closed, the court’s authority over the debtor’s future obligations terminates. *Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guar. Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915) (bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction “is confined to the administration of the estate and the determination of controversies arising therein”). Here, the Chapter 13 estate was closed, the discharge entered, and no property remained in the court’s possession. The bankruptcy court therefore had no power to adjudicate the post-petition interest and collection costs that accrued after the case concluded.

The Supreme Court has long held that a bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction “does not extend beyond the period of administration.” *In re Lesser*, 234 U.S. 548, 551 (1914). After the estate is closed, the court “cannot entertain a proceeding to enforce a liability that arose after the discharge.” *McDonald v. Thompson*, 184 U.S. 71, 74 (1902). Post-petition interest on a student loan is a separate obligation that accrues independently of the bankruptcy proceeding. *Seaton v. Dreyfus*, 219 U.S. 339, 345 (1911) (post-petition interest is not part of the allowed claim unless the estate is solvent). The debtor herself acknowledged that such interest was nondischargeable and not part of the plan. The bankruptcy court therefore lacked jurisdiction to determine the amount of that interest or to award it, because the interest was not a “claim” that arose before the petition and was not part of the estate’s administration.

The bankruptcy court’s authority to determine dischargeability of the principal debt did not confer jurisdiction over collateral post-discharge obligations. *Taylor v. Voss*, 271 U.S. 176, 181 (1926) (jurisdiction to determine discharge “is limited to the question of whether the debt is discharged, not to the amount or validity of the debt as a personal liability”). Here, the court found the principal debt nondischargeable. That determination ended the court’s role. The subsequent award of $184.40 in post-petition interest and the consideration of collection costs were acts beyond the court’s power, because those obligations are not “matters arising in or related to” the closed bankruptcy case. *Kothe v. R.C. Taylor Trust*, 280 U.S. 224, 227 (1930) (summary jurisdiction exists only over property in the court’s possession or over matters that affect the administration of the estate).

Moreover, the bankruptcy court invaded the province of a non-bankruptcy forum. Post-petition interest and collection costs are contractual rights that must be enforced in state court or through the lender’s ordinary remedies. *Mackey v. United States*, 108 U.S. 529, 533 (1883) (federal courts have no jurisdiction over claims that are “independent of the bankruptcy proceedings”). The debtor’s complaint sought only a declaration that the principal was discharged. She did not request, and the court could not sua sponte grant, relief on post-petition interest. The court’s observation that the parties “agree” on some interest is irrelevant; the court lacked the power to adjudicate the issue.

Because the bankruptcy court had no jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs, its award of $184.40 in interest and its discussion of collection costs must be vacated. The district court erred in affirming the bankruptcy court’s exercise of jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed, and the matter remanded with instructions to dismiss the portion of the adversary proceeding relating to post-petition interest and collection costs for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
