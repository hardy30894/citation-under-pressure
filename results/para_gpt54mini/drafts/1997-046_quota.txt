**Argument**

I. **No inmate has a protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings.**

The Due Process Clause protects only interests that are independently secured by the Constitution or created by state law. *Board of Regents of State Colleges v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972). But executive clemency is different. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that a prisoner has no liberty interest in commutation because the power to grant clemency is committed to executive grace, not legal entitlement. The Court explained that pardon and commutation decisions “have not traditionally been the business of courts.” *Id.* at 464.

That rule controls here. Ohio’s clemency process is a mechanism for asking the Executive to exercise mercy; it is not part of the adjudication of guilt or sentence. In *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272 (1998), the Court reaffirmed *Dumschat*’s core holding that “some minimal procedural safeguards apply to clemency proceedings,” but only against truly arbitrary state action; the Court rejected any claim that clemency itself creates a freestanding due process entitlement. *Id.* at 289 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment); *id.* at 289-90 (Stevens, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (recognizing that only the “protection against arbitrary denial of access” survives). The plurality likewise emphasized that Ohio’s process did not offend due process simply because it afforded no enforceable substantive entitlement. *Id.* at 285-86 (Rehnquist, C.J.).

Petitioner’s claim also fails under the Court’s clemency precedent because the state may define clemency as wholly discretionary. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), the Court held that a liberty interest arises only where state law uses mandatory language and substantive predicates limiting official discretion. *Id.* at 11-12. Ohio’s clemency scheme does the opposite: it leaves the decision to executive mercy. Under *Dumschat* and *Greenholtz*, there is no protected liberty interest.

Nor is there a protected “life” interest in the abstract. The life interest at stake in capital clemency derives from the underlying valid judgment of death, not from the clemency process itself. Once the conviction and sentence are final, due process does not convert a discretionary mercy procedure into a constitutionally mandated adjudication. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464; *Woodard*, 523 U.S. at 285-86.

II. **Offering a voluntary interview as part of clemency does not violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment is violated only by compelled self-incrimination. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316-18 (1976). It does not forbid the government from offering an inmate a choice, even a difficult choice, so long as the state does not require testimony under threat of direct criminal sanction. *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 286-87 (1998) (plurality opinion); *id.* at 290 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment).

Ohio’s clemency interview is voluntary. The regulation at issue permits an inmate to request a personal interview, but it does not compel the inmate to speak, answer questions, or waive the privilege. A voluntary interview that may help persuade the Executive to extend mercy is not compulsion. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 427 (1984) (compulsion requires more than a choice to speak or remain silent); *McKune v. Lile*, 536 U.S. 24, 41 (2002) (plurality opinion) (not every adverse consequence attached to silence is unconstitutional compulsion).

This Court has repeatedly held that the State may draw adverse inferences or condition benefits in noncriminal settings without violating the Fifth Amendment. *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 318; *Murphy*, 465 U.S. at 435 n.7. Ohio’s procedure is even less coercive: it offers an optional opportunity to speak in support of clemency. Nothing in the Constitution forbids the State from considering a prisoner’s voluntary statements if he elects to make them. *Garner v. United States*, 424 U.S. 648, 655-56 (1976).

Petitioner’s theory would transform a discretionary plea for mercy into a constitutional trap. That is not what *Woodard* permits. The Court there rejected the suggestion that a voluntary clemency interview, standing alone, creates unconstitutional compulsion. 523 U.S. at 285-87 (plurality opinion); *id.* at 290 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment). At most, a prisoner remains free to remain silent and submit whatever written materials he chooses. *Id.* That is not a Fifth Amendment violation.

III. **Because clemency is discretionary and the interview is voluntary, the judgment below should be reversed.**

The Sixth Circuit’s approach conflicts with *Dumschat*, *Greenholtz*, and *Woodard* by constitutionalizing what this Court has treated as an act of grace. It likewise conflicts with *Baxter* and *Murphy* by treating a voluntary interview as compelled testimony. The Constitution forbids coercion; it does not forbid Ohio from offering a condemned prisoner one last chance to seek mercy. The judgment should be reversed.
