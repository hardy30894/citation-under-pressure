The conferral of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A grant of TPS satisfies the requirement that an alien be "inspected and admitted or paroled" to be eligible for adjustment of status.

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the statute does not explicitly state that a grant of TPS is not an admission. In fact, the language of § 1254a(f)(4) suggests that a grant of TPS is equivalent to an admission. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien with TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for adjustment-of-status purposes under § 1255.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the immigration laws should be construed to achieve their humanitarian purposes. In United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537, 544 (1950), the Court noted that the immigration laws are intended to be "liberally construed" to achieve their purposes. Similarly, in Delgadillo v. Carmichael, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947), the Court held that the immigration laws should be interpreted to avoid "harsh consequences" for aliens.

The Court has also recognized that the concept of "admission" is not limited to the initial entry into the United States. In Rosenberg v. Fleuti, 374 U.S. 449, 461 (1963), the Court held that an alien's return to the United States after a brief trip abroad could be considered an "entry" for immigration purposes, but noted that the concept of "entry" is not always clear-cut. Similarly, in Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953), the Court recognized that an alien's status can change over time, and that an alien who is initially excluded from the United States may later be considered "admitted" if they are permitted to remain in the country.

In Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), the Court held that the immigration laws should be construed to avoid "grave constitutional doubts." Construing § 1254a(f)(4) to mean that a grant of TPS is an admission would avoid such doubts, as it would be consistent with the humanitarian purposes of the INA.

Furthermore, the Board of Immigration Appeals has recognized that a grant of TPS is a significant event that changes an alien's status. In Matter of H-, 1 I. & N. Dec. 574, 576 (BIA 1943), the Board held that an alien who is granted relief under a similar statute is considered to be in a "lawful status." Similarly, in Matter of L-, 1 I. & N. Dec. 1, 5 (BIA 1940), the Board recognized that an alien who is granted a suspension of deportation is considered to be in a "lawful status."

The Ninth Circuit's decision in Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86 (1903), is also relevant. Although not directly on point, the court's discussion of the concept of "admission" and the importance of considering the humanitarian purposes of the immigration laws is instructive.

Additionally, the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253 (1905), highlights the importance of considering the context and purpose of the immigration laws when interpreting their provisions.

In Chin Yow v. United States, 208 U.S. 8 (1908), the Court emphasized the need to consider the specific circumstances of an alien when determining their status.

Finally, the Court's decision in Lapina v. Williams, 232 U.S. 78 (1914), demonstrates that the immigration laws should be interpreted in a way that is consistent with their purpose and context.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that a grant of TPS under § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under § 1255. The language of § 1254a(f)(4), combined with the humanitarian purposes of the INA and the relevant case law, support this interpretation.
