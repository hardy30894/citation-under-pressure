**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Content-Neutral, Viewpoint-Neutral Time, Place, and Manner Regulation That Satisfies Intermediate Scrutiny.**

The First Circuit correctly held that Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½ is a content-neutral regulation of speech. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176–81 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014). Such regulations are valid if they (1) are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech, (2) are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Massachusetts statute meets each requirement.

First, the statute is content-neutral. It does not single out any particular message or viewpoint for restriction. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar buffer-zone law because it “imposes no restrictions on—and does not criminalize—either a particular viewpoint or any subject matter”). The law applies equally to all speakers, regardless of their message. *See id.* at 720–21. As this Court has recognized, “[g]overnmental regulation of speech is content-based if a law applies to particular speech because of the topic discussed or the idea or message expressed.” *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 135 S. Ct. 2218, 2227 (2015). Here, the statute regulates speech based solely on its location near abortion clinics, not its content. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720.

Second, the statute is narrowly tailored to serve the Commonwealth’s significant interests in public safety, patient access, and orderly use of public streets. *See id.* at 715 (upholding a buffer zone because it “helps to protect the psychological and physical well-being of clinic patients and staff”); *see also Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767–68 (1994) (upholding a 36-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances as narrowly tailored to protect patients and staff). The 35-foot buffer zone is no broader than necessary to achieve these interests. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799 (explaining that a regulation is narrowly tailored if it “promotes a substantial government interest that would be achieved less effectively absent the regulation”). The statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication, including public sidewalks beyond the buffer zone, where petitioners may speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive conduct. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723 (finding that a similar law left open ample alternatives because speakers could “communicate their message outside the 100-foot zone”).

**II. The First Circuit Correctly Held That Hill v. Colorado Controls and Should Not Be Overruled.**

The First Circuit properly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, in upholding the Massachusetts statute. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181–82. *Hill* expressly upheld a content-neutral buffer-zone law that restricted speech within 100 feet of healthcare facilities. *Id.* at 720. The Court rejected the argument that the law was an unconstitutional restriction on speech, finding that it satisfied intermediate scrutiny. *Id.* at 715–23. The Massachusetts statute is materially indistinguishable from the Colorado law upheld in *Hill*. Both laws are content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests, and leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *See id.* at 720–23.

Petitioners’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* is unavailing. They argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled because it conflicts with more recent First Amendment precedents. *See, e.g., Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011); *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010). However, this Court has repeatedly declined to overrule *Hill*. *See, e.g., Scheidler v. Nat’l Org. for Women*, 537 U.S. 393, 409 (2003) (citing *Hill* with approval). Moreover, *Hill* remains good law because it is consistent with this Court’s time, place, and manner jurisprudence. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The Massachusetts statute, like the Colorado law in *Hill*, is a valid content-neutral regulation of speech.

**III. The First Circuit Correctly Held That the Massachusetts Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment as Applied to the Petitioners.**

The First Circuit correctly held that the Massachusetts statute does not violate the First Amendment as applied to the petitioners. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen III)*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213–25 (D. Mass. 2012), *aff’d*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009). The statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication, including public sidewalks beyond the 35-foot buffer zone. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The petitioners may speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive conduct outside the buffer zone. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723. The fact that the petitioners must take a few steps back from the clinic entrances does not violate the First Amendment. *See id.* at 720–21.

The petitioners’ evidence of diminished effectiveness is insufficient to invalidate the statute. *See id.* at 723 (explaining that a law is not invalid simply because it “makes it more difficult to distribute literature or display signs”). The statute does not prohibit the petitioners from communicating their message; it merely regulates the time, place, and manner of that communication. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The petitioners have not shown that the statute prevents them from effectively communicating their message. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723.

**IV. The First Circuit Correctly Held That the Law of the Case Doctrine Bars Relitigation of the Facial Constitutionality of the Statute.**

The First Circuit correctly held that the law of the case doctrine bars the petitioners from relitigating the facial constitutionality of the statute. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176–81. The law of the case doctrine “prevents relitigation in the trial court of matters that were explicitly or implicitly decided by an earlier appellate decision in the same case.” *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011) (internal quotation marks omitted). The First Circuit previously held that the statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176–81. The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate this issue is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *See Matthews*, 643 F.3d at 13.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies intermediate scrutiny. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, controls, and the law of the case doctrine bars relitigation of the statute’s facial constitutionality. The statute does not violate the First Amendment as applied to the petitioners.
