**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

Respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court has long held that “[t]he cause-and-prejudice test is a prerequisite to federal habeas review of procedurally defaulted claims.” *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). The Court has further explained that “[t]he cause requirement is satisfied when the petitioner shows that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” *Id.* at 753. However, the Court has never recognized ineffective assistance of counsel as a basis for cause to excuse a procedural default. To the contrary, the Court has made clear that “attorney error short of ineffective assistance of counsel does not constitute cause to excuse a procedural default.” *Id.* at 754.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding conflicts with this Court’s precedent. In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Court held that “[a]n ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim, standing alone, is not sufficient to establish cause.” *Id.* at 488. The Court reasoned that “[t]he existence of a constitutional violation does not by itself mean that the prisoner is entitled to federal habeas relief,” because “[t]he procedural default rule serves the important interest in the finality of state court judgments.” *Id.* at 488-89. The Court further explained that “[i]f a prisoner could establish cause for a procedural default by showing that his counsel was ineffective in failing to raise the claim in state court, the cause requirement would be meaningless.” *Id.* at 489.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision in this case is irreconcilable with *Carrier*. The court below held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim itself was procedurally defaulted. *See Edwards v. Carpenter*, 200 F.3d 395, 400 (6th Cir. 2000). This holding is inconsistent with *Carrier*, which makes clear that an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse a procedural default, whether or not the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim is itself procedurally defaulted.

**II. Even if an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause, the Sixth Circuit erred in finding that petitioner’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective.**

Respondent further submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in finding that petitioner’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective. To establish ineffective assistance of counsel, a petitioner must show that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). The Court has explained that “[j]udicial scrutiny of counsel’s performance must be highly deferential,” and that “a court must indulge a strong presumption that counsel’s conduct falls within the wide range of reasonable professional assistance.” *Id.* at 689.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision is inconsistent with *Strickland*. The court below held that petitioner’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective for failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal. *See Carpenter*, 200 F.3d at 401. However, the Court has made clear that “[a]n error by counsel, even if professionally unreasonable, does not warrant setting aside the judgment of a criminal proceeding if the error had no effect on the judgment.” *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 691. The Court has further explained that “[t]he defendant must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different.” *Id.* at 694.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision is inconsistent with these principles. The court below held that petitioner’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective for failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, even though the court acknowledged that the evidence supporting the conviction was “overwhelming.” *Carpenter*, 200 F.3d at 401. This holding is inconsistent with *Strickland*, which requires a showing of prejudice to establish ineffective assistance of counsel.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Sixth Circuit and hold that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.
