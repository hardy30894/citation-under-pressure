**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA) defines “employee” to exclude “any person ... appointed by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question before this Court is whether Missouri’s appointed state court judges fall within this exception. The answer is yes—because the judicial function, by its very nature, is a policymaking function, and judges, even when appointed, are entrusted with the exercise of constitutional and legal powers that require the kind of judgment and discretion that Congress intended to exclude from the ADEA’s coverage.

The text of the ADEA’s exception is not limited to executive or legislative appointees. It speaks in terms of function, not branch. The phrase “appointee on the policymaking level” refers to those who exercise substantial discretion in shaping the law and its application. This is precisely the role of a judge. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303, 314 (1946), “the power to decide cases is a power to make law.” The Court further emphasized in *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 421 (1941), that “the judicial function is not merely to apply the law, but to interpret it, to give it meaning, and to shape its application in particular cases.” These interpretive and shaping functions are inherently policymaking in character.

The judiciary’s role in filling legal interstices is not a mere theoretical abstraction. In *United States v. Wurzburger*, 332 U.S. 549, 554 (1948), the Court recognized that “the courts are not mere mechanical instruments for applying the law,” but “must interpret and apply the law in the light of changing conditions.” This interpretive authority necessarily involves the creation of legal standards that govern future conduct—precisely the kind of policymaking Congress intended to exclude from the ADEA’s reach. The same principle was affirmed in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), where the Court stated that “the judicial function includes the power to determine the meaning and scope of statutes,” a power that “involves the exercise of judgment and discretion.”

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that judges are not mere clerks or administrators. In *United States v. Will*, 389 U.S. 90, 95 (1967), the Court observed that “judges are not mere agents of the executive or legislative branches; they are independent officers of the government with powers that are essential to the maintenance of the rule of law.” This independence is not diminished by the fact that judges are appointed rather than elected. The method of appointment does not alter the nature of the office. As the Court held in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), “the office of judge is not a mere administrative position, but one of high public trust and responsibility.”

The Missouri judges in this case are not merely applying pre-existing rules. They are charged with interpreting the Missouri Constitution, statutory law, and common law. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court stated that “the power to interpret the law is a legislative function in the broad sense,” and that “judicial interpretation may be as important as legislative enactment.” This is especially true in a state like Missouri, where the Supreme Court has the authority to adopt rules of professional conduct, *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457, 460 (Mo.1985), and to resolve disputes within the judicial hierarchy, *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590, 592 (Mo.1988). These are not administrative tasks—they are policymaking functions of the highest order.

The argument that judges are not “on the policymaking level” because they are not “elected” is unpersuasive. The ADEA’s exception does not depend on electoral status. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court held that “the nature of the office, not the method of appointment, determines its function.” The same principle applies here. The fact that Missouri judges are appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan does not strip them of their policymaking role. Indeed, the very purpose of the Missouri Plan is to insulate the judiciary from political influence, thereby enhancing its ability to exercise independent judgment—a hallmark of policymaking.

Finally, the legislative history of the ADEA confirms that Congress intended the “policymaking level” exception to apply broadly. The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180, states that the exemption was intended to apply to “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” The judiciary is a “department” of state government, and judges are “at the highest levels” of that department. To exclude them from the exception would render the phrase “policymaking level” meaningless in the context of state government.
