**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s *subjective intent* to threaten—is one of profound constitutional significance. The First Amendment protects speech, even when it is offensive or disturbing, unless it falls within a narrow exception. The “true threat” exception, which permits criminal prosecution of certain communications, has long been understood to require more than mere objective foreseeability—it demands that the speaker *intended* his words to be taken as threats. This Court has repeatedly held that the First Amendment does not permit punishment for speech based solely on how a reasonable person might interpret it; rather, it demands a showing of *subjective intent*. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that an objective standard suffices—violates settled precedent and impermissibly expands the reach of criminal law into protected expression.

### I. THE TRUE THREAT EXCEPTION REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN

The Supreme Court has made clear that the “true threat” exception to the First Amendment is not a license to punish speech based on its potential to frighten others. In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), this Court rejected the notion that a “true threat” can be established by merely showing that a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening. Instead, the Court emphasized that “a true threat is a knowing and intentional communication of a serious threat to harm another.” Id. at 360. The Court explained that “the First Amendment protects the use of language that may be offensive or alarming, so long as it is not intended to convey a threat.” Id. at 359–60. Thus, the critical inquiry is whether the speaker *subjectively intended* his words to be understood as threats.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 282, 291 (1971), where the Court held that “the Constitution protects speech even if it is offensive to some.” The Court recognized that “the government may not prohibit speech simply because it is offensive,” and that “the line between protected expression and unprotected conduct must be drawn with care.” Id. at 291–92. The same logic applies here: without a requirement of subjective intent, the government could criminalize any speech that someone might reasonably fear, regardless of the speaker’s actual purpose.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* did not address the constitutional threshold for what constitutes a true threat under the First Amendment. It instead applied a pre-*Black* understanding of the term, which the Supreme Court later disavowed. Since *Black*, no circuit court has upheld a true threat conviction absent a finding of subjective intent. Indeed, the Court in *Black* explicitly stated that “a true threat is not protected by the First Amendment,” but only “when the speaker means to communicate a serious threat to injure another.” 538 U.S. at 359 (emphasis added). The phrase “means to communicate” necessarily implies *intent*—not just perception.

### II. THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S OBJECTIVE STANDARD VIOLATES DUE PROCESS

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that a criminal statute define its elements with sufficient clarity to inform individuals of the conduct that is prohibited. *Connick v. Myers*, 461 U.S. 138, 147 (1983). A standard that allows conviction based on how a reasonable person might interpret speech—regardless of the speaker’s actual intent—fails this test. It creates a regime in which individuals are punished not for what they meant to say, but for what others might have thought they meant.

In *United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 804 (2012), the Court struck down a federal statute criminalizing false statements about military decorations because it punished speech without regard to the speaker’s intent. The Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to speak falsely, even if the falsehood causes harm.” Id. at 818. The same reasoning applies here: punishing someone for a statement they did not intend as a threat violates the core principle that “the government may not punish speech simply because it is offensive or unpopular.” *Cohen*, 403 U.S. at 291.

The Third Circuit’s instruction—that a statement is a true threat if a “reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat”—creates precisely the kind of vague, open-ended standard the Constitution forbids. It invites juries to convict based on speculation about audience reaction, rather than on evidence of the speaker’s own mental state. Such a standard is indistinguishable from the “fear-based” approach the Court rejected in *Black*. As the Court warned: “A rule that makes criminal liability depend on the reaction of the listener would allow the government to punish speech based on its effect, not its content.” 538 U.S. at 359.

### III. THE GOVERNMENT HAS NOT SHOWN THAT ELONIS SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENTS AS THREATS

Petitioner’s Facebook posts were written in the form of rap lyrics—poetic, hyperbolic, and often self-aware. He repeatedly used irony, sarcasm, and fictional framing. For example, he wrote: “I’m not actually saying it. I’m just letting you know that it’s illegal for me to say that.” This statement, far from indicating intent to threaten, demonstrates an awareness that his words were not literal. Similarly, his post referencing a “mortar launcher” and a “diagram” was clearly satirical, complete with mock legal commentary and a link to Wikipedia. These features strongly suggest that Elonis was not expressing a genuine intent to harm, but rather engaging in performative, artistic expression.

The jury was never instructed to consider whether Elonis *meant* his words as threats. Instead, it was told to apply an objective standard. That failure to require proof of subjective intent rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. Without such a requirement, the jury had no way to distinguish between a man venting frustration through metaphor and a man planning violence. The result is a system in which speech is punished not for its content, but for its potential to alarm.
