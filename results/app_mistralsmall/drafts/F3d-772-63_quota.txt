**ARGUMENT**

I. **WELLS FARGO BREACHED THE SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT BY FAILING TO CONSIDER FOLEY FOR A HAMP OR MAP2R MODIFICATION BEFORE INITIATING FORECLOSURE.**

The First Circuit construes a complaint’s allegations liberally, accepting all well-pleaded facts as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat*, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014). Here, Foley plausibly alleges that Wells Fargo violated the class action settlement agreement by failing to consider him for a HAMP or MAP2R modification before pursuing foreclosure. Under Massachusetts contract law, a party breaches a contract when it fails to perform a material obligation without legal excuse. *See Stop & Shop Supermarket Co. v. Ganem*, 473 Mass. 198, 204 (2015). The settlement agreement explicitly required Wells Fargo to first consider Settlement Class B members—including Foley—for a HAMP modification and, if ineligible, to proceed through the MAP2R “waterfall” process. The bank’s failure to adhere to this sequential process, as alleged, constitutes a breach.

Wells Fargo’s argument that Foley failed to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6) is unavailing. To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint need only provide “fair notice” of the claim and plausible factual allegations supporting each element. *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). Foley’s complaint sufficiently alleges that Wells Fargo breached the agreement by ignoring its modification obligations while simultaneously pursuing foreclosure. *See Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (plausibility standard does not require detailed factual recitations).

II. **FOLEY’S STATE-LAW CLAIMS ARE NOT PREEMPTED BY THE HOME OWNERSHIP AND EQUITY PROTECTION ACT (HOEPA).**

Wells Fargo contends that Foley’s state-law claims are preempted by HOEPA, 15 U.S.C. § 1641(a). However, HOEPA’s preemptive scope is limited to claims arising under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) itself. *See 15 U.S.C. § 1641(a) (“[N]o civil action ... may be maintained ... with respect to any act done or omitted in good faith in conformity with [TILA]”).* Foley’s claims arise from the settlement agreement’s contractual obligations, not TILA. *See Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 518 (1992) (preemption applies only where state law conflicts with federal law or where Congress expressly so provides).

Moreover, HOEPA does not preempt state-law claims predicated on a lender’s failure to comply with a separate contractual agreement. *See In re Stewart*, 493 B.R. 285, 293 (Bankr. D. Mass. 2013) (HOEPA does not preempt state-law breach of contract claims). Foley’s claims are thus not preempted.

III. **FOLEY’S CLAIMS UNDER MASSACHUSETTS GENERAL LAWS CHAPTER 244 ARE PLAUSIBLY STATED.**

Foley’s Chapter 244 claims allege that Wells Fargo failed to comply with Massachusetts foreclosure laws by pursuing foreclosure without first considering him for a modification. *See Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 35B* (requiring lenders to consider loan modifications before foreclosure). The First Circuit has repeatedly held that state-law claims alleging violations of foreclosure statutes survive Rule 12(b)(6) if they plausibly allege noncompliance. *See, e.g., In re Bank of N.Y. Mellon*, 866 F.3d 207, 215 (1st Cir. 2017) (upholding Chapter 244 claims where lender allegedly failed to comply with modification requirements).

Wells Fargo’s reliance on *U.S. Bank N.A. v. Ibanez*, 458 Mass. 637 (2011), is misplaced. *Ibanez* addressed procedural defects in foreclosure sales, not substantive obligations to consider modifications. *Id.* at 651. Foley’s claims, by contrast, challenge the bank’s failure to follow the settlement agreement’s modification process—a distinct legal duty.

IV. **FOLEY’S CLAIMS FOR BREACH OF THE IMPLIED COVENANT OF GOOD FAITH AND FAIR DEALING ARE PLAUSIBLY STATED.**

Under Massachusetts law, every contract implies a covenant of good faith and fair dealing. *See Anthony’s Pier Four, Inc. v. HBC Assocs.*, 411 Mass. 451, 472 (1991). Foley plausibly alleges that Wells Fargo acted in bad faith by ignoring its contractual obligation to consider him for a modification while pursuing foreclosure. *See id.* (covenant prohibits “arbitrary or unreasonable” conduct undermining the contract’s purpose). The First Circuit has recognized that bad faith claims survive Rule 12(b)(6) where the complaint alleges specific acts inconsistent with the parties’ reasonable expectations. *See, e.g., Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co. v. PHL Variable Ins. Co.*, 883 F.3d 99, 108 (1st Cir. 2018).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Foley’s claims are properly stated and should not have been dismissed. The district court erred in ruling that Foley failed to state a claim or that his state-law claims were preempted. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
