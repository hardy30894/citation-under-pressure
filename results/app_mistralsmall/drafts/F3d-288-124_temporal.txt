**ARGUMENT**

**I. STANDING TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD**

Bryant has standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award under well-established principles of contract law. In *Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills*, 353 U.S. 448 (1957), the Supreme Court recognized that arbitration awards arising from collective bargaining agreements are enforceable under § 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 185. *See id.* at 456 ("The duty to arbitrate being clear ... the federal courts have jurisdiction to enforce the arbitration award."). Here, the arbitration award was issued pursuant to the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA, of which Bryant is a member. Thus, Bryant, as a party to the underlying dispute resolved by arbitration, has standing to seek its enforcement. *See United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593 (1960) (holding that a party to an arbitration proceeding may enforce the resulting award).

**II. APPLICABLE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK UNDER TITLE VII AND § 1981**

The district court erred in failing to apply the burden-shifting framework articulated in *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), as clarified by subsequent decisions. However, even under the pre-*McDonnell Douglas* standard, which was derived from *Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567 (1978), and *Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248 (1981), the burden-shifting analysis remains the same. *See Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971) (establishing the framework for disparate treatment claims under Title VII).

Under this framework, Bryant must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination by showing that he (1) belongs to a protected class, (2) was qualified for the position, (3) suffered an adverse employment action, and (4) was treated less favorably than similarly situated employees outside the protected class. *See McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 802. Once Bryant makes this showing, the burden shifts to Bell Atlantic to articulate a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for its actions. *See id.* If Bell Atlantic meets this burden, the presumption of discrimination disappears, and Bryant must then prove that Bell Atlantic’s proffered reason was pretextual. *See id.* at 804.

Here, Bryant has presented sufficient evidence to establish a prima facie case of discrimination based on his race, color, and gender. He is African-American, and his termination followed a pattern of discipline that was not applied consistently to similarly situated non-African-American employees. *See Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567 (1978) (holding that a plaintiff may establish a prima facie case by showing that he was treated less favorably than similarly situated employees outside the protected class). Bell Atlantic has not met its burden of articulating a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for its actions, as Bryant’s termination was based on insubordination, a pretext for discrimination. *See Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 254-56 (explaining that the employer’s burden is to produce evidence, not to persuade the court).

**III. GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN IN DISPUTE**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine issues of material fact remain in dispute. Under Rule 56 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, summary judgment is appropriate only if there is no genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *See Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co.*, 398 U.S. 144 (1970). Here, there are genuine disputes of fact regarding Bell Atlantic’s discriminatory intent and the pretextual nature of its proffered reasons for Bryant’s termination.

First, the temporal proximity between Bryant’s complaints about the Red Letter Day policy and his termination supports an inference of discriminatory intent. *See United States v. Georgia Power Co.*, 474 F.2d 906 (5th Cir. 1973) (holding that temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action may support an inference of discrimination). Second, Bell Atlantic’s failure to accommodate Bryant’s child care responsibilities, despite the CWA’s offer to assist, further supports an inference of discrimination. *See Phillips v. Martin Marietta Corp.*, 400 U.S. 542 (1971) (holding that an employer may not refuse to hire women with young children while hiring men with young children).

Finally, the inconsistent application of the Red Letter Day policy to Bryant, as compared to similarly situated non-African-American employees, creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding pretext. *See McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804-05 (holding that a plaintiff may prove pretext by showing that the employer’s proffered reasons were not applied consistently). Thus, summary judgment was inappropriate, and the case should proceed to trial.
