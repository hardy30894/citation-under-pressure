The question presented should be answered yes. Where the government seeks to convict a defendant of conspiracy within the limitations period, and the defendant has produced evidence of withdrawal before that period, due process requires the government to prove the defendant’s participation during the actionable time frame beyond a reasonable doubt. The burden is not a mere production burden; it is the ultimate burden of persuasion. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1895). Because conspiracy is a continuing offense, the government must prove that the defendant remained a member during the period charged or limitations is not satisfied. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401-02 (1912).

This Court has long recognized that withdrawal is not presumed from the mere passage of time or dissociation in fact; rather, it is an affirmative event that must be established by proof. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70. But once the defendant comes forward with evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, the government must still prove the continuing offense element that keeps the prosecution alive. In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216-17 (1946), the Court explained that conspiracy continues until accomplishment, abandonment, or withdrawal, and that the statute of limitations runs from the last overt act as to each conspirator only so long as membership continues. Fiswick thus treats continued membership as a fact the prosecution must establish to avoid the limitations bar. See also Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957) (statute of limitations “is designed to limit exposure to criminal prosecution to a certain fixed period of time”).

The government’s contrary position would improperly convert limitations into a defense the accused must negate. That is inconsistent with the structure of the criminal law and with the constitutional allocation of proof. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1895), the Court held that once the defendant has put a fact in issue, the burden may rest on the government to prove it beyond a reasonable doubt. And in Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469 (1943), the Court rejected statutory presumptions that relieve the government of proving the facts that establish guilt. A defendant’s evidence of withdrawal is not an admission of ongoing guilt; it is evidence that the government must overcome if it wishes to place the defendant inside the limitations period. The same principle is reflected in Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942), which emphasizes that conspiracy liability does not continue indefinitely after a defendant’s active participation ends.

The D.C. Circuit’s rule below also conflicts with the established treatment of limitations in criminal cases. Limitation statutes are designed to bar stale prosecutions and “promote repose by giving security and stability to human affairs.” See United States v. Mack, 295 U.S. 480, 489 (1935). Earlier decisions already recognized the same principle. See United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-80 (1872) (when facts appear to bring the prosecution within the statute, the government must allege and prove timeliness if the indictment does not negate the bar).

This Court should therefore hold that, once the accused offers evidence of withdrawal before the limitations window, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a conspirator during the relevant period. That rule follows from Davis, from the continuing-offense nature of conspiracy recognized in Hyde and Fiswick, and from the settled principle that the prosecution must prove every fact essential to criminal liability. The judgment below should be reversed.
