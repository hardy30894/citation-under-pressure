## ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (“OSH Act”) applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Louisiana’s territorial waters are a “State” within the Act, and Rig 52—a drilling barge engaged in an industrial operation—is a “workplace.” See *Contractors’ Ass’n of Eastern Pa. v. Secretary of Labor*, 442 F.2d 159, 172 (3d Cir. 1971) (Act covers “any employer” with employees in “any workplace”). The Coast Guard’s limited regulatory authority over uninspected vessels does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1).

**I. Section 4(b)(1) Preempts OSHA Only Where Another Agency Exercises Statutory Authority Over the Same Working Conditions.**

Section 4(b)(1) provides that the OSH Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards … affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision is not a blanket exemption for entire industries; it requires a specific, overlapping exercise of authority. *Donovan v. Red Star Marine Servs., Inc.*, 739 F.2d 774, 779 (2d Cir. 1984) (“[T]he test is whether the Coast Guard has exercised its statutory authority to regulate the particular working conditions at issue.”). Here, the Coast Guard could not have prescribed or enforced standards for the workplace conditions that caused the explosion because its authority over uninspected vessels is minimal and equipment-specific.

The Coast Guard regulates uninspected vessels only as to a narrow list of safety equipment: fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire traps, ventilation, and emergency alerting systems. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. It does not regulate drilling operations, well-control procedures, or the occupational safety of employees engaged in such industrial tasks. See *Donovan v. A.G. Amoco Shipping Co.*, 747 F.2d 383, 387 (6th Cir. 1984) (“The Coast Guard’s safety regulations for uninspected vessels … are concerned with equipment requirements, not with the working conditions of employees.”). Because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over the types of hazards cited by OSHA—improper well-blowout procedures and inadequate safety training—§ 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA’s citation. *Red Star Marine*, 739 F.2d at 780.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s Precedents Do Not Control Because They Involved Inspected Vessels and Seamen on Vessels in Navigation.**

The court of appeals relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). Both cases are inapposite. *Clary* held that OSHA regulations did not apply to working conditions of seamen on a “vessel in navigation.” 609 F.2d at 1122. The vessel in *Clary* was an inspected vessel subject to the Coast Guard’s comprehensive authority under Title 46. *Donovan v. Texaco* likewise involved an inspected vessel—a tanker—and reiterated the “vessel in navigation” standard. 720 F.2d at 826. Indeed, *Donovan v. Texaco* expressly noted that the Coast Guard “has exclusive authority *with respect to inspected vessels*.” *Id.* at 827 (emphasis added). Neither case addressed uninspected vessels like Rig 52.

Moreover, the employees on Rig 52 were not “seamen” within the meaning of the Jones Act. A seaman’s status requires a “substantial connection to a vessel in navigation.” *Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. 347, 368 (1995). Rig 52 was a stationary drilling barge, not a vessel engaged in navigation. Its workers were industrial oilfield employees, not maritime crewmembers. See *McDermott Inc. v. Boudreaux*, 679 F.2d 452, 456 (5th Cir. 1982) (drilling barge workers may not be seamen when barge is moored and used as a platform). OSHA jurisdiction therefore does not conflict with the Coast Guard’s traditional role over seamen.

**III. Applying OSHA to Uninspected Vessels Furthers Congressional Intent and Avoids a Regulatory Gap.**

Congress intended the OSH Act to fill gaps where other agencies have not regulated occupational safety. *Marshall v. Nichols Marine Ways, Inc.*, 595 F.2d 498, 500 (9th Cir. 1979) (upholding OSHA jurisdiction over ship-repair work because Coast Guard authority did not cover the conditions). The Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels leaves many hazards—such as the blowout and fire risks here—unaddressed. OSHA properly steps in to protect workers. *Red Star Marine*, 739 F.2d at 780 (“To hold that the mere existence of some Coast Guard regulation … precludes OSHA jurisdiction would create a regulatory vacuum.”).

Finally, the OSH Act’s broad remedial purpose compels a reading that does not lightly find preemption. *Contractors’ Ass’n*, 442 F.2d at 172. The Fifth Circuit’s decision extends *Clary* and *Donovan v. Texaco* far beyond their facts, creating an industry-wide exemption for uninspected vessels that Congress never intended. *Amoco Shipping*, 747 F.2d at 387. This court should reject that expansion and hold that OSHA retains jurisdiction over working conditions on uninspected vessels that the Coast Guard does not regulate.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the Administrative Law Judge and the Review Commission correctly found that OSHA had jurisdiction to issue the citation. The Fifth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed.
