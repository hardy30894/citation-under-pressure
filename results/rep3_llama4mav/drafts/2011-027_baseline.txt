The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court properly applied the test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes. The equal footing doctrine, which grants title to the beds of navigable rivers to the state upon admission to the Union, requires a determination of navigability at the time of statehood. Utah v. United States, 403 U.S. 9, 10 (1971); Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 87-88 (1922). The trial court's finding that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889 was supported by substantial evidence, including historical records and expert testimony.

PPL's argument that the trial court erred in relying on hearsay evidence is unpersuasive. The Montana Rules of Evidence permit the admission of certain types of hearsay evidence, including public records and reports, and ancient documents. M. R. Evid. 803(8), 901(8). The State's expert, Foley, provided a proper foundation for the admission of the historical records and documents she presented, and the trial court properly considered this evidence.

The United States Supreme Court has long recognized that the determination of navigability for title purposes is a question of fact that must be determined on a case-by-case basis. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 76-77 (1931). The trial court's determination that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is supported by the evidence and is consistent with the principles established by the Supreme Court.

Furthermore, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court's findings were not based on speculation or conjecture, but rather on a thorough review of the evidence presented. The court's decision is consistent with the principles of the equal footing doctrine and the relevant case law. See, e.g., Oregon v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co., 429 U.S. 363, 370-71 (1977); Packer v. Bird, 137 U.S. 661, 669 (1891).

In conclusion, the trial court properly applied the test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes, and the Montana Supreme Court correctly affirmed the trial court's findings.
