ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act exempts working conditions of employees with respect to which other federal agencies exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). To trigger this exemption, the other federal agency's authority must be exercised with respect to the same working conditions as OSHA, and its regulations must be comprehensive and pervasive. See Guss v. Utah Labor Relations Board, 353 U.S. 1, 10-11 (1957); Bethlehem Steel Co. v. NLRB, 120 F.2d 641, 645-46 (D.C. Cir. 1941).

The Coast Guard's authority to regulate uninspected vessels is limited, and its regulations do not comprehensively cover the working conditions of employees on these vessels. While the Coast Guard has authority to regulate certain aspects of maritime safety, such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation of engine and fuel tank compartments, 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 391a), its regulations are not as comprehensive as those applicable to inspected vessels. See Kelly v. Washington, 302 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1937); The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558, 574-75 (1874).

The Supreme Court has recognized that the Coast Guard's authority is not exclusive, and that other federal agencies may regulate aspects of maritime employment not covered by the Coast Guard. See Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47-48 (1942); NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 214-15 (1940); International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50, 52-53 (1926). In Southern S.S. Co., the Court held that the National Labor Relations Act applied to maritime employers, even though the Coast Guard had authority to regulate certain aspects of their operations. 316 U.S. at 47-48.

The Court of Appeals' reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced because those cases involved inspected vessels, which are subject to comprehensive Coast Guard regulation. See United States v. Andrews, 207 U.S. 229, 234-35 (1907); The Pennsylvania, 86 U.S. 125, 136-37 (1873). Uninspected vessels, on the other hand, are subject to a more limited regime of Coast Guard regulation. See United States v. Hana, 153 F.2d 536, 538-39 (7th Cir. 1946); The Silvia, 171 U.S. 462, 464-65 (1898).

In light of the limited scope of the Coast Guard's regulations applicable to uninspected vessels, OSHA's jurisdiction is not preempted. The term "working conditions" in section 4(b)(1) refers to specific conditions of employment, rather than the entire range of occupational safety and health issues. See Guss, 353 U.S. at 10-11; Bethlehem Steel Co., 120 F.2d at 645-46. Therefore, OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels, and the Court of Appeals' judgment should be reversed.
