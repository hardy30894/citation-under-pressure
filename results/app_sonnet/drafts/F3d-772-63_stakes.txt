ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Foley's Breach-of-Contract Claim Because He Plausibly Alleged Third-Party-Beneficiary Status, and HOLA Does Not Preempt That Claim.

A complaint survives dismissal if it states "enough facts to raise a right to relief above the speculative level," Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007), and pleads "factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged." Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). This Court must accept Foley's well-pleaded allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in his favor. Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014).

Foley is a member of Settlement Class B and was therefore entitled, under the express terms of the settlement agreement, to be considered first for HAMP and then for a MAP2R modification through the seven-step "waterfall." Massachusetts law permits a non-signatory to enforce a contract where the contracting parties clearly and definitely intended the third party to benefit from the promised performance. Rae v. Air-Speed, Inc., 386 Mass. 187, 194-95 (1982). Here, the settlement agreement was drafted for the very purpose of conferring modification consideration on borrowers like Foley; the "servicing commitments" requiring written explanations of denials and continued-consideration notices during foreclosure communications were adopted, in the agreement's own words, "to ensure that Borrowers are appropriately considered for a MAP2R Modification in a timely manner." Foley alleged that Wells Fargo never ran the waterfall, never explained a denial, and instead pushed him toward short sale and deed-in-lieu options while scheduling foreclosure. Those allegations plausibly state a breach.

Wells Fargo's preemption argument fares no better. The Home Owners' Loan Act ("HOLA") regulation on which Wells Fargo relies, 12 C.F.R. § 560.2, preempts state laws that regulate specified categories of lending activity, but it expressly exempts state "[c]ontract and commercial law," 12 C.F.R. § 560.2(c)(1), because such laws are of general applicability and only incidentally affect lending operations. See Silvas v. E*Trade Mortg. Corp., 514 F.3d 1001, 1005 (9th Cir. 2008) (describing the two-step § 560.2 analysis and the "incidental effect" exception). Foley's claim does not ask a court to impose a state-created lending requirement on Wells Fargo; it asks the court to hold Wells Fargo to a bargain it voluntarily struck in the settlement agreement to resolve its own Truth-in-Lending liability. Enforcing a bank's voluntarily assumed contractual undertaking is fundamentally different from enforcing a state-imposed regulatory duty, and the former is not preempted merely because it touches the same subject matter as HOLA. Cf. Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc., 505 U.S. 504, 526 (1992) (distinguishing between preempted state-imposed duties and non-preempted obligations that a party "voluntarily undertaken" itself). The district court should not have dismissed this claim.

II. Foley Stated a Claim Under Chapter 93A.

Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A, § 2(a), prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce." The statute's unfairness standard asks "whether the practice is within at least the penumbra of some common-law, statutory, or other established concept of unfairness . . . whether it is immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous." PMP Assocs., Inc. v. Globe Newspaper Co., 366 Mass. 593, 596 (1975).

Foley alleged that Wells Fargo repeatedly told him for seven months that HAMP was his only option, delayed sending a HAMP application, then claimed it never received the completed application he had promptly returned, ignored his subsequent calls to its own "Home Preservation Specialist," and instead mailed him short-sale and deed-in-lieu materials — all while it was contractually obligated to run him through the MAP2R waterfall and to notify him in writing of any denial. These are not mere allegations of negligent paperwork; they plausibly describe a course of conduct that is unscrupulous within the meaning of chapter 93A, particularly because Wells Fargo's own settlement obligations gave it specific, enforceable duties toward Foley that it disregarded while simultaneously moving forward with foreclosure. The district court's contrary conclusion improperly resolved factual disputes about Wells Fargo's conduct at the pleading stage, contrary to Twombly and Iqbal.

III. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Foley's Chapter 244 Claim Because Wells Fargo Failed to Strictly Comply with Foreclosure Procedure.

Massachusetts imposes a strict-compliance requirement on parties exercising a statutory power of sale. "One who sells under a power [of sale] must follow strictly its terms. If he fails to do so there is no valid execution of the power, and the sale is wholly void." U.S. Bank Nat'l Ass'n v. Ibanez, 458 Mass. 637, 646-47 (2011) (quoting Moore v. Dick, 187 Mass. 207, 211 (1904)). The settlement agreement's servicing commitments — requiring notice of continued modification consideration in any foreclosure-related communication — were incorporated into the terms governing how Wells Fargo could permissibly proceed toward foreclosure on Settlement Class B members' homes. Foley alleged Wells Fargo scheduled foreclosure without the required notices and without completing the waterfall analysis to which he was entitled. Under Ibanez, that failure to strictly adhere to the applicable procedural prerequisites renders any resulting foreclosure infirm, and Foley's chapter 244 claim challenging that noncompliance should have survived dismissal.

For these reasons, the judgment of dismissal should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
