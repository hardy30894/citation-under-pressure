**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act Adopts State Law Only When Necessary to Fill a Gap in Federal Law.**

The text, structure, and purpose of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), require that state law apply as surrogate federal law only when federal law is absent or incomplete. The Act provides that state law is adopted “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with…other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting qualifier, not an open-ended invitation to supplement federal law whenever state law pertains to the subject matter. This Court has long recognized that OCSLA incorporates state law only to fill gaps in federal law. See *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (stating that OCSLA was designed to “define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” and that state law fills the interstices where federal law is silent).

The legislative history confirms this gap-filling purpose. Congress expressly feared that “federal law, standing alone, would be inadequate because it ‘was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.’” *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 357 (quoting 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953)). Accordingly, OCSLA borrowed state law to provide a comprehensive legal regime for the Outer Continental Shelf, but only where federal law itself did not already cover the field. This Court in *Huson v. Chevron Oil Co.*, 394 U.S. 634, 649 (1969), explained that OCSLA’s incorporation of state law was “designed to fill the gaps in federal law” and that “the applicability of state law” depends on whether “there is a void in federal law that must be filled.”

**II. The Fair Labor Standards Act Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Wage and Hour Law.**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”), 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, provides a comprehensive regulatory scheme governing minimum wage, overtime, and meal periods. This Court has repeatedly described the FLSA as a “comprehensive” and “carefully calibrated” statute. *Powell v. United States Cartridge Co.*, 339 U.S. 497, 507 (1950) (noting that “the FLSA establishes a comprehensive scheme”); *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945) (stating that the FLSA was “designed to eliminate” substandard working conditions “through a comprehensive scheme”). Because the FLSA addresses the very claims Newton brings—minimum wage, overtime, and meal periods—there is no gap for California law to fill. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), which permits more protective state law, does not create a gap; it merely preserves concurrent state enforcement for employers already subject to state law. That savings clause does not itself *adopt* state law on the OCS.

The Fifth Circuit’s test in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), correctly requires a “significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be assimilated. That test flows directly from OCSLA’s design. The Ninth Circuit erred by discarding this gap-filling requirement. State law is not “applicable” under OCSLA when federal law already governs the precise conduct at issue. *See Union Oil Co. v. Smoot*, 445 F.2d 1165, 1170 (5th Cir. 1969) (state law applies only “where federal law is not complete or fails to provide a rule of decision”). Newton’s meal-period and overtime claims fall squarely within the FLSA’s ambit.

**III. Applying State Law When Federal Law Already Exists Contravenes OCSLA’s Text and This Court’s Precedent.**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule—that state law may apply whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—turns OCSLA into an automatic adoption of the adjacent state’s entire legal code, limited only by direct conflict. That interpretation reads the phrase “to the extent that” out of the statute. This Court has never approved such a capacious reading. In *Rodrigue*, the Court applied Louisiana wrongful-death law precisely because federal death-on-the-high-seas law did *not* apply to fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. In *Huson*, the Court applied Louisiana’s statute of limitations because federal law “provided no rule of decision” on limitations. 394 U.S. at 649. Both cases involved gaps. Neither case adopted state law where federal law covered the subject.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit’s approach conflicts with the settled principle that OCSLA treats platforms as “federal enclaves” akin to land within United States territory. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366. On a federal enclave, state law applies only if Congress has adopted it. *See James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 148 (1937) (federal enclave subject to exclusive federal jurisdiction unless state law is expressly adopted). The FLSA governs the OCS as if it were “an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). No further state supplementation is needed.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Creates a Circuit Split and Encourages Forum Shopping.**

The Fifth Circuit has consistently held that state law does not apply to wage and hour disputes on the OCS when the FLSA already governs. *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036; *Smoot*, 445 F.2d at 1170. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule disadvantages employers operating off the California coast, who must now comply with California’s uniquely protective wage-and-hour regime even though the FLSA already applies. This Court should reject a rule that allows the substantive law to vary by adjacent state without any statutory mandate.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
