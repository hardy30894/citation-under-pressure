**ARGUMENT**

I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE DOES NOT VIOLATE DUE PROCESS

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding right to introduce expert evidence of mental illness to negate mens rea, independent of an insanity defense. *See Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 446 (1992) (holding that due process does not require states to adopt particular procedures for asserting insanity defenses). Arizona’s rule is a permissible exercise of its authority to define the scope of defenses and evidentiary rules in its criminal justice system. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 202 (1977) (upholding a state’s allocation of burdens of proof in criminal cases).

A defendant’s constitutional rights are not violated when a state confines expert testimony about mental illness to the insanity defense. *Cf. Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (upholding a state’s requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt). The Due Process Clause does not mandate that mental health evidence be admissible for any purpose other than that chosen by the state. *See Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 67-68 (1991) (holding that due process does not require states to admit evidence favorable to the defense unless it is material to guilt or innocence under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963)).

II. ARIZONA’S RULE IS RATIONALLY RELATED TO LEGITIMATE STATE INTERESTS

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence serves the state’s legitimate interests in maintaining clear lines between defenses and avoiding confusion among jurors. *See Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 234 (1987) (upholding a state’s allocation of the burden of proof on self-defense). The state has a substantial interest in ensuring that the insanity defense remains distinct and is not conflated with mens rea arguments. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 752 (2006) (recognizing Arizona’s interest in preventing the insanity defense from subsuming the entire guilt phase).

The rule is not arbitrary or irrational. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 521 (1995) (holding that due process requires that a defendant’s reasonable-doubt burden be tied to an element of the offense). Arizona’s approach prevents defendants from circumventing the state’s insanity framework by using mental health evidence to indirectly negate mens rea. *See Shannon v. United States*, 512 U.S. 573, 583 (1994) (upholding a federal statute that confined mental health evidence to the insanity defense).

III. THE ARIZONA COURTS’ INTERPRETATION OF STATE LAW IS BINDING ON THIS COURT

This Court lacks jurisdiction to review the Arizona courts’ interpretation of state law. *See Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 685 n.1 (1975) (declining to review state law interpretations). The Arizona Supreme Court’s construction of its own statutes and rules is entitled to deference. *See Johnson v. Fankell*, 520 U.S. 911, 916 (1997) (holding that federal courts must defer to state courts’ interpretations of state law).

IV. PETITIONER’S PROPOSED RULE WOULD UNDULY BURDEN THE STATES

Adopting Petitioner’s argument would require states to permit mental health evidence to negate mens rea, disrupting their carefully crafted insanity frameworks. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535-36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (recognizing the state’s interest in regulating the insanity defense). Such a rule would invite collateral attacks on state criminal procedures and undermine the finality of judgments. *See Barefoot v. Estelle*, 463 U.S. 880, 893 (1983) (emphasizing the importance of finality in criminal proceedings).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
