**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION BY ADDING UNAUTHORIZED QUALIFICATIONS FOR MEMBERS OF CONGRESS**

The Constitution of the United States establishes the exclusive qualifications for membership in the United States Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that to be a Representative, a person must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for seven Years, and [must] have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for at the Time of his Election.” Article I, Section 3, Clause 1 similarly specifies that a Senator must be “at least thirty Years of Age, a Citizen of the United States for nine Years, and [must] have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for at the Time of his Election.” These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the sole constitutional standards for eligibility to serve in Congress.

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by barring individuals who have served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, imposes a new and additional qualification not found in the Constitution. This is not a mere restriction on eligibility to hold office after election—it is a direct, constitutionally impermissible addition to the qualifications for office. As this Court held in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 790 (1995), “the Constitution’s qualifications clauses are exclusive.” The Court emphasized that “the Framers did not leave open the possibility that the States could add to the qualifications for federal office.” Id. at 791. To allow states to impose term limits would “undermine the uniformity of federal qualifications” and “threaten the structural integrity of the federal system.” Id. at 792.

The Arkansas Amendment does not merely regulate the conduct of elections—it seeks to disqualify candidates based on their past service, a criterion entirely absent from the Constitution. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that states may impose additional qualifications. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), the Court stated that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no State can pass a law which contradicts or adds to the qualifications for federal office.” Similarly, in *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1, 45 (1849), the Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no State can interfere with the qualifications prescribed by the Constitution for the members of Congress.”

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the Constitution’s exclusive qualifications clauses. The fact that the provision is framed as a ballot-access rule does not alter its substance. As the Court explained in *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788 (1983), “a state may not impose qualifications for federal office that are not found in the Constitution.” The mere label of “ballot access” cannot transform a disqualifying criterion into a permissible election regulation. The effect is the same: a candidate is barred from the ballot based on a condition not authorized by the Constitution.

The dissent’s argument that the amendment is merely a “ballot-access” rule is legally untenable. The Supreme Court has long rejected the idea that states may use procedural mechanisms to circumvent constitutional limits on qualifications. In *Morgan v. Virginia*, 328 U.S. 373, 376 (1946), the Court held that “a state cannot use procedural rules to evade constitutional mandates.” The same principle applies here: Arkansas cannot use the guise of ballot access to impose a disqualifying term limit that the Constitution does not permit.

**II. THE ARKANSAS AMENDMENT IS NOT SEVERABLE FROM ITS UNCONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the severability clause in Amendment 73 does not save the remainder of the amendment. The test for severability is whether the legislature or people would have enacted the remaining provisions without the unconstitutional ones. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 749, 375 S.W.2d 809, 811 (1964). In this case, the record shows that the amendment was presented to voters as a single, unified package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The preamble spoke of “elected officials who remain in office too long,” and Section 6 stated that the amendment applied to “all persons thereafter seeking election to the offices specified in this Amendment”—a phrase that includes both state and federal offices.

The sponsors of the amendment deliberately bundled state and federal term limits into one measure. The voters were not given a choice between limiting state officials and limiting federal officials. The amendment was marketed as a single reform: “term limits for all elected officials.” This is not a case where the unconstitutional provision could be cleanly separated from the rest. As the Court recognized in *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 420, 316 S.W.2d 185, 188 (1958), “when a constitutional amendment is presented as a single package, and the voters are not given a clear choice, the severability clause cannot be invoked to save the remainder.”

The dissent’s assertion that the amendment could have passed without Section 3 is speculative. There is no evidence that the voters would have approved term limits for state officials if they had known that federal term limits were being rejected. The sponsors created the confusion by combining the two issues. The Court should not allow them to benefit from the very deception they engineered.
