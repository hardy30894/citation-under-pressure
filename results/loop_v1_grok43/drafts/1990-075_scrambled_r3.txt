ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act supplies an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority. In J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), the Court held that Congress may delegate authority when it lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized * * * is directed to conform.” The Act meets this standard by requiring the Attorney General to consider the history, pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse together with the risk to public health before finding an imminent hazard. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988).

This guidance is comparable to that sustained in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), which authorized the President to impose duties once he ascertained the existence of reciprocal trade concessions. Similar principles were applied in In re Kollock, 165 U.S. 526, 533 (1897), United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892), and St. Louis, Iron Mountain & Southern Railway Co. v. Taylor, 210 U.S. 281, 287 (1908).

The Court has invalidated delegations only where the statute did not declare any policy or establish any criterion to govern the executive’s course, or supplied no standards whatever. Here the enumerated factors and the imminent-hazard limitation supply the missing criteria.

Congress may also authorize the Attorney General to subdelegate. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court approved the transfer of functions vested in the President to subordinate officers. See also United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892), recognizing that Congress may commit the details of execution to executive officers. Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364, 386 (1907), confirms that administrative officers may promulgate rules carrying criminal consequences when acting within statutory bounds.

The temporary scheduling provisions therefore rest on a valid delegation of authority that the Attorney General lawfully transmitted to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.
