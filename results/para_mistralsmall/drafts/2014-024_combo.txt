**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Anthony Elonis respectfully submits that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for “transmit[ting] in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another” requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. The First Amendment’s protection of free speech would be illusory if the government could punish mere utterances that a jury, applying an objective standard, deems threatening, regardless of the speaker’s intent. This Court’s precedents confirm that the constitutional prohibition on content-based restrictions demands a showing of scienter.

The Court has long held that statutes regulating speech must require proof of the speaker’s intent to bring about a proscribed effect. In *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), the Court struck down a state law banning the display of a red flag “as a sign, symbol or emblem of opposition to organized government” because it punished speech without regard to the speaker’s intent. The Court explained that the law “would reach words and conduct which, under an equally plausible construction, would have no such tendency” and thus impermissibly chilled protected speech. *Id.* at 369. Similarly, in *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242 (1937), the Court invalidated a conviction under a statute punishing “attempts to incite insurrection,” holding that the statute could not constitutionally apply to mere advocacy without proof that the defendant intended to incite imminent lawless action. *Id.* at 263-64.

The same principle governs the “true threat” exception to First Amendment protection. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1 (1949), the Court cautioned that speech may not be punished merely because it stirs people to anger or invites dispute; rather, the government must demonstrate a “clear and present danger” that the speech is intended to provoke unlawful conduct. *Id.* at 4. This requirement of scienter ensures that speech is not suppressed based on the reaction of the audience alone. *See also Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 320 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) (“The alternative is to leave the uncontrolled power in the hands of authorities to silence speakers when they merely disagree with or dislike what is said.”).

The Court’s decision in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), underscores that the First Amendment tolerates content-based restrictions only when the speech falls within a historically unprotected category, such as “fighting words” or threats. But even in those contexts, the Court has required proof of intent. In *Chaplinsky*, the Court upheld a conviction for using “offensive, derisive or annoying” words in a public place, but only because the statute targeted words “which by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace.” *Id.* at 572. The Court emphasized that the statute punished words “of such slight social value as a step to truth that any benefit that may be derived from them is clearly outweighed by the social interest in order and morality.” *Id.* The decision thus rested on the inherently provocative nature of the words, not their mere tendency to offend.

This Court’s subsequent decisions have reinforced the requirement of scienter in speech prosecutions. In *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252 (1941), the Court held that criticism of a pending judicial proceeding could not be punished as contempt unless the speaker intended to obstruct the administration of justice. The Court explained that “freedom of utterance is the matrix, the indispensable condition, of nearly every other form of freedom.” *Id.* at 270. Similarly, in *Pennekamp v. Florida*, 328 U.S. 331 (1946), the Court reversed a contempt conviction for newspaper criticism of a state court, holding that the First Amendment barred punishment unless the speaker acted with “actual malice”—a term the Court borrowed from defamation law to describe knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. *Id.* at 335.

The Court’s decision in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), is particularly instructive. There, the Court reversed a conviction under a statute prohibiting threats against the President, holding that the defendant’s statement at a political rally—“If they ever make me carry a rifle, the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.”—was protected hyperbole. The Court explained that the statute required proof that the defendant intended to threaten the President, not merely that a reasonable person would construe the statement as threatening. *Id.* at 707-08. The Court noted that “[t]aken in context, and regarding the expressly conditional nature of the statement and the reaction of the listeners, we do not think the statute can constitutionally be applied to this kind of statement.” *Id.* at 708.

The Court’s precedents thus establish that the First Amendment prohibits the punishment of speech unless the government proves the speaker acted with a culpable mental state. This principle applies with equal force to the “true threat” exception. As the Court held in *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), the government may not suppress speech based on its tendency to cause harm unless the speaker intended to bring about that harm. *Id.* at 52. The same rule governs threats. In *Debs v. United States*, 249 U.S. 211 (1919), the Court upheld a conviction under the Espionage Act for obstructing military recruitment, but only because the defendant intended to interfere with the war effort. *Id.* at 216-17.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. The Third Circuit held that a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat, without requiring proof of the speaker’s subjective intent. *Id.* at 557. But this objective standard permits punishment of speech based solely on its tendency to cause fear, without regard to the speaker’s intent. As the Court held in *Stromberg*, such a standard impermissibly chills protected speech. *See also Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson*, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931) (“The fact that the liberty of the press may be abused by miscreant purveyors of scandal does not make any less necessary the immunity of the press from previous restraint.”).

The government’s reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), is misplaced. In *Black*, the Court upheld a Virginia statute that criminalized cross burning with the intent to intimidate, but the statute included an evidentiary presumption that all cross burnings were done with intent to intimidate. The Court struck down the presumption as unconstitutional but left open the question whether a subjective intent standard is required. *Id.* at 367-68. The Court’s decision in *Black* thus does not resolve the question presented here.

In sum, the First Amendment requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. The Third Circuit’s objective standard violates this principle and must be reversed.
