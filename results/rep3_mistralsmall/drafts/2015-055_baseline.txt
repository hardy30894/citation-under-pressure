**ARGUMENT**

I. **Johnson’s Rule Regarding the Residual Clause Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion). The Court has further clarified that a rule applies retroactively if it is “substantive,” meaning it narrows the scope of a criminal statute or otherwise alters the range of conduct or the class of persons punishable under the statute. *Id.* at 311. The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) is unconstitutionally vague—is a substantive rule that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review.

In *Johnson*, the Supreme Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA, which defined a “violent felony” as conduct that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it proscribed and invited arbitrary enforcement. *Id.* at 2556-57. The Court concluded that the residual clause violated the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment because it was impermissibly vague. *Id.* at 2557. This holding was substantive in nature, as it narrowed the scope of the ACCA by invalidating a key provision that had previously expanded the range of predicate offenses. *See Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1263 (2016) (per curiam) (noting that *Johnson* “narrowed the scope of the ACCA’s ‘violent felony’ definition”).

The Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply *Johnson* retroactively to Welch’s case. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a new constitutional rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it is substantive or “watershed” in nature. *See, e.g., Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351-52 (2004); *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998). The rule in *Johnson* is substantive because it invalidated the residual clause of the ACCA, thereby narrowing the statute’s reach. *See Welch*, 136 S. Ct. at 1263. Accordingly, the rule applies retroactively to Welch’s case, and the Eleventh Circuit erred in affirming his sentence under the residual clause.

II. **Welch’s Florida Robbery Conviction Does Not Qualify as a Violent Felony Under the ACCA**

Even if *Johnson* did not apply retroactively, Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA’s elements clause. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as a crime that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). The Supreme Court has held that this clause requires a “forceful” taking, meaning that the use of physical force must be an element of the offense. *See Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010) (plurality opinion) (“The phrase ‘physical force’ means violent force—that is, force capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person.”).

Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not satisfy this standard. Under Florida law, robbery is defined as the taking of property from a person or their custody “with intent to permanently or temporarily deprive” the person of the property, “when in the course of the taking there is the use of force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1). The Florida Supreme Court has held that the force required for robbery must be “more than the force necessary to remove the property from the person.” *Robinson v. State*, 692 So. 2d 883, 886 (Fla. 1997). However, the Florida courts have also recognized that mere snatching—such as grabbing an item from a person’s hand—can constitute robbery if the victim is aware of the taking. *See, e.g., Andre v. State*, 431 So. 2d 1042, 1043 (Fla. 5th Dist. Ct. App. 1983) (“The act of ‘snatching’ ... money from another’s hands is force and that force will support a robbery conviction.”).

This precedent demonstrates that Florida robbery does not require the use of violent force as an element of the offense. Instead, it can be satisfied by conduct that does not involve force capable of causing physical pain or injury. *See Johnson*, 559 U.S. at 140. Therefore, Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA’s elements clause.

III. **The Residual Clause Is Unconstitutionally Vague and Cannot Be Applied to Welch’s Conviction**

Even if Welch’s Florida robbery conviction qualified as a violent felony under the elements clause, the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague and cannot be applied to his case. In *Johnson*, the Supreme Court held that the residual clause failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it proscribed and invited arbitrary enforcement. 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Court concluded that the residual clause violated the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment because it was impermissibly vague. *Id.* at 2557.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in applying the residual clause to Welch’s conviction. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that vague criminal laws violate the Due Process Clause because they fail to provide fair notice of the conduct they proscribe and invite arbitrary enforcement. *See, e.g., Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357-58 (1983); *Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972). The residual clause of the ACCA suffers from these same infirmities. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. Therefore, the Eleventh Circuit erred in applying the residual clause to Welch’s conviction.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed, and Welch’s sentence should be vacated.
