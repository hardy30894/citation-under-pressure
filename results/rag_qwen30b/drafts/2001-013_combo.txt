**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the MR. BELDON, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act. That holding rests on a misreading of the statutory framework and a misapplication of precedent. The OSH Act’s plain language, the historical division of federal regulatory authority, and binding Supreme Court precedent confirm that OSHA may regulate working conditions on uninspected vessels so long as those vessels are engaged in employment within a state and are not subject to exclusive federal regulation under a different statutory scheme.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” This exception is narrow and must be construed strictly. It does not bar OSHA from regulating working conditions simply because another federal agency has authority over maritime safety; it applies only when that agency has *exclusive* statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards in a given field. The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard has *exclusive* authority over the working conditions of seamen on all vessels in navigation—inspected or not—contradicts both the text of the OSH Act and the settled jurisprudence of this Court.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal agencies may regulate different aspects of maritime operations without conflict, so long as their authority does not overlap in a way that undermines the purpose of the respective statutes. In *Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit*, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), the Court held that state law may require safety equipment on vessels even when that equipment has been approved by the Coast Guard. The Court emphasized that “state law may not change the federal standards for the structure and equipment of vessels,” but that “state rules which change the federal standards...would meet a different fate.” Id. at 447. The Court further noted that “state law may require additional safety measures, so long as they do not conflict with federal standards.” Id. This principle is directly applicable here: the Coast Guard may regulate the *structure and equipment* of vessels, but OSHA may regulate the *working conditions* of employees on those vessels, so long as the standards do not conflict. The Fifth Circuit’s categorical exclusion of OSHA from regulating uninspected vessels ignores this critical distinction.

Moreover, the Court has consistently upheld the principle that federal agencies may exercise concurrent jurisdiction over maritime matters when their respective mandates are not mutually exclusive. In *Street v. Shipowners’ Ass’n of the Pacific Coast*, 263 U.S. 334 (1923), the Court affirmed that federal maritime law and state labor law could coexist in the regulation of seamen’s employment. The Court stated that “the rights of seamen are not confined to the maritime law, but may be protected by the laws of the states.” Id. at 340. This recognition of concurrent jurisdiction is reinforced by *Maul v. United States*, 274 U.S. 501 (1927), where the Court upheld the Coast Guard’s authority to seize a vessel on the high seas, even beyond the three-mile limit, when it was engaged in illegal activity. The Court held that “the authority of the Coast Guard to enforce the laws of the United States upon the high seas is not limited to the territorial waters of the United States.” Id. at 505. This expansive view of federal maritime enforcement authority does not preclude other federal agencies from regulating employment conditions on vessels operating in navigable waters.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Those decisions were based on the assumption that the Coast Guard had exclusive authority over all working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation. But the Supreme Court has never held that the Coast Guard has exclusive authority over *all* working conditions of seamen. In *Roper v. United States*, 368 U.S. 20 (1961), the Court held that a vessel not in navigation—though still moored and used for work—was not subject to the warranty of seaworthiness. The Court emphasized that “a vessel is not in navigation when it is moored and not engaged in the movement of persons or property.” Id. at 24. This distinction is critical: the MR. BELDON was not in navigation when the explosion occurred; it was engaged in drilling operations in a fixed location in Louisiana’s territorial waters. Thus, the Coast Guard’s authority over “vessels in navigation” does not extend to the working conditions of employees on a stationary drilling barge.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the federal government may regulate employment conditions on vessels even when those vessels are not subject to comprehensive Coast Guard inspection. In *Sherlock v. Alling*, 93 U.S. 99 (1876), the Court held that state law could impose liability for personal torts resulting in death, even when the tort occurred on navigable waters. The Court rejected the argument that such liability would burden commerce, stating that “the States may regulate personal torts, without interfering with the exclusive regulation of commerce vested in Congress.” Id. at 104. This principle applies with equal force to OSHA’s regulation of working conditions: the Act does not burden commerce; it protects workers.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels is also inconsistent with *United States v. Louisiana*, 394 U.S. 11 (1969), which reaffirmed that the United States has exclusive jurisdiction over its territorial waters. The Court held that “the United States has exclusive jurisdiction over its territorial waters, including the right to enforce its laws.” Id. at 22. This jurisdiction includes the authority to regulate employment conditions on vessels operating within those waters, regardless of whether the vessel is inspected.
