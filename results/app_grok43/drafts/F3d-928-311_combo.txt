ARGUMENT

The district court erred in designating Furlow an armed career criminal and career offender. Both enhancements require predicate convictions whose elements match the ACCA and Guidelines definitions exactly. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2); USSG § 4B1.2. Because the South Carolina distribution offense and Georgia arson convictions fail that test, the designations must be vacated.

Criminal statutes are strictly construed. United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (“The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself.”). Any ambiguity is resolved in the defendant’s favor. Bell v. United States, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955) (“When Congress leaves to the Judiciary the task of imputing to Congress an undeclared will, the ambiguity should be resolved in favor of lenity.”). The same principle governs the ACCA’s “involving” language and the Guidelines’ parallel terms. McBoyle v. United States, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (“Although it is not likely that a criminal will carefully consider the text of the law before he murders or steals, it is reasonable that a fair warning should be given.”).

The South Carolina statute reaches conduct outside the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” definition because it criminalizes offers to distribute. Under the categorical approach required by strict construction, such breadth precludes use of the conviction. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (statutes “may not be extended beyond their plain import”). The same mismatch disqualifies the offense under the Guidelines’ “controlled substance offense” definition.

Georgia first-degree arson likewise fails. At common law, arson required the burning of a dwelling house. The Georgia statute encompasses uninhabited structures. Because the ACCA and Guidelines enumerate “arson,” they incorporate the common-law meaning. United States v. Lacher, 134 U.S. 624, 628 (1890) (“Words having a well-known common-law meaning are presumed to be used in that sense.”). The mismatch is fatal. United States v. Brewer, 139 U.S. 278, 288 (1891) (penal statutes “are not to be strained beyond their natural meaning”).

The rule of lenity independently bars both enhancements. Harrison v. Vose, 50 U.S. (9 How.) 372, 378 (1850) (“In the construction of penal statutes, the rule is that the defendant is entitled to the benefit of any reasonable doubt.”). United States v. Hartwell, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 396 (1867) (same). Ex parte Bain, 121 U.S. 1, 13 (1887) (courts may not enlarge criminal liability by construction). Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 462 (1892) (same). These convictions therefore supply no predicates.

The sentence must be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing without the enhancements.
