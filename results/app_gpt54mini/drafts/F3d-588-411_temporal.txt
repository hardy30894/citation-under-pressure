**Argument**

The district court’s supplemental definition of “shielding” was error. A criminal statute may not be applied so expansively that it gives jurors “a broad and undefined discretion” to supply the operative meaning of the offense. *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). That concern is especially acute where, as here, the Government relied on a statutory term that Congress did not further define. The Seventh Circuit should construe § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) in light of the rule that penal laws must give fair warning of the conduct they reach. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); *Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 515–16 (1948).

The court’s instruction was too broad because it allowed conviction on proof of routine employment-related conduct rather than concealment directed at evading detection. This Court has long held that criminal liability cannot rest on a definition that sweeps in ordinary conduct without a clear limiting principle. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101–03 (1945) (plurality opinion) (requiring narrow construction to avoid unconstitutional vagueness). The word “shielding,” if given a loose common-sense meaning, could encompass any act that makes an employee less visible to authorities: hiring, housing, transporting, paying in cash, or failing to keep records. But Congress did not criminalize mere employment of unauthorized workers under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii); it separately enacted the hiring offense in § 1324a. The district court’s instruction blurred that statutory distinction and permitted the jury to convict Ye for conduct Congress placed in a different misdemeanor provision. See *Dunn v. United States*, 284 U.S. 390, 393 (1932) (criminal statutes should not be extended by implication beyond their plain scope).

The instruction was also inconsistent with the established principle that when Congress uses terms with established legal content, courts should not enlarge them beyond their ordinary legal import. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). “Shielding from detection” naturally denotes affirmative acts taken to conceal a person from discovery, not merely the provision of employment, housing, or transportation. The evidence here showed that Ye hired workers, paid them in cash, supplied housing, and failed to complete paperwork. Those acts may support the hiring count, but they do not by themselves establish “shielding” unless the Government proves an intent to hide the workers from detection. A supplemental instruction that equated shielding with broad assistance to undocumented employees relieved the Government of that burden.

This Court’s decision in *United States v. Spector*, 343 U.S. 169, 172–73 (1952), confirms the need for restraint where a statutory term implicates serious criminal sanctions and uncertain boundaries. Courts should avoid constructions that make “men of common intelligence . . . necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.” *Connally*, 269 U.S. at 391. Ye objected to any definition, but once the district court chose to define the term, it had to do so narrowly and in a manner that preserved the statute’s requirement of purposeful concealment. Instead, the instruction invited the jury to treat normal employer conduct as “shielding,” which is far broader than the statute permits.

The evidence was likewise insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Ye acted with the specific intent to prevent the Government from detecting his employees. In reviewing sufficiency, the question is whether the record contains evidence from which a rational juror could find the essential elements proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942). Speculation cannot substitute for proof of intent. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467–68 (1943).

Here, the proof showed that Ye knew some employees were unauthorized and that he employed them, paid them in cash, and provided housing. But those facts are equally consistent with evasion of ordinary employment regulations, not concealment from immigration detection. The Government pointed to Ye’s failure to keep I-9 forms and payroll records, but failure to maintain records is addressed by the hiring statute and does not alone prove a purpose to hide workers from the authorities. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 270–71. Likewise, Ye’s suggestion that workers obtain false documents may show unlawful hiring practices, but it does not necessarily establish that he intended to prevent federal detection of their presence. The record contains no evidence that Ye used aliases, false identities, hidden compartments, secret locations, or other traditional indicia of concealment. Compare *United States v. Romano*, 382 U.S. 136, 141–42 (1965) (rejecting conviction where evidence did not sufficiently connect defendant to the prohibited conduct).

The Government’s proof therefore established, at most, that Ye employed illegal aliens and failed to comply with documentation requirements. That is not enough to sustain a conviction for “concealing, harboring, or shielding from detection” under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). Because the jury was instructed under an overbroad definition and the evidence did not show the requisite intent to obstruct detection, Ye’s conviction on Count One should be reversed.
