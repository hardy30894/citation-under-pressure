The Massachusetts statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation. It applies evenhandedly to all speakers on public sidewalks. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) ("The right of assembly is not absolute."). The measure is content-neutral because its prohibitions turn on location rather than the substance of any message. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940). Such regulations are permissible when they serve significant governmental interests and are narrowly drawn. Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949).

The statute advances the State's interests in protecting public safety, preventing congestion, and ensuring unimpeded access to medical facilities. These interests are substantial. Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 160 (1939). The fixed buffer zones are drawn to reach only the immediate vicinity of entrances, exits, and driveways, thereby avoiding unnecessary suppression of speech. Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 554 (1965).

The regulation leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. Speakers may still converse at normal volume from beyond the marked zones, display signs, distribute literature, and pray audibly. Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953). The exemptions for clinic employees and passersby do not introduce viewpoint discrimination; they merely accommodate ordinary pedestrian and emergency traffic. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 571 (1942).

The statute is not a prior restraint. It imposes no licensing requirement and no official discretion to suppress particular messages in advance. Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931). Any incidental burden on expression is no greater than necessary to achieve the State's legitimate ends. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368 (1931).

The law therefore satisfies every requirement for a constitutional time, place, and manner restriction. Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
