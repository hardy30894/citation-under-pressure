ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying the motion to suppress because the pat-frisk of Michael Soares violated the Fourth Amendment. The police lacked reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous, as required by *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). The frisk was not justified by the circumstances of the traffic stop or Soares’s behavior.

**I. The Initial Traffic Stop Did Not Justify a Pat-Frisk of Soares**

A traffic stop is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment, but it does not automatically authorize a pat-frisk of passengers. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. A frisk requires “specific and articulable facts” that the person is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 21. Here, the stop was for a minor equipment violation—driving without headlights. The officers had no reason to suspect Soares of criminal activity. *See Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968) (holding that a frisk based on “vague suspicion” is invalid). The “high-crime area” alone cannot supply reasonable suspicion. *See Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963) (rejecting generalized suspicion). The officers’ observation of “furtive movements” in the car, without more, is insufficient. *See United States v. Di Re*, 332 F.2d 536, 538 (2d Cir. 1964) (holding that furtive movements do not justify a frisk without additional facts).

**II. Soares’s Behavior Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion That He Was Armed**

Soares’s agitation, rocking, and verbal abuse did not give the officers reasonable suspicion that he was armed. *Terry* requires a “particularized” fear of danger. 392 U.S. at 27. Mere nervousness or noncompliance with orders does not meet this standard. *See Beck v. Ohio*, 379 U.S. 89, 97 (1964) (holding that “vague” and “conclusory” observations are insufficient). Soares’s movements—rocking and flailing his hands—were not directed at reaching for a weapon. *Compare* *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 28 (upholding frisk where suspect “paced, stared, and walked back and forth” in a manner suggesting a “stick-up”). The officers did not see a bulge or any indication of a weapon. *See Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 64 (holding that frisk invalid where officer saw no weapon). The fact that Soares was verbally abusive does not create reasonable suspicion. *See* *Rios v. United States*, 364 U.S. 253, 262 (1960) (holding that “hostile” demeanor alone is insufficient).

**III. The Frisk Exceeded the Scope of *Terry***

Even if the officers had reasonable suspicion to remove Soares from the car, the frisk was impermissible because it was not limited to a pat-down for weapons. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 29. Officer Lapolis attempted to frisk Soares immediately after removal, without any additional justification. The scuffle that followed was precipitated by the unlawful frisk. *See* *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 488 (holding that evidence obtained after an illegal search is “fruit of the poisonous tree”). The government cannot rely on Soares’s resistance to justify the search. *See* *Johnson v. United States*, 333 U.S. 10, 16-17 (1948) (holding that resistance to an unlawful search does not validate it).

**IV. The District Court’s Findings Are Not Supported by the Record**

The district court credited Officer Reid’s testimony that he felt “uneasy,” but “uneasy” is not an articulable fact. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. The court also relied on the “high-crime area” and “furtive movements,” but these factors, without more, do not satisfy the Fourth Amendment. *See* *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 484; *Di Re*, 332 F.2d at 538. The court’s conclusion that Soares’s behavior justified a frisk is not supported by the evidence. *See* *Beck*, 379 U.S. at 97.

**Conclusion**

Because the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment, the handgun should have been suppressed. The judgment of conviction should be reversed.
