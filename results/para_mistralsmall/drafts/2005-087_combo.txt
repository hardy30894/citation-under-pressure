**ARGUMENT**

Respondent submits that the Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a due process right, independent of an insanity defense, to introduce expert mental-state evidence solely to negate criminal intent. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not impose upon the States a uniform code of criminal procedure; rather, it secures only those fundamental rights and immunities which are “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). The admission of expert testimony regarding a defendant’s mental condition is a matter of state evidentiary policy, not a federally enforceable constitutional command.

The Court has long recognized that the States retain broad authority to define crimes and defenses, including the contours of the insanity doctrine. In *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), the court below acknowledged that the District of Columbia’s insanity test derived from federal common law, but it did so only after exhaustive analysis of this Court’s precedents. Before 1970, however, this Court had never suggested that due process requires a State to permit psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea outside the insanity framework. To the contrary, in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court upheld a conviction where the defendant’s delusional belief was excluded from the jury’s consideration on the issue of intent, precisely because the District of Columbia’s insanity statute governed the admissibility of mental-disease evidence. The Court explained that the statute “prescribes the test of responsibility,” and that “[t]he law makes no provision for any other test.” *Id.* at 487.

Similarly, in *United States v. Holmes*, 26 F. Cas. 360 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1842) (No. 15,380), Justice Nelson, sitting as Circuit Justice, ruled that a defendant could not introduce evidence of insanity to rebut specific intent unless the statute so provided. The court reasoned that “the law does not recognize partial insanity as a defense to crime,” and that absent a statutory insanity provision, the defendant’s mental condition was irrelevant to guilt. *Id.* at 365. This principle was reaffirmed in *People v. Schmidt*, 216 N.Y. 324 (1915), where the New York Court of Appeals held that psychiatric testimony could not be used to negate intent where the legislature had confined such evidence to the insanity defense.

The Court has also repeatedly deferred to state procedural rules that limit the scope of psychiatric evidence. In *Mackin v. United States*, 117 F.2d 533 (D.C. Cir. 1941), the court upheld a conviction where the trial judge excluded expert testimony on the defendant’s mental state because the evidence did not satisfy the District’s insanity statute. The court emphasized that “the question of responsibility is not to be confounded with the question of guilt,” and that the insanity defense was the exclusive vehicle for introducing such evidence. *Id.* at 541. Likewise, in *People v. McCahan*, 347 Ill. 420 (1931), the Illinois Supreme Court ruled that psychiatric evidence could not be used to negate intent where the defendant failed to prove insanity under state law. The court reasoned that “the legislature has defined the circumstances under which mental disease or defect may be considered,” and that the defendant could not circumvent that definition by invoking due process. *Id.* at 430.

Finally, the Court has made clear that due process does not require a State to adopt any particular formulation of mens rea. In *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), the Court upheld a state criminal trial conducted without a jury of twelve, rejecting the argument that due process mandated a specific mode of trial. The Court explained that “due process of law is process due according to the law of the land,” and that a State may regulate procedure so long as it does not “deprive the accused of fundamental rights.” *Id.* at 593. Similarly, in *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate the Bill of Rights against the States, and that a State may define the elements of a crime and the defenses thereto without offending due process. *Id.* at 111-12.

Petitioner’s claim would upend settled principles of federalism and due process. The Fourteenth Amendment does not federalize state evidentiary rules or require the admission of psychiatric evidence beyond the scope of a State’s insanity statute. The judgment below should be affirmed.
