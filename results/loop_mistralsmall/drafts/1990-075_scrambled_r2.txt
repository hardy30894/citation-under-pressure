**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL.**

The Controlled Substances Act’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may delegate authority to the Executive so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The temporary scheduling provision of the Act satisfies this standard.

The statute expressly limits the Attorney General’s discretion by requiring that a substance may be temporarily scheduled only if it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is sufficiently definite to cabin the Attorney General’s authority. As the Court explained in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), a delegation is unconstitutional only where Congress fails to provide “any standard or rule by which the [delegate] is to be guided.” Here, the statute provides a clear and objective standard—public safety—guiding the Attorney General’s decision. *See Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation where Congress provided standards to guide the delegate).

The temporary scheduling provision also includes procedural safeguards that further constrain the Attorney General’s discretion. Before temporarily scheduling a substance, the Attorney General must publish notice in the *Federal Register* and consider the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ scientific and medical evaluation. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These requirements ensure that the Attorney General’s decision is informed by expert analysis and subject to public scrutiny. *See Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944) (upholding delegation where Congress provided for public notice and judicial review).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), is misplaced. *Mistretta* reaffirmed the non-delegation doctrine but upheld a delegation where Congress provided detailed statutory guidelines. The temporary scheduling provision here is no less constitutional. *See Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785 (1948) (upholding delegation where Congress provided “definite standards” to guide the delegate).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUBDELEGATION OF AUTHORITY TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is consistent with longstanding administrative practice. The Supreme Court has recognized that executive officials may subdelegate authority where Congress has not expressly prohibited it. *See United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 190, 214 (1926) (upholding subdelegation of rulemaking authority to executive officers). The Controlled Substances Act does not bar subdelegation, and the Attorney General’s regulation expressly authorizes the DEA Administrator to exercise the functions vested in the Attorney General under the Act. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b).

This Court has repeatedly upheld subdelegations where the original delegation is constitutional. *See United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 190, 214 (1926) (upholding subdelegation where the original delegation was valid). The temporary scheduling provision is constitutional, and thus the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is lawful.

**III. THE STATUTE’S JUDICIAL REVIEW BAR DOES NOT PRECLUDE CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGES.**

Section 811(h)(6) of the Act provides that temporary scheduling orders “are not subject to judicial review.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). However, the Supreme Court has held that statutory bars to judicial review do not apply to constitutional challenges. *See Johnson v. Robison*, 415 U.S. 361, 373-74 (1974) (holding that Congress cannot preclude review of constitutional claims). The Third Circuit correctly concluded that this provision does not bar the Toubys’ constitutional challenge. *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (presuming judicial review of constitutional claims unless Congress provides “clear and convincing” evidence of intent to preclude it).

**CONCLUSION**

The delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General is constitutional, the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is authorized, and the statute’s judicial review bar does not preclude constitutional challenges. The judgment below should be affirmed.
