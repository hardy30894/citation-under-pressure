**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN NATIONAL PARK SYSTEM UNITS IN ALASKA IS UNAFFECTED BY ANILCA § 103(c).**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not strip the National Park Service (NPS) of its statutory and regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within conservation system units (CSUs) in Alaska. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 822 F.3d 1192, 1198 (9th Cir. 2016). Section 103(c) provides:

> *Only those lands within the boundaries of [a CSU] which are owned by the United States ... shall be deemed to be included as a part of such unit. The Secretary shall not, in the administration of the National Park System, impose any additional restrictions on the use of non-Federal public lands or non-Federal public waters within the boundaries of such unit.* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added).

Contrary to Petitioner’s contention, this provision does not exempt state-owned lands and waters from NPS regulation. The plain text of § 103(c) limits its applicability to “non-Federal public lands or non-Federal public waters.” *Id.* (emphasis added). State-owned lands and waters are not “public lands” in the sense used in § 103(c). *See United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 57 (1997) (Scalia, J., concurring) (“The term ‘public lands’ ... does not include lands owned by a State.”); *Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698, 706 (9th Cir. 1995) (“The term ‘public lands’ ... refers to federal lands.”).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal government retains broad regulatory authority over navigable waters, even where title to the underlying lands has passed to a state. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) (“The power of the United States over navigable waters is not limited to the control of navigation. It extends to the protection of the entire stream and all its navigable branches and tributaries.”); *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 172 (1979) (“The Federal Government has traditionally exercised its authority to protect the public interest in the waters of the United States.”). This authority is not diminished by ANILCA § 103(c). *See Sturgeon*, 822 F.3d at 1198 (“ANILCA § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands and waters from NPS regulation.”).

Petitioner’s reliance on *Katie John I*, 72 F.3d 698, is misplaced. In *Katie John I*, the Ninth Circuit held that ANILCA § 103(c) did not authorize the Secretary to regulate subsistence fishing on state-owned waters within a CSU. *Id.* at 706. However, the court explicitly distinguished between regulatory authority over *subsistence fishing* and broader regulatory authority over *navigable waters*. *Id.* at 707 (“The Secretary’s authority to regulate subsistence fishing is not at issue here.”). The court further noted that the federal government’s authority over navigable waters is “plenary” and not limited by § 103(c). *Id.* at 706.

The NPS’s hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of its authority over navigable waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. *See 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)* (“The operation or use of hovercraft is prohibited” within NPS-administered lands and waters). This regulation applies to all waters within the preserve, regardless of ownership. *See United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 266 (1927) (“The United States has the power to regulate the use of navigable waters within its jurisdiction, even where the underlying lands are privately owned.”).

**II. THE NPS’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY IS NOT LIMITED TO FEDERALLY OWNED LANDS.**

Petitioner’s argument that ANILCA § 103(c) limits NPS’s regulatory authority to federally owned lands is belied by the plain text of the statute. *See Sturgeon*, 822 F.3d at 1198. Section 103(c) does not mention “federally owned lands.” Instead, it refers to “non-Federal public lands or non-Federal public waters.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The statute’s use of the term “public lands” has been consistently interpreted to mean federal lands. *See United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. at 57 (Scalia, J., concurring); *Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d at 706.

Moreover, the legislative history of ANILCA confirms that § 103(c) was not intended to limit NPS’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters. *See H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569*, at 24 (1976), *reprinted in* 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4290, 4310 (“Nothing in this Act shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing ... existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.”). This provision explicitly preserves the NPS’s existing regulatory authority. *See 16 U.S.C. § 3207*.

**III. PETITIONER LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE NPS’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Petitioner lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters. *Sturgeon*, 822 F.3d at 1199. To establish standing, a plaintiff must show (1) an injury in fact, (2) causation, and (3) redressability. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). Petitioner has failed to satisfy the redressability requirement.

Petitioner’s alleged injury—his inability to use a hovercraft on state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley—is not redressable by a declaration that the NPS’s regulations are invalid. *Id.* at 564. The NPS’s hovercraft ban applies to all waters within the preserve, regardless of ownership. *See 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)*. A favorable ruling would not permit Petitioner to use his hovercraft on state-owned waters, as the ban applies to all waters within the preserve. *See Sturgeon*, 822 F.3d at 1199.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The NPS’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within CSUs in Alaska is unaffected by ANILCA § 103(c). Petitioner lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s regulatory authority. The judgment below is correct and should be affirmed.
