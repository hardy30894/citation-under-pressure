# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT OF § 1254a(f)(4) SATISFIES § 1255(a)'S ADMISSION REQUIREMENT

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Nonimmigrant status, by definition, requires admission. As the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized, "by the very nature of obtaining nonimmigrant status, the alien is deemed to have been inspected and admitted." *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). This Court has long treated statutory categories as bearing their ordinary incidents; a status defined by the INA as "nonimmigrant" cannot be divorced from the admission that status presupposes. Congress did not merely grant TPS recipients some undefined lawful presence—it directed that they "shall be considered" as occupying the specific legal category of nonimmigrants, a category that under the INA's own architecture is inseparable from admission.

The Sixth Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), holding that a grant of TPS satisfies § 1255(a)'s admission requirement because § 1254a(f)(4) "expressly provides" that the recipient is to be treated as though lawfully admitted as a nonimmigrant. *Id.* at 553–54. Two courts of appeals have thus construed this statutory text to mean precisely what it says.

## II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE TPS PROGRAM CONFIRMS THAT ADMISSION FOLLOWS FROM STATUS

Congress designed TPS as a comprehensive alternative immigration status, complete with its own admission-like formalities: an application process, background checks, biometric screening, and formal approval by the Government granting the alien permission to remain. These procedures mirror the "inspection and authorization by an immigration officer" that defines admission under § 1101(a)(13)(A). The Third Circuit's insistence that admission requires a "physical event of entering the country," *Sanchez v. Mayorkas*, 967 F.3d 242, 249 (3d Cir. 2020) (quoting *Hanif v. Att'y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012)), reads a geographic limitation into the statute that Congress never imposed. Nothing in § 1101(a)(13)(A)'s text requires that the "lawful entry" occur through a border crossing; it requires only inspection and authorization, both of which the TPS process provides.

Moreover, treating TPS as effecting a constructive admission harmonizes § 1254a(f)(4) with the adjustment-of-status framework Congress built for other lawfully present nonimmigrants. Adjustment of status "generally constitutes an admission," *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011), and Congress's use of nonimmigrant terminology in § 1254a(f)(4) signals its intent to place TPS recipients within that same framework, not to create an anomalous status utterly disconnected from admission.

## III. THE PURPOSE OF TPS SUPPORTS PETITIONERS' INTERPRETATION

TPS exists to protect vulnerable individuals whose home countries have become unsafe due to armed conflict, environmental disaster, or similar crises. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Reading § 1255(a)'s admission requirement to categorically bar TPS recipients who entered without inspection from ever adjusting status—even after marrying U.S. citizens or otherwise becoming eligible for permanent relief—undermines this humanitarian purpose. Such recipients would be trapped in perpetual temporary status, unable to normalize their status despite Congress's evident intent that TPS serve as a bridge to stability for individuals from crisis-stricken nations.

The Ninth Circuit recognized this concern, explaining that requiring TPS recipients "to leave the country and reenter lawfully before seeking adjustment of status" is often not merely inconvenient but dangerous, given that their home countries remain unsafe by definition. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964. Congress could not have intended to grant these individuals humanitarian protection from removal while simultaneously foreclosing the ordinary path to permanent residence available to other nonimmigrants.

## IV. THE THIRD CIRCUIT'S CONTRARY STRUCTURAL ARGUMENTS DO NOT WITHSTAND SCRUTINY

The court below reasoned that Congress's express admission exceptions for other categories—such as § 1255(h) for special immigrants—implies the absence of such an exception for TPS recipients under the *Cardoza-Fonseca* canon. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987). But § 1254a(f)(4) does not merely except TPS recipients from the admission requirement; it affirmatively deems them to occupy nonimmigrant status, which itself entails admission. No separate exception was necessary because § 1254a(f)(4) operates through a different mechanism—not by waiving the requirement, but by satisfying it.

The court's superfluity concern regarding § 1254a(h)(2)'s supermajority requirement for "special legislation" adjusting TPS recipients' status is likewise unpersuasive. That provision addresses categorical legislative adjustments for TPS holders as a class; it says nothing about individual eligibility determinations under generally applicable adjustment provisions like § 1255(a). Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a)'s admission element does not implicate the distinct legislative mechanism Congress created in § 1254a(h)(2).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the conferral of Temporary Protected Status under § 1254a constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255(a), and reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit.
