# ARGUMENT

## I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF ARTICLE I ESTABLISH A FIXED, EXCLUSIVE, AND NATIONALLY UNIFORM STANDARD OF ELIGIBILITY FOR CONGRESS THAT NO STATE MAY SUPPLEMENT

Article I, Sections 2 and 3 of the Constitution set forth, with specificity, the age, citizenship, and residency requirements for Representatives and Senators. These provisions were not accidental omissions inviting state supplementation; they were the product of exhaustive debate at the Constitutional Convention, and the framers' considered rejection of additional qualifications—including rotation-in-office and term-limit proposals—is itself powerful evidence that the enumerated qualifications were meant to be exclusive. As the court below correctly recognized, "the action finally taken by the framers of the constitution, following exhaustive debates, is strong evidence that term limits for senators and representatives was rejected."

This Court's precedents confirm that the office of federal Representative or Senator is a creature of the federal Constitution alone, not of state law. In *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58 (1900), the Court explained that "the right to vote for a member of Congress" does not depend on state law because "[t]he office . . . is created by that Constitution and by that alone. It also declares how it shall be filled." The Court further observed that although states may prescribe voter qualifications for their own legislatures, "[t]he States . . . do not do this with reference to the election for members of Congress," and states "can[not] prescribe the qualification for voters for those eo nomine." *Id.* The logic of *Wiley* applies with equal force to candidate qualifications: if the *office* is exclusively a creature of the federal Constitution, the qualifications for *holding* that office must likewise derive exclusively from that instrument, not from the shifting enactments of fifty different state electorates.

This national, uniform character of congressional offices was reaffirmed in *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597 (1929), where the Court recognized that the Senate itself—not any state—possesses the constitutional authority to "judge of the elections, returns and qualifications of its members," acting "as a judicial tribunal." *Id.* If the Constitution vests exclusive authority to judge qualifications in the House and Senate themselves, it necessarily follows that individual states lack authority to add new substantive qualifications through their own constitutions or statutes. A state term-limit provision purporting to disqualify otherwise-eligible candidates intrudes directly upon this exclusively federal domain.

## II. THE STRUCTURE OF ARTICLE V CONFIRMS THAT CHANGES TO THE QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESS MUST PROCEED THROUGH FEDERAL CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT, NOT UNILATERAL STATE ACTION

The Constitution provides its own exclusive mechanism for altering federal qualifications: Article V. This Court's decision in *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), is instructive. There, the Court held that a state constitutional provision purporting to require a popular referendum on ratification of a federal constitutional amendment was invalid, because Article V "is a grant of authority by the people to Congress" and the method of ratification "is a federal function which . . . is not subject to the alteration by resort to state government." The same principle governs here: the qualifications for federal office, like the ratification process for federal amendments, present a matter of exclusively federal concern. Just as Ohio could not use its own constitutional processes to add a referendum requirement to the federal amendment process, Arkansas cannot use its own constitutional processes to add substantive qualifications to federal candidacy that Article I does not contain.

The equal-suffrage principle underlying Article V, discussed in *Barry*, 279 U.S. 597, further illustrates that the Constitution treats representation in Congress as a matter of careful, deliberately calibrated federal design—not one susceptible to piecemeal state modification. Permitting each state to impose its own disqualifications would recreate precisely the "hodge-podge of qualifications" that the court below rightly warned would result from a contrary holding, undermining the uniformity that Article I was designed to secure.

## III. AMENDMENT 73's "BALLOT ACCESS" LABEL CANNOT DISGUISE ITS SUBSTANCE AS AN ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATION

Petitioners' attempt to recharacterize Section 3 of Amendment 73 as a mere "ballot access" restriction, rather than a qualification, cannot withstand scrutiny. This Court has consistently examined the practical effect of state election regulations rather than resting on labels. In *Brockington v. Rhodes*, 396 U.S. 41 (1969), the Court confronted a state ballot-access requirement that effectively foreclosed candidates from the ballot, recognizing that such restrictions bear directly on the fundamental right of citizens to select their federal representatives and cannot be evaluated in formalistic isolation from their practical effect.

Likewise, in *Fortson v. Morris*, 385 U.S. 231 (1966), the Court examined the actual operation of a state's electoral machinery to determine its constitutional validity, rather than accepting the state's own characterization of the mechanism. Applying that same functional analysis here, Amendment 73's bar on ballot access for otherwise-qualified candidates who have served the specified number of terms operates as a complete and absolute disqualification from federal office. A candidate barred from appearing on the ballot is, in every practical sense, barred from that office. The Arkansas Supreme Court was correct to look past the "ballot-access terminology" to the Amendment's substantive effect, concluding that "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point" to save the provision from constitutional infirmity.

## IV. CONGRESSIONAL ELECTIONS IMPLICATE VOTERS' ASSOCIATIONAL AND SPEECH RIGHTS THAT ARE PROTECTED REGARDLESS OF STATE-LAW CHARACTERIZATION

Respondents Hill and Herget, and the League of Women Voters, possess cognizable constitutional interests in the integrity of congressional elections that Amendment 73 threatens. As the Arkansas Supreme Court recognized below, the exclusion of qualified candidates "brings into play impairment of speech and association rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments," citing *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983). These associational interests are not diminished merely because Arkansas labels its exclusion mechanism a "ballot access" rule rather than a formal disqualification. The right of voters to choose among candidates otherwise fully qualified under Article I is a right rooted in the federal Constitution itself, as *Wiley*, 179 U.S. 58, makes clear, and no state relabeling exercise can extinguish it.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court insofar as it held that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution.
