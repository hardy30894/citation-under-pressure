**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480, 147 L.Ed.2d 597 (2000), squarely governs this case and forecloses the petitioners’ challenge. The Court should affirm the judgment below.

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROTECT PROXIMITY-BASED COMMUNICATIONS THAT INTERFERE WITH THE RIGHT TO ACCESS ABORTION CLINICS**

The First Amendment protects speech, but not speech that is so coercive or obstructive in nature that it infringes upon the fundamental right of individuals to enter and exit healthcare facilities. In *Hill*, the Supreme Court upheld a Colorado statute that prohibited “any person from knowingly approaching within 8 feet of any individual entering or leaving a health care facility” if the purpose was to communicate. 530 U.S. at 707, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court recognized that “the right to enter and exit a health care facility is a fundamental right,” and that “the State has a compelling interest in protecting that right.” Id. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court emphasized that “the sidewalks and areas outside health care facilities are quintessential public forums,” but that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech in such forums to protect the rights of others.” Id. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480.

The Massachusetts law is indistinguishable in substance and purpose from the Colorado statute upheld in *Hill*. Both laws create fixed buffer zones around entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities (RHCFs) to ensure the free and unimpeded access of patients and staff. The Massachusetts law, like its Colorado counterpart, is content-neutral and viewpoint-neutral. It applies equally to all individuals regardless of the message they seek to convey—whether pro-life, pro-choice, or neutral. See *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 177. The law does not target any particular speaker or message; it regulates conduct—proximity to a facility—based on the potential for interference with access, not on the content of speech.

The petitioners’ claim that the law violates the First Amendment because it prevents “close personal contact” with patients is foreclosed by *Hill*. The Court in *Hill* rejected the argument that a 100-foot buffer zone was overbroad, noting that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech in public forums to protect the rights of others.” 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court observed that “the right to enter and exit a health care facility is a fundamental right,” and that “the State has a compelling interest in protecting that right.” Id. The Massachusetts law, which creates a 35-foot buffer zone—less than half the size of the Colorado law—further narrows the scope of regulated conduct and thus is even more narrowly tailored than the statute in *Hill*.

**II. THE LAW LEAVES ADEQUATE ALTERNATIVE CHANNELS OF COMMUNICATION**

The First Circuit correctly concluded that the law leaves ample alternative channels for communication. The petitioners may still speak, distribute literature, display signs, wear symbolic clothing, pray, and use amplification devices—all from outside the buffer zone. The record confirms that petitioners have successfully engaged in counseling, distributed materials, and even persuaded women to forgo abortions. See *McCullen III*, 844 F.Supp.2d at 213–25. The fact that some interactions are less effective from a distance does not render the law unconstitutional. As the Court held in *Hill*, “the First Amendment does not require the government to accommodate all forms of expression in all places.” 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480.

The petitioners’ argument that they are prevented from “demonstrating a caring demeanor” or making “eye contact” with patients is not a constitutional claim. The First Amendment does not guarantee a particular method of communication, nor does it require the government to create conditions for maximum persuasive effect. The law does not prohibit speech; it merely limits where that speech may occur. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “a regulation that is content-neutral, that serves a significant governmental interest, and that leaves open ample alternative channels of communication satisfies the First Amendment.” *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791, 109 S.Ct. 2746, 105 L.Ed.2d 661 (1989). The Massachusetts law satisfies all three prongs.

**III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RE-ARGUMENT OF FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY**

The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law is barred by the law of the case doctrine. This doctrine “prevents relitigation in the trial court of matters that were explicitly or implicitly decided by an earlier appellate decision in the same case.” *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir.2011). The First Circuit previously upheld the law’s facial constitutionality in *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176–81. The district court properly applied the doctrine, and the First Circuit affirmed. The petitioners have not shown any of the narrow exceptions—such as a dramatic change in controlling legal authority, new evidence, or a blatantly erroneous prior decision.
