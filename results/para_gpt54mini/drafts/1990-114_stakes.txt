**ARGUMENT**

I. **Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the ADEA because state judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.”**

The ADEA excludes from the definition of “employee” “any person elected to public office” and “any person appointed by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Supreme Court has instructed that where federal law intrudes upon a traditional area of state authority, courts should require a clear statement from Congress before construing the statute to displace state law. Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 460–61 (1991); see also United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). That rule is dispositive here.

In **Gregory v. Ashcroft**, the Court held that Missouri’s state court judges fall within the ADEA’s policymaking-level exception. 501 U.S. at 460–65. The Court reasoned that “judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents,” and therefore judges necessarily exercise policymaking judgment. Id. at 464. The Court also emphasized that Missouri judges possess “considerable policymaking responsibility” in the administration of the state judiciary. Id. at 464–65. Because appointed state judges are excluded from the ADEA’s definition of employee, Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule does not conflict with federal law. Id. at 465.

That conclusion accords with the lower-court decisions recognizing that judges perform policymaking functions sufficient to place them outside the ADEA’s coverage. See EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988); EEOC v. Illinois, 721 F. Supp. 156, 159–60 (N.D. Ill. 1989); EEOC v. Vermont, 717 F. Supp. 261, 263–65 (D. Vt. 1989); In re Stout, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489, 491–93 (1989); Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 144–46 (1988). The contrary district court decisions in Schlitz v. Virginia, 681 F. Supp. 330 (E.D. Va. 1988), and the Vermont decision below were rejected because they unduly minimized the judicial role. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 462–65.

II. **The Missouri retirement provision does not violate equal protection because age-based retirement for judges is rationally related to legitimate state interests.**

Age is not a suspect classification, and public employment is not a fundamental right. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam); San Antonio Independent Sch. Dist. v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973). Accordingly, Missouri’s constitutional retirement age is subject only to rational-basis review. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 470; Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979).

Under that deferential standard, a classification must be upheld if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis. FCC v. Beach Commc’ns, Inc., 508 U.S. 307, 313–15 (1993). Gregory confirmed that Missouri’s retirement rule satisfies that test. 501 U.S. at 470–72. The Court identified several legitimate state interests, including assuring a competent judiciary, avoiding the administrative burdens of individualized competence evaluations, promoting public confidence in the courts, and facilitating judicial turnover. Id. at 472–73. The Court specifically rejected the contention that the judiciary is irrationally treated differently from other state employees. Id. at 472.

That conclusion is consistent with prior precedent upholding mandatory retirement provisions against equal protection challenges. In Murgia, the Court sustained a Massachusetts law requiring police officers to retire at age 50, explaining that the State may address concerns about declining physical capacity by a mandatory age line rather than individualized testing. 427 U.S. at 314–17. Likewise, Vance upheld a mandatory retirement age for Foreign Service employees because Congress could rationally conclude that age correlates with the demands of the service. 440 U.S. at 106–08. Missouri’s judgment about the bench is no less rational.

The Court in Gregory also recognized that states may rationally seek to preserve the public’s confidence in a judiciary that is competent and representative. 501 U.S. at 471–73. For those reasons, Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment below should be affirmed.
