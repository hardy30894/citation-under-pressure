# ARGUMENT

## The Rule Announced in Johnson v. United States Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review

### A. A New Rule That Narrows the Scope of Conduct or Status Subjecting a Person to Punishment Applies Retroactively on Collateral Review.

This Court has long recognized that retroactivity is not governed by a single inflexible principle but depends on the nature of the rule at issue and its relationship to the integrity of the underlying conviction or sentence. See Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965) (holding that "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect" and that the answer depends on "weigh[ing] the merits and demerits in each case"). In assessing whether a new rule should be given retroactive effect, this Court has looked to considerations including the purpose served by the new rule, the extent to which authorities reasonably relied on the old standard, and the consequences that would follow from applying the new rule to final judgments. Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967); accord Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 728–29 (1966); Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott, 382 U.S. 406, 413–17 (1966).

Where the purpose of the new rule is to correct a defect that goes to the very authority of the State to impose the punishment at all—rather than merely to improve the procedures by which guilt or sentence is determined—retroactive application follows almost automatically, because the old rule threatens to leave in place a punishment the State never had power to impose. This distinction between rules altering the substance of what conduct or status may be punished and rules merely regulating adjudicative procedure is embedded in this Court's own retroactivity jurisprudence, which examines the purpose to be served by the new rule as a threshold consideration bearing on whether the underlying judgment retains its legitimacy. Stovall, 388 U.S. at 297.

This principle has deep roots. In Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), this Court held that prisoners held under judgments of conviction rendered pursuant to an unconstitutional statute were entitled to release on habeas corpus, reasoning that a court has no authority to render judgment of conviction under a law that is beyond the power of Congress to enact, and that such a judgment is therefore void and may be collaterally attacked notwithstanding its formal finality. Id. at 375–77.

### B. Johnson's Invalidation of ACCA's Residual Clause Is a Substantive Rule Narrowing the Class of Predicate Offenses, and Therefore Applies Retroactively.

The rule announced in Johnson v. United States is substantive in precisely this sense. It did not merely adjust the procedure for determining whether a prior conviction qualifies as a "violent felony"; it held that the residual clause itself could no longer be used to classify any offense as a predicate offense, because the clause was too vague to provide a constitutionally adequate standard. That holding rests on this Court's long-established void-for-vagueness doctrine, under which "a statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law." Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). As this Court explained in Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), "[n]o one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." Id. at 453. And in United States v. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81 (1921), this Court invalidated a criminal statute that failed to fix an ascertainable standard of guilt, holding that a law which leaves the fixing of criminal responsibility "so vague, indefinite and uncertain" that it fails to give fair notice of what is forbidden cannot support a criminal judgment. Id. at 89.

A statute condemned on that basis is not merely flawed in application; it is void, and sentences enhanced by reliance on it stand on the same footing as convictions obtained under judgments rendered pursuant to an unconstitutional statute, which this Court held void and subject to collateral attack in Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 375–77 (1880). Because Johnson removed an entire category of predicate offenses from ACCA's reach as a matter of substantive law—altering who may be subjected to the fifteen-year mandatory minimum—the rule falls squarely within the category of rules that must apply retroactively. A prisoner sentenced solely on the strength of the residual clause is, after Johnson, serving a sentence the district court never had lawful authority to impose, no different in kind from the void judgments addressed in Siebold.

### C. The Traditional Retroactivity Factors Confirm This Result.

Applying the considerations set out in Stovall, Johnson v. New Jersey, and Tehan, retroactivity is warranted. The purpose of the Johnson rule—ensuring that no person is imprisoned under a standard "so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning," Connally, 269 U.S. at 391—goes to the heart of fair notice and the legitimacy of the sentence itself, not to the reliability of trial procedure. Reliance interests are minimal, since the residual clause's demise reflects a recognition that the clause never supplied a workable standard in the first place, just as the statute in Cohen Grocery Co. never supplied an ascertainable standard of guilt. See Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. at 89. And the interests this Court has weighed in evaluating whether retroactive application would unduly disrupt the administration of justice, see Tehan, 382 U.S. at 413–17, are served, not disserved, here: ensuring that no prisoner remains confined under a sentence that rests on a constitutionally void basis vindicates rather than undermines those interests, just as this Court concluded that prisoners held under void judgments in Ex parte Siebold were entitled to relief. 100 U.S. at 375–77.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to cases, like petitioner's, on collateral review.
