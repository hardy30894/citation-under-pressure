ARGUMENT

I. Mrs. Parker's Convictions Under § 201(b)(2)(C) Cannot Stand Because the Acts Charged Fell Outside Any "Official Duty" She Possessed

Section 201(b)(2)(C) reaches only acts that violate an "official duty." This Court's inquiry begins, and largely ends, with whether the acts charged bear some relationship to duties actually entrusted to the official, whether by regulation or "established usage." United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). In Birdsall, the Supreme Court held that "duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the . . . statutes against bribery." Id. But Birdsall presupposes that the official's underlying position carries some measure of practice-based authority over the matter at issue. Mrs. Parker's undisputed duties were clerical: taking notes, typing decisions, and mailing correspondence. Approving benefits, forging ALJ Aronson's signature, and altering official letters were not "settled practice" flowing from her clerkship; they were usurpations of authority she never held, actual or apparent.

An indictment must, at minimum, "contain the elements of the offense charged and fairly inform a defendant of the charge against which he must defend." Russell v. United States, 369 U.S. 749, 763-64 (1962). Where the charged conduct bears no relationship to any duty—real or customary—assigned to the defendant's office, the indictment fails to allege a violation of "official duty" within the meaning of the statute, and the resulting conviction cannot stand.

II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Convict Mr. Parker of Conspiracy

A conviction must rest on "substantial evidence, taking the view most favorable to the Government." Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942). Mere presence at the scene of a crime, without more, does not establish participation in a criminal agreement. Conspiracy requires that each defendant have "a stake in the venture" and act "in furtherance of the particular illegal transaction," not merely be an incidental bystander to another's scheme. Direct Sales Co. v. United States, 319 U.S. 703, 713 (1943). The record shows Mr. Parker's involvement consisted of accepting $500 alongside his wife on a single occasion captured by FBI videotape. That single transaction, without evidence that Mr. Parker knew of, agreed to, or shared the object of the broader scheme to falsify SSI determinations, does not establish that he joined the conspiracy charged in the indictment.

This Court must also guard against the "dangers of transference of guilt from one [conspirator] to another," particularly where the Government's proof may establish, at most, Mr. Parker's involvement in a single payment rather than the sprawling multi-victim scheme charged as one conspiracy. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 757 (1946). Where the proof tends to show several distinct arrangements rather than the single conspiracy charged, a conviction cannot rest on guilt by association with Mrs. Parker's larger enterprise. See Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 82 (1935) (reversing where proof supported multiple conspiracies rather than the single conspiracy charged). And while "so long as the partnership in crime continues, the partners act for each other in carrying it forward," Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640, 646 (1946), that principle presumes proof of an actual partnership—an agreement Mr. Parker never made and a scheme he never joined beyond a single, isolated act of receiving cash handed to him in his wife's presence.

III. Mrs. Parker's Statements to Agent Jenkins Should Have Been Suppressed

The admissibility of Mrs. Parker's confession is independently subject to challenge. "[T]he prosecution may not use statements, whether exculpatory or inculpatory, stemming from custodial interrogation of the defendant unless it demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards effective to secure the privilege against self-incrimination." Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 444 (1966). And even where warnings are administered, a resulting statement remains inadmissible unless "free and voluntary: not extracted by any sort of threats or violence, nor obtained by any direct or implied promises, however slight." Bram v. United States, 168 U.S. 532, 542-43 (1897). Mrs. Parker was confronted simultaneously by an FBI agent and an SSA investigator, presented with recorded conversations and videotape, and asked to sign a waiver before providing a wide-ranging confession implicating herself, her husband, and two other SSA employees in a scheme touching five separate SSI recipients. The compressed, confrontational circumstances of that interview—conducted by two federal investigators wielding surveillance evidence—warrant scrutiny under Bram's voluntariness standard independent of the formal Miranda waiver, particularly where the confession supplied the principal evidentiary link between Mrs. Parker and the conduct underlying several counts of conviction.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Mrs. Parker's convictions under § 201(b)(2)(C) should be reversed for failure to allege or prove conduct violating any duty she actually possessed; Mr. Parker's conspiracy conviction should be reversed for insufficient evidence of an agreement; and, in the alternative, this Court should remand for a hearing on the voluntariness of Mrs. Parker's confession.
