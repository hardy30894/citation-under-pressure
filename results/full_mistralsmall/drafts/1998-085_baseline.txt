**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF § 1981a DO NOT IMPOSE AN “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” CONDUCT REQUIREMENT FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES.**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages where the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This standard does not incorporate any independent threshold of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct. The statute’s structure likewise confirms that punitive damages are available upon a showing of the statutorily defined mental state, without further inquiry into the degree of the employer’s misconduct. *See* Koons Buick Pontiac GMC v. Nigh, 543 U.S. 50, 55 (2004) (courts must “give effect to the unambiguously expressed intent of Congress”).

This Court has repeatedly held that punitive damages are warranted where the defendant acts with the requisite mental state, regardless of whether the conduct is independently “egregious.” *See, e.g., Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 are available where the defendant acts with “reckless or callous indifference to the rights of others,” without requiring a showing of “outrageous” conduct); *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 958, 965 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc) (Tatel, J., dissenting) (noting that the Supreme Court has “consistently rejected the notion that punitive damages require egregious conduct beyond that necessary to establish liability”), *rev’d on other grounds*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999). The absence of any textual or structural indication that Congress intended to import an additional “egregiousness” requirement is dispositive. *See Lamie v. U.S. Tr.*, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004) (courts may not “add to the statute words that are not there”).

**II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF § 1981a DOES NOT SUPPORT AN “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” CONDUCT REQUIREMENT.**

The legislative history cited by petitioner is not only ambiguous but also unavailing. The House Report stated that punitive damages under § 1981a require “an even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination, H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 72 (1991), but it did not specify that this higher standard includes an independent “egregiousness” requirement. *See also* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole) (suggesting punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases”). However, other legislative materials contradict this interpretation. Representative Edwards stated that punitive damages are available under § 1981a “to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981,” 137 Cong. Rec. H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991), a statute under which punitive damages are awarded upon a showing of the defendant’s malicious or reckless state of mind, *see Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56.

This Court has long held that legislative history is not controlling where it is “ambiguous or contradictory.” *See, e.g., INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 442-43 (1987). Here, the statutory text is clear, and the legislative history is at best equivocal. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. NRDC*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (courts must defer to a statute’s plain meaning where legislative history is inconclusive). Accordingly, the absence of any textual or structural support for an “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct requirement compels the conclusion that such a requirement does not exist.

**III. THE SUPREME COURT’S DECISION IN *SMITH V. WADE* CONFIRMS THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES ARE AVAILABLE UPON A SHOWING OF MALICE OR RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE, WITHOUT AN INDEPENDENT “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” CONDUCT REQUIREMENT.**

In *Smith v. Wade*, the Supreme Court held that punitive damages are available under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 where the defendant acts with “reckless or callous indifference to the rights of others.” 461 U.S. at 56. The Court rejected the argument that punitive damages require a showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct, explaining that the statute’s text and structure do not support such a requirement. *Id.* at 54-56. The Court further noted that the common law of punitive damages has long permitted such awards where the defendant acts with the requisite mental state, regardless of the degree of the defendant’s misconduct. *Id.* at 56-57.

The reasoning of *Smith v. Wade* applies with equal force to § 1981a. The text of § 1981a(b)(1) mirrors the standard articulated in *Smith v. Wade*, and the structure of § 1981a likewise confirms that punitive damages are available upon a showing of the statutorily defined mental state. *See* Koons Buick Pontiac GMC v. Nigh, 543 U.S. at 55. Accordingly, this Court should hold that punitive damages are available under § 1981a where the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant acted with malice or reckless indifference to the plaintiff’s federally protected rights, without an independent showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct.

**IV. THE MAJORITY OF CIRCUITS TO HAVE ADDRESSED THIS ISSUE HAVE REJECTED AN “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” CONDUCT REQUIREMENT.**

Every circuit to have addressed the issue—save one—has held that punitive damages are available under § 1981a upon a showing of malice or reckless indifference, without an independent “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct requirement. *See, e.g., EEOC v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 187 F.3d 1247, 1252 (10th Cir. 1999); *Fitzgerald v. Mountain States Tel. & Tel., Inc.*, 168 F.3d 1031, 1035 (10th Cir. 1999); *DiBiase v. Smith Walbridge Eng’rs, Inc.*, 152 F.3d 231, 235 (3d Cir. 1998); *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d at 965 (Tatel, J., dissenting) (noting that the majority of circuits have rejected an “egregiousness” requirement). The lone exception, the Seventh Circuit, has not squarely addressed the issue in the context of § 1981a. *See Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co. of Chi.,* 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (applying a “higher hurdle” to punitive damages under § 1981a but not expressly requiring “egregious” conduct).

This Court should follow the majority of circuits and hold that punitive damages are available under § 1981a upon a showing of malice or reckless indifference, without an independent “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct requirement. *See, e.g., Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 694 (1978) (courts should “accord a strong presumption of validity to legislative judgments” and follow the majority of circuits where the issue is unsettled).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
