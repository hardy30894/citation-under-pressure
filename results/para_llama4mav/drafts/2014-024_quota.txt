The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because the trial court's jury instruction on the standard for a "true threat" was erroneous. The instruction failed to require the jury to find that Elonis subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment does not protect "true threats." Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam). However, the Court has also emphasized the importance of carefully defining what constitutes a "true threat" to avoid chilling protected speech. In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Court held that a "true threat" is a statement that a speaker "means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence" to a particular individual or group. Id. at 359. This definition implies that a speaker's subjective intent is a critical factor in determining whether a statement is a "true threat."

The Third Circuit's decision in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a "true threat" when a reasonable speaker would foresee that it would be interpreted as a threat, is no longer good law in light of Black. See United States v. Parr, 545 F.3d 491, 499-500 (7th Cir. 2008) (holding that Black requires a subjective intent to threaten). The trial court's instruction, which followed Kosma, was therefore erroneous.

Requiring a subjective intent to threaten is consistent with the principle that the government must prove a defendant's culpable state of mind to secure a conviction. See Elonis v. United States, 897 F. Supp. 2d 335, 341 (E.D. Pa. 2012) (noting that "the mens rea requirement is a crucial aspect of criminal law"). This approach also aligns with the Supreme Court's decisions in other areas of First Amendment law, such as libel, where the Court has required a showing of actual malice. New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 279-80 (1964).

Furthermore, an objective standard for determining "true threats" can lead to the prosecution of individuals who did not intend to threaten anyone. See United States v. Cassel, 408 F.3d 622, 631-33 (9th Cir. 2005) (noting the potential for overbreadth). In contrast, a subjective standard ensures that only those who intentionally communicate threats are held criminally liable.

The government's reliance on the Third Circuit's decision in United States v. MacEwan, 445 F.3d 237 (3d Cir. 2006), is misplaced. While MacEwan held that transmission over the internet is sufficient to establish interstate commerce, it does not address the issue of whether a subjective intent is required for a "true threat."

In conclusion, the trial court's jury instruction on the standard for a "true threat" was erroneous, and Elonis' conviction should be reversed. The correct standard requires a subjective intent to threaten, as implied by the Supreme Court's decision in Virginia v. Black.
