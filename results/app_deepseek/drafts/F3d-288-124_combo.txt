**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Bell Atlantic’s Proffered Reason for Bryant’s Termination Was Pretextual.**

Summary judgment is proper only when “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact.” *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962). In discrimination cases, the court must “view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion.” *Id.* at 473. Here, Bryant presented direct and circumstantial evidence that Bell Atlantic’s stated reason for his termination—insubordination for failing to work overtime—was a pretext for discrimination based on race and family responsibilities.

Under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973), a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination. Bryant, an African-American, was qualified for his position and suffered an adverse employment action—termination—while similarly situated white employees were treated more favorably. *See id.* at 804. The burden then shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason. *Id.* at 802. Bell Atlantic claims Bryant was terminated for insubordination. However, Bryant produced evidence that white employees who refused overtime assignments were not disciplined or terminated. This raises a genuine issue of pretext. *See Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981) (plaintiff may show pretext by demonstrating that the employer’s reason is “unworthy of credence”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that an employer’s failure to accommodate an employee’s religious or family obligations may constitute evidence of discrimination. *See Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 404 (1963) (holding that a state may not condition benefits on conduct forbidden by religious beliefs without compelling justification). Here, Bell Atlantic refused Bryant’s union’s offer to have co-workers perform his overtime assignments, despite knowing his child-care responsibilities. This refusal, combined with the company’s shifting explanations for its actions, demonstrates pretext. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 147 (2000) (citing *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 256).

**II. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Bryant’s Claim to Enforce the Arbitration Award.**

Bryant has standing to enforce the arbitration award because he is a third-party beneficiary of the collective bargaining agreement. *See United Steelworkers of America v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 578 (1960) (arbitration is a “substitute for industrial strife” and awards are binding on both parties). The award, which found that Bell Atlantic violated the agreement by failing to accommodate Bryant, is enforceable under Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 185. *See Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills*, 353 U.S. 448, 456 (1957) (federal courts may enforce arbitration awards). The district court’s dismissal of this claim was error.

**III. The District Court Erred in Failing to Apply the Correct Burden-Shifting Standard.**

The district court failed to recognize that *McDonnell Douglas* and *Burdine* require the plaintiff to show only that the employer’s reason is pretextual, not that discrimination was the sole motive. *See Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 256. Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic’s proffered reason was false, including testimony that white employees were not disciplined for similar conduct. This is sufficient to survive summary judgment. *See Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473.

**IV. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Bryant’s Retaliation Claim.**

Bryant engaged in protected activity by filing a grievance and opposing the Red Letter Day policy. *See NLRB v. Scrivener*, 405 U.S. 117, 121 (1972) (protected activity includes filing grievances). Bell Atlantic then subjected him to escalating discipline, culminating in termination. This temporal proximity establishes a prima facie case of retaliation. *See Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 256.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for trial.
