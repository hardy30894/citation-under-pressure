**I. The Inherited IRA Qualifies as “Retirement Funds” Under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C)**

The statutory language of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is unambiguous: it exempts from creditors’ claims any “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” An inherited IRA is an account exempt from taxation under IRC § 408(d)(3). The term “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code. Therefore, the term must be interpreted in light of the statute’s purpose: to protect savings for retirement from the reach of creditors.

The Fifth Circuit correctly interpreted this language in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012). The court held that the exemption applies to funds that were “retirement funds” for the decedent, regardless of who currently holds the account. The court reasoned, “It is enough, Chilton concludes, if they were ever anyone’s retirement funds.” This interpretation is consistent with the general rule that exemptions in bankruptcy often protect assets based on their nature or function, not merely the debtor’s current intent or use. *See* *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312, 317 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010) (adopting the view that money representing “retirement funds” in the decedent’s hands must be treated the same way in successors’ hands).

**II. The Purpose of the Exemption Requires Protection of Inherited IRAs**

The purpose of the retirement exemption is to prevent creditors from accessing a debtor’s retirement savings. An inherited IRA is a tax-deferred vehicle designed to provide income for the beneficiary in retirement. While the distribution rules differ from a traditional IRA, the funds are still intended for the beneficiary’s use after they stop working. To exclude inherited IRAs would frustrate the legislative intent to protect retirement savings. *See* *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525 (1987) (stating that interpreting statutes to further their primary objective is the essence of legislative choice).

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the “current owner’s retirement” requirement is a misreading of the statute. The Code does not require the funds to be held for the *debtor’s* retirement, only that they be “retirement funds.” The Fifth Circuit’s approach is sound because it focuses on the functional nature of the asset. The fact that the beneficiary must take distributions earlier than the original owner would have does not change the fact that the funds are a retirement fund. The funds are not “current consumption” in the sense of being immediately spendable cash; they are a tax-deferred asset intended for future use.
