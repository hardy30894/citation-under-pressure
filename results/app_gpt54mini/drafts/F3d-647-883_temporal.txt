Buckles’s § 2255 motion was timely because the period for seeking certiorari did not run from the April 3, 2007 denial of rehearing, but from this Court’s June 12, 2007 order recalling the mandate to address his Rule 4-1(e) motion. At minimum, any contrary conclusion rests on a misleading statement from the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office that warrants equitable tolling.

I. This Court’s June 12, 2007 order restarted the certiorari clock.

Supreme Court Rule 13 governs the time to petition for certiorari, and the controlling principle is that the deadline runs from the “denial of rehearing.” Sup.Ct. R. 13(3). But where a court of appeals again takes up the matter and enters a new disposition, the finality of the prior judgment is displaced and the certiorari period runs from the later action. That is the teaching of the Supreme Court’s finality cases. See Berman v. United States, 302 U.S. 211, 212-13 (1937) (final judgment is the judgment that disposes of the case for purposes of review); St. Louis, Iron Mountain & S. Ry. Co. v. Taylor, 210 U.S. 281, 287-88 (1908) (a later action affecting the judgment changes the time for review); Hamilton-Brown Shoe Co. v. Wolf Bros. & Co., 240 U.S. 251, 258-59 (1916) (review time is measured from the operative final action, not from an earlier interlocutory event).

Here, the Court’s June 12 order was not a ministerial acknowledgement. It was an order entered after the mandate had been recalled and after Buckles’s filing had been construed as a Rule 4-1(e) motion for appointment of counsel. A recalled mandate is not a nullity; it is a judicial act that reopens the proceeding for a limited purpose. See Greater Boston Television Corp. v. Federal Communications Commission, 263 F.2d 48, 51-52 (D.C. Cir. 1958) (recognizing that recall of mandate is an exceptional judicial act restoring jurisdiction to the court). Once this Court recalled its mandate and then denied the motion, the operative event for certiorari purposes was the June 12 denial, not the earlier denial of rehearing. Cf. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 491-92 (1953) (finality in collateral review depends on the actual terminal state of direct review, not a formalistic label).

Because Buckles filed his certiorari petition within 90 days of the June 12, 2007 order, his petition was timely under Supreme Court Rule 13(1), and his § 2255 motion was timely as well. See United States v. Morgan, 346 U.S. 502, 506-07 (1954) (postconviction timing should be measured with attention to practical finality, not technical traps).

II. Alternatively, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling because he was affirmatively misled.

Even if the Court concludes that certiorari was due by July 2, 2007, equity should prevent the government from profiting from misinformation supplied by the Clerk’s office. The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a litigant is misled by court personnel or by an official action, rigid enforcement of a filing deadline may be inequitable. See Harris Truck Lines, Inc. v. Cherry Meat Packers, Inc., 371 U.S. 215, 216-17 (1962) (per curiam) (party entitled to rely on judicially induced extension); Thompson v. Immigration & Naturalization Service, 375 U.S. 384, 387 (1964) (per curiam) (same principle where litigant relied on official misinformation).

The record here is especially compelling. Buckles declared that the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office told his sister that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” If that statement was made, it was plainly inaccurate and reasonably calculated to induce delay. A litigant who acts in reliance on erroneous official advice should not be forfeited by a deadline he was told had been suspended. See The West Wing, Inc. v. Commissioner, 315 U.S. 641, 647-48 (1942) (equitable relief appropriate where government conduct induces detrimental reliance).

Nor does Buckles’s pro se status defeat relief. The Court has repeatedly held that procedural rules are not applied to work injustice where a prisoner has been misled by official conduct. See Houston v. Lack, 487 U.S. 266, 270-71 (1988) (recognizing the special procedural burdens of prisoners); but see no authority holding that a prisoner who relies on clerk misinformation must nonetheless lose all collateral review. Equity exists precisely to prevent that result. See Hazel-Atlas Glass Co. v. Hartford-Empire Co., 322 U.S. 238, 244-45 (1944) (courts have inherent power to prevent injustice obtained by misleading conduct).

III. At minimum, the case should be remanded for a factual determination on clerk misinformation.

The district court dismissed Buckles’s motion as untimely without resolving whether the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office gave the misleading advice described in his declaration. That was error. Where timeliness depends on disputed facts bearing on equitable tolling, the court must permit development of the record rather than impose forfeiture on the papers alone. See Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266, 291 (1948) (courts should afford a fair opportunity to present facts material to a prisoner’s claim).

For these reasons, the dismissal should be reversed and the case remanded for merits review, or at minimum for an evidentiary hearing on equitable tolling.
