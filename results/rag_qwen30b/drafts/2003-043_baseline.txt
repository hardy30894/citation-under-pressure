**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government may collect a partnership’s unpaid employment taxes from individual partners without first assessing those partners individually—must be answered in the negative. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code bars any collection from them, even where the partnership was timely assessed. The Government’s attempt to circumvent this statutory bar by relying on the partnership assessment or state-law joint liability fails as a matter of law.

**I. The IRS Must Assess Individual Partners to Collect from Them; the Partnership Assessment Alone Is Insufficient**

The Internal Revenue Code treats partners and partnerships as distinct taxpayers. Section 7701(a)(1) defines “person” to include “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation,” and § 7701(a)(14) defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax.” This statutory framework confirms that each partner is a separate taxpayer with an individual taxpayer identification number. As such, the IRS may not collect from a partner without first assessing that partner under § 6501(a), which requires assessment within three years of the return’s filing. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

The Government’s argument that a partnership assessment extends the statute of limitations for collection from individual partners misreads the plain language of § 6502(a), which provides that a tax “may be collected by levy or by a proceeding in court, but only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—(1) within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” (Emphasis added.) The statute speaks only of the “assessment of the tax,” not of the assessment of a partnership. The “tax” assessed must be the tax of the taxpayer against whom collection is sought. *See* *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien under § 6321 “does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203”). The IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not constitute an assessment against the individual partners. Thus, the ten-year collection period under § 6502(a) applies only to the partnership, not to the partners.

This interpretation is consistent with *Francis v. McNeal*, 228 U.S. 695, 700 (1913), which recognized that the Bankruptcy Act “assumed that the partnership and individual estates both were to be administered.” The Court emphasized that “it would be an anomaly to allow proceedings in bankruptcy against joint debtors from some of whom, at any time before, pending, or after the proceeding, the debt could be collected in full.” *Id.* at 701. If the IRS could collect from partners without individual assessment, it would allow the Government to bypass the statutory limitations period entirely—precisely the anomaly the Court warned against.

**II. State Law Does Not Create a Right to Collect Without a Judgment or Assessment**

The Government also argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and thus the IRS may collect from partners without individual assessment. This argument fails because California law does not permit automatic collection from a partner’s personal assets based solely on a partnership judgment. *See* Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c): “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” *See also* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (“[A] partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”).

The IRS has not obtained a judgment against any of the individual partners. Nor can it now do so, as the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a) has expired. *See* *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562 (7th Cir. 1995) (distinguishing cases where assessments were made against both partnership and partners). The Government’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. *Young* held only that a partner could not recover a refund of taxes paid under a partnership assessment, not that the Government could collect from a partner without individual assessment. *Id.* at 910. The court in *Young* explicitly acknowledged that “the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). The IRS did not resort to court action, and thus the collection was improper.

**III. The Bankruptcy Court Properly Disallowed the Claims**

Under § 502(b)(1) of the Bankruptcy Code, a claim may be disallowed if it is “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor, under applicable law.” The IRS’s claim is unenforceable because it lacks a valid assessment against the individual partners and no judgment has been entered against them. *See* *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458. The Government’s claim is not “a right to payment” under § 101(5)(A) because it is barred by the statute of limitations. *See* *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 264 U.S. 499, 503 (1924) (holding that a petition in bankruptcy may be maintained only if the debtor is insolvent and the petition is filed in good faith). The bankruptcy court did not err in disallowing the claim.
