The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), which triggers the three-year statute of limitations for ERISA claims. The court held that a plaintiff's claim is not barred by the statute of limitations unless the plaintiff had actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence. However, this interpretation is inconsistent with the plain meaning of the statute and the relevant case law.

The statute requires "actual knowledge of the breach or violation," not knowledge of the breach and its illegality. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 992 (9th Cir. 1985), the Ninth Circuit itself held that "the statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law." This principle has been consistently applied in other circuits. See, e.g., Flanigan v. General Electric Co., 242 F.3d 78, 87-88 (2d Cir. 2001); Gluck v. Unisys Corp., 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992).

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit's own precedent in Meagher v. International Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), supports the proposition that knowledge of the transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations. In Meagher, the court held that the plaintiff's claim was barred because he had knowledge of the reduced benefits more than three years before filing the action, even though he did not know that the reduction was unlawful under ERISA.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case is also inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Tibble v. Edison Int'l, 135 S. Ct. 1823 (2015), which emphasized the importance of the "actual knowledge" requirement in § 1113(2). While Tibble did not directly address the issue presented here, it underscored the significance of the statute of limitations in ERISA cases.

Furthermore, other circuits have held that a plaintiff's claim is barred by the statute of limitations if they have actual knowledge of the facts underlying the claim, even if they do not know that those facts constitute a breach of ERISA. See, e.g., Brown v. Owens Corning Inv. Review Comm., 622 F.3d 564, 571-72 (6th Cir. 2010); Maher v. Strachan Shipping Co., 68 F.3d 951, 954-56 (5th Cir. 1995).

In this case, Sulyma had access to numerous documents disclosing the investment decisions made by Intel, including Fund Fact Sheets, Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notices, and Summary Plan Descriptions. These documents provided Sulyma with actual knowledge of the transactions that he now alleges were imprudent. Under the correct interpretation of § 1113(2), this knowledge is sufficient to trigger the three-year statute of limitations.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary is based on a flawed understanding of the "actual knowledge" requirement. By requiring knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence, the court has effectively read the "actual knowledge" standard out of the statute. This interpretation is inconsistent with the plain meaning of § 1113(2) and the relevant case law.

In Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991), the Ninth Circuit held that "actual knowledge is measured from the 'earliest date' on which [the plaintiff] knew of the breach." This principle supports the conclusion that Sulyma's claims are barred by the statute of limitations, as he had actual knowledge of the transactions underlying his claims more than three years before filing the action.

The decision below should be reversed, and the district court's grant of summary judgment to Intel should be reinstated. The "actual knowledge" requirement under § 1113(2) is met when a plaintiff has knowledge of the transactions underlying their claim, and Sulyma's claims are therefore barred by the three-year statute of limitations.
