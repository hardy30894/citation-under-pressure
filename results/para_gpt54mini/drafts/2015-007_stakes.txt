The Florida death-sentencing scheme, as applied here, violates neither the Sixth Amendment nor the Eighth Amendment. The controlling question after Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), is whether any fact necessary to expose a defendant to a death sentence is found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Under Ring and its predecessors, the answer must be yes. But Ring did not require jury unanimity in the final sentencing recommendation, and it did not constitutionalize every state’s capital procedure. Nor does Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), require a jury to decide mental retardation in the first instance.

First, Florida’s scheme satisfies the Sixth Amendment because the jury here found guilt and recommended death after hearing the aggravation evidence. Ring held that “capital defendants are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment.” 536 U.S. at 589. That principle is fully honored when the jury makes the critical factual findings that authorize death. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment requires jury findings on aggravating facts that operate as the functional equivalent of elements. See Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000); Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002). Florida’s scheme, by contrast, did not permit death absent a jury verdict of guilt for first-degree murder and a penalty-phase recommendation made after the jury heard the aggravating evidence. The Constitution does not require the jury’s advisory vote to be unanimous. In McKoy v. North Carolina, 494 U.S. 433, 444 (1990), the Court rejected a unanimity requirement for mitigating circumstances, and nothing in Ring displaced the longstanding rule that states retain flexibility in structuring capital sentencing so long as the jury finds the facts necessary to make death legally available.

Nor does the absence of unanimity in the recommendation itself violate the Sixth Amendment. The Florida Supreme Court has long recognized that the jury’s recommendation is advisory in the sense that the judge imposes sentence, while still requiring the jury to perform a critical factual role. See Hildwin v. Florida, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam); Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 459-60 (1984). Ring overruled Hildwin and Spaziano only to the extent those cases permitted judicial factfinding of aggravators necessary for death. Ring, 536 U.S. at 609. It did not hold that unanimity is constitutionally required for the advisory sentencing recommendation. Indeed, the Court has never announced such a rule. Because the aggravating facts were presented to, and found by, the jury in the guilt and penalty phases, Florida’s procedure did not offend the jury-trial guarantee.

Second, the Eighth Amendment does not require a jury to determine mental retardation, and it does not require jury unanimity as a condition of a death sentence. In Atkins, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment categorically bars the execution of the mentally retarded, but it expressly left “to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Atkins, 536 U.S. at 317. Florida’s statute did exactly that by assigning the trial court responsibility to determine whether the defendant is mentally retarded, while allowing the defendant to present such evidence in mitigation. See Florida v. Nixon, 543 U.S. 175, 191 (2004) (describing the State’s role in structuring capital proceedings). Because Atkins did not specify a federal procedural allocation, there is no basis for converting that substantive Eighth Amendment rule into a Sixth Amendment jury-trial requirement.

The same is true of unanimity. The Court has repeatedly stated that “death is different,” but that principle has been used to require heightened reliability, not to constitutionalize one specific sentencing form. Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. at 605-06; Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349, 357 (1977); Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). The Eighth Amendment forbids arbitrary death sentencing, but it leaves the States latitude to design procedures that channel discretion. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976). Florida’s scheme channels discretion through aggravating circumstances and judicial sentencing, and the jury’s nonunanimous recommendation does not by itself render the sentence cruel and unusual. The Supreme Court has never held that jury unanimity is constitutionally mandated in capital sentencing, and it has upheld capital procedures containing less-than-unanimous jury functions. See Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, 179 (1988); Tuilaepa v. California, 512 U.S. 967, 973 (1994).

Finally, Hurst’s mental-retardation claim fails on the facts. The Florida Supreme Court found that he had already received an evidentiary hearing on mental retardation, that expert evidence did not establish the diagnosis, and that the trial court allowed him to present mental impairments as mitigation. Those findings are entitled to respect. Atkins, 536 U.S. at 317. The Constitution forbids executing the mentally retarded; it does not guarantee a jury trial on that issue. Nor does it require reversal simply because Florida did not demand a unanimous jury recommendation.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
