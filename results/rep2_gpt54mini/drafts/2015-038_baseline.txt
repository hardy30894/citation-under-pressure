**ARGUMENT**

The judgment should be affirmed. ANILCA does not disable the National Park Service from enforcing generally applicable regulations merely because regulated conduct occurs on nonfederal lands or waters within an Alaska park unit. The statute’s text, structure, and history confirm that § 103(c) preserves the State’s title and ordinary jurisdiction over “non-public lands,” but does not create a regulatory-free enclave inside park boundaries. 16 U.S.C. § 3102(c); *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 577 U.S. 424, 432-40 (2016).

I. **Section 103(c) withholds NPS management authority over nonpublic lands, but not every federal regulation that touches them.**

ANILCA defines “public lands” as federal lands and “non-public lands” as all other lands. 16 U.S.C. § 3102(3), (4). Section 103(c) then provides that “only those lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit … which are public lands” are part of the unit for “purposes of this Act,” and that no provision of ANILCA “shall be construed as authorizing the regulation of, or the acquisition by the United States of, any lands which are not public lands.” 16 U.S.C. § 3102(c).

But the Supreme Court has already held that this language does not bar all federal regulation affecting nonfederal land inside park boundaries. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 577 U.S. 424, 438-40 (2016). The Court explained that § 103(c) “deprives the federal government of authority to regulate nonpublic lands under ANILCA” but “does not strip the Government of all power to regulate nonpublic lands within Alaska parks.” *Id.* at 438. Rather, the provision “means only that ANILCA itself does not authorize federal regulation of nonpublic lands.” *Id.* at 439.

That construction accords with the ordinary distinction between land ownership and regulatory jurisdiction recognized in other contexts. Congress often preserves state title while allowing federal regulation arising from independent sources of authority. See *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 543 (1976) (Congress’s power over federal property is “without limitations” save those in the Constitution); *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 525-26 (1897) (federal regulation may reach private lands where necessary to protect federal interests). ANILCA contains no clear statement displacing that background principle. See *Sturgeon*, 577 U.S. at 439-40.

II. **The hovercraft ban is a generally applicable Park Service regulation, not an ANILCA-based assertion of power over state land.**

The challenged regulation forbids hovercraft use in national parks. 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). The Park Service did not invoke ANILCA as the source of authority for that rule; it relied on its general management authority over National Park System units. The Supreme Court held that such a rule is valid so long as it is not “regulation of nonpublic lands under ANILCA.” *Sturgeon*, 577 U.S. at 440.

That is exactly this case. The regulation governs conduct within a park unit and is directed to the Park System as a whole. As the Court explained, § 103(c) “does not withdraw federal authority that exists independently of ANILCA.” *Id.* at 439. So long as the regulation is grounded in an independent federal source, it may apply even where the underlying surface title is nonfederal. *Id.* at 439-40.

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would turn § 103(c) into a sweeping immunity provision for any state, Native, corporate, or private parcel lying inside an Alaska park boundary. Nothing in the text supports that result. The Supreme Court rejected the notion that ANILCA creates “a state-within-a-state” immune from ordinary federal park regulation. *Id.* at 438-39. 

III. **ANILCA’s structure and history confirm that Congress preserved existing federal regulatory authority.**

Section 1319 reinforces this reading. It states that “[n]othing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Congress thus preserved preexisting federal regulatory regimes unless ANILCA expressly displaced them.

The Court’s earlier ANILCA decisions likewise recognize that the statute must be read in light of other federal authorities. In *Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698, 701-02 (9th Cir. 1995), and *John v. United States (Katie John II)*, 247 F.3d 1032, 1037-38 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc), the court construed ANILCA to accommodate existing federal power over Alaska waters rather than to eliminate it. The Supreme Court’s interpretation in *Sturgeon* confirms that approach. 577 U.S. at 439-40.

Sturgeon relies heavily on the phrase “only those lands … which are public lands” in § 103(c). But that clause is best understood as a definitional limitation on what ANILCA itself covers, not as a universal jurisdictional bar. *Sturgeon*, 577 U.S. at 439. Congress knew how to say that nonfederal land would be exempt from federal control if it wanted to do so; it did not do so here. See *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 28-29 (2001) (statutes should be read to give effect to all their words and avoid surplusage).

Because the challenged regulation is not an exercise of ANILCA-based control over state land, the Court should affirm.
