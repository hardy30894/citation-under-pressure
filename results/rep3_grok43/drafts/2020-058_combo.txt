The text of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1254a(f)(4) and 1255(a) must be read together to effectuate congressional intent. Where Congress includes particular language in one section but omits it in another of the same Act, it is presumed to act intentionally. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 413 (1819); United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805). Section 1254a(f)(4) expressly places TPS recipients in “lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” thereby satisfying the inspection-and-admission prerequisite of § 1255(a). This Court has long recognized that statutory terms are to be given their ordinary meaning unless the context requires otherwise. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892).

The structure of the INA confirms that a grant of TPS operates as an admission. Admission and status are not invariably distinct; rather, the conferral of lawful nonimmigrant status has historically been treated as entailing the requisite entry authorization. See Nishimura Ekiu v. United States, 142 U.S. 651, 659 (1892); Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 713 (1893). Petitioners’ original unlawful entries are therefore irrelevant once TPS is granted, for the statute deems them lawfully present as nonimmigrants. A contrary reading would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous, contrary to the rule that every clause must be given effect. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (quoting earlier authorities); Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 489 (1954).

The purpose of the TPS statute—to afford temporary but lawful protection—likewise supports treating the grant as an admission. This Court has repeatedly construed immigration provisions liberally to avoid results that would nullify statutory benefits. See Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953); Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 216 (1953). Requiring TPS recipients who entered without inspection to depart and reenter would frustrate that purpose. See Greene v. McElroy, 360 U.S. 474, 496 (1959); Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. 116, 125 (1958).

Finally, the Third Circuit’s textual distinction between “status” and “admission” finds no support in the statutory scheme or in this Court’s pre-1970 precedents interpreting parallel provisions. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 94 (1958). The judgment should be reversed.
