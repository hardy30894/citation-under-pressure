**ARGUMENT**

I. **Guam’s claim against the United States is barred because the 2004 consent decree triggered CERCLA § 113(f), making contribution—not cost recovery—its exclusive remedy.**

CERCLA gives a potentially responsible party two distinct paths: § 107 cost recovery and § 113 contribution. But the Supreme Court has held that when a party’s liability has been resolved in a judicially approved settlement, § 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution. *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 165-67 (2004). Once § 113 applies, a plaintiff may not bypass its shorter limitations period by recasting the claim as one under § 107. *Atlantic Research Corp. v. United States*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007); *Bernstein v. Bankert*, 733 F.3d 190, 201-02 (7th Cir. 2013); *Solutia, Inc. v. McWane, Inc.*, 672 F.3d 1230, 1237-38 (11th Cir. 2012).

The 2004 consent decree with EPA falls squarely within § 113(f)(3)(B). That provision applies when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has explained that the statutory trigger is a settlement that resolves liability for a response action, not one that conclusively adjudicates fault. *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. at 139; *Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167. Courts of appeals likewise look to whether the settlement imposes cleanup obligations that settle a response action. *Bernstein*, 733 F.3d at 201-02; *Hobart Corp. v. Waste Mgmt. of Ohio, Inc.*, 758 F.3d 757, 767-68 (6th Cir. 2014); *ASARCO LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1122-24 (9th Cir. 2017).

That is exactly what the decree here did. It required Guam to close Ordot, design and install a cover system, and undertake remedial measures to address the dump’s discharge of leachate. Those obligations resolved Guam’s liability to the United States for the response action that EPA had demanded in the CWA suit. A settlement need not use talismanic words like “CERCLA liability” to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B); substance controls over form. *Bernstein*, 733 F.3d at 202; *ASARCO*, 866 F.3d at 1123; *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1001-02 (6th Cir. 2015).

Guam’s reliance on the decree’s no-admission clause does not alter that result. Courts consistently hold that an express disclaimer of liability does not prevent a settlement from resolving liability within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B) where the agreement imposes concrete response obligations. *ASARCO*, 866 F.3d at 1123; *Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Indus. Inc.*, 937 F.3d 928, 931-32 (8th Cir. 2019); *Hobart Corp.*, 758 F.3d at 768. CERCLA asks whether the party has “resolved its liability,” not whether it has admitted fault. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Guam’s decree did resolve liability: it fixed Guam’s obligations to close and cap the dump and thereby ended EPA’s enforcement action.

II. **Because Guam’s § 113 claim accrued in 2004, its 2017 suit is untimely, and § 107 cannot resurrect it.**

CERCLA’s statute of limitations for contribution claims is three years from the date of a judicially approved settlement. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). The Supreme Court has recognized that § 113(f) and § 107 are distinct remedies, each with its own limitations rules. *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 139-40. Once a plaintiff has a § 113 claim, however, it must sue within § 113(g)(3)’s three-year period. *Bernstein*, 733 F.3d at 201; *Hobart Corp.*, 758 F.3d at 771. Guam waited until 2017—thirteen years after the decree—to sue. Its claim is therefore time-barred.

Allowing Guam to proceed under § 107 would nullify Congress’s carefully calibrated structure. The Supreme Court in *Cooper Industries* rejected the notion that a party may use § 107 to obtain contribution-style relief outside § 113’s limits. 543 U.S. at 168-69. And *Atlantic Research* held that § 107 remains available only to a party that seeks to recover costs it incurred directly, not to evade § 113’s contribution regime. 551 U.S. at 139-40. Guam seeks reimbursement for costs imposed by the 2004 decree; that is contribution, not direct cost recovery.

III. **The Navy—not Guam—should bear the environmental burden of the Ordot Dump.**

CERCLA was enacted to ensure that “the costs of remediation are borne by those responsible for the contamination.” *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 602 (2009); *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55-56 (1998). The Navy operated the dump for decades and used it for military waste, including hazardous substances. But CERCLA’s allocation rules do not authorize Guam to shift that burden through an untimely § 107 claim after it resolved its liability in a judicially approved settlement. *Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167; *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 139.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
