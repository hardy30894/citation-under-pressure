**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal courts possess broad equitable authority to order structural relief—including a population limit calibrated to a prison system's design capacity—when such relief is necessary to eliminate a proven Eighth Amendment violation.**

Once a constitutional violation has been established, "equity has been characterized by a practical flexibility in shaping its remedies and by a facility for adjusting and reconciling public and private needs." *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955) (*Brown II*). The remedial power of a court of equity is not confined to the precise mechanism that produced the violation; rather, "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321, 329-30 (1944). A continuing decree, moreover, is not static: it must remain responsive to the facts as they develop, for a court supervising compliance retains authority to adapt its remedy as circumstances require. *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114-15 (1932).

These principles compelled the result below. Having found, after extensive evidentiary hearings, that overcrowding was the primary cause of the ongoing constitutional deprivation, the three-judge court did not fashion an arbitrary sanction; it identified the single variable—population in excess of design capacity—that was producing the violation and tailored relief directly to that cause. That is precisely the kind of "practical flexibility" *Brown II* contemplates. The State's insistence that a population-reduction order is categorically impermissible cannot be reconciled with the settled understanding that federal courts sitting in equity may order the state actor to dismantle the very structural condition responsible for the deprivation of constitutional rights. Nor may a State avoid a remedy properly addressed to a proven violation by invoking administrative inconvenience or the burdens of compliance; "the constitutional rights of [inmates]" are not to be sacrificed to the difficulty of the State's own choosing. *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 16 (1958).

**II. Overcrowding of the magnitude found below works the kind of systemic deprivation the Eighth Amendment forbids, and a remedy addressed to population is therefore addressed to the violation itself.**

The Eighth Amendment is not "fastened to the obsolete," *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910); it "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). Applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, that guarantee condemns conditions that, "in the light of contemporary human knowledge," would be "universally thought to be an infliction of cruel and unusual punishment." *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962). The district court's findings—undisturbed here—establish that overcrowding at nearly double design capacity was the root cause of a health-care delivery system incapable of providing constitutionally adequate care, producing, in the language of *Robinson*, precisely the sort of condition contemporary standards forbid a State to inflict. See also *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459 (1947) (Eighth Amendment inquiry turns on the character of the punishment actually imposed, not merely its label). A remedy that reduces the population to the level the system can constitutionally sustain is therefore not an intrusion collateral to the violation; it is the remedy for the violation.

**III. The order is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary, and rests on factual findings entitled to deference.**

Any remedy of this kind must, of course, be no broader than required to cure the violation—but that limiting principle was honored here, not violated. The three-judge court did not order release of any particular number of prisoners; it set a population ceiling tied to the design capacity of the facilities themselves, leaving the State maximum discretion in choosing among methods of compliance. That approach reflects the same restraint this Court has long required of equity courts, which must "mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case," *Hecht Co.*, 321 U.S. at 329-30, without exceeding what those necessities demand.

The State's disagreement with the district court's predicate findings—that overcrowding caused the violation and that no lesser remedy would suffice—does not warrant reversal. Such findings are factual, made after years of litigation, extensive evidentiary hearings, and firsthand supervision of a receivership whose failure the State itself precipitated. They may be set aside only if "the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). No such showing has been made. The three-judge court's careful, record-based conclusion that population reduction was the necessary and narrowest available remedy is entitled to the deference that findings of this character have always received.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the order below should be affirmed.
