**ARGUMENT**

**I. CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS DO NOT CREATE A PROTECTED LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST UNDER THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that clemency proceedings are an "integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). This misapprehension ignores the longstanding principle that clemency is an act of executive grace, not a judicial function. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Ex parte Grossman*, 277 U.S. 87, 120 (1928), "[t]he power of the President to pardon ... is an act of grace." This principle applies equally to state governors exercising clemency authority. *See* *Seling v. Young*, 531 U.S. 250, 262 (2000) (noting that clemency is "an exercise of grace").

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that due process protections do not attach to executive clemency. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), the Court held that a pardon "is the private, though official, act of the executive himself," and that its validity "cannot be questioned." Similarly, in *Ex parte Wells*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 311 (1856), the Court stated that clemency is "a matter of executive discretion" and "not subject to judicial review." These cases establish that clemency proceedings do not implicate due process protections because they are not adjudicative in nature.

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS COMPELLING AN INMATE TO PARTICIPATE IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW WHERE HIS STATEMENTS MAY BE USED AGAINST HIM IN FUTURE PROCEEDINGS.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that "[n]o person ... shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. This right applies not only to formal criminal trials but also to any proceeding where an individual's statements may be used against him in a criminal case. *See Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (holding that the Fifth Amendment "is as applicable to a proceeding for removal from office as it is to a proceeding for indictment for crime").

In *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616, 633 (1886), the Court held that the Fifth Amendment "applies to all invasions on the part of the government and its employees of the sanctity of a man's home and the privacies of life." The Court further explained that the Amendment "forbids the compulsory production of a man's private papers" and "forbids any compulsory discovery by extorting the party's oath or compelling the production of his private books and papers." *Id.* at 633-34. This principle extends to compelled statements in any context where they may later be used against the individual.

The Sixth Circuit's suggestion that an inmate may voluntarily participate in a clemency interview without waiving his Fifth Amendment rights is misguided. As the Court held in *United States v. Murdock*, 284 U.S. 141, 148 (1931), "[t]he privilege against self-incrimination is not to be trifled with." The Court further explained that "[i]f the individual is compelled to incriminate himself, he is thereafter protected against the use of his compelled testimony in any criminal case against him." *Id.* at 148-49. Thus, even if an inmate voluntarily participates in a clemency interview, his statements may not be used against him in future criminal proceedings.

**III. THE "HOBSON'S CHOICE" OF PARTICIPATING IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW OR ASSERTING THE FIFTH AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

The Sixth Circuit's conclusion that an inmate faces no unconstitutional "Hobson's choice" in deciding whether to participate in a clemency interview is erroneous. The Due Process Clause prohibits the government from imposing conditions that effectively compel an individual to waive his constitutional rights. *See* *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972) (holding that due process prohibits the government from "imposing a condition on the receipt of a benefit" that infringes on constitutional rights).

In *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 581 (1968), the Court struck down a federal kidnapping statute that imposed the death penalty only upon defendants who exercised their right to a jury trial. The Court held that the statute "needlessly penalize[d] the assertion of a constitutional right" and thus violated the Due Process Clause. Similarly, Ohio's clemency process needlessly penalizes an inmate's assertion of his Fifth Amendment rights by creating a situation where participation in the interview may result in self-incrimination.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit's decision and hold that clemency proceedings do not create a protected life or liberty interest under the Due Process Clause and that the Fifth Amendment prohibits compelling an inmate to participate in a clemency interview where his statements may be used against him in future proceedings.
