The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires that a tax be assessed against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, both a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. Galletti v. United States (In re Galletti), 298 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir. 2002).

The assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. The Code's assessment provision, 26 U.S.C. § 6203, requires that the assessment "identify[ ] the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The assessment against the partnership identifies the partnership as the taxpayer, not the individual partners. As the Ninth Circuit noted, "the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified 'taxpayer.'" Galletti, 298 F.3d at 1112.

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. Id. at 910. However, the court also noted that the government would have had to "resort to court action" or individually assess the taxes against the partner to collect the tax. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D.Cal.1959). The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. Galletti, 298 F.3d at 1113-14.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson supports the conclusion that an assessment against a partnership is insufficient to collect the tax from individual partners. 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). In Coson, the court held that a tax lien against a partner's property was invalid because the partner had not been individually assessed. Id. at 462-63. While the court relied on a different ground than the district court, the decision reinforces the importance of identifying the taxpayer in an assessment.

Under California law, a creditor must obtain a judgment against a partner to collect a partnership debt from the partner's assets. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The government has not obtained a judgment against the individual partners, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Therefore, the government's claim against the individual partners is unenforceable, and the bankruptcy court properly disallowed the claim. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

In conclusion, the government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is contrary to the Internal Revenue Code and relevant case law. The assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners, and the government has not obtained a judgment against the partners as required by California law. Therefore, the bankruptcy court's disallowance of the government's claim should be affirmed.
