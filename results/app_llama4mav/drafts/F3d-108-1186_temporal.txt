## Argument

The district court erred in interpreting the jury's verdicts, and Unit is entitled to a judgment of $70,768.02. The jury's award of $70,768.02 on Form 1 and a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3 is ambiguous, and the district court should have clarified the verdict by questioning the jury.

The district court's interpretation of the verdicts was incorrect because it failed to consider the possibility that the jury had already taken into account Enron's set-off claim when awarding damages to Unit on Form 1. As the court held in Fairmount Glass Works v. Cub Fork Coal Co., 287 U.S. 474, 485 (1933), "where the meaning of a verdict is so ambiguous that it is impossible to tell what the jury intended, the court may interrogate the jury to clarify its meaning." The district court's refusal to do so was error.

Unit requested that the trial judge question the jury to clarify the damage awards, but the court refused. This refusal was an abuse of discretion, as the court had a duty to ensure that the verdict was clear and unambiguous. See Chicago & N.W. Ry. Co. v. Dunleavy, 129 F. 745, 748 (8th Cir. 1904) ("It is the duty of the trial court to see that the verdict is not only not defective in form, but that it is also free from ambiguity in its meaning.").

Furthermore, the jury instructions were confusing and misleading, which contributed to the ambiguity in the verdicts. Although Unit did not object specifically to the instructions at trial, the court's error in interpreting the verdicts is still reviewable. The court's instruction on damages allowed the jury to measure Unit's damages in one of two ways, but it did not clearly indicate that the jury should not award Enron a set-off if it had already taken that amount into account in awarding damages to Unit. The court's failure to provide clear instructions led to the ambiguous verdict.

The case of Texas & P. Ry. Co. v. Buckles, 232 F. 508, 512 (5th Cir. 1916), is instructive here. In that case, the court held that "where the jury's verdict is ambiguous or uncertain, it is the duty of the court to direct the jury to reconsider it and put it in proper form." The district court should have followed this procedure to clarify the verdict.

In conclusion, the district court erred in interpreting the jury's verdicts, and Unit is entitled to a judgment of $70,768.02. The court's refusal to clarify the verdict by questioning the jury was an abuse of discretion, and the case should be remanded for entry of judgment in favor of Unit.
