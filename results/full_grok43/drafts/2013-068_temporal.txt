The Massachusetts statute is a permissible regulation of the time, place, and manner of expression. It advances the State's interest in protecting access to medical facilities and ensuring public safety on streets and sidewalks. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) ("The right of assembly is not absolute."); Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949) (recognizing that "the power of a municipality to impose reasonable restrictions on the use of its streets" is well established).

The restriction is content neutral on its face. It applies equally to all speakers within the fixed zones and does not turn on the message conveyed. See Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368 (1931) (invalidating only those statutes that "make[] it a crime to communicate" particular ideas). Because the measure is neutral, it need not satisfy the stricter scrutiny reserved for content-based enactments. See Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931) (distinguishing prior restraints aimed at specific publications from general regulatory measures).

The zones are narrowly drawn to serve the identified interests without suppressing more speech than necessary. The thirty-five-foot radius leaves open the adjacent public ways for oral advocacy, signs, and literature distribution. See Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 160 (1939) (noting that "a municipality may control the use of its streets" when the regulation "does not abridge the constitutional liberty" of expression). Petitioners retain the ability to approach patients before they enter the zones, to display messages from outside the zones, and to use amplification where otherwise permitted. See Milk Wagon Drivers Union v. Meadowmoor Dairies, Inc., 312 U.S. 287, 294 (1941) (upholding limits on picketing that left "other means of communication" available).

Alternative channels remain adequate. Sidewalks outside the zones, nearby public property, and private property across the street continue to permit face-to-face conversation at conversational distance, distribution of leaflets, and display of placards. See Breard v. Alexandria, 341 U.S. 622, 631 (1951) (sustaining a prohibition that "does not forbid the distribution of literature" by other means). The statute therefore satisfies the requirements for a valid time, place, and manner regulation.

The exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and emergency personnel do not render the law content based or underinclusive. They are tied to the functional needs of the regulated sites rather than to any speaker's viewpoint. See Giboney v. Empire Storage & Ice Co., 336 U.S. 490, 502 (1949) (upholding an injunction that "was reasonably necessary to protect" legitimate state interests). Because the measure leaves open ample means of communication while addressing documented safety and access concerns, it does not offend the First Amendment as applied to the three clinics at issue.
