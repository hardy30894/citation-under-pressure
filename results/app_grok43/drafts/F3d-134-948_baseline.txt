I. The District Court Erred in Deeming Selam’s Confrontation Claim Waived for Lack of Tribal Exhaustion.

The Indian Civil Rights Act guarantees that “[n]o Indian tribe in exercising powers of self-government shall . . . deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right . . . to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). Federal habeas review under 25 U.S.C. § 1303 is the sole federal mechanism to vindicate that right. Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 66-67 (1978). Although tribal remedies must ordinarily be exhausted before resort to federal court, the requirement is not jurisdictional and yields when a petitioner has no meaningful opportunity to present the federal claim in tribal proceedings. See National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe of Indians, 471 U.S. 845, 856 n.21 (1985); see also United States v. Superior Court, 144 F.3d 1210, 1213 (9th Cir. 1998).

Selam’s tribal appeal raised compulsory-process violations arising from the same trials in which the five-year-old’s out-of-court statements were introduced through four witnesses without any opportunity for cross-examination. The tribal appellate decision dismissing the appeal without hearing necessarily adjudicated the fairness of those proceedings. Requiring a second, separate confrontation objection in the same appeal would impose a hypertechnical exhaustion rule foreign to federal habeas practice. See Rose v. Lundy, 455 U.S. 509, 518-19 (1982) (exhaustion satisfied when substance of federal claim fairly presented). The district court therefore erred in holding the claim waived.

II. Selam Was Denied Compulsory Process Guaranteed by the Indian Civil Rights Act.

The same statute secures “the right . . . to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This guarantee is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right recognized in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). Selam supplied his lay spokesperson with the names of five character witnesses. Although the tribal judge and counsel advised him of the subpoena power, the record shows no mechanism by which an incarcerated defendant could compel attendance without affirmative judicial assistance. The failure to issue process when requested by an indigent defendant violates the compulsory-process guarantee. See Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 407-08 (1988). The district court’s contrary finding is unsupported by the record and must be reversed.
