**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a) Unambiguously Requires Remand After Pretrial Proceedings; a Transferee Court Lacks Authority to Invoke Section 1404(a) to Retain a Case for Trial.**

The language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) is crystalline: after coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings conclude, each transferred action “shall be remanded by the panel.” *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Use of the word “shall” imposes a mandatory duty upon the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation, not a discretionary option for the transferee court. *Cf. Supervisors v. United States*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 435, 446 (1866) (“shall” is “imperative” and “leaves no discretion”). The statute commits the remand decision solely to the Panel; no provision delegates that authority to the transferee district court.

Section 1404(a) authorizes a district court to transfer a civil action “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). By its plain text, this provision permits transfer to another district, not retention by the same court. A district court cannot “transfer” a case to itself; such an act is a legal impossibility. *See General Electric Co. v. Marvel Rare Metals Co.*, 287 U.S. 430, 435 (1932) (statutory construction “must be reasonable” and avoid “absurd consequences”). The district court’s self-assignment under § 1404(a) thus contravenes both the statutory text and the settled principle that venue provisions are to be strictly construed. *See Ex parte Schollenberger*, 96 U.S. 369, 376-77 (1877).

**II. The Structure of Section 1407 Forecloses Transferee Courts from Short-Circuiting the Panel’s Remand Duty.**

Congress enacted § 1407 to centralize pretrial proceedings while preserving plaintiffs’ traditional venue choices for trial. *See H.R. Rep. No. 90-1130* (1968), *reprinted in* 1968 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1898, 1900 (statute designed to “avoid the necessity of transferring cases for trial away from the district where they were originally brought”). The Panel selects the transferee district based solely on pretrial efficiency, not trial convenience. *Id.* at 1901. Allowing a transferee court to retain a case for trial nullifies Congress’s careful balance, subverting the right of plaintiffs to litigate in their chosen forum. *See St. Louis & S.F. Ry. v. McBride*, 141 U.S. 127, 131 (1891) (venue statutes reflect deliberate legislative policy).

The statute’s mandatory remand clause leaves no room for transferee courts to preempt the Panel’s role. *Compare* 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (Panel “shall” remand), *with* 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) (court “may” transfer). Congress knew how to grant transfer authority to district courts; it did so in § 1404(a). But in § 1407(a), it expressly reserved the remand function to the Panel. A transferee court that invokes § 1404(a) to retain a case usurps that exclusive statutory power. *See M’Culloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819) (“[W]here the Constitution gives a power, it does not forbid the exercise of that power, but it may be regulated by the legislature.”).

**III. The Transferee Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Entertain a Section 1404(a) Motion for Self-Transfer.**

A district court’s authority during pretrial proceedings under § 1407 is limited to “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). A motion to transfer venue under § 1404(a) does not pertain to pretrial matters common to the multidistricted litigation; it is a dispositive question of forum, collateral to the coordinated proceedings. *See United States v. New York Telephone Co.*, 326 U.S. 638, 643 (1946) (jurisdiction “is limited by the terms of the statute” conferring it). The transferee court’s power extends only as far as necessary to accomplish the Panel’s mandate. *See Ex parte Reed*, 100 U.S. 13, 17 (1879) (courts of limited jurisdiction must act “within the bounds prescribed”). Because § 1407(a) contemplates remand before trial, any § 1404(a) motion must be addressed by the original transferor court after remand. *Cf. The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913) (venue is determined as of the time of filing; subsequent events cannot retroactively alter proper forum).

**IV. The Practice of Self-Transfer Violates Fundamental Principles of Venue and Forum Non Conveniens.**

Even before Congress enacted § 1404(a), this Court recognized that venue restrictions protect defendants from litigation in inconvenient forums and plaintiffs from coercive forum manipulation. *See Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. v. Vigor*, 90 U.S. 611, 614 (1874) (venue statutes “prevent the injustice and oppression of being compelled to litigate in a foreign tribunal”). Section 1404(a) codified the common-law doctrine of forum non conveniens, authorizing transfer only when the convenience of parties and witnesses and the interests of justice so require. *See Norwood v. Kirkpatrick*, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955). A transferee court that “transfers” a case to itself cannot engage in the balancing essential to § 1404(a) analysis because it already possesses the case; the motion is circular. Such a practice strips the plaintiff of any meaningful opportunity to oppose transfer to a district never chosen by the Panel. *See Swift & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 311, 325 (1928) (“[P]rocedural rights are not to be taken away by implication.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
