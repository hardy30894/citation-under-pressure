## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Denying Tarmac's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on Stelwagon's Robinson-Patman Act Claim Because Stelwagon Failed to Prove Actual Antitrust Damages

The Robinson-Patman Act prohibits price discrimination that may substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. 15 U.S.C. § 13(a) (1982). To recover damages under the Clayton Act, a plaintiff must prove that it suffered actual antitrust damages as a result of the defendant's violation. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562-63 (1931). The Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff must show a causal connection between the defendant's wrongful act and the plaintiff's injury. Id. at 566.

In this case, Stelwagon failed to present sufficient evidence of actual antitrust damages. The record shows that Stelwagon's damages calculations were based on speculative assumptions and lacked a clear causal link to Tarmac's alleged price discrimination. As the Court held in Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946), "the most elementary conceptions of justice and public policy require that the wrongdoer shall bear the risk of the uncertainty which his own wrong has created." However, the plaintiff still bears the burden of proving that it suffered actual damages. Id. at 265. Stelwagon's failure to meet this burden precludes it from recovering damages under the Clayton Act.

The district court's denial of Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law was therefore erroneous. The court should have granted Tarmac's motion, as Stelwagon failed to prove a crucial element of its claim.

### II. The District Court's Award of Treble Damages Was Erroneous

The district court's award of treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act was also erroneous. As established in Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562-63 (1931), a plaintiff must prove actual antitrust damages to recover under the Clayton Act. Since Stelwagon failed to meet this burden, the district court should not have trebled the damages award.

The Supreme Court has also held that the treble damages provision is intended to punish and deter anticompetitive conduct, not to provide a windfall to plaintiffs. See Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp., 392 U.S. 481, 494 (1968). In this case, the district court's award of treble damages was not justified, as Stelwagon failed to prove actual antitrust damages.

### III. The District Court's Judgment Should Be Vacated Insofar as It Awards Stelwagon Treble Damages Under Section 4 of the Clayton Act

Given Stelwagon's failure to prove actual antitrust damages, the district court's judgment should be vacated insofar as it awards Stelwagon treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act. The Court should reverse the district court's denial of Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law and vacate the treble damages award.
