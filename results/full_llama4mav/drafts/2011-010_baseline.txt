ARGUMENT

I.   THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO WORKERS INJURED AS A DIRECT RESULT OF OPERATIONS CONDUCTED ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct causal link between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted this provision to require a "situs-of-injury" test, holding that an employee must be injured on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it to be eligible for OCSLA benefits. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986), suggesting that § 1333(b) superimposes a status requirement on the OCSLA situs requirement.

In contrast, the Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test, adopted in this case, is overly broad and ignores the statutory language requiring a direct causal link between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP, 693 F.3d 6936, 6941 (9th Cir. 2010). The Third Circuit's "but for" test, adopted in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Serv., Inc., 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988), is similarly flawed, as it would extend OCSLA coverage to employees injured in circumstances only tangentially related to outer Continental Shelf operations.

The correct interpretation of § 1333(b) is that it requires a direct causal link between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf, and that the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. This interpretation is consistent with the statutory language and the Supreme Court's dictum in Tallentire.

II.  THE BRB CORRECTLY DENIED BENEFITS UNDER THE LHWCA

The Benefits Review Board ("BRB") correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA because Juan Valladolid's injury did not occur on a maritime situs. The LHWCA requires that an employee be injured on "navigable waters" or on an "adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a).

The Ninth Circuit has adopted a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is an "adjoining area" under § 903(a). Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). Applying this test, the BRB correctly found that La Conchita, the onshore oil-processing facility where Valladolid was killed, was not a maritime situs.

La Conchita is not adjacent to any maritime facilities, and its primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, a non-maritime activity. Although scrap metal from the platforms was dumped at La Conchita, this activity does not convert the facility into a maritime situs. The handling of scrap metal at La Conchita did not involve "moving cargo directly from ship to land transportation," as required by the Supreme Court's decision in P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69, 82-83 (1979).

The BRB's denial of benefits under the LHWCA was therefore correct, and the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of this decision should be upheld.
