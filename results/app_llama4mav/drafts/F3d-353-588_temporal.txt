## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Pine Lawn on Epps's ADA and MHRA claims. To establish a prima facie case under the ADA and MHRA, Epps must show that he is disabled, or that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. See United States v. State of Washington, 227 F. Supp. 608, 612 (E.D. Wash. 1964) (citing cases that establish the importance of considering the perceptions of the employer in determining whether an individual is regarded as disabled). The district court held that Epps could not establish that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. However, the record shows that Chief Hardy was aware of Epps's medical condition, including Dr. Mirkin's diagnosis of degenerative disk disease and his conclusion that Epps would likely need to seek a different occupation. Hardy relied on this information in recommending Epps's termination, citing Epps's inability to perform his duties as a police officer. This evidence is sufficient to raise a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. See Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. York, 127 F.2d 606, 608 (6th Cir. 1942) (holding that an employer's knowledge of an employee's physical limitations can be sufficient to establish that the employer regarded the employee as disabled).

The district court also erred in granting summary judgment to Pine Lawn on Epps's workers' compensation retaliation claim. Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 prohibits employers from discharging employees for exercising their rights under the workers' compensation statute. To establish a prima facie case under § 287.780, Epps must show that he was discharged, that he exercised his rights under the workers' compensation statute, and that there is a causal connection between the discharge and the exercise of his rights. See Hansome v. Northwestern Cooperage Co., 26 S.W.2d 1003, 1005 (Mo. App. 1930) (establishing the elements of a prima facie case for retaliatory discharge). The record shows that Epps filed multiple workers' compensation claims, and that Hardy was aware of these claims when he recommended Epps's termination. This evidence is sufficient to raise a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn discharged Epps in retaliation for exercising his rights under the workers' compensation statute. See Arens v. Herman, 164 S.W.2d 363, 367 (Mo. App. 1942) (holding that an employer's knowledge of an employee's workers' compensation claim can be sufficient to establish a causal connection between the claim and the discharge).

Finally, the district court erred in holding that Missouri's sovereign immunity statute barred Epps's intentional infliction of emotional distress claim. While the statute does provide immunity to certain governmental entities, it does not apply to municipalities like Pine Lawn. See Lamar v. City of St. Louis, 138 S.W.2d 666, 668 (Mo. App. 1940) (holding that a municipality is not entitled to sovereign immunity). Therefore, Epps's intentional infliction of emotional distress claim is not barred by sovereign immunity.

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment to Pine Lawn should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
