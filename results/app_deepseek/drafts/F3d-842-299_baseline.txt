**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying the Pressls’ motion to remand because it lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this purely state-law declaratory judgment action. Neither 28 U.S.C. § 1331 nor 16 U.S.C. § 825p provides a basis for federal jurisdiction.

**I. The Complaint Does Not Arise Under Federal Law**

The well-pleaded complaint rule governs whether a case “arises under” federal law for purposes of § 1331. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Jurisdiction exists only if a federal question appears on the face of the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint. *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987). The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under a flowage easement—a state-law property instrument. The complaint does not mention the Federal Power Act, FERC, or any federal statute. It asks the court to interpret the easement’s language and declare that APCO lacks regulatory authority beyond the rights defined in that easement. Such a claim is quintessentially a matter of Virginia property law.

APCO cannot create federal jurisdiction by asserting a federal defense, such as the existence of a FERC license. *Mottley*, 211 U.S. at 152. The district court therefore erred in concluding that the Pressls’ claim “arises under” federal law simply because APCO’s hydroelectric project is federally licensed. The well-pleaded complaint rule prohibits removal based on a federal defense. *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust*, 463 U.S. 1, 10 (1983).

**II. Section 825p Does Not Confer Jurisdiction**

The district court also relied on 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants federal courts jurisdiction over “suits brought to enforce any liability or duty created by, or to enjoin any violation of,” the Federal Power Act. But the Pressls’ suit does not seek to enforce a federal duty or enjoin a federal violation. They seek only a declaration of their state-law rights under a private easement. Section 825p does not create a private right of action for landowners to challenge a licensee’s interpretation of an easement. *See California v. FERC*, 495 U.S. 490, 497 (1990) (recognizing that state property law governs easements unless preempted). The Pressls’ claim does not “arise under” the Federal Power Act merely because APCO holds a FERC license. *See Pan Am. Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court*, 366 U.S. 656, 663 (1961) (federal jurisdiction requires that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial federal question).

**III. The District Court’s Alternative Holding on Exhaustion Is Irrelevant**

The district court’s dismissal also rested on the notion that the Pressls should have raised their complaints with FERC before filing suit. But the Pressls do not challenge APCO’s license or any FERC order; they seek only to enforce their state-law property rights. Exhaustion of administrative remedies is not required when the claim does not implicate federal regulatory authority. *See McCarthy v. Madigan*, 503 U.S. 140, 144 (1992) (exhaustion applies only when Congress has required it). The Pressls’ suit is a straightforward state-law declaratory judgment action over which the federal courts lack jurisdiction.

**Conclusion**

Because the Pressls’ complaint raises no federal question, the district court should have remanded the case to state court. This Court should vacate the judgment and remand with instructions to remand to the state court.
