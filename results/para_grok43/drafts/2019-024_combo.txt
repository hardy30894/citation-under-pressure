ARGUMENT

The three-year bar of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) runs from the earliest date a plaintiff possessed actual knowledge of the breach. That knowledge is supplied when the fiduciary furnishes the operative facts, for the statute measures awareness of the transaction itself, not of its legal consequences. Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (limitations “begins to run from the time the fraud is discovered” by the means provided); Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 141 (1879) (“the means of knowledge are the same thing in effect as knowledge itself”); Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435, 447 (1918) (record facts “charge the party with knowledge”).

This Court has long held that a party who receives documents disclosing the essential acts cannot later disclaim knowledge. Stearns v. Page, 48 U.S. 819, 829 (1849) (plaintiff “chargeable with knowledge of all facts which he might have learned by an examination” of furnished papers); Kirby v. Lake Shore & M.S.R. Co., 120 U.S. 130, 138 (1887) (service of statements “imparted actual knowledge” of the transactions). The same rule governs trustees and fiduciaries. Michoud v. Girod, 45 U.S. 503, 553 (1846) (beneficiary “presumed to have knowledge” of investments once accounts are rendered); Michoud, 45 U.S. at 554 (“actual knowledge” arises from “the means of information which were within his reach”).

Congress eliminated the constructive-knowledge clause in 1987, leaving only the actual-knowledge trigger. That change did not alter the settled meaning of actual knowledge. Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946) (equitable tolling “vindicated” only where plaintiff “neither knew nor by reasonable diligence could have known” the facts); United States v. Diamond Coal & Coke Co., 255 U.S. 323, 334 (1921) (limitations runs once “the facts constituting the fraud were known or could have been known”). Disclosure therefore starts the period; failure to read the documents does not suspend it. Wood, 101 U.S. at 143 (“negligent ignorance” supplies no excuse).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary view—that knowledge of imprudence itself is required—contradicts these authorities. The statute demands knowledge of “the breach or violation,” which is knowledge of the acts alleged to constitute the breach. Bailey, 88 U.S. at 348; Wood, 101 U.S. at 141. Requiring awareness of legal impropriety would revive the repealed constructive-knowledge provision by another name. Stearns, 48 U.S. at 829.

Because the disclosures placed the investment decisions before Sulyma more than three years before suit, the action is time-barred. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
