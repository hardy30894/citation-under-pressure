**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO THE FIRST AMENDMENT REQUIRES PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis’s conviction under an objective standard that dispenses with any showing of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten. This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects even repugnant speech unless it falls within a narrow, historically rooted exception. *See, e.g., Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–72 (1942) (listing “fighting words” as a proscribable category). The “true threat” exception is no exception to this rule. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that “true threats” fall outside First Amendment protection).

In *Black*, this Court expressly held that the First Amendment requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten. *Id.* at 360 (“[T]he speaker must have intended the statement as a threat.”). The plurality cautioned that an objective standard—one that asks only whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening—risks punishing protected speech. *Id.* at 360–61. The Court reiterated this principle in *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015), vacating and remanding the Third Circuit’s decision, and emphasizing that the First Amendment demands a showing of subjective intent. *Id.* at 733–34.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—adopted in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991)—is irreconcilable with *Black*. The Third Circuit held that a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. *Kosma*, 951 F.2d at 557. But this objective standard conflicts with *Black*’s holding that the First Amendment requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. The Third Circuit’s rule also risks punishing protected speech, as it permits conviction based on how a statement is perceived, rather than what the speaker intended. *Id.* at 360–61.

This Court should reject the Third Circuit’s objective standard and hold that the First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent to threaten. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 360; *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 733–34. Without such a showing, Elonis’s conviction cannot stand.

II. **THE JURY INSTRUCTIONS WERE ERRONEOUS AND PREJUDICIAL.**

The district court instructed the jury that a “true threat” is one that a reasonable speaker would foresee would be interpreted as a threat. This instruction misstated the law by omitting any requirement of subjective intent. The error was plain, as it relieved the government of its burden to prove Elonis’s intent to threaten. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 9 (1999) (holding that a jury instruction omitting an element of the offense is structural error).

The Third Circuit’s attempt to reconcile its objective standard with *Black* is unavailing. The Third Circuit reasoned that even under a subjective intent standard, the jury could have found that Elonis intended his statements to be threats based on the surrounding circumstances. *United States v. Elonis*, 730 F.3d 321, 333 (3d Cir. 2013). But this reasoning conflates the standard of proof with the elements of the offense. The First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent as an element of the offense, not as a factual inference. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360.

The erroneous instruction was prejudicial, as it permitted the jury to convict Elonis based on how his statements were perceived, rather than what he intended. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) (holding that a jury must find every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt). The government’s evidence of Elonis’s intent was thin, resting largely on the content of his posts and the reactions of his victims. Without proof of subjective intent, the jury’s verdict cannot stand.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and remand with instructions to vacate Elonis’s conviction.
