## ARGUMENT

Section 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of "any communication containing any threat . . . to injure the person of another." 18 U.S.C. §875(c). The statute does not specify the mental state that the government must prove with respect to the threatening character of the communication. The Third Circuit filled that gap with an objective, negligence-like standard, asking only whether a "reasonable person" would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. That holding contravenes two independent bodies of this Court's precedent: the longstanding presumption that criminal statutes require proof of a culpable mental state, and the First Amendment's true-threats doctrine, which turns on the speaker's own intent. Both bodies of law require reversal.

**I. The Presumption in Favor of Scienter Requires Proof that Elonis Intended His Statements to Be Understood as Threats.**

This Court has "long ... resisted reading criminal statutes to eliminate a mens rea requirement." *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994). That interpretive rule rests on the recognition that the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention "is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Silence in a statute "as to whether mens rea is required . . . should not be construed as eliminating that element." *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 605.

The presumption applies with particular force where an objective standard would criminalize a substantial amount of protected or innocent conduct. In *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64 (1994), the Court read a knowledge requirement into a statute that, as a matter of grammar, did not naturally carry one, explaining that the Court did "not think Congress intended" to allow "a defendant [to] be convicted . . . without any knowledge of the age" of the performers. *Id.* at 68, 78. The same logic governs here. Absent a subjective-intent requirement, §875(c) sweeps in speakers who never meant to threaten anyone—rap lyrics, dark jokes, therapeutic venting—so long as some hypothetical reasonable listener might read the words as threatening. That is precisely the kind of strict-liability regime for otherwise ambiguous expressive conduct that *Staples* and *X-Citement Video* forbid absent clear congressional intent to dispense with mens rea. Congress gave no such indication in §875(c)'s text.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Exception Requires Proof of the Speaker's Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

Even apart from the general presumption of scienter, the Constitution independently demands a subjective standard here because §875(c) reaches pure speech. Threatening speech falls outside the First Amendment's protection only when it constitutes a "true threat." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). This Court defined that category by reference to what the *speaker* means to do, not merely how a listener might perceive the statement: "'True threats' encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." *Id.* (emphasis added). The word "means" is a statement about the defendant's own state of mind—not an objective, reasonable-listener proxy for it.

That focus on the speaker's intent is consistent with this Court's earlier true-threats decision, *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), which held that a statute proscribing threats against the President "must be interpreted with the commands of the First Amendment clearly in mind," and that political "hyperbole" is not a true threat even if a listener might momentarily take it as one. *Id.* at 707–08. The distinction *Watts* drew between punishable threats and protected, if provocative, speech depends on whether the speaker actually meant to threaten—an inquiry the Third Circuit's objective test erases entirely by asking only what a reasonable observer would think, regardless of what the defendant intended to convey.

The Third Circuit's own decision below acknowledged that it was relying on pre-*Black* Third Circuit precedent, *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which asked only whether "a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat." Pet. App. 3. That standard is functionally a negligence test: it asks what a reasonable person *should* have foreseen, not what Elonis *actually intended*. A negligence standard is the "least culpable" mental state, and this Court does not lightly presume Congress intended to impose criminal liability on that basis for core protected expression. *See Staples*, 511 U.S. at 616–19 (declining to adopt a standard that would criminalize otherwise innocent conduct absent a clearer signal from Congress).

**III. An Objective Standard Chills Protected Speech and Convicts the Blameless.**

The practical stakes confirm the constitutional and statutory analysis. Elonis's Facebook posts were, on his account, rap-style venting following the collapse of his marriage and loss of his job—conduct he says he never intended anyone to take as a genuine threat. Under the instruction given below, however, the jury never had to decide whether Elonis *meant* to threaten his wife, coworkers, or the FBI agent; it needed only to decide whether a reasonable person reading the posts might think so. That standard punishes a defendant for the *reactions of others* to his words, not for his own culpable purpose—precisely the result *Black* and *Morissette* forbid.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial under a jury instruction requiring proof that Elonis subjectively intended his statements as threats.
