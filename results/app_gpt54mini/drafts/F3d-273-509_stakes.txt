**ARGUMENT**

The judgment should be reversed because neither the 1988 release nor the statute of limitations bars Bancorp’s claims.

**I. The 1988 release does not unambiguously waive the claims asserted here.**

Under New York law, a release is governed by ordinary principles of contract interpretation and must be enforced according to its unambiguous terms. See *Booth v. 3669 Delaware, Inc.*, 92 N.Y.2d 934, 935 (1998); *Mangini v. McClurg*, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969). But a release “cannot be read to cover matters which the parties did not intend to dispose of” and will not be construed to extinguish claims outside its scope. *Kaminsky v. Gamache*, 298 A.D.2d 361, 362 (2d Dep’t 2002); *Global Minerals & Metals Corp. v. Holme*, 35 A.D.3d 93, 98 (1st Dep’t 2006). Where language is general, courts examine the surrounding circumstances to determine whether the asserted claim was knowingly and fairly compromised. *Mangini*, 24 N.Y.2d at 563-64; *Lucio v. Curran*, 2 N.Y.2d 157, 161 (1956).

Here, the release relinquished claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language reaches claims challenging the OCC’s closure decision itself, not later and distinct claims arising from the FDIC’s post-closing conduct as receiver. Bancorp’s present claims concern the FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest to itself, its charging of receivership expenses, its alleged waste of estate assets, and its inadequate accounting. Those claims depend on the FDIC’s administration of the receivership after the bank was closed. They are therefore not claims “with respect to” the OCC’s closure decision in the sense intended by the release. See *Mangini*, 24 N.Y.2d at 562-64; *Kaminsky*, 298 A.D.2d at 362.

The district court’s contrary reading improperly collapses the OCC’s closure decision into every later act by the receiver. But New York law requires that a release be construed narrowly where the language does not clearly encompass the disputed claims. *Mangini*, 24 N.Y.2d at 563-64. If the FDIC wanted a release of all claims arising out of the receivership, it could have said so. It did not. Because the release does not unmistakably cover Bancorp’s claims for post-closing misconduct, summary judgment on that basis was error. See *Lucio*, 2 N.Y.2d at 161; *Kaminsky*, 298 A.D.2d at 362.

**II. Bancorp’s claims accrued, at the earliest, when the receivership ended, so the suit filed on October 31, 1995 was timely.**

Under New York law, a cause of action generally accrues when all of the facts necessary to the cause of action have occurred and the plaintiff can first sue. See *Ely-Cruikshank Co. v. Bank of Montreal*, 81 N.Y.2d 399, 402-03 (1993); *IDT Corp. v. Morgan Stanley Dean Witter & Co.*, 12 N.Y.3d 132, 140 (2009). For fiduciary-duty claims, New York recognizes that accrual may be delayed where the fiduciary relationship continues and the wrongdoing is not fully complete until the relationship ends. *Westchester Religious Inst. v. Kamerman*, 262 A.D.2d 131, 131-32 (1st Dep’t 1999); *Williamson v. PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP*, 9 N.Y.3d 1, 7-8 (2007). Similarly, claims of unjust enrichment and waste accrue when the alleged wrongful retention or use of property is complete, not merely when an underlying transaction begins. See *Ely-Cruikshank*, 81 N.Y.2d at 402-03.

That rule matters here because the FDIC remained in control of the receivership estate until November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995, one day before the FDIC terminated the receivership. Until that termination, the FDIC continued to possess and administer the estate, and Bancorp’s claims—particularly those concerning an accounting, improper expenses, and self-payment of post-insolvency interest—were still bound up with the ongoing receivership. Under *Westchester Religious Institute*, 262 A.D.2d at 131-32, the continuing fiduciary relationship delayed accrual until the relationship ended. The district court therefore erred in holding the claims untimely under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7).

The FDIC may argue that Bancorp knew of the challenged conduct earlier. But knowledge alone does not always start the limitations period when the claim depends on a continuing fiduciary relationship and unresolved administration of an estate. *Williamson*, 9 N.Y.3d at 7-8. Because Bancorp sued before the receivership ended, its claims were timely as a matter of law. See *Ely-Cruikshank*, 81 N.Y.2d at 402-03; *Westchester Religious Inst.*, 262 A.D.2d at 131-32.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court should reverse the judgment and remand for further proceedings.
