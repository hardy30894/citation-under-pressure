**ARGUMENT**

**I. Arizona’s Limitation on Expert Mental-State Evidence Does Not Violate Due Process Because the State May Constitutionally Channel Such Evidence to the Insanity Defense.**

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense. *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986). But this right is not absolute; States may establish neutral procedural rules that govern the admissibility of evidence, so long as those rules are not arbitrary or disproportionate. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42 (1996) (plurality opinion). Arizona’s rule—confining expert evidence of mental disease or defect to the insanity defense—is a permissible exercise of its authority to define the elements of crimes and the defenses available.

**A. The State May Define the Mens Rea Element Without Requiring Consideration of Mental Disease.**

A State may constitutionally define the mental state required for a crime and may exclude evidence of mental disease from the mens rea inquiry. In *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 49–50, this Court upheld a Montana statute that prohibited a defendant from introducing voluntary intoxication evidence to negate mens rea, reasoning that the State could “determine that intoxication does not diminish a defendant’s culpability.” Similarly, Arizona may determine that paranoid schizophrenia does not negate the specific intent to kill a police officer. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535–36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (States may define criminal responsibility without requiring consideration of mental disease).

**B. The Insanity Defense Provides an Adequate Forum for Mental-State Evidence.**

Arizona’s rule does not bar expert evidence of mental disease; it merely channels it to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 770–71 (2006) (upholding Arizona’s rule). This Court has long recognized that States may allocate burdens of proof for affirmative defenses, including insanity. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952) (upholding requirement that defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt). The availability of the insanity defense satisfies due process because it gives the defendant a full opportunity to present expert testimony on mental state. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 207 (1977) (States may require defendant to prove affirmative defenses without violating due process).

**C. The Rule Does Not Undermine the Presumption of Innocence or the Prosecution’s Burden of Proof.**

The presumption of innocence and the prosecution’s burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt are fundamental. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). But Arizona’s rule does not shift the burden of proof on mens rea; it merely limits the evidence that may be used to negate it. The prosecution must still prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt using other evidence. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 770 (noting that Arizona’s rule “does not prevent the defendant from introducing evidence of mental disease to rebut the prosecution’s evidence of intent”). The rule is a permissible evidentiary restriction, not a burden shift. *See United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (States may exclude relevant evidence if the exclusion serves legitimate interests).

**D. The Rule Serves Legitimate State Interests in Reliability and Avoiding Jury Confusion.**

Arizona’s rule prevents jury confusion and ensures reliable verdicts by avoiding the “battle of the experts” on mens rea. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 771–72 (recognizing that mental-disease evidence “can easily mislead” juries). This Court has upheld similar evidentiary restrictions that serve the “integrity of the trial process.” *Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 309. The rule also avoids the “moral inconsistency” of allowing a defendant to claim both sanity (for mens rea purposes) and insanity (as a defense). *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 771.

**E. The Rule Does Not Violate the Right to Present a Defense.**

The right to present a defense is not unlimited. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). A State may exclude evidence that is “repetitive, only marginally relevant, or poses an undue risk of prejudice.” *Crane*, 476 U.S. at 689–90. Arizona’s rule excludes evidence only for the limited purpose of negating mens rea, while preserving it for the insanity defense. This is a “reasonable and constitutional” accommodation. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 772.

**F. The Rule Is Consistent with Historical Practice.**

At common law, mental disease was relevant only to the insanity defense, not to mens rea. *See Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 44–46 (plurality opinion) (discussing historical treatment of intoxication). Arizona’s rule thus reflects a “longstanding practice” that this Court has declined to disturb. *See Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 202.

**G. The Defendant’s Expert Evidence Was Not Wholly Excluded.**

Clark was permitted to present expert testimony on his mental state for the insanity defense. The trial court’s ruling merely limited the purpose for which that evidence could be used. This is not a case where the defendant was “denied the opportunity to present any evidence at all.” *Crane*, 476 U.S. at 690. The rule is therefore constitutional.

**H. The Arizona Supreme Court’s Decision Is Entitled to Deference.**

This Court reviews state evidentiary rules with deference, particularly when they involve “complex and sensitive” issues of criminal responsibility. *Powell*, 392 U.S. at 536. Arizona’s rule is a rational policy choice that does not offend fundamental fairness. *See Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445 (1992) (States have “substantial leeway” in defining criminal procedure).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
