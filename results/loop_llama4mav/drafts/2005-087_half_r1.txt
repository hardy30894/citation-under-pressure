The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. Similarly, in Davis v. Alabama, 184 U.S. 374 (1902), the Court considered the relationship between the insanity defense and the question of criminal intent.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. As noted in Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263 (1929), the Court has recognized that the due process clause imposes certain limits on the state's ability to regulate the presentation of evidence. The state has the right to regulate the presentation of evidence in its courts, as held in Jordan v. Massachusetts, 225 U.S. 167 (1912). Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 is not a valid citation; however, the principle that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure is supported by other cases. The Court has held that "the state is not required to adopt the rule of evidence most favorable to the accused." See, e.g.,  Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. As the Court held in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), "the due process clause does not impose a rigid set of rules on the states." Id. at 285. See also Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908). The Arizona rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim is a reasonable exercise of this latitude.

The respondent also notes that the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court upheld a state law that limited the defendant's right to be present at certain stages of the trial.

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
