**Argument**

Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy creates a limited public forum for the use of school facilities by district residents. Under this policy, the school may permit the use of its facilities for "moral and character development" while expressly prohibiting "religious purposes." The Second Circuit correctly held that the Good News Club’s activities constitute "religious instruction and prayer" rather than "moral and character development." Consequently, the school’s exclusion of the Club is valid subject matter discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.

First, the Club’s activities are "quintessentially religious" and fall outside the bounds of permissible "moral and character development." The Club’s own materials and lesson plans demonstrate that its purpose is to teach the Bible and lead children to salvation, not merely to impart secular moral values. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Supreme Court noted that "singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles" are religious activities. Here, the Club’s "Israel Demands a King" lesson plan explicitly instructs that the "saved child will desire God's best" and invites children to "believe on the Lord Jesus." These activities are indistinguishable from religious worship. As the Second Circuit observed, the Club’s "moral vision entails religious activity." 202 F.3d at 511. Because the Club’s message is religious, it is excluded by the school’s policy.

Second, the school’s exclusion constitutes valid subject matter discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), held that excluding religious speech from a student fund was viewpoint discrimination because it targeted a specific viewpoint. However, *Widmar* established that a public university may exclude "religious worship" from a general student forum. 454 U.S. at 273. The distinction is between "religious worship" and "religious speech" in a secular context. *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993). In *Lamb's Chapel*, the Court permitted a film about the Bible to be shown in a secular context, but the film was not a religious worship service. Here, the Club seeks to hold a religious worship service, complete with prayer and scripture memorization. The school is not discriminating against the viewpoint that morality should be based on God; it is excluding the subject matter of religion itself. This exclusion is a reasonable accommodation of the Establishment Clause, which forbids the government from endorsing religion. Therefore, the school’s policy is a valid content-neutral regulation of a limited public forum.
