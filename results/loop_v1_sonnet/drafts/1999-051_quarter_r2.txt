**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedural Default That Is Not the Product of the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Bar Reliance on the Underlying Constitutional Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

This Court has long recognized that the writ of habeas corpus is not to be shackled by mechanical procedural rules where a petitioner's own conduct did not produce the default. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that a habeas petitioner forfeits federal review only where he personally and knowingly bypassed available state remedies; a default attributable to counsel's own tactical or negligent omission stands on a different footing than one flowing from the petitioner's considered choice. Carpenter did not himself choose to abandon his sufficiency claim; his appellate lawyer did. Under *Fay*, that distinction is decisive.

This principle rests on the settled understanding, articulated in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. A defendant cannot be said to have relinquished a constitutional claim merely because appointed counsel failed to raise it. Attributing counsel's default to the petitioner as though it were his own considered waiver inverts the very premise of *Zerbst*—that fundamental rights are surrendered only by the client's own knowing act, not by counsel's oversight.

**The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a mere procedural nicety; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court declared that "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours." *Id.* at 344. That guarantee of meaningful representation extends to appellate proceedings as well: in *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), the Court explained that "[t]here can be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of money he has," *id.* at 19, a principle this Court has applied to require that indigent defendants receive genuine, effective assistance in pursuing their appeals of right. Carpenter's claim—that his appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the kind of constitutional deprivation *Gideon* and *Griffin* were designed to remedy. A default of that claim, itself born of the same disabling failure of counsel, should not extinguish its capacity to explain and excuse the companion default it caused.

**Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require an Independently Exhausted Cause Claim Where the Default Flows from Counsel's Own Failure**

The exhaustion requirement exists, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241 (1886), to allow the state courts the first opportunity to consider claims arising under federal law before a federal court intervenes. It is a rule of comity among sovereigns, not a rule designed to forfeit meritorious federal claims outright. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise grounded the exhaustion doctrine in considerations of comity between the state and federal judicial systems, rather than in any purpose to bar claims from ultimate federal review. Ohio had every opportunity to correct counsel's failure when Carpenter sought reopening of his appeal; the state court declined only on a timeliness ground unrelated to the merits. Nothing in the comity rationale underlying *Royall* or *Darr* requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself first survive a second, independent gauntlet of exhaustion before it may serve its traditional function of explaining a related default.

**This Court's Broader Habeas Corpus Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Rules**

The great writ has always been treated with a flexibility that guards against the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103 (1935), the Court recognized that federal habeas relief must remain available where a state proceeding has been fundamentally undermined by a deprivation of constitutional rights, regardless of the formal posture in which the deprivation is presented. This Court's decision in *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), reinforces the point: there the Court cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake." *Sanders*, 373 U.S. at 8. The *Sanders* Court further explained that procedural bar doctrines must be applied with equitable discretion rather than rigid formalism. *Id.* at 17–18. Separately, *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), confirmed that federal courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims presented in habeas, rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive of the federal question. *Id.* at 458.

Taken together, *Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Griffin*, *Royall*, *Darr*, *Mooney*, *Sanders*, and *Brown* establish a consistent principle: procedural defaults attributable to counsel's own failings, rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice, should not be permitted to compound themselves into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient constitutional substance to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
