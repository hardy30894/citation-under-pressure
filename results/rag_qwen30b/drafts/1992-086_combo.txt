**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s blanket prohibition on religious use of its school facilities—without regard to content, viewpoint, or the nature of the speech—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a religiously themed film series on family values, despite the availability of the facilities for non-religious community use, constitutes impermissible content-based discrimination in a limited public forum. This Court has long held that when a government creates a forum for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech based on its religious content. The District’s policy, which bars religious groups from using school premises while permitting secular organizations to do so, is not viewpoint-neutral and thus violates the First Amendment.

The First Amendment protects the right to speak, assemble, and petition the government for redress of grievances. *Santos v. Holy Roman Catholic and Apostolic Church, Tambobong Parish*, 212 U.S. 463, 470 (1909), recognized that religious institutions have a legitimate interest in the use of property for worship and community purposes. Though that case involved property ownership, it reflects the broader principle that religious expression is entitled to constitutional protection. The government may not suppress religious speech simply because it is religious. *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962), held that the Establishment Clause does not permit the government to compel religious observance, but it does not permit the government to suppress religious expression in public forums. Indeed, the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause “was designed to protect the expression of ideas, including religious ideas, from governmental suppression.” *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952).

The District’s school facilities constitute a limited public forum. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when a government opens a forum for public expression, it must do so on a non-discriminatory basis. *Illinois Ex Rel. McCollum v. Board of Ed. of School Dist. No. 71, Champaign Cty.*, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948), established that a public school may not permit religious instruction during school hours, but that does not mean the government may exclude religious groups from non-instructional use of school property. The Court in *McCollum* emphasized that the government may not “establish” religion, but it may not “suppress” it either. The same principle applies when the government opens its facilities to the public for non-curricular use. *Minersville School District v. Gobitis*, 310 U.S. 586, 595 (1940), affirmed that the First Amendment protects the right to hold religious beliefs and to express them, even when such expression is unpopular or controversial.

The District’s policy of excluding religious groups while allowing secular groups to use the facilities is a content-based restriction that fails even the most lenient scrutiny. *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370, 375 (1930), held that the government may not deny access to public property based on the content of speech. The Court stated that “the State may not, under the guise of regulation, suppress expression because it disapproves of the ideas expressed.” The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use the auditorium for a film series on family values—despite the fact that the same space has been used for secular lectures and performances—demonstrates that the exclusion is not based on time, place, or manner, but on the religious content of the speech. This is precisely the kind of discrimination the First Amendment forbids.

The District’s argument that it has not opened the forum to religious use is irrelevant. The government may not create a forum and then exclude religious speech simply because it is religious. *Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236, 242 (1968), held that the government may not discriminate against religious groups in the provision of public services. The Court stated that “the First Amendment does not permit the government to favor secular over religious expression.” The District’s policy of denying access to religious groups while allowing secular groups to use the facilities is a form of religious discrimination that violates the Free Speech Clause.

Moreover, the District’s claim that its policy is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is without merit. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952), held that the Establishment Clause does not require the government to suppress religious expression. The Court stated that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state, but that wall is not a wall of separation so complete as to prevent all contact.” The government may allow religious groups to use public facilities so long as it does not endorse or promote religion. The District’s refusal to allow religious use of its facilities is not a neutral policy—it is a policy that discriminates against religion.

The District’s reliance on *Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education*, 175 U.S. 528, 530 (1899), is misplaced. That case involved a state’s decision to fund only white high schools, which the Court upheld as a matter of discretion. But that case did not involve the First Amendment or the right to free speech. The Court in *Cumming* did not address the government’s obligation to provide equal access to public forums. The District’s argument that it may exclude religious groups because it has not previously allowed such use is contrary to the principle that once a forum is opened, it must be open to all comers on equal terms.

Finally, the District’s claim that its policy is consistent with *University v. People*, 99 U.S. 309, 315 (1879), is inapposite. That case involved a tax exemption for property used for school purposes, not the use of public facilities for expressive activity. The Court in *University v. People* did not address the First Amendment or the right to free speech. The District’s reliance on that case is therefore irrelevant.
