**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the use of Clark’s expert testimony to the issue of insanity, a determination governed by state law and consistent with long-standing constitutional precedent. The Due Process Clause does not compel a state to allow a defendant to use psychiatric evidence to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such evidence is offered in support of a recognized affirmative defense. Here, Clark sought to use expert testimony not to establish a defense, but to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof—a function that lies within the province of the state to define.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that states possess broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the scope of defenses. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime, stating that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 798. This principle remains binding and controls the outcome here.

Clark’s attempt to use expert testimony to show he lacked criminal intent—without asserting a recognized defense—falls outside the scope of any constitutional right. The Court in *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), held that a defendant may not introduce evidence to negate an element of the crime unless such evidence is part of a defense recognized by law. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 77. This rule applies with equal force when the defense is not insanity but a claim that the defendant lacked the requisite mental state.

Moreover, the Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), reaffirmed that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it,” and that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 395–396. This principle was reaffirmed in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), where the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime. Id. at 797.

The Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce expert evidence to negate criminal intent unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. The Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), made clear that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. This principle is not altered by the fact that the evidence is expert in nature. The Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948).

For these reasons, the Arizona trial court did not violate Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense. The Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), held that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it,” and that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 797–798. Clark’s effort to use expert testimony to negate criminal intent outside the framework of a recognized defense was properly excluded under this settled constitutional principle.
