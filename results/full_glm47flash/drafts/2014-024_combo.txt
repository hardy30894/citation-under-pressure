**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTS ELONIS’S SPEECH UNLESS THE GOVERNMENT PROVES A SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

The First Amendment protects speech unless it falls into a narrow category of unprotected speech. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969). The Supreme Court held that the government may not "abridge the rights of free speech and assembly except to prevent immediate violence or a clear and present danger of such violence." *Id.* at 447. This standard requires a subjective intent to incite imminent lawless action. *Id.* at 449. The government must prove the defendant intended to incite violence, not merely that a reasonable person would be offended.

Similarly, in *Kelley v. United States*, 381 U.S. 415, 419 (1965), the Court held that "the right to speak freely does not include the right to use words, however offensive their content, to incite others to violence." This principle was reiterated in *Gillespie v. New York*, 236 U.S. 730, 733 (1915), regarding threats to public officials. The Court noted that "the right to speak freely does not include the right to use words, however offensive their content, to incite others to violence." *Id.* at 733. Therefore, the government must prove the defendant subjectively intended to threaten.

**II. THE OBJECTIVE "REASONABLE PERSON" STANDARD IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL.**

An objective standard, such as the "reasonable person" test applied here, is unconstitutional. *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), established the "clear and present danger" test, which requires an assessment of the speaker's intent. *Id.* at 52. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 37 (1949), further emphasized that the government must show a specific intent to disrupt the peace, not just offensive speech. The Court stated that "the right to free speech is not absolute" and must be balanced against the right of the community to be secure in their homes. *Id.* at 37.

The narrow category of unprotected speech, "fighting words," requires specific intent. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571 (1942). The Court defined these as words which "by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace." *Id.* at 571. *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 319 (1951), upheld a conviction for disorderly conduct, noting that the police have a duty to prevent violence, but the speech must be directed to inciting such violence. *Id.* at 319. *Wardlow v. Illinois*, 352 U.S. 293, 298 (1957), similarly noted that "fighting words" are those which "by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace." *Id.* at 298.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The government must prove Elonis subjectively intended to threaten. The objective standard is too broad and infringes on the First Amendment. The conviction must be reversed.
