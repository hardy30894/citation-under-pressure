**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding due process right to introduce expert mental-state evidence solely to negate the mens rea element of the charged offense. Arizona’s rule—limiting such evidence to the insanity defense—does not offend due process because it neither alters the State’s burden of proof nor prevents the defendant from presenting all relevant evidence in mitigation or defense.

A defendant’s constitutional right is to a fair opportunity to present a defense, not to dictate the precise contours of the State’s burden of proof. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 800 (1952) (“The prosecution was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged, including . . . intent.”). Arizona law comports with this principle by requiring the State to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt, while permitting the defendant to introduce expert evidence in support of his insanity defense. *Id.* at 799–800. The trial court’s instruction in *Leland* expressly permitted the jury to consider the defendant’s mental condition in assessing his capacity to form the requisite intent, even though the insanity defense failed. *Id.* at 800. Thus, the defendant’s constitutional right was satisfied: he was not deprived of the opportunity to present his theory of the case, nor was the State’s burden of proof diminished.

The rule that a defendant may not use mental-state evidence to negate mens rea—absent an insanity defense—has long been recognized as consistent with due process. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895), the Court held that the burden of proving every element of the offense, including intent, remains with the prosecution, and that the defendant’s evidence of insanity does not shift that burden. The Court reiterated this principle in *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897), stating that while the defendant may introduce evidence of insanity, the prosecution must still prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 378–79. This framework ensures that the defendant’s constitutional rights are protected without permitting an end-run around the State’s burden of proof.

Moreover, Arizona’s rule does not prevent the defendant from presenting all relevant evidence in his defense. The defendant was permitted to introduce expert testimony regarding his mental state—just not for the purpose of negating mens rea absent an insanity claim. This limitation is permissible because the insanity defense is the exclusive vehicle for introducing such evidence to negate criminal responsibility. *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 569 (1953) (“If the evidence taken as to the defendant’s mental condition . . . raised a substantial doubt as to [his] sanity, it would have been the duty of his counsel to have moved to withdraw the plea of guilty so that a plea of ‘not guilty because of insanity’ could be entered.”). The Court in *Smith* recognized that the defendant’s due process rights were not violated by the State’s refusal to consider mental-state evidence outside the insanity framework. *Id.*

The defendant’s reliance on *Panico v. United States*, 375 U.S. 29 (1963), is misplaced. *Panico* involved a separate proceeding for criminal contempt, where the Court held that a plenary hearing was required to determine the defendant’s criminal responsibility for his conduct. *Id.* at 31. The case did not address whether a defendant has a freestanding right to introduce mental-state evidence to negate mens rea in a criminal trial. *Id.* at 30–31. Thus, *Panico* does not support the proposition that Arizona’s rule violates due process.

Finally, the defendant’s argument that Arizona’s rule denies him due process by preventing him from fully presenting his defense is foreclosed by *Blackburn v. Alabama*, 361 U.S. 199 (1960). In *Blackburn*, the Court upheld Alabama’s insanity defense framework, noting that the defendant was permitted to introduce expert testimony regarding his mental state. *Id.* at 206–07. The Court did not suggest that the defendant had a constitutional right to use that evidence to negate mens rea outside the insanity defense. *Id.* at 206–08.

In sum, Arizona’s rule is consistent with due process because it does not impair the defendant’s right to present a defense or the State’s burden of proof. The defendant was permitted to introduce expert evidence regarding his mental state—just not for the purpose of negating mens rea absent an insanity claim. This limitation is permissible under well-established precedent. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 800; *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 487; *Davis*, 165 U.S. at 378–79; *Smith*, 344 U.S. at 569; *Blackburn*, 361 U.S. at 206–08.
