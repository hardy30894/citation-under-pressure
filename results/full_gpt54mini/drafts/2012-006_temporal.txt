Petitioner’s burden argument is mistaken because withdrawal from a conspiracy is not a limitation-bar rule that shifts the burden of persuasion to the Government; it is an affirmative matter that the defendant must establish. The prosecution’s burden remains to prove the conspiracy charged and the defendant’s participation in it, but once membership is shown, the law presumes continuance absent proof of withdrawal. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910); Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912).

Kissel made clear that conspiracy is a continuing offense and that liability does not evaporate merely because the agreement began earlier than the limitations period. 218 U.S. at 607-08. Hyde likewise held that a conspirator “must show” withdrawal; mere cessation of activity is not enough. 225 U.S. at 369-70. Those decisions reflect the settled principle that once a defendant joins an unlawful agreement, the conspiracy continues until it is abandoned or the defendant affirmatively withdraws. Id.; Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 400-01 (1912).

That allocation of proof is consistent with the Court’s general limitations jurisprudence. The statute of limitations is ordinarily a matter of defense, not an element of the offense that the Government must negate in every case. Biddinger v. Commissioner of Police, 245 U.S. 128, 135-36 (1917). In Biddinger, the Court explained that limitations “is a defense” that may be raised by the accused. Id. at 135. The same principle applies here: if a defendant contends that he withdrew before the limitations period, he is asserting a personal defense to continued responsibility, not denying the existence of the conspiracy itself.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would invert the settled law of conspiracies by requiring the Government to prove a negative uniquely within the defendant’s knowledge. The Court has long recognized that the law may place the burden of producing and proving an affirmative defense on the party asserting it, especially where the facts are peculiarly within that party’s knowledge. Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 88-89 (1934). Withdrawal is such a fact. It depends on the defendant’s own acts and communications: disavowal, confession to authorities, or conduct clearly communicated to co-conspirators. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70.

Nothing in the pre-1970 cases suggests that once withdrawal is asserted, the Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member during the limitations period. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly treated continued membership as presumed once unlawful combination is shown, unless the defendant demonstrates withdrawal. Kissel, 218 U.S. at 607-08; Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70. The Government still bears the ordinary burden to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, but that does not transform withdrawal into an element of the offense. See Morrison, 291 U.S. at 88-89.

The D.C. Circuit therefore correctly rejected petitioner’s attempt to turn withdrawal into a limitations-based burden shift. The proper rule is that the Government must prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation within the charged period; the defendant who claims earlier withdrawal bears the burden of establishing that defense. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; Biddinger, 245 U.S. at 135-36. Petitioner offers no pre-1970 decision holding otherwise, and this Court should not adopt one now.
