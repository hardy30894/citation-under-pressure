**ARGUMENT**

I. **A MSPB DISMISSAL FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION OVER A MIXED-CLAIM APPEAL IS REVIEWABLE IN DISTRICT COURT UNDER 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2)**

The Federal Circuit’s long-standing rule that a jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed claim must be reviewed in the Federal Circuit conflicts with the plain text and structure of the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA), 5 U.S.C. § 7703, and with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012). The CSRA provides that “[a]ny employee ... adversely affected or aggrieved by a final order or decision of the [MSPB] ... may obtain judicial review ... in the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.” 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, it carves out an exception for discrimination claims:

> “In the case of an appeal brought under section 7702 of this title, [review shall be] in the United States district court for the district in which the employee resides or for the District of Columbia.”

5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). Section 7702, in turn, governs “[a]ctions involving discrimination” and defines a mixed case as one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and ... alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” certain federal statutes. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1).

The D.C. Circuit correctly recognized in *Kloeckner* that this statutory scheme “draws no distinction between a Board decision that reaches the merits of a discrimination claim and one that does not.” 568 U.S. at 52. The Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds—here, untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court. *Id.* at 53. The Court reasoned that the text of § 7702(a)(1) “does not require the Board to decide the merits of the discrimination claim before the employee can obtain judicial review in district court.” *Id.* at 52. The same logic applies with equal force when the Board dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction. *See id.* at 53 (“[T]he text of § 7703(b)(2) does not require the Board to reach the merits of the discrimination claim before the employee can obtain judicial review in district court.”).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary decision in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is inconsistent with *Kloeckner* and must be overruled. *Powell* held that a jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case must be reviewed in the Federal Circuit, reasoning that the Board’s lack of jurisdiction meant the case was not “appealable to the MSPB” under § 7702(a)(1)(A). *Id.* at 599. But *Kloeckner* rejected a similar argument, explaining that the statute “does not require the Board to decide the merits of the discrimination claim before the employee can obtain judicial review in district court.” 568 U.S. at 52. The Court further noted that the Board’s lack of jurisdiction over a procedural defect did not change the fact that the employee had been “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” *Id.* at 53 (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A)). The same is true here: Perry’s retirement and suspension were actions appealable to the MSPB, and he alleged discrimination as a basis for those actions. The Board’s conclusion that the actions were voluntary does not alter the fact that they were appealable or that Perry’s claim falls within § 7702(a)(1).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule in cases such as *Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 713 F.3d 1115 (Fed. Cir. 2013), is likewise inconsistent with *Kloeckner*. The Federal Circuit reasoned that a jurisdictional dismissal meant the case was not “appealable to the MSPB” under § 7702(a)(1)(A). *Id.* at 1118. But *Kloeckner* squarely rejected this textual argument, holding that the statute “does not require the Board to reach the merits of the discrimination claim before the employee can obtain judicial review in district court.” 568 U.S. at 52. The Court’s holding controls this case.

II. **THE BOARD’S JURISDICTIONAL DISMISSAL DOES NOT REMOVE THE CASE FROM THE MIXED-CASE EXCEPTION**

The Federal Circuit and the D.C. Circuit have erroneously conflated jurisdictional dismissals with procedural dismissals. *See Conforto*, 713 F.3d at 1118; *Powell*, 158 F.3d at 599. But the Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* drew a clear distinction between the two. 568 U.S. at 53. A jurisdictional dismissal occurs when the Board lacks authority to hear the case at all—such as when the employee has not suffered an appealable adverse action. *See id.* at 50. A procedural dismissal, by contrast, occurs when the Board has jurisdiction but the employee has failed to comply with procedural requirements—such as untimeliness. *Id.* at 53.

Here, the Board had jurisdiction over Perry’s appeal because he challenged an appealable adverse action—his suspension and retirement. The Board’s dismissal was based on its conclusion that the actions were voluntary, not on a lack of jurisdiction over the type of action. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7512 (defining appealable actions). Thus, Perry’s case falls within the mixed-case exception, and judicial review lies in district court under § 7703(b)(2).

III. **UNIFORM REVIEW IN DISTRICT COURT PROMOTES JUDICIAL EFFICIENCY AND CONSISTENCY**

Permitting review in district court for all mixed cases—whether the Board reaches the merits or dismisses on procedural or jurisdictional grounds—promotes judicial efficiency and consistency. *See Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 53. If the Board dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction, the employee must file in district court to challenge both the jurisdictional ruling and the merits of the discrimination claim. Requiring the employee to file in the Federal Circuit for jurisdictional dismissals and in district court for procedural dismissals creates unnecessary confusion and duplicative litigation. *See id.* at 52-53.

The Federal Circuit’s rule also risks inconsistent results. A discrimination claim may be intertwined with the Board’s jurisdictional determination—such as when the employee alleges that an action was involuntary due to discrimination. *See Perry v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 824 F.3d 1378, 1381 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (per curiam). Requiring the employee to litigate the discrimination claim in district court while the jurisdictional issue is resolved in the Federal Circuit creates the potential for conflicting rulings and piecemeal litigation. *See Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 53.

**CONCLUSION**

The plain text of the CSRA, the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner*, and the policy of judicial efficiency all require that a MSPB dismissal of a mixed-case appeal—whether on procedural or jurisdictional grounds—be reviewed in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The decisions below should be reversed, and this case remanded for review in the appropriate district court.
