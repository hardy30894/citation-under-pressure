**ARGUMENT**

**I. Montana’s Exercise of Specific Jurisdiction Over Ford Comports With Due Process Because Ford’s Forum-Related Conduct Provides a Sufficient Basis to Assert Specific Jurisdiction.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the defendant’s forum-related activities give rise to the plaintiff’s claims. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This Court has repeatedly held that specific jurisdiction exists where the defendant’s in-state conduct is sufficiently connected to the plaintiff’s claims. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985); *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). Here, Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts, advertising, and the design and distribution of the allegedly defective vehicle—provide a constitutionally sufficient basis for jurisdiction.

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle was not first sold in Montana is unavailing. *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 137 (2014), does not require that the defendant’s initial sale occur in the forum state; it requires only that the defendant’s forum-related activities be sufficiently related to the plaintiff’s claims. *BNSF Ry. Co. v. Tyrrell*, 137 S. Ct. 1549, 1559 (2017). Ford’s nationwide distribution of the allegedly defective vehicle, coupled with its ongoing marketing and parts sales in Montana, satisfies this standard. *J. McIntyre Mach., Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 881 (2011) (plurality opinion).

**II. Ford’s Nationwide Contacts With Montana Support the Exercise of Specific Jurisdiction.**

Ford’s assertion that its Montana contacts are too attenuated is contradicted by this Court’s precedent. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 925 (2011), does not impose a per se rule that a defendant must have a physical presence in the forum; rather, it examines whether the defendant’s forum-related activities are sufficiently related to the plaintiff’s claims. Ford’s nationwide distribution of the vehicle at issue, including sales in Montana, and its ongoing marketing and parts distribution in the state, provide a sufficient basis for jurisdiction. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of Cal., San Francisco Cty.*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017), does not alter this analysis, as the claims here arise directly from Ford’s Montana-related conduct.

**III. Ford’s Reliance on *Daimler* and *BNSF* Is Misplaced.**

Ford’s attempt to analogize this case to *Daimler* and *BNSF* fails. In *Daimler*, the Court held that general jurisdiction was improper because the defendant’s contacts with California were not sufficiently continuous and systematic to render it “at home” there. 571 U.S. at 137. Here, Ford does not contest that it lacks general jurisdiction; instead, it argues that specific jurisdiction is improper. *BNSF*, 137 S. Ct. at 1559, similarly addressed general jurisdiction, not specific jurisdiction. Ford’s reliance on these cases is therefore misplaced.

**IV. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Specific Jurisdiction Framework.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied the specific jurisdiction analysis, concluding that Ford’s forum-related conduct provided a sufficient basis for jurisdiction. The court’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedent, which permits jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum-related activities are sufficiently related to the plaintiff’s claims. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 472. Ford’s argument that the vehicle’s initial sale in Washington State severs the connection to Montana is unpersuasive, as this Court has repeatedly rejected such formalistic arguments. *Nicastro*, 564 U.S. at 881.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed. Ford’s contacts with Montana provide a sufficient basis for the exercise of specific jurisdiction, and its reliance on *Daimler* and *BNSF* is misplaced. The Due Process Clause does not bar Montana from exercising jurisdiction over Ford in this case.
