**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of a broad, unbounded “predominantly internal” standard transforms Exemption 2 into a catch-all shield for any sensitive operational data, thereby undermining the statutory requirement that exemptions be narrowly construed and explicitly exclusive. This expansion of Exemption 2 violates both the text of the FOIA and binding Supreme Court precedent, and it contravenes the foundational principle that the public has a right to know about government activities that directly affect their safety and well-being.

### I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “PREDOMINANTLY INTERNAL” STANDARD EXCEEDS THE SCOPE OF EXEMPTION 2

Exemption 2 protects only those materials “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that this exemption is not a blank check for agencies to withhold any information they deem sensitive. In *Rose*, the Court emphasized that Exemption 2 “is not a general exemption for all internal agency matters,” but rather applies only to “matters that are so clearly internal in nature that they are not of genuine and significant public interest.” 425 U.S. 352, 363 (1976). The Court further cautioned that exemptions must be “narrowly construed” and are “explicitly exclusive.” *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989).

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—under which any information that serves an internal function, even if it also has external consequences, may be withheld—ignores this narrow construction. The D.C. Circuit’s approach in *Crooker* and *Schwaner* was premised on the idea that law enforcement manuals, which are inherently internal to enforcement operations, may be exempt if disclosure risks circumvention. But the Ninth Circuit’s extension of that standard to non-law enforcement, non-adjudicative, and non-regulatory data—such as ESQD arc maps used for facility design and risk mitigation—goes far beyond the scope of *Rose* and *Tax Analysts*.

The ESQD arc maps at issue here are not “internal personnel rules and practices.” They are technical safety calculations used to determine safe storage distances for explosives, to prevent sympathetic detonations, and to guide the physical layout of a munitions depot. These maps are not internal directives for Navy personnel on how to perform their jobs; they are engineering tools for managing public safety risks. As the Supreme Court held in *Rose*, “the public has a right to know what the government is doing, especially when the government is engaged in activities that may affect the safety of the public.” 425 U.S. at 361. The Navy’s own affidavit confirms that the ESQD data is used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not internal personnel practice—it is public safety planning.

### II. THE ESQD DOCUMENTS ARE NOT “RELATED SOLELY” TO INTERNAL PERSONNEL RULES AND PRACTICES

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the ESQD maps are “predominantly internal” fails the statutory test. The phrase “solely” in Exemption 2 is not a mere formality—it is a limiting term. The Supreme Court has repeatedly stressed that exemptions must be interpreted narrowly and that “the burden of proving exemption rests with the agency.” *Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173 (1991). Here, the Navy has not shown that the ESQD maps are “solely” about internal personnel practices. To the contrary, the maps are used to determine where explosives may be stored, how far apart they must be, and what areas are at risk in the event of an explosion. These are matters of public safety, not internal personnel management.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy* and *Maricopa Audubon Society* is misplaced. In *Hardy*, the court upheld the withholding of an ATF raid manual because it contained procedures for executing search warrants—information that is inherently tied to law enforcement operations and subject to the risk of circumvention. But the ESQD maps are not law enforcement materials. They are not compiled for enforcement, adjudication, or investigation. As the dissent correctly notes, the Navy’s own affidavit confirms that the maps are used for “risk mitigation and enhanced safety,” not for law enforcement purposes. *Church of Scientology v. IRS*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979) (holding that materials not compiled for law enforcement purposes are not exempt under Exemption 7(F)).

### III. THE RISK OF CIRCUMVENTION IS NOT PROVEN AND IS SPECULATIVE

Even if the documents were internal, the risk of circumvention must be “real and substantial,” not speculative. *Morley v. CIA*, 508 F.3d 1108, 1124 (D.C. Cir. 2007). The Navy’s claim that disclosure would allow someone to “reverse engineer” the maps and discover details about specific weapons systems is not supported by any evidence. The maps are not classified. The Navy has not demonstrated that the information is sensitive in a way that would threaten national security. Indeed, the Navy voluntarily released identical ESQD maps for the Bangor facility—located just four to five miles from a town—without any classification or security concerns. The fact that the Navy withheld the same data for Indian Island, located less than two miles from Port Townsend, suggests political sensitivity, not national security risk.

The dissent correctly observes that if the maps truly posed a national security threat, the Navy would have classified them under Exemption 1. The failure to classify is itself evidence that the information is not of the kind protected by FOIA’s most stringent exemption. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Navy’s refusal to classify the maps, while claiming they are too dangerous to disclose, undermines its own justification for withholding them under Exemption 2.
