**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. The Clause protects a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy” for the “same offence,” U.S. Const. amend. V, but the Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished sentencing from the adjudication of guilt. In **Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949)**, the Court upheld a sentence based on information outside the trial record and explained that sentencing has not traditionally been governed by the full panoply of trial protections. The Court stated that the Due Process Clause should not be treated as “a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251.

That principle remains controlling outside the capital context. In **McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 U.S. 79 (1986)**, the Court explained that legislatures may assign sentencing consequences to facts found in sentencing proceedings without converting those facts into elements of the offense. Likewise, in **Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389 (1995)**, the Court reaffirmed that consideration of relevant conduct at sentencing does not constitute punishment for that conduct in the double jeopardy sense. *Id.* at 398-99. And in **United States v. Watts, 519 U.S. 148 (1997)**, the Court again recognized the wide latitude historically afforded sentencing courts to consider uncharged or unproven conduct. *Id.* at 151-57.

The Court’s capital-sentencing cases do not compel a different rule here. In **Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981)**, the Court held that a capital sentencing proceeding could, in limited circumstances, resemble a trial sufficiently to trigger double jeopardy protections. But the Court grounded that holding in the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” present in Missouri’s capital sentencing scheme. *Id.* at 439. The same approach was applied in **Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984)**, where the sentencing determination was the functional equivalent of an acquittal on the death penalty. *Id.* at 211-12. And in **Poland v. Arizona, 476 U.S. 147 (1986)**, the Court emphasized that only a true acquittal on the capital sentencing issue invokes double jeopardy. *Id.* at 154-57.

But the Court has never extended Bullington beyond capital cases. To the contrary, in **Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721 (1998)**, decided after the state court proceedings below, the Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Court explained that Bullington’s rationale was tied to the “unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Id.* at 734. It reaffirmed that “the pronouncement of sentence simply does not ‘have the qualities of constitutional finality that attend an acquittal.’ ” *Id.* at 728 (quoting **United States v. DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. 117, 134 (1980)**). The Court further observed that “[t]he pronouncement of sentence has never carried the finality that attaches to an acquittal.” *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 729 (quoting **DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. at 133-34**).

That reasoning is dispositive here. A prior conviction allegation under California’s Three Strikes law is a sentencing factor, not a substantive offense. The trial court’s true finding on that allegation does not determine guilt or innocence of the underlying drug offenses; it affects only the range of punishment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated such sentencing determinations differently from verdicts of acquittal. See **DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. at 137** (no double jeopardy bar to sentence review); **Witte, 515 U.S. at 397-98** (sentencing consideration of conduct not a second prosecution). Because the proceeding at issue is noncapital, it lacks the trial-like finality that triggered double jeopardy concerns in Bullington and Rumsey.

Respondent’s position is also consistent with the Court’s broader sentencing jurisprudence. In **Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984)**, the Court noted that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not generally apply to sentencing proceedings because the sentencer is not deciding guilt or innocence. *Id.* at 459-60. And in **Schiro v. Farley, 510 U.S. 222 (1994)**, the Court reiterated that a sentencing determination is not an acquittal unless it actually resolves the issue in the defendant’s favor in the constitutionally relevant sense. *Id.* at 232-33.

In short, the Constitution does not forbid the State from retrying the prior conviction allegation after the Court of Appeal found the proof insufficient. The relevant Supreme Court cases distinguish capital from noncapital sentencing, reject an across-the-board application of double jeopardy to sentencing issues, and preserve the State’s ability to seek a lawful sentence based on prior convictions. Under **Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721 (1998)**, the judgment below should be affirmed.
