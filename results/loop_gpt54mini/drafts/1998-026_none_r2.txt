### Argument

The judgment should be affirmed because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal does not warrant habeas relief when the defendant already knew of that right and chose not to appeal. Habeas corpus is not a substitute for direct review, nor does it supply relief for every procedural omission. *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202–03 (1830); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 181 (1947) (“The writ of habeas corpus is not to be used as a substitute for the regular appellate process.”). As this Court explained in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), “collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a rule. Petitioner therefore must show prejudice, not merely a technical violation.

He cannot. Rule 32(a)(2) is designed to ensure that a defendant who might otherwise be unaware of appellate rights does not lose an appeal through ignorance. Where the defendant already knows of the right, the rule’s purpose has been achieved despite the judge’s omission. The harmless-error principle confirms that result. In *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), the Court refused to reverse for technical error that did not affect substantial rights. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760 (1946), the Court held that an error warrants relief only when it had a substantial influence on the outcome. And in *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), the Court reiterated that reversal is not required absent a real effect on the verdict or judgment. Here, the district court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at sentencing. That finding defeats any claim that the omitted advisement caused him to forfeit an appeal he otherwise would have taken.

This Court has repeatedly distinguished between substantial error and nonprejudicial irregularity. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942), makes clear that errors must be assessed in context, not treated as automatically fatal. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), likewise limits relief to errors affecting substantial rights. And *Riddle v. Dyche*, 262 U.S. 333, 335–36 (1923), rejects collateral disturbance of a judgment for defects that do not undermine the legality of the conviction or sentence. An omitted advisement, without more, is just such a defect when the defendant already knew his appeal right and elected not to exercise it.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would convert a procedural safeguard into an automatic-vacatur mechanism. That is inconsistent with this Court’s longstanding insistence on finality. *Baldwin v. Traveling Men’s Assn.*, 283 U.S. 522, 525 (1931), emphasizes the importance of repose in judgments, and *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67–68 (1914), confines postjudgment relief to serious defects going to the validity of the proceeding. There is no warrant for upsetting a sentence where the record shows the defendant knew of his right to appeal and decided against an appeal. To grant habeas relief on these facts would elevate form over substance and expand collateral review beyond its proper office.

The district court’s factual finding that Peguero knew of his appeal right is also entitled to deference. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). On that finding, there was no prejudice, no lost appeal, and no miscarriage of justice. Because habeas relief requires more than a formal omission, the decision below should be affirmed.
