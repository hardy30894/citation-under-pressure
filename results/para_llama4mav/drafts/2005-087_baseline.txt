The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to negate the prosecution's evidence of mens rea, separate from an insanity defense. The Supreme Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto, as well as the rules governing the admissibility of evidence. See, e.g., Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977).

Arizona's rule limiting the use of expert testimony on mental state to the insanity defense is a reasonable exercise of this latitude. As the Arizona Supreme Court held, the state's insanity defense statute, Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502, is a comprehensive scheme that governs the use of mental health evidence in criminal cases. The statute's limitation on the use of such evidence to the insanity defense is a rational means of ensuring that defendants do not confuse the jury by introducing potentially irrelevant or unreliable evidence. See State v. Mott, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. 1997).

The Due Process Clause does not require Arizona to permit Clark to introduce expert evidence on his mental state to negate mens rea. The Supreme Court has held that the Constitution does not mandate a particular formulation of the insanity defense, see Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 536-37 (1968) (plurality opinion), and it follows that a state is not required to permit a defendant to use expert evidence on mental state to negate mens rea outside the context of an insanity defense.

Moreover, as the Arizona Supreme Court noted, allowing defendants to introduce expert evidence on mental state to negate mens rea could create significant practical difficulties, including the potential for confusion and abuse. See State v. Clark, 112 P.3d 13, 18-19 (Ariz. 2005). The Due Process Clause does not require Arizona to adopt a rule that could lead to such problems.

In sum, Arizona's rule limiting the use of expert testimony on mental state to the insanity defense is a reasonable exercise of the state's authority to define the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto.
