Petitioner submits that proof of withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period commences shifts to the government the burden of persuasion to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member during the limitations period. This follows from the principle that the government must prove every element of the offense, including the defendant's connection to the conspiracy at the relevant time.

In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court explained that "after a conspiracy has come to an end... the statute of limitations begins to run." Id. at 369. Once withdrawal is shown, the continuing nature of the offense ceases as to that defendant, and the government bears the affirmative obligation to demonstrate ongoing participation. See United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910) (a conspiracy "may be a continuing one" only while the agreement and overt acts persist).

The requirement that the government prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt is longstanding. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) ("the burden of proof... is upon the prosecution"). Shifting that burden would contravene the rule that "the prosecution must prove every essential element." See Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 90 (1934). Pre-1970 authority confirms that withdrawal terminates liability for subsequent acts, placing the onus on the government to rebut the withdrawal with evidence of continued membership. See Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401 (1912).

The court of appeals erred in relieving the government of this burden. Reversal is required.
