**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in holding that the '880 application may claim the April 13, 1989 filing date of the '566 application through the intervening '838 and '768 continuations. Section 120 grants an earlier filing date only when the later application is “disclosed in the manner provided by the first paragraph of section 112” and is “co-pending” as a continuation or divisional of the prior application. 35 U.S.C. § 120. That statutory benefit is not automatic; the later application must itself satisfy the written description requirements for the subject matter on which priority is sought. See Muncie Gear Works, Inc. v. Outboard Marine & Manufacturing Co., 315 U.S. 759, 768-69 (1942); Schriber-Schroth Co. v. Cleveland Trust Co., 305 U.S. 47, 57-58 (1938).

The disputed issue is whether the intervening '838 and '768 applications sufficiently incorporated the '579 application so that the '880 application can inherit the '566 filing date for that subject matter. They did not. Incorporation by reference may not be used to enlarge a patent disclosure beyond what the later application itself clearly identifies. See General Electric Co. v. Wabash Appliance Corp., 304 U.S. 364, 371-73 (1938) (patent claims must be supported by a definite disclosure, not by ambiguity or later reconstruction); Permutit Co. v. Graver Corp., 284 U.S. 52, 60-61 (1931) (patent disclosure must inform those skilled in the art with reasonable precision); cf. Schriber-Schroth, 305 U.S. at 57-58 (no right to claim subject matter not adequately disclosed).

Harari’s priority theory depends on a chain of papers in which only the original '566 application contained the incorporation statement referring to the '579 application as “filed on the same day as the present application.” That statement was not carried forward in a manner that, standing alone, made the identity of the incorporated reference clear in the intervening applications for purposes of § 120. The Board therefore erred by treating a copied parenthetical and a later filing history as sufficient to supply continuity. Section 120 requires continuity in the disclosure itself; an applicant may not rely on after-the-fact explanation to make an ambiguous disclosure do work it did not do when filed. See Deering v. Winona Harvester Works, 155 U.S. 286, 302-03 (1894) (patent rights are confined to what the application fairly discloses); Topliff v. Topliff, 145 U.S. 156, 171-73 (1892) (priority cannot rest on matter not adequately and distinctly disclosed in the earlier filing).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a patentee is bound by the disclosures actually made, and that claims may not be expanded by implication to cover what was not clearly presented. See United States v. Dubilier Condenser Corp., 289 U.S. 178, 188-89 (1933); Snow v. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co., 121 U.S. 617, 630-31 (1887). That principle is fatal here. Harari seeks to carry forward the '579 disclosure through a succession of continuations, yet the relevant continuity documents do not unmistakably identify the '579 application in the manner required to support the claimed priority date. At most, the record shows an ambiguity later clarified by a preliminary amendment and by the disclosure in the '708 application. But the '708 application’s disclosure cannot retroactively cure the deficiency in the '838 and '768 applications for purposes of § 120 priority back to the '566 filing date. See Muncie Gear, 315 U.S. at 768-69.

The Board’s error is especially significant because the effect of its ruling is to reach back nearly a decade and deprive Hollmer of the earlier conception date associated with the '601 patent. Priority disputes are resolved by strict application of the statute, not by equitable reconstruction. See Kendall v. Winsor, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 322, 328-29 (1858) (patent rights are created and defined by statute and must be strictly observed); McClain v. Ortmayer, 141 U.S. 419, 424-25 (1891) (the public is entitled to rely on the exact scope of the disclosure and claims). On this record, the '880 application is entitled at most to the December 20, 1996 filing date of the '708 application, not to the April 13, 1989 date of the '566 application.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed.
