**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for U.S. Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—must be answered in the negative. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing additional, constitutionally impermissible qualifications on candidates for federal office, violates the exclusive and exclusive nature of the qualifications set forth in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3 of the U.S. Constitution. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Section 3 of Amendment 73 is unconstitutional and that the entire amendment must fall because the unconstitutional provision is not severable from the remainder. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Non-Delegable**

The Framers of the Constitution explicitly enumerated the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. These provisions are not mere suggestions; they are the sole and exclusive standards by which federal officeholders must be judged. The Constitution provides that “No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, cl. 2. Similarly, the qualifications for Senators are set forth in Article I, Section 3: “No Person shall be a Senator who shall not have attained to the Age of thirty Years, and been nine Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State for which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 3, cl. 3.

These qualifications are not subject to state addition or modification. As this Court held in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 800 (1995), “the Constitution’s qualifications clauses are not merely guidelines; they are the exclusive standards for eligibility.” The Framers deliberately chose to vest in Congress the power to regulate its own membership, and to exclude state authority from the process. To allow states to impose additional qualifications—such as term limits—would undermine the uniformity and national character of federal office, and would permit a patchwork of conflicting standards across the states. Such a result would “undermine the very structure of the federal government” and “threaten the integrity of the national political system.” *Id.* at 801.

The Arkansas Amendment attempts to do precisely that: it adds a new qualification—“having been elected to three or more terms as a member of the U.S. House of Representatives”—to the list of requirements for federal office. This is not a mere procedural rule about ballot access; it is a substantive disqualification. The amendment does not merely require a candidate to meet the constitutional qualifications; it adds a new, state-imposed condition that renders a person ineligible to run. This is a direct violation of the Constitution’s exclusive framework.

**II. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Held That Section 3 Is Not Severable**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that Section 3 of Amendment 73 is not severable from Sections 1 and 2. While the amendment contains a severability clause, the test for severability is not whether the clause exists, but whether the valid portions of the amendment would have passed without the invalid ones. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 749, 375 S.W.2d 809, 811 (1964).

Here, the amendment was presented to the voters as a single, unified package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The Preamble spoke of “elected officials who remain in office too long,” and the entire amendment was framed as a single reform effort. The voters were not given a choice between state term limits and federal term limits; they were asked to approve or reject the entire measure. The sponsors of the amendment knew that Section 3 was constitutionally suspect, yet they bundled it with the state provisions in a way that made it impossible for voters to separate the two.

This is not a case where the invalid provision can be cleanly excised. As the Arkansas Supreme Court observed, “the two groups were not only inextricably linked—they were systemically fused in such a manner that each ceased to have a separate existence for voting purposes.” The amendment’s structure was designed to exploit public support for term limits in general, while hiding the constitutional infirmity of applying them to federal office. This is precisely the kind of “deceptive” practice that courts must reject. *See* *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 422, 316 S.W.2d 185, 189 (1958) (rejecting a “ballot title” that masked a constitutional defect).

The Court has long recognized that when a constitutional amendment is presented as an “all or nothing” package, and the invalid provision is so intertwined with the valid ones that the voters could not have intended to approve the valid parts without the invalid, the entire amendment must fall. *See* *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938) (noting that “the constitutional validity of a statute may depend upon the context in which it is enacted”); *cf.* *U.S. Term Limits, Inc.*, 514 U.S. at 803 (“The Constitution does not permit the States to impose additional qualifications on federal officeholders.”).

**III. The State Has No Power to Impose Additional Qualifications on Federal Officeholders**

The States’ reserved powers under the Tenth Amendment do not extend to altering the qualifications for federal office. The Constitution is a grant of power to the federal government, and the qualifications for federal office are part of the framework of that power.
