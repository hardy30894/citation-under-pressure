**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, forbids the government from “subject[ing] [a person] for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection is not confined to the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution but extends to sentencing proceedings where prior convictions are alleged for enhancement purposes. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that sentencing determinations—particularly those involving recidivist allegations—are integral to the criminal prosecution and thus subject to double jeopardy constraints. *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919); *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187–88 (1957). To hold otherwise would permit the State to relitigate factual issues resolved in a defendant’s favor, thereby exposing him to cumulative punishment for the same conduct in violation of fundamental principles of justice.

The Court of Appeal correctly concluded that retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding is barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Supreme Court has long held that the Clause protects against repeated prosecutions for the same offense, even when those prosecutions occur in the context of sentencing. In *Stroud*, the Court reversed a death sentence imposed after retrial on a murder charge where the jury at the first trial had declined to impose capital punishment. 251 U.S. at 18. The Court reasoned that the jury’s determination at the first trial—implicitly acquitting the defendant of capital murder—precluded a subsequent sentence of death. *Id.* at 18–19. Similarly, in *Green*, the Court held that a defendant acquitted of first-degree murder could not later be convicted of that same offense at a retrial following a successful appeal. 355 U.S. at 187–88. These cases establish that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial not only of formal acquittals but also of factual determinations resolved in a defendant’s favor during sentencing.

The State’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. In *Williams*, the Court upheld a death sentence imposed based on information not presented to the jury, emphasizing the historical flexibility of sentencing procedures. *Id.* at 246–47. However, *Williams* did not address double jeopardy protections in sentencing and expressly distinguished its holding from cases involving prior convictions. *Id.* at 249 n.18. Moreover, *Williams* was decided in the context of a capital sentencing scheme that did not involve retrial of a prior conviction allegation. The Court has since clarified that sentencing proceedings involving recidivist enhancements are subject to double jeopardy constraints. *See United States v. Givens*, 767 F.2d 574, 581 (9th Cir. 1985) (applying *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), to noncapital sentencing).

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Benz*, 282 U.S. 304 (1931), further supports the petitioner’s position. In *Benz*, the Court held that a prior conviction used for sentencing enhancement could not be relitigated after an initial finding in the defendant’s favor. 282 U.S. at 307. The Court reasoned that the prior conviction allegation was “a distinct issue” in the sentencing process, and retrial of that issue would violate the Clause’s prohibition on double jeopardy. *Id.* at 307–08. This principle applies with equal force to the California “three-strikes” scheme, where the existence of a prior conviction directly determines the length of the sentence.

The State’s argument that sentencing proceedings are not subject to double jeopardy because they are not “trials” is unavailing. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that the Clause applies only to guilt-phase proceedings. In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163 (1873), the Court held that a defendant sentenced to a term of imprisonment beyond the statutory maximum could not be resentenced to the same term after the original sentence was vacated. *Id.* at 173–74. The Court reasoned that the sentencing court’s initial determination of the appropriate punishment was final and could not be revisited. *Id.* Similarly, in *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117 (1980), the Court recognized that sentencing determinations are subject to double jeopardy protections, particularly when they involve factual findings that increase the defendant’s punishment.

The State’s attempt to distinguish capital and noncapital sentencing proceedings is unsupported by precedent. The Supreme Court has never held that double jeopardy protections are limited to capital cases. To the contrary, the Court has applied the Clause to sentencing proceedings regardless of whether the ultimate punishment is death or imprisonment. *See, e.g., Stroud*, 251 U.S. at 18; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187–88. The fact that California’s sentencing scheme provides procedural protections for prior conviction allegations does not diminish the constitutional force of the Double Jeopardy Clause. *See In re Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d 857, 862 (1974) (holding that prior conviction allegations must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt).

Finally, the State’s reliance on *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), is misplaced. In *Palko*, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause did not apply to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Id.* at 328. However, the Court overruled *Palko* in *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), holding that the Clause is “fundamental to the American scheme of justice” and thus enforceable against the States. *Id.* at 796. Since *Benton*, the Supreme Court has consistently applied the Clause to sentencing proceedings, including those involving prior conviction allegations. *See, e.g., Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding. The judgment of the California Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
