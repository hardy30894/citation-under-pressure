**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court erred in exercising jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs attributable to Debtor Lisa M. Kirkland’s student loan obligation after the Chapter 13 estate was closed and the discharge entered. The court’s determination that it could adjudicate these post-discharge financial consequences—despite the absence of a claim for relief under § 523(a)(8) and the lack of any pleading or evidence of undue hardship—exceeds the scope of its statutory authority and violates fundamental principles of bankruptcy jurisdiction and finality. This Court should reverse.

I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs After the Estate Was Closed and the Discharge Entered.

The bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1334(a) is limited to “cases under title 11” and “proceedings arising under title 11.” *See In re LTV Corp.*, 252 F.3d 300, 305 (4th Cir. 2001). Once a Chapter 13 case is closed and a discharge is entered, the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over the debtor’s obligations terminates unless a specific proceeding is pending under the Bankruptcy Code. *See In re Hensley*, 237 F.3d 353, 357 (4th Cir. 2001) (“Jurisdiction over a case ends when the case is closed.”). The bankruptcy court here attempted to determine post-petition interest and collection costs *after* the estate was closed and the discharge entered—proceedings that are neither “arising under” nor “related to” the bankruptcy case under the statutory definition. *See 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b)*.

The court’s reliance on § 157(b)(2)(A), which lists “determinations of the validity, extent, or priority of liens” as a core proceeding, is misplaced. *See In re B & B Enters., Inc.*, 135 F.3d 288, 291 (4th Cir. 1998). That provision applies only to liens *within* the bankruptcy estate. Once the estate is closed and the discharge entered, the debtor’s obligations are no longer subject to the bankruptcy court’s core jurisdiction. *See In re Hensley*, 237 F.3d at 357; *In re B & B Enters.*, 135 F.3d at 291. The court’s attempt to retroactively determine post-petition interest and collection costs on a discharged debt constitutes a “post-discharge” determination that falls outside the scope of its jurisdiction.

Moreover, the court’s conclusion that it could award post-petition interest based on the parties’ “agreement” that interest had accrued is legally unsound. *See In re B & B Enters.*, 135 F.3d at 291 (“A bankruptcy court may not exercise jurisdiction over a matter that is not a ‘core proceeding’ or a ‘related case’ under § 1334.”). The mere acknowledgment of interest accrual does not confer jurisdiction to determine its amount or enforceability. *See In re LTV Corp.*, 252 F.3d at 305. The court’s action effectively created a new cause of action outside the bankruptcy framework, which is impermissible.

II. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Awarding Post-Petition Interest Without a Finding of Undue Hardship.

Student loans are generally nondischargeable under § 523(a)(8) of the Bankruptcy Code unless the debtor proves “undue hardship.” *See In re Kline*, 237 F.3d 353, 357 (4th Cir. 2001). The burden is on the debtor to establish undue hardship by a preponderance of the evidence. *See In re Hensley*, 237 F.3d at 357. Here, Kirkland did not plead or prove undue hardship. Indeed, she acknowledged in her complaint that post-petition interest was non-dischargeable. *J.A. 5*. Yet the bankruptcy court awarded $184.40 in post-petition interest without any finding of undue hardship—contrary to the express requirements of § 523(a)(8).

The court’s reasoning—that Kirkland “cannot keep the money refunded to her and, at the same time, claim that she paid it to Sallie Mae”—misapprehends the nature of the discharge. *See In re Kline*, 237 F.3d at 357. The discharge does not extinguish the obligation to pay interest that accrued during the pendency of the bankruptcy, but only if the debt is otherwise nondischargeable. *See In re Hensley*, 237 F.3d at 357. However, the court’s award of interest without a finding of undue hardship renders the discharge ineffective as to that portion of the debt, which is contrary to the Code’s structure. *See In re B & B Enters.*, 135 F.3d at 291.

III. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Award Collection Costs.

The bankruptcy court declined to award collection costs because ECMC “has not provided any statutory or factual basis for the accrual of such costs.” *J.A. 87*. This conclusion is correct, but the court’s attempt to adjudicate the issue at all was improper. Collection costs are not recoverable in bankruptcy absent a contractual or statutory basis. *See In re Hensley*, 237 F.3d at 357. The court’s jurisdiction to determine such costs is limited to proceedings within the bankruptcy estate. *See In re LTV Corp.*, 252 F.3d at 305. Once the estate is closed, the court lacks authority to determine post-discharge collection costs, even if the debtor has not objected.

Moreover, the court’s award of interest—without a finding of undue hardship—effectively creates a new obligation that is not subject to the discharge. *See In re Kline*, 237 F.3d at 357.
