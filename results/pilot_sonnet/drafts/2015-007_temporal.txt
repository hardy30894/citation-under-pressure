**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Procedure Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment's Jury-Trial Guarantee.**

**A. Neither the historical content of the jury-trial right nor this Court's precedent requires unanimity in every determination bearing on sentence.**

The Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee has never been treated as a rigid catalogue of common-law incidents immune from state adaptation. In *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276 (1930), this Court identified the "essential elements of a jury trial at common law" — a jury of twelve, presided over by a judge, returning a unanimous verdict — yet held that even those elements may be altered by the parties with the court's consent, because the Constitution guarantees "a trial by jury as understood and applied at common law," not an immutable procedural code. *Id.* at 288, 312. If the core features of jury trial can be waived altogether, they cannot be constitutionally compelled in every ancillary sentencing determination.

This Court confirmed that states retain "wide discretion" in shaping jury procedure so long as an impartial tribunal decides the ultimate question, upholding New York's use of specially qualified "blue ribbon" juries against a due process and jury-trial challenge. *Fay v. New York*, 332 U.S. 261, 288 (1947). The same latitude governs the division of labor between judge and jury at sentencing. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court held that a sentencing judge could rely on information never submitted to the jury under the reasonable-doubt standard, explaining that modern "concepts individualizing punishment" counsel against freezing sentencing procedure into the mold of guilt-phase trial procedure. *Id.* at 247. If due process tolerates a judge sentencing on information the jury never adjudicated, it necessarily tolerates a jury's non-unanimous *advisory* recommendation followed by independent judicial findings — precisely Florida's structure.

**B. States retain wide leeway to allocate fact-finding responsibility between judge and jury in matters bearing on punishment.**

In *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), this Court upheld Texas's recidivist procedure permitting the jury to hear prior-conviction evidence for sentence enhancement, recognizing that "the States have wide leeway" in structuring such procedures and that due process does not impose a single national model. *Id.* at 564. Florida's allocation — an advisory jury recommendation, followed by judicial fact-finding on aggravation — falls comfortably within that leeway.

**C. States may likewise assign the burden of proving a capacity-based mitigating condition to the defendant, and adjudicate it through ordinary sentencing procedure rather than a jury-determined absolute bar.**

In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court held that a state may require a defendant to establish insanity — a condition bearing directly on culpability — without offending due process, because "the existence of a burden on the defendant of... proving insanity" has never been thought incompatible with a fair trial. *Id.* at 799. The same reasoning supports Florida's choice to treat mental retardation as mitigation to be weighed by the sentencer, rather than a threshold question a jury alone must resolve unanimously.

**II. Florida's Treatment of Mental Capacity as a Mitigating Circumstance Comports With the Eighth Amendment.**

The Eighth Amendment's touchstone is whether a punishment offends "the dignity of man" and society's "evolving standards of decency," *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100–01 (1958); it does not dictate the procedural mechanism by which contested facts bearing on that punishment are found. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (Eighth Amendment concerned with "traditional humanity" in the *manner* of punishment, not the procedural apparatus surrounding sentencing determinations).

This Court has likewise declined to constitutionalize a single doctrinal approach to mental impairment in criminal law. In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), a plurality refused to displace a state's chosen framework for accounting for diminished capacity, emphasizing that such matters are properly left to "wide latitude" in state legislative and judicial judgment rather than a uniform constitutional rule. *Id.* at 535–36. Florida's procedure — permitting a defendant to present evidence of intellectual limitation as mitigation while the sentencer makes the ultimate capacity finding — is a legitimate exercise of that latitude.

Finally, due process affords states substantial room to structure criminal procedure so long as the result does not offend "a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Florida's scheme — an advisory jury recommendation, judicial aggravation findings, and judicial resolution of capacity-based mitigation — reflects considered legislative judgment, not fundamental unfairness.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
