ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Concluding that the Asserted Claims are Not Entitled to the Benefit of the Filing Date of the Original Application

The district court's conclusion that the asserted claims are not entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application is based on its finding that the earlier application did not provide a written description of the invention claimed in the asserted patents, as required by 35 U.S.C. § 112. However, this conclusion is not supported by the law.

To satisfy the written description requirement, the original application must "reasonably convey to those skilled in the art that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter as of the filing date." Ariad Pharms., Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co., 598 F.3d 1336, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (en banc). The written description requirement is not a "rigid" or "inflexible" test, but rather a "flexible" and "fact-specific" inquiry. Id. at 1351-52. The district court erred in failing to consider the entirety of the original application, including the drawings and the specification, in determining whether the written description requirement was satisfied. See Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563-64 (Fed. Cir. 1991).

The district court's reliance on the addition of "substantial new matter" to the 2000 CIP Application is misplaced. The addition of new matter does not necessarily mean that the original application did not provide a sufficient written description of the invention. See In re Wright, 866 F.2d 422, 425 (Fed. Cir. 1989). Furthermore, the district court failed to identify any specific claim limitation that was not supported by the original application. See Tronzo v. Biomet, Inc., 156 F.3d 1154, 1158 (Fed. Cir. 1998).

The asserted claims depend from independent claim 1, which recites a "vending machine for vending telecommunications channel access to a customer." The original application discloses a vending machine that provides telecommunications access to customers, which is sufficient to support the claimed invention. See Univ. of W. Va. v. VanVoorhies, 278 F.3d 1288, 1296-97 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The district court erred in failing to consider the similarity between the claimed invention and the disclosure in the original application. See Lockwood v. Am. Airlines, Inc., 107 F.3d 1565, 1572 (Fed. Cir. 1997).

If the asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application, then the MobileStar Network is not prior art under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), and the district court's grant of summary judgment of anticipation must be reversed.

II. The District Court's Construction of "Customer Interface" is Supported by the Specification

The district court's construction of "customer interface" as encompassing an interface that is located on the customer's laptop is supported by the specification. The specification describes the customer interface as "an interface that enables information to be passed between a human user and hardware or software components of a system." This construction is consistent with the language of the claim and the disclosure in the specification. See Phillips v. AWH Corp., 415 F.3d 1303, 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (en banc).

T-Mobile's argument that the customer interface must be located on the vending machine itself is not supported by the specification. The specification does not limit the location of the customer interface to the vending machine. See Rexnord Corp. v. Laitram Corp., 274 F.3d 1336, 1344 (Fed. Cir. 2001). The district court's construction of "customer interface" is entitled to deference, and should be affirmed. See Cybor Corp. v. FAS Techs., Inc., 138 F.3d 1448, 1456 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (en banc).

In conclusion, the district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims are not entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application. The asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application, and the MobileStar Network is not prior art under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). The district court's grant of summary judgment of anticipation must be reversed. See Glaxo Grp. Ltd. v. Apotex, Inc., 376 F.3d 1339, 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2004).
