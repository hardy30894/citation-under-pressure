**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Ohio’s clemency process does not implicate a constitutionally protected liberty interest. This Court has long recognized that clemency proceedings are an integral part of the criminal justice system, and thus, due process protections attach. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 281 (1998) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that clemency is "an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant"). The Court in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), distinguished between the existence of a liberty interest and the extent of due process protections, holding that while no protected interest may exist in clemency itself, the process by which clemency is granted or denied must comport with due process when it is part of the adjudicatory system.

Here, Ohio’s clemency process is not merely a formality but a critical stage in the administration of justice. The Ohio Supreme Court has recognized that clemency decisions are subject to judicial review when they implicate constitutional rights. *See State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994) (holding that the legislature cannot limit the Governor’s discretion in clemency but that the process must still comply with constitutional norms). Thus, the Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that no protected interest exists is inconsistent with this Court’s jurisprudence.

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A "HOBSON’S CHOICE" ON INMATES BY REQUIRING PARTICIPATION IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW THAT MAY BE USED AGAINST THEM.**

The Sixth Circuit’s analysis of Woodard’s Fifth Amendment claim was incomplete. The Fifth Amendment provides that "[n]o person shall ... be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. The Court has held that the Fifth Amendment applies not only to formal criminal proceedings but also to any proceeding where the state seeks to compel testimony that may later be used in a criminal case. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976) (holding that the Fifth Amendment applies to prison disciplinary hearings where the inmate’s statements could be used in a criminal prosecution).

Ohio’s clemency process, by offering an interview that may be used in subsequent proceedings, creates a direct conflict with the Fifth Amendment. The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that the process is voluntary ignores the coercive nature of the choice: inmates face the dilemma of either waiving their Fifth Amendment rights or forgoing the opportunity to present mitigating evidence to the clemency authority. This "Hobson’s choice" violates the Fifth Amendment’s prohibition on compelled self-incrimination. *See Lefkowitz v. Cunningham*, 431 U.S. 801, 808 (1977) (holding that the state cannot condition a benefit on the waiver of constitutional rights).

**III. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THAT THE CLEMENCY PROCESS MUST INCLUDE MINIMAL DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

Even if clemency itself does not create a protected liberty interest, the process by which it is administered must comply with due process when it is part of the criminal justice system. *See Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985) (holding that due process protections apply to state-created appellate procedures). The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that the clemency process is too attenuated from the trial to require due process protections is inconsistent with this Court’s recognition that clemency is an integral part of the adjudicatory system. *See Woodard*, 523 U.S. at 281.

At minimum, due process requires that inmates be informed of their rights before participating in a clemency interview and that any statements made cannot be used against them in future criminal proceedings. *See Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 324 (holding that due process protections apply to prison disciplinary hearings where the inmate’s statements could be used in a criminal prosecution). The Sixth Circuit’s remand for further proceedings was correct in principle, but the lower court must be instructed to apply these minimal due process protections to Ohio’s clemency process.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s decision and hold that Ohio’s clemency process creates a protected liberty interest that triggers due process protections and that the Fifth Amendment prohibits Ohio from compelling participation in a clemency interview that may be used against the inmate.
