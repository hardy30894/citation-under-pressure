**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve substantial government interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 120 S. Ct. 2480, 147 L. Ed. 2d 597 (2000), squarely governs this case and forecloses the petitioners’ challenge. The Court should affirm.

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROTECT PROXIMITY-BASED COMMUNICATIONS THAT INTERFERE WITH THE RIGHT TO ACCESS HEALTHCARE FACILITIES**

The First Amendment protects speech, but not speech that is so coercive or obstructive in its physical manifestation that it infringes upon the fundamental right of individuals to enter and exit a healthcare facility. In *Hill v. Colorado*, the Supreme Court upheld a Colorado statute that prohibited any person from “knowingly approaching within 8 feet of any individual entering or leaving a health care facility” for the purpose of influencing that individual’s decision about abortion. 530 U.S. at 707, 120 S. Ct. 2480. The Court emphasized that the law was “content-neutral,” “narrowly tailored,” and “designed to protect the right of access to a health care facility.” Id. at 715, 120 S. Ct. 2480. The Court recognized that “the right of access to a health care facility is a fundamental right,” and that “the State has a substantial interest in protecting that right.” Id. at 716, 120 S. Ct. 2480.

The Massachusetts law at issue here is indistinguishable in substance and purpose. It prohibits any person from knowingly entering or remaining within a 35-foot buffer zone around the entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(b). Like the Colorado law, it is content-neutral, applies equally to all speakers regardless of viewpoint, and is designed to protect the right of patients and staff to enter and exit clinics without harassment or obstruction. The law does not prohibit speech; it merely regulates the time, place, and manner of speech to ensure that access to medical care is not impeded by coercive or intimidating conduct.

The petitioners argue that the buffer zone prevents them from engaging in “effective” counseling. But the First Amendment does not guarantee a right to communicate at a particular physical proximity. As the Court held in *Hill*, “the First Amendment does not require the government to allow speech that obstructs access to a facility.” 530 U.S. at 716, 120 S. Ct. 2480. The government may regulate speech in public forums—such as sidewalks adjacent to clinics—when the regulation serves a significant interest and leaves open alternative channels of communication. Id. at 715, 120 S. Ct. 2480. The Massachusetts law satisfies all three prongs of the *Hill* test.

**II. THE LAW IS NARROWLY TAILORED AND LEAVES ADEQUATE ALTERNATIVE CHANNELS OF COMMUNICATION**

The petitioners’ claim that the buffer zone renders their speech ineffective is belied by the record. At the Boston clinic, McCullen, Zarrella, and Cadin continue to distribute literature, display signs, and speak from a distance. Smith uses a loudspeaker to amplify prayers on designated days. At the Worcester clinic, Bashour and Clark engage in prayer and display signs. Shea at the Springfield clinic continues to hold signs and pray aloud. These activities occur outside the buffer zone and are fully protected under the law.

The First Circuit correctly concluded that “adequate communicative channels remain available” to the petitioners. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F. Supp. 2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010). The law does not prohibit speech; it merely requires that speech occur at a distance that does not interfere with access. The petitioners may still speak, distribute literature, display signs, wear symbolic clothing, and pray—activities that are constitutionally protected and fully effective in conveying their message. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that “the First Amendment does not require the government to provide a particular forum or a particular method of communication.” *United States v. Grace*, 461 U.S. 171, 177, 103 S. Ct. 1702, 75 L. Ed. 2d 736 (1983). The Massachusetts law does not deny the petitioners any meaningful opportunity to communicate.

**III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RE-LITIGATION OF THE FACIAL CHALLENGE**

The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law is barred by the law of the case doctrine. In *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009), this court held that the Massachusetts buffer zone law was a valid time-place-manner regulation under *Hill v. Colorado*. The Supreme Court denied certiorari, 544 U.S. 974, 125 S. Ct. 1827, 161 L. Ed. 2d 724 (2005), thereby affirming the correctness of that decision. The doctrine of law of the case “prevents relitigation in the trial court of matters that were explicitly or implicitly decided by an earlier appellate decision in the same case.” *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). The petitioners have not demonstrated any of the narrow exceptions—such as a dramatic change in controlling legal authority or a blatantly erroneous decision that would work a miscarriage of justice.
