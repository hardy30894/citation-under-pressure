# ARGUMENT

## I. A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES ALONE DOES NOT SATISFY ARTICLE III WHEN THE UNDERLYING CONDUCT HAS CEASED AND CANNOT RECUR.

Article III confines the judicial power to "Cases" and "Controversies," U.S. Const. art. III, § 2, a limitation that "subsists through all stages of federal judicial proceedings." *Spencer v. Kemna*, 523 U.S. 1, 7 (1998). A case becomes moot when "the issues presented are no longer 'live.'" *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). This Court has long recognized that voluntary cessation of challenged conduct moots a case unless "subsequent events ma[ke] it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000) (quoting *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Ass'n*, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). GGC's revision of its speech-zone policy—removing the very restrictions Petitioners challenged—satisfies that standard, as the courts below correctly held and as Petitioners do not contest.

Petitioners nonetheless argue that a bare request for nominal damages keeps a case alive regardless of whether any other relief remains available. That argument cannot be reconciled with this Court's settled mootness jurisprudence, which asks not whether *some* damages label appears in the complaint, but whether a decision on the merits will have any "effect in the real world." *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013). A live controversy requires that the plaintiff have "a personal stake in the outcome" sufficient to support a live remedy, not a symbolic one. *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962).

This Court's decision in *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299 (1986), confirms that nominal damages serve a narrow, formal function: they "recognize[] the importance to organized society that [a] right be scrupulously observed," *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978), but they do not compensate for any actual injury, since by definition no such injury has been proven. Where, as here, the plaintiff alleges no compensatory injury and the policy that occasioned the alleged constitutional violation no longer exists, allowing the case to proceed for the sole purpose of awarding a nominal sum "would convert the judicial process into a vehicle for vindication of value interests," a role that Article III does not assign to federal courts. *Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church & State, Inc.*, 454 U.S. 464, 473 (1982).

## II. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT PETITIONERS' COMPLAINT SOUGHT ONLY NOMINAL, NOT COMPENSATORY, DAMAGES.

The court of appeals, applying the familiar rule that "compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated," *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986), correctly concluded that Petitioners' First Amended Complaint pleaded no actual injury of the kind that compensatory damages redress. This Court has instructed that such injury may include "impairment of reputation . . . , personal humiliation, and mental anguish and suffering," *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 264 (1978), but Petitioners' complaint contained no factual allegations of any such harm—only unadorned assertions that they had "suffered, and continue to suffer, irreparable harm," without specifying its nature. A court is not required to credit conclusory allegations of injury unmoored from supporting facts. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) ("threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice"). The Eleventh Circuit's construction of the complaint—including its prayer for relief, which requested only "nominal damages" alongside injunctive and declaratory relief—was faithful both to the pleadings and to this Court's precedent distinguishing abstract constitutional harm from compensable injury.

## III. PETITIONERS' AS-APPLIED CHALLENGE DOES NOT ALTER THE MOOTNESS ANALYSIS BECAUSE NO THREAT OF FUTURE INJURY REMAINS.

Petitioners' effort to recast their suit as an as-applied challenge fares no better, because mootness turns on whether "a live controversy" persists as to the *parties before the court*, not merely on the doctrinal label attached to the claim. *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013). Once the challenged policy was rescinded and neither Petitioner faced any prospect of renewed enforcement, the underlying constitutional dispute—whether facial or as-applied—ceased to be "live" within the meaning of *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). This Court has cautioned that a party may not manufacture a continuing case or controversy through creative pleading after the fact; the proper inquiry looks to whether "the practical consequences of a decision" remain meaningful for the litigants at hand. *Chafin*, 568 U.S. at 172. Petitioners identify no such consequence here, nor could they, since GGC's policy no longer applies to them or to any GGC student.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
