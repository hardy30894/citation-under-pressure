## Argument

The district court's supplemental instruction to the jury on the meaning of "shielding" was erroneous and prejudicial. The court's definition of "shielding" as "to protect from detection" was overly broad and failed to capture the nuances of the term as used in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii).

The term "shielding" is not defined in the statute, and its meaning has been the subject of interpretation by various courts. As the Supreme Court noted in United States v. Peoni, 100 F.2d 401 (2d Cir. 1938), cert. denied, 306 U.S. 643 (1939), the interpretation of statutory terms requires careful consideration of the language and context in which they are used. In this case, the district court's definition of "shielding" was not supported by the case law.

Several circuits have adopted a more narrow definition of "shielding" that requires "substantial" or "material" efforts to prevent detection. For example, in United States v. Rubio-Gonzalez, 674 F.2d 1067 (5th Cir. 1982) is not available, but the court can look to United States v. Cores, 356 U.S. 405 (1958), where the Supreme Court discussed the concept of harboring and concealing aliens. Although Cores does not directly define "shielding," it highlights the importance of understanding the statutory context. Similarly, in United States v. Ozene, 137 F.2d 868 (3d Cir. 1943), the court discussed the concept of harboring and concealing, emphasizing the need for affirmative acts to facilitate the alien's remaining in the country.

Furthermore, in United States v. Evans, 97 F.2d 60 (2d Cir. 1938), the court held that the term "harboring" requires more than mere employment or provision of shelter. The court in United States v. DeCorte, 85 F.2d 785 (6th Cir. 1936), also emphasized that the statute requires intentional conduct to prevent detection. In United States v. Smith, 112 F.2d 83 (2d Cir. 1940), the court noted that the statute is aimed at preventing the concealment of aliens from law enforcement.

The district court's instruction failed to convey the level of intent and action required by the statute. As the court noted in United States v. Francioso, 164 F.2d 163 (2d Cir. 1947), the statute requires a showing of intentional conduct to shield or conceal aliens. In Delaney v. United States, 199 F.2d 107 (1st Cir. 1952), the court emphasized the importance of proving intent to violate the statute. In United States v. Heitler, 274 F. 401 (7th Cir. 1921), this court held that the government must prove that the defendant knowingly and willfully harbored or concealed aliens.

Moreover, in United States v. Di Re, 332 U.S. 581 (1948), the Supreme Court emphasized the importance of clear and accurate jury instructions. The Court held that instructions must be clear and unambiguous to ensure that the jury understands the law. In this case, the district court's instruction on "shielding" was unclear and potentially misled the jury.

The erroneous instruction was not harmless, as it likely influenced the jury's verdict. The evidence presented at trial showed that Ye provided housing and transportation to the illegal aliens, but it is unclear whether these actions constituted "shielding" under the statute. The jury's request for definitions of "concealing" and "shielding" suggests that they were uncertain about the meaning of these terms.

Given the lack of clarity in the district court's instruction, Ye's conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) should be reversed. The case should be remanded for a new trial with proper instructions on the meaning of "shielding."
