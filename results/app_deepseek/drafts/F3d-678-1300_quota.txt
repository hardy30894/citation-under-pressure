ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Leader’s Motion for JMOL Because Facebook Failed to Prove by Clear and Convincing Evidence That the Asserted Claims Were in Public Use or on Sale Before the Critical Date.

A patent is invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) only if the invention was both “ready for patenting” and “the subject of a commercial offer for sale” more than one year before the patent’s effective filing date. *Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998). The “ready for patenting” prong requires that the invention be reduced to practice—either actually or constructively—before the critical date. *Id.* at 67–68. Facebook bore the burden of proving invalidity by clear and convincing evidence. *Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. P’ship*, 564 U.S. 91, 106 (2011). The district court’s denial of JMOL must be reversed because no reasonable jury could have found that Leader’s pre-critical-date activities satisfied either prong of the *Pfaff* test.

A. Facebook Did Not Prove That the Leader2Leader® Product Was a Reduction to Practice of the Asserted Claims.

To establish a reduction to practice, Facebook had to show that Leader “actually built and tested” an embodiment that “embodie[d] every claim limitation.” *Invitrogen Corp. v. Biocrest Mfg., L.P.*, 424 F.3d 1374, 1379 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The evidence at trial showed that the Leader2Leader® product was still under development in 2002. Jeffrey Lamb testified that the project was completed only in the “2002ish time frame,” and that the product was still being refined. J.A. 24829. Moreover, the white paper’s statement that the platform was “operational now with low user volumes” and “[f]ully developed” is ambiguous and does not establish that the specific claimed features—such as “dynamically associating metadata” and “tracking movement of the user”—were actually implemented. *See New Railhead Mfg., L.L.C. v. Vermeer Mfg. Co.*, 298 F.3d 1290, 1297 (Fed. Cir. 2002) (holding that mere “field testing” of a prototype does not constitute a reduction to practice absent proof that the embodiment met all claim limitations). The district court therefore erred in permitting the jury to infer reduction to practice from such equivocal evidence.

B. The White Paper Did Not Constitute a “Commercial Offer for Sale” Under § 102(b).

The on-sale bar requires a “commercial offer for sale” that would be “understood as such in the commercial community.” *Group One, Ltd. v. Hallmark Cards, Inc.*, 254 F.3d 1041, 1046–47 (Fed. Cir. 2001). The white paper was a preliminary proposal, not a definitive offer. It stated that Leader was “offering 20,000 software licenses,” but it did not include price terms, delivery dates, or other essential terms that would make it binding upon acceptance. *See Linear Tech. Corp. v. Impala Linear Corp.*, 379 F.3d 1311, 1320 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (holding that a “mere invitation for offers” does not trigger the on-sale bar). The document was a marketing pitch, not a contract. Indeed, the white paper’s statement that Leader was “already commercializing” the product is aspirational and does not transform the proposal into a commercial offer. *See Ferag AG v. Quipp Inc.*, 45 F.3d 1562, 1566 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (holding that “an offer for sale” must be “a communication that is sufficient to constitute a legally binding contract”). Facebook presented no evidence of any actual sale or contract for sale arising from the white paper. Without a firm offer, the on-sale bar cannot apply.

C. The Jury’s Verdict Was Based on Impermissible Speculation, Not Clear and Convincing Evidence.

Clear and convincing evidence requires “a firm belief or conviction that the facts are true.” *Buildex Inc. v. Kason Indus., Inc.*, 849 F.2d 1461, 1463 (Fed. Cir. 1988). The jury’s verdict rested on the white paper and Lamb’s vague testimony about a “2002ish” completion date. Such evidence is insufficient to meet the heightened standard. *See Medtronic, Inc. v. Cardiac Pacemakers, Inc.*, 721 F.2d 1563, 1565 (Fed. Cir. 1983) (reversing invalidity finding where evidence was “ambiguous and inconclusive”). Moreover, the district court’s claim construction was not challenged, and the patent’s claims require “dynamically associating metadata” and “tracking movement of the user”—limitations that Facebook never proved were present in the Leader2Leader® product. *See Crown Operations Int’l, Ltd. v. Solutia Inc.*, 289 F.3d 1367, 1377 (Fed. Cir. 2002) (finding no public use where the accused product did not practice the claimed invention). Because Facebook’s case was built on inference and speculation, the district court abused its discretion in denying Leader’s motion for a new trial. *See Unisplay, S.A. v. Am. Elec. Sign Co.*, 69 F.3d 512, 517 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (holding that a new trial is warranted when the verdict is against the great weight of the evidence).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and hold that the asserted claims are not invalid under § 102(b), or, in the alternative, remand for a new trial.
