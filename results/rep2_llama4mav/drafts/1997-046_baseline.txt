The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency procedure does not implicate a protected life or liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. As the Supreme Court held in Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), a state's clemency procedure does not create a federally protected liberty interest simply by virtue of its existence. The Court in Dumschat rejected the argument that a prisoner's liberty interest was implicated by the state's commutation procedure, emphasizing that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Id. at 464. Similarly, Ohio's clemency procedure, as outlined in Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, does not create a protected life or liberty interest.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause does not apply to clemency proceedings in the same way it does to other stages of the adjudicatory process. As Judge Nelson noted in his concurrence, "judicial review of clemency proceedings is simply not 'appropriate.'" Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464. The Supreme Court's decision in Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), which held that due process guarantees a criminal defendant the effective assistance of counsel on their first appeal as of right, does not extend to clemency proceedings. Clemency is a matter of executive discretion, and the Constitution does not require that it be subject to the same procedural safeguards as the adjudicatory process.

Furthermore, Ohio's clemency procedure does not violate Woodard's Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. The regulation governing the clemency interview, Ohio Department of Rehabilitation and Correction's "Death Penalty Clemency Procedure," explicitly states that any statement given by the prisoner at the interview shall be considered at the clemency hearing, but it does not compel the prisoner to answer any questions. As the Supreme Court held in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), a state's procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment simply because it may create a difficult choice for the individual. The Court in Baxter emphasized that the relief granted by the lower courts was "premature" because it was based on a hypothetical risk of self-incrimination. Similarly, Woodard's Fifth Amendment claim is premature, as he has not yet participated in the clemency interview and may never be required to incriminate himself.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's judgment should be affirmed. Ohio's clemency procedure is constitutional, and Woodard's claims under the Fourteenth and Fifth Amendments are without merit.
