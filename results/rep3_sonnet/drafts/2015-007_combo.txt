# ARGUMENT

## I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Procedure, Which Assigns the Jury an Advisory Role and the Judge the Final Sentencing Determination, Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee protects the defendant's right to have a jury determine guilt of the offense charged; it has never been understood to require that every fact bearing on the *selection* of a sentence, once guilt is established, be found by a jury applying a unanimous, beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. This Court settled the constitutional allocation of sentencing authority in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), holding that a sentencing judge may rely on information never submitted to the jury, because "modern concepts individualizing punishment have made it all the more necessary that a sentencing judge not be denied an opportunity to obtain pertinent information." *Id.* at 247. If due process permits a judge to consider unadjudicated facts affecting an ordinary sentence, the Sixth Amendment likewise tolerates a scheme in which the judge, informed by an advisory jury verdict, makes the ultimate capital-sentencing determination.

This Court reaffirmed the point in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), sustaining a recidivist sentencing procedure in which the judge, not the jury, resolved the predicate facts enhancing punishment, and rejecting the claim that due process compelled jury fact-finding at sentencing. Nothing in *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968), which incorporated the jury-trial right against the States, disturbs this framework; *Duncan* addressed the right to a jury at the guilt phase and said nothing to convert the historically judicial function of sentencing into a jury function. Indeed, this Court has long recognized that the specific incidents of jury trial guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment are not rigidly fixed but may be adapted by the States to their own criminal-justice traditions. *See Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581 (1900) (States retain latitude to define the composition and procedures of their juries); *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934) (due process does not freeze state criminal procedure into a single federal mold).

Nor does the Constitution demand a unanimous jury recommendation before a judge may impose death. Although this Court observed in *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948), that unanimity is required "in criminal cases" tried under federal rule, that requirement rested on federal practice, not a freestanding Sixth Amendment command binding the States in every context. Florida's jury performs an advisory function; the ultimate legal responsibility for weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances rests, as it has traditionally rested, with the trial judge. Because the jury's verdict is advisory rather than adjudicative of an element of the offense, the absence of unanimity in that recommendation does not implicate the constitutional guarantee recognized in *Duncan*.

## II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require That a Jury, Rather Than the Judge, Determine the Fact of Mental Retardation as a Bar to Execution.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition of cruel and unusual punishments governs the *substance* of a punishment—its nature and proportionality—not the identity of the factfinder who determines a defendant's eligibility for it. See *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910) (Eighth Amendment inquiry directed to whether the punishment itself is disproportionate to the offense); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (the constitutional inquiry concerns whether the punishment inflicts "torture or a lingering death," not the procedural mechanism by which it is imposed). This Court's cruel-and-unusual-punishment jurisprudence, from *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), through *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), and *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), evaluates punishments against "evolving standards of decency," *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101, but none of these decisions suggests that the Eighth Amendment dictates *which* institutional actor—judge or jury—must resolve a predicate factual issue such as intellectual capacity. States have always possessed broad discretion to structure the procedural mechanisms of their criminal justice systems, so long as the punishment ultimately imposed is not itself disproportionate or barbarous. *See Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105 (due process leaves States free "to abolish old and to introduce new practices" in criminal procedure).

Florida's procedure—permitting the trial judge, following a full evidentiary hearing, to determine whether a defendant meets the clinical and statutory criteria for mental retardation—satisfies this standard. The judge applied the same three-part standard this Court has recognized as constitutionally adequate to identify offenders exempt from execution, and Hurst received a full opportunity to present his evidence, consistent with the individualized-consideration principle of *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 247. That the determination was made by the court rather than the jury raises no Eighth Amendment concern, since the Amendment's guarantee runs to the reliability and proportionality of the *punishment*, not to the formal allocation of fact-finding responsibility between judge and jury.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Florida's capital sentencing scheme comports with both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments, and the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
