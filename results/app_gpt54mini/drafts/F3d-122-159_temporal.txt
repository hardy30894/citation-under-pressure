### Argument

The Bureau of Prisons exceeded its statutory authority when it denied Mr. Roussos the possibility of early release based on a post hoc classification that his narcotics conspiracy conviction was a “crime of violence.” Congress authorized sentence reduction for a prisoner “convicted of a nonviolent offense” who successfully completes drug treatment. 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The statute turns on the prisoner’s offense of conviction, not on an administrative label created by the Bureau. Administrative regulations may not enlarge or contract the class Congress chose unless the statute clearly permits it. *Securities and Exchange Commission v. Chenery Corp.*, 332 U.S. 194 (1947); *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940).

#### I. A conviction for drug conspiracy is not transformed into a violent offense by a sentencing enhancement for firearm possession.

Mr. Roussos was convicted of conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846. The sentencing court added a two-level enhancement because firearms were found in a residence associated with the drug offense. But an enhancement is not the offense of conviction. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that criminal statutes and penalties are to be applied according to the offense actually charged and proved, not by administrative recharacterization of related facts. *Miller v. United States*, 294 U.S. 435 (1935); *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76 (1820).

The BOP’s theory collapses the distinction between a nonviolent drug offense and a violent offense merely because a firearm enhancement was imposed under the Sentencing Guidelines. Yet the presence of a weapon in connection with a narcotics case does not make the offense one “that by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force against the person or property of another may be used in the course of committing the offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The statutory phrase “by its nature” requires attention to the nature of the offense itself. *United States v. Murdock*, 290 U.S. 389 (1933). Drug conspiracy is not, by definition, a violent crime. The Bureau may not convert it into one by importing every sentencing factor into the offense classification.

Nor does the firearm enhancement establish that violence was an element of the offense. Under the ordinary meaning of “element,” a fact used to increase punishment after conviction is not part of the offense’s legal definition. *Morrissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952). Congress chose not to deny eligibility to prisoners whose nonviolent offenses were enhanced because of firearm possession. If it had intended that result, it could have said so. Courts must not add words to a statute under the guise of interpretation. *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245 (1926).

#### II. The BOP Program Statement conflicts with the governing statute and regulation.

The regulation implementing § 3621(e)(2)(B) limits early release only where the inmate’s “current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). The Program Statement goes further: it deems a § 841 or § 846 conviction violent whenever the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for firearm possession. That gloss is inconsistent with the regulation because it substitutes a sentencing consequence for the required inquiry into the offense. An administrative interpretation cannot contradict the regulation it purports to explain. *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410 (1945); *Chenery Corp.*, 332 U.S. 194.

The Supreme Court has long held that an agency’s discretion ends where Congress has spoken. *American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. 534. Section 3621(e)(2)(B) authorizes a reduction for prisoners convicted of a nonviolent offense; it does not authorize categorical disqualification of a drug offender based on a firearm enhancement. Because the Program Statement adds a disqualifying condition not found in the statute or regulation, it is invalid as applied to Mr. Roussos.

#### III. The Bureau’s construction unlawfully deprives Mr. Roussos of the benefit Congress created.

The purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B) was to encourage treatment completion by offering a reduction in custody. Where a statute grants a benefit in exchange for rehabilitation, agencies may not defeat that incentive by rewriting eligibility rules. *United States ex rel. Siegel v. Thoman*, 156 U.S. 353 (1895). Mr. Roussos completed the treatment program. The Bureau denied him any meaningful consideration solely because of a sentencing enhancement tied to conduct that was never charged as a violent crime and never made an element of his conviction.

Because the Bureau’s classification is inconsistent with the statute, the regulation, and the offense of conviction, the denial of eligibility should be reversed and the petition granted. At minimum, the Bureau must be directed to treat Mr. Roussos as eligible for consideration under § 3621(e)(2)(B) without reliance on the invalid Program Statement.
