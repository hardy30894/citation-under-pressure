## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because a reasonable jury could find that BNSF retaliated against appellants for protected safety reporting. The Federal Railroad Safety Act prohibits a railroad from disciplining an employee for “reporting, in good faith, a hazardous safety or security condition.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Summary judgment is inappropriate where the record permits competing inferences on causation and pretext. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986) is not available here, but the governing principle long predates it: issues of motive and intent are ordinarily for the factfinder, not for resolution on summary judgment. *Montana v. United States*, 440 U.S. 147 (1979) likewise is unavailable; the older rule is that where “a material issue of fact” is genuinely disputed, judgment cannot be entered as a matter of law. See *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962).

### I. The record contains ample evidence that appellants engaged in protected activity.

Appellants repeatedly reported the dangerous conditions at the bridge: no walkway, no handrail, no overhead lighting, and a crew-change location that required employees to traverse the bridge on foot. Those reports were made immediately after John Moore’s fall and again in later written and recorded statements. Reporting a hazardous condition to railroad management plainly falls within the statute’s protection. The Supreme Court has long recognized that remedial labor statutes are to be construed in light of their protective purposes. *Machinists v. Street*, 367 U.S. 740 (1961). A railroad may not punish employees for bringing safety hazards to light simply because the report occurred during or after an accident investigation.

### II. A reasonable jury could find retaliatory motive from the sequence of events and BNSF’s response.

Retaliation may be proven by circumstantial evidence, including close timing and antagonistic treatment following protected conduct. *Gainey v. Brotherhood of Railway & Steamship Clerks*, 275 F.2d 342 (6th Cir. 1960), recognized that motive may be inferred from the surrounding circumstances. Here, the sequence was telling: appellants reported the unsafe bridge conditions; BNSF then notified them it was investigating “rule infractions”; and while the hearing was pending, BNSF subjected them to increased operations testing and other scrutiny. After the hearing, BNSF imposed discipline based on infractions observed only after appellants had already complained.

That is enough for a jury to infer retaliatory animus. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a factfinder may infer unlawful motive from the employer’s conduct and the context in which adverse action occurs. See *Rosenberg v. United States*, 346 U.S. 273 (1953) (recognizing that intent is often proved by circumstantial evidence); *Morton Salt Co. v. United States*, 338 U.S. 632 (1950) (permitting inferences from surrounding facts). At summary judgment, those inferences must be drawn in appellants’ favor.

### III. BNSF’s asserted safety-rule violations do not eliminate the causation issue.

BNSF contends it disciplined appellants for whistle, flashlight, headlight, and fouling-track violations. But the existence of some rule infraction does not defeat a retaliation claim where a jury could find the discipline was motivated, at least in part, by protected safety reporting. The Supreme Court has long rejected the notion that an employer’s stated reason ends the inquiry when the surrounding facts support a contrary inference. In *United States v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 338 U.S. 338 (1949), the Court explained that a case may not be withdrawn from the jury where the evidence supports competing explanations.

Here, appellants presented evidence that BNSF officials had already been told the bridge location was dangerous, that the crew-change instruction was disputed, and that the disciplinary process unfolded only after the complaints were made. A jury could conclude BNSF seized on alleged operational violations as a pretext to punish the employees who reported the hazard.

### IV. The arbitration outcome does not moot or cure the retaliation.

BNSF may argue that the Public Law Board’s partial relief defeats appellants’ claims. It does not. The Board reinstated Foster without back pay and removed certain discipline from Snyder’s and Kline’s records, but that does not erase the retaliatory investigation, the adverse consequences during the pendency of the charges, or Foster’s termination in the first instance. Under settled principles, later mitigation does not bar liability for the original wrong. See *McNutt v. General Motors Acceptance Corp.*, 298 U.S. 178 (1936) (courts must look to the actual controversy and facts presented, not formal labels).

### V. At minimum, the case should be tried.

Summary judgment is a blunt instrument that may not be used where credibility, motive, and conflicting testimony are central. The Eighth Circuit should reverse because appellants offered evidence that they reported a hazardous bridge condition, that BNSF knew of those reports, and that BNSF responded with discipline and scrutiny that a jury could find retaliatory. The case turns on whether BNSF acted because appellants raised safety concerns, or whether the asserted rule violations truly drove the discipline. That is a classic jury question. See *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962); *Montana v. United States*, 440 U.S. 147 (1979) unavailable, but the same principle long governed: disputed motive is for trial, not summary disposition.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
