The Massachusetts statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation. It restricts speech only within fixed 35-foot zones around clinic entrances, exits, and driveways, leaving open ample alternative channels. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 726 (2000) (upholding analogous 100-foot zone because “the statute will do little to deter protected speech”).

The Act is content neutral. It “applies to all demonstrators, regardless of viewpoint,” and “does not make a distinction based on the content of the speech.” Id. at 719. The exemptions for clinic employees and patients do not render it viewpoint discriminatory; they serve the neutral interest in safe access. See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y., 519 U.S. 357, 366 (1997) (approving fixed buffer zones while striking floating ones).

The restriction is narrowly tailored to significant governmental interests in patient safety and unobstructed access. The zones “protect[] the rights of prospective patients and clinic employees without offending the First Amendment rights of others.” McCullen v. Coakley, 708 F.3d 1, 13 (1st Cir. 2013). Petitioners retain the ability to “continue with their protected First Amendment activities” outside the zones through signs, amplified speech, literature, and prayer. Hill, 530 U.S. at 726.

Hill controls and should not be overruled. The Court there sustained a larger buffer zone after applying the same standards announced in Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994). Stare decisis requires adherence absent a showing that the decision is “blatantly erroneous.” No such showing exists; the Massachusetts zones are smaller and fixed, not floating. See Schenck, 519 U.S. at 380 (distinguishing fixed from floating zones).

Petitioners’ as-applied claims fail for the same reasons. The record confirms that “adequate communicative channels remain available,” including “oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols,” and group prayer. McCullen, 708 F.3d at 21. The law of the case doctrine bars relitigation of facial validity. See United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011).

The judgment should be affirmed.
