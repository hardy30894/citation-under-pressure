**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that due process demands a competency inquiry whenever a trial court has or should have a good-faith doubt about a defendant’s ability to make a reasoned choice among available alternatives. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 267 (9th Cir. 1992) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871 (1988)). This standard is rooted in the principle that waivers of fundamental constitutional rights must be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242–43 (1969). The Court has long recognized that competency to waive such rights requires more than a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings—it requires the capacity for reasoned choice. *Id.* at 242; *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399–400 (1993) (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)).

The Ninth Circuit’s articulation of the “reasoned choice” standard is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that due process requires a competency determination whenever there is substantial evidence of incompetence. The Ninth Circuit applied this principle in *Moran*, finding that the record contained substantial evidence—including Moran’s recent suicide attempt, his medication regimen, and his monosyllabic responses at the plea hearing—that should have triggered a competency hearing. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 268–69.

II. The state court erred by applying the wrong legal standard in evaluating Moran’s competency.

The state court relied exclusively on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s competency to stand trial, not his capacity for reasoned choice. *Id.* at 271–72. Competency to stand trial requires only a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172; *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). By contrast, competency to waive constitutional rights demands a higher level of mental functioning—the ability to make a reasoned choice among alternatives. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 271; *Evans*, 800 F.2d at 887.

The state court’s reliance on the wrong standard was not harmless. The psychiatric reports did not address the effects of Moran’s medication or his ability to make reasoned decisions about waiving his rights. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 272–73. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record, when viewed under the proper standard, did not support a finding that Moran was competent to waive his rights. *Id.* at 273–74.

III. The state court’s post-conviction competency determination does not cure the error.

The state court’s post-conviction ruling is entitled to no deference because it applied the wrong legal standard. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963). Even if the state court’s factual findings were entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), the Ninth Circuit correctly held that the record did not support a finding of competency under the heightened “reasoned choice” standard. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 271–74.

This Court has long held that federal courts must conduct an independent review of whether a waiver was knowing and intelligent. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 116–17 (1985). The Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with this principle, as it reviewed the record de novo and concluded that Moran’s waiver was not valid. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 266.

**CONCLUSION**

The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights. The state court erred by applying the wrong standard, and the Ninth Circuit correctly reversed the denial of habeas relief. This Court should affirm.
