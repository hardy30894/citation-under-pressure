**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to ensure that a defendant who seeks to waive counsel and plead guilty possesses the capacity for a *reasoned choice* among the available alternatives. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399–401 (1993) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981), and *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973)). This standard is more exacting than the *Dusky* test for competency to stand trial, which merely requires a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to consult with counsel. *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385–86 (1966); *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975). The Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights—such as the right to counsel and the privilege against self-incrimination—demands the “utmost solicitude” of the trial court. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969); *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214.

II. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this heightened standard in reversing the district court’s denial of habeas relief. The state court erred by conflating the *Dusky* competency standard with the *reasoned choice* standard required for a valid waiver of constitutional rights. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. The psychiatric evaluations relied upon by the state court were conducted solely to assess Moran’s competency to stand trial, not his capacity to waive counsel or plead guilty. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399–401. The evaluations did not address whether Moran possessed the mental capacity to make a *reasoned choice* among the alternatives before him—namely, proceeding to trial, accepting appointed counsel, or representing himself while presenting mitigating evidence. *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243; *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214.

III. The record contained substantial evidence triggering a *bona fide* doubt as to Moran’s competency to waive his rights. Moran had attempted suicide months before his plea hearing, was taking multiple medications with known sedative effects, and responded to the court in monosyllables. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399–401; *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. Under *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. at 384–85, a trial court must *sua sponte* conduct a competency hearing whenever it has reason to doubt a defendant’s mental competence. The state court’s failure to hold such a hearing violated due process. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 384–85; *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 181.

IV. The state court’s post-conviction ruling did not cure this error. The state court applied the wrong legal standard—*Dusky* rather than *reasoned choice*—and its factual findings were premised on psychiatric evaluations that did not address the relevant inquiry. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399–401; *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. Under *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 313–14 (1963), a federal habeas court must conduct an independent review of the voluntariness of a constitutional waiver, and the state court’s erroneous standard deprived its findings of any presumptive weight. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 116–17 (1985) (applying *Townsend* to competency determinations).

V. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record did not support a finding that Moran possessed the capacity for a *reasoned choice* at the time of his waiver. The state court’s reliance on evaluations that assessed only *Dusky* competency—without considering the effects of medication or Moran’s ability to weigh the consequences of his decisions—was legally insufficient. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399–401; *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. The trial court’s failure to inquire further into Moran’s mental state, given the red flags in the record, violated the Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 384–85; *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 181.

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
