**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Supreme Court’s Decision in *Kloeckner* Controls, and the D.C. Circuit’s Precedent in *Powell* Is Eviscerated.**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit is erroneous. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis* controls, and the D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the basis of “procedural” versus “jurisdictional” dismissals is unpersuasive. In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, review lies in district court. 568 U.S. 41, 604 (2012). The D.C. Circuit relies on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which held that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit. However, *Kloeckner* effectively overrules *Powell*.

The D.C. Circuit argues that *Kloeckner* only addressed procedural dismissals, such as untimeliness, and did not address jurisdictional dismissals, such as the voluntariness of the employment action. This distinction is artificial. The statute governing judicial review, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), defines a mixed case as one where the employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merits Systems Protection Board.” The focus of the statute is on the “action” and the “discrimination,” not the procedural posture of the appeal. In *Kloeckner*, the Court noted that “no one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB and that she alleged discrimination.” 568 U.S. at 604. Here, Perry was “affected by an action” (retirement and suspension) that he believed was appealable. The fact that the Board later found the action voluntary does not change the nature of the “action” itself.

The D.C. Circuit’s *Powell* decision is incompatible with *Kloeckner*. Under *Dellums v. U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Comm’n*, 863 F.2d 968 (D.C. Cir. 1988), a circuit precedent is “no longer binding” if “eviscerated” by subsequent Supreme Court cases. See *United States v. Williams*, 194 F.3d 100, 105 (D.C. Cir. 1999). The distinction drawn by the D.C. Circuit is not supported by the text or the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner*. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), further cautions that a change in the law is required to overrule precedent, and the D.C. Circuit’s distinction does not rise to that level.

**II. The Statutory Text Supports District Court Review.**

The statutory framework for judicial review of MSPB decisions supports district court jurisdiction in this case. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2) provides that “cases of discrimination” shall be filed in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s interpretation of this provision in *Powell* is contrary to the Supreme Court’s interpretation in *Kloeckner*. The Federal Circuit has already recognized that procedural dismissals go to district court. *Conforto v. Department of the Army*, 713 F.3d 1118, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). The Board has the authority to cure procedural defects, but it may not hear a case over which it lacks jurisdiction. *Id.* This reinforces the need for district court review in mixed cases, regardless of the specific ground of dismissal.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals creates an artificial split that the Supreme Court has already resolved. The Tenth Circuit and Second Circuit previously recognized this split, but the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner* resolved the split regarding procedural dismissals. *Harms v. IRS*, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007 (10th Cir. 2003); *Downey v. Runyon*, 160 F.3d 139, 145 (2d Cir. 1998). The Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner* should not be limited to procedural grounds, as the statutory language focuses on the nature of the action, not the procedural posture of the appeal. Therefore, the D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit is incorrect.
