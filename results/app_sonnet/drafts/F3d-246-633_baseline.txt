# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot's Conspiracy Claim Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Judgment as a Matter of Law

Summary judgment is proper only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). In evaluating the record, the court must draw all justifiable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). The question is not whether the moving party has presented a more persuasive case, but whether "the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party." *Id.* at 248.

Smoot's evidence—Earley's removal from the grievance, the coordinated timing of the internal union charges against Earley by both Smoot and Ruck, and CSX's active participation in securing the Maryland and Ohio injunctions against Smoot's use of the tape—raises a triable inference that UTU and CSX acted in concert to suppress Smoot's meritorious stock claim and to punish him for exposing what occurred in the executive session. A conspiracy claim, by its nature, rarely rests on direct evidence; courts have long recognized that such claims may be proven through circumstantial evidence and the reasonable inferences that flow from a course of conduct. *See Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 588 (1986) (noting that on summary judgment the record must be viewed "in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion").

Moreover, the Railway Labor Act imposes on a union a duty of fair representation that forbids conduct that is "arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith." *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 190 (1967). A union breaches that duty when its conduct is "so far outside a wide range of reasonableness" as to be irrational. *Air Line Pilots Ass'n, Int'l v. O'Neill*, 499 U.S. 65, 67 (1991). Elswick's initial refusal to pursue Smoot's claim, reversed only after Smoot's own internal appeal succeeded, followed by Earley's substitution and the disputed events in the executive session, together create a factual dispute as to whether UTU's representation of Smoot—and its subsequent conduct toward him—fell within that "wide range of reasonableness" or instead reflected a concerted effort with CSX to defeat his claim. Because this is a factual question turning on credibility and motive, it was error to resolve it on summary judgment. *See Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255 ("Credibility determinations, the weighing of the evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge.").

## II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Awarding Statutory and Punitive Damages Under the Federal Wiretap Act

Even if liability under the Act were properly established, the damages awarded here—$250,000 in statutory damages and $100,000 in punitive damages—cannot stand. Under 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2), statutory damages are to be calculated as "the greater of" a per-day amount or $10,000, and courts retain discretion in applying that formula in light of the equities of the case. *See Reynolds v. Spears*, 93 F.3d 428, 434-35 (8th Cir. 1996) (recognizing that district courts must exercise judgment, not award damages mechanically, under the statutory scheme). Here, Smoot recorded a single executive session on a single occasion; the district court's award bears no relationship to the single, discrete act at issue and instead appears to have been driven by hostility toward Smoot's broader litigation conduct rather than by the statutory factors properly before it.

Punitive damages under the Act are reserved for violations that are "willful or intentional." 18 U.S.C. § 2520(b)(2). While the district court found that Smoot intentionally recorded the session, intent to record is not the equivalent of the malicious or reckless disregard for statutory rights that justifies a punitive award. Courts applying analogous federal remedial statutes have cautioned that punitive damages require more than proof of the underlying violation; they require conduct demonstrating a reckless or callous indifference to the plaintiff's rights. *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages appropriate only where conduct involves "reckless or callous indifference" to protected rights). Smoot's belief—however ultimately mistaken—that he was entitled to preserve a record of proceedings directly affecting his own claim for stock is not the sort of malicious conduct for which the "extraordinary remedy" of punitive damages is reserved. The award should therefore be vacated or, at minimum, substantially reduced on remand.

## III. The Attorneys' Fee Award Was Excessive and Should Be Reversed

The $86,308.74 attorneys' fee award likewise cannot stand independent of the damages judgment. A "reasonable" fee under fee-shifting principles must bear a reasonable relationship to the results obtained and the complexity of the litigation. *See Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 436 (1983) ("the extent of a plaintiff's success is a crucial factor in determining the proper amount of an award of attorney's fees"). Because the underlying damages award rests on legal error, the fee award premised upon it must likewise be vacated and reconsidered on remand.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment on Smoot's RLA conspiracy claim, vacate the statutory and punitive damages awarded under the Wiretap Act, and vacate the associated award of attorneys' fees and costs, remanding for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
