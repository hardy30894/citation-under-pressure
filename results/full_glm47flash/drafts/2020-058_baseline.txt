**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit erred by rigidly separating "admission" and "status" without considering the functional equivalence in this context. While "admission" and "status" are distinct concepts, the grant of TPS satisfies the statutory definition of "admission" because it constitutes the "lawful entry" element required by the statute.

First, the statutory text supports the view that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission. The Immigration and Nationality Act defines "admission" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). Conversely, the TPS statute provides that an alien with TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant." 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Ninth Circuit correctly observed that the title of § 1255 suggests Congress intended TPS recipients to "make use" of the adjustment process. *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 961 (9th Cir. 2017). Furthermore, the Board of Immigration Appeals has held that "an adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission." *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). By placing TPS holders in "lawful status" and requiring inspection and authorization for TPS eligibility, Congress effectively satisfied the "lawful entry" component of the admission definition.

Second, the Third Circuit’s concern that reading TPS as admission renders § 1254a(h) superfluous is misplaced. Section 1254a(h) requires a supermajority of the Senate to pass legislation adjusting the status of TPS holders. *Sanchez*, 2020 WL 4108420, at *19. This procedural safeguard is designed to protect against the legislative adjustment of status, not to bar the Attorney General’s discretionary authority under § 1255. The Attorney General has broad discretion to adjust status for aliens in lawful status, and the TPS statute explicitly places petitioners in such status. Construing TPS as an admission does not undermine the supermajority requirement; rather, it allows the Attorney General to exercise that discretion without circumventing legislative intent.

Finally, the government’s own interpretation supports the petitioners. In the INS General Counsel Opinion issued just after the TPS statute was enacted, the agency advised that a grant of TPS should not be construed as an admission *at the time of enactment* to prevent abuse. *Temporary Protected Status and Eligibility for Adjustment of Status under Section [1255]*, INS Gen. Counsel Op. No. 91-27, 1991 WL 1185138, at *1 (Mar. 4, 1991). However, the current statutory text, which explicitly places TPS holders in "lawful status," supersedes that historical concern. The purpose of the TPS program is to allow aliens present in the United States to remain during emergencies. *Sanchez*, 2020 WL 4108420, at *3. Denying TPS holders the ability to adjust their status to permanent residence would frustrate this purpose, trapping them in a state of limbo.
