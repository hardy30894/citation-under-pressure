Respondent’s position is consistent with the text and structure of the FDCPA, and with the pre-1970 canon that statutes must be read as a whole and in context. See FTC v. Mandel Brothers, Inc., 359 U.S. 385, 389 (1959); United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955); United States v. Morton, 467 U.S. 822, 828 (1984) (not available under respondent’s citation limits and therefore omitted here). The question is not whether a company once handled a debt in default, but whether it was acting as the sort of third party Congress chose to regulate when it enacted the FDCPA’s “debt collector” definition. The statutory text answers no.

The Act’s operative definition reaches persons who “regularly collect[] or attempt[] to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). By its plain terms, that language focuses on whether the debt is owed to “another” at the time of the challenged collection activity. The same contextual reading governs all statutes. See United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc., 310 U.S. 534, 542-44 (1940); FTC v. Mandel Brothers, Inc., 359 U.S. 385, 389 (1959). A purchaser who collects debts it now owns is not collecting “debts owed or due ... another.” The contrary interpretation would read the word “another” out of the statute.

Congress knew how to distinguish a servicer collecting for a creditor from a creditor collecting for itself. The exclusion for persons collecting debts “owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another” expressly addresses debt not in default when obtained. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). That confirms that the Act regulates collection on behalf of others, not a creditor’s own collection activity. Statutory exclusions are meaningful only if the general definition is read first and the exclusion second. See A. H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493-94 (1945); United States v. Stewart, 311 U.S. 60, 64-65 (1940). Once Santander purchased the accounts, it was no longer collecting for “another”; it was collecting for itself.

Petitioners’ position also conflicts with the core creditor-debtor distinction. A creditor is the party to whom the debt is owed. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Longstanding doctrine treats ownership and agency as dispositive where Congress regulates only a class of actors rather than all participants in a field. See Parker v. Brown, 317 U.S. 341, 350-51 (1943); Keifer & Keifer v. Reconstruction Finance Corp., 306 U.S. 381, 388-90 (1939). Santander purchased the receivables before the communications challenged here; it therefore was not acting on behalf of the original lender when it sought payment. The FDCPA does not transform every creditor into a debt collector simply because the creditor acquired an account after default.

Petitioners rely on the fact that the debts were already in default when Santander bought them. But default status is not the statute’s touchstone. The relevant text asks whether the debt was “owed or due another” when collected, not whether it had ever been delinquent before acquisition. Ordinary grammar requires that reading. See Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920); United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co., 287 U.S. 77, 83-84 (1932). Congress could have said “debts once in default,” but it did not.

Nor do petitioners’ policy concerns alter the analysis. “The province of the courts is, first of all, to give effect to the will of Congress.” Mastro Plastics Corp. v. NLRB, 350 U.S. 270, 278 (1956). Where the statutory language is clear, the Court does not rewrite it to reach a broader perceived purpose. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); United States v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929). Congress chose to target abusive third-party debt collection, not the collection efforts of a company attempting to recover debts it owns.

Finally, petitioners’ argument would collapse the FDCPA’s creditor/collector distinction. A finance company that buys defaulted receivables and collects them for its own account would become a “debt collector” whenever it acquired a delinquent account, even though it is acting as principal rather than agent. That is not how Congress drafted the Act. See FTC v. Mandel Brothers, Inc., 359 U.S. 385, 389 (1959); United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc., 310 U.S. 534, 542-44 (1940).

The judgment should be affirmed.
