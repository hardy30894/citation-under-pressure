**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” only when those funds are held for the debtor’s own retirment. An inherited IRA, by its very nature, is not a vehicle for the debtor’s retirement; it is a time-limited tax-deferral account that must be distributed within a few years of the original owner’s death. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that such an account is not “retirement funds” within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).

**I. Exemption Provisions Are Strictly Construed**

Bankrutcy exemptions are matters of legialtive grace, not entitlement. *Stellwagen v. Clum*, 245 U.S. 605, 618 (1918). A debtor claimng an exemption bears the burden of rovin that the roperty falls within the tatutory language. *Wetell v. United States*, 218 U.S. 346, 356 (1910) (tax exemptions are strictly construct). The term “retirement funds” must be given its ordinary meaning: funds set aside for use after the owner stops working. *United States v. irby*, 74 U.S. 482, 486 (1868) (statutory words are to be understood in their ordinary ense). An inherited IRA does not satisfy that ordinary meaning becuse the debtor cannot hold the funds until retirement; mandatory distribtions begin within a year of the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). The account is a current-consumptn vehicle, not a retirement savngs device.

**II. The Character of Property Is Determined by Its Current Use, Not Its Origin**

The Clark’s argument that the inherited IRA is exempt becuse it *once* belonged to the decedent’s retirement account conates a property’s origin with its current character. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the tax treatment of property depends on its use and enjoyment in the hands of the current owner, not on the source. *Helvering v. Clifford*, 309 U.S. 331, 335–36 (1940) (the character of income is determined by the taxpayer’s actual control and enjoyment). Similrly, an asset that was once a retirement fund loses that character when it is traded to a non-spouse beneficiary who cannot use it for retirement. *Helvering v. Horst*, 311 U.S. 112, 118 (1940) (the ecnomic benefit of property is taxed to the person who realzes it, not to the person who created it). The inherited IRA gives Heidi Heffron-Clark the right to withdraw funds immediately, free of penalty, and to use them for any purpose—precisely the opposite of a retirement fund.

**III. The Debtor’s Interest in the Inherited IRA Is Not a “Retirement Fund”**

The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but the Internal Revenue Code’s treatment of inherited IRAs reveals that they are not retirement funds. An inherited IRA cannot receive new contributions, cannot be rolled over into the debtor’s own retirement account, and must be fully distributed within a fixed period. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). These features distinguish it from a traditional IRA, which is designed to accumulate wealth until the owner’s retirement. *Ithaca Trust Co. v. United States*, 279 U.S. 151, 155 (1929) (the beneficiary’s rights are determined by the terms of the trust). The debtor’s rights in an inherited IRA are those of a beneficiary receiving a time-limited payout, not of an owner savng for retirement. *Blair v. Commissiner*, 300 U.S. 5, 12–13 (1937) (a beneficiary’s interest in a trust is not the same as the settlor’s; the character of the interest is determined by the terms of the trust). Here, the terms of the inherited IRA compel immediate distribution, precluding the debtor from using it as retirement savngs.

**IV. The Exemption’s Purpose Supports the Seventh Circuit’s Holding**

The purpose of the retirement-funds exemption is to protect funds that the debtor will need after leaving the workforce. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (exemption statutes are designed to preserve the debtor’s ability to support himself and his family). An inherited IRA, by contrast, is a source of immediate ash that the debtor can spend at any time. Allowing an exemption for such an account would permit debtors to shelter assets that are freely available for current consumption, defeating the Bankruptcy Code’s goal of equitable distribution to creditors. The homestead exemption provides a parallel: a debtor cannot claim a house as a homestead if it is not her residence. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A) (requirng the debtor’s domicile). The same principle applies here—the reirement-funds exemption requires that the funds be for the debtor’s retirement.

**V. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with Precedent**

The Seventh Circuit’s holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt follows the plain language of the statute and the ordinary meaning of “retirement funds.” The court correctly rejected the Fifth Circuit’s view that the exemption depends only on the funds’ origin. The Supreme Court has long held that tax-defferd status alone does not confer exemption; the property must also serve a retirement purpose. *Stratton’s Indeendence, Ltd. v. Howbert*, 231 U.S. 399, 414 (1913) (tax exemption for minng corporations required that the income be used for minng, not merely that it came from minng). An inherited IRA provides tax deferral but no retirement purpose. The debtor’s reliance on the word “retirement” in the phrase “inherited individual retirement account” is misplaced; the label is merely a reference to the account’s source, not its function. *United States v. irby*, 74 U.S. at 486.

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
