# ARGUMENT

## I. The Ship's Crew Breached Its Duty of Reasonable Care by Leaving an Unsafe Piece of Cargo-Handling Gear in the Longshoremen's Work Area

A vessel owner "owes to all who are on board for purposes not inimical to his legitimate interests the duty of exercising reasonable care under the circumstances of each case." *Kermarec v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique*, 358 U.S. 625, 632 (1959). That duty of reasonable care attaches with particular force to the vessel's own gear and appliances, since the ship, not the stevedore, controls their condition and placement. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96 (1944), held that a shipowner cannot escape responsibility for defective gear furnished for cargo work merely because the crew's negligence in maintaining it was the immediate cause of harm. That principle was extended to longshoremen performing seamen's work aboard ship in *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), and to areas of the vessel actually being worked by stevedores in *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953).

Here, the snatch block was the crew's exclusive instrumentality — "moving the snatch blocks is the responsibility of the crew" — and the crew left it improperly lowered, resting atop a cargo unit in the exact spot "where the hookup man would normally stand." A shipowner is liable when its crew's improper handling of vessel's gear renders the working area unsafe for longshoremen, even though the stevedore's own employees are the ones exposed to the hazard. *Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson*, 347 U.S. 396 (1954) (shipowner liable where longshoreman injured by defective winch used in cargo operations); *Crumady v. The Joachim Hendrik Fisser*, 358 U.S. 423 (1959) (improper rigging of the ship's own gear by the crew created the dangerous condition). The rule of these cases does not turn on whether the crew member who mishandled the gear was, at the moment of injury, physically present; it turns on whether the vessel's instrumentality was left in an unreasonably dangerous condition that foreseeably would be encountered by longshoremen doing their ordinary work. *Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 373 U.S. 206 (1963) (shipowner liable for injury caused by defective cargo containers left in the hold, regardless of whether stevedore or crew last handled them); *West v. United States*, 361 U.S. 118 (1959) (duty of care as to gear used in cargo operations is not discharged simply because longshoremen, rather than crew, are working in proximity to it).

The district court's contrary conclusion — that the crew's absence at the moment of the accident insulated the ship from liability — cannot be squared with these decisions. The relevant negligence was completed the moment the crew left the block down and unattended in an area where longshoremen were known to work; what followed, including Serbin's own attempt to correct the hazard, was a foreseeable consequence of that antecedent breach. Custom or habitual practice among crews of leaving such tasks for convenience does not excuse the omission, for "there are precautions so imperative that even their universal disregard will not excuse their omission." *The T.J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d 737, 740 (2d Cir. 1932) (L. Hand, J.).

## II. Serbin's Decision to Move the Block Himself Does Not Bar Recovery, Because Contributory Fault in Admiralty Only Diminishes Damages

Even if the ship contends that Serbin should have waited for the crew, that contention goes at most to comparative fault, not to the existence of the ship's duty or its breach. Admiralty law has long rejected the common-law rule that a plaintiff's own negligence bars recovery outright. In *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424 (1939), the Court held that contributory negligence in a maritime injury case operates only to diminish the damages recoverable in proportion to the plaintiff's fault, not to defeat the claim. *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), reaffirmed that comparative-fault principle in the specific context of a longshoreman injured while working aboard ship, holding that his own carelessness reduces but does not eliminate the shipowner's liability. Nor does the fact that the stevedore, rather than the crew, was the immediate actor at the time of injury relieve the vessel; the warranty-of-workmanlike-service line of cases, beginning with *Ryan Stevedoring Co. v. Pan-Atlantic S.S. Corp.*, 350 U.S. 124 (1956), presupposes precisely this kind of shared responsibility between ship and stevedore for conditions created by the vessel's own equipment. Serbin's motive for moving the block — to avoid delay in a competitive fruit-unloading operation — is, at most, evidence for the jury on apportionment; it is not a basis for summary judgment against him.

## III. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because the Record Presented Disputed Questions for the Trier of Fact

Whether the crew's conduct in leaving the block down was unreasonable, whether the danger was open and obvious, and whether Serbin's response was foreseeable are all questions "which under the applicable law are to be resolved by the trier of the facts." *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Because reasonable minds could differ on each of these points, the district court erred in resolving them as a matter of law.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
