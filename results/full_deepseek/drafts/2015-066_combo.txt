**ARGUMENT**

**I. Unconselled Tribal Court Convictions Are Constitutionally Valid Because the Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Indian Tribes.**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not constrain tribal court proceedings. Since the founding, this Court has recognized that Indian tribes are “distinct, independent political communities” retaining sovereign powers not delegated to the federal government. *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515, 559 (1832). The Bill of Rights limits only federal and state action, not tribal self-government. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 382 (1896) (holding that the Fifth Amendment does not apply to tribal councils). Tribal courts exercise “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders” as an “inherent tribal right.” *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 571 (1883). Consequently, a tribal court conviction that complies with the Indian Civil Rights Act—which does not require appointed counsel for misdemeanors—is not “constitutionally infirm” for lack of counsel. *See Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. 1, 16 (1831) (tribes are “domestic dependent nations” not subject to constitutional constraints). The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a Sixth Amendment requirement where none exists.

**II. Valid Tribal Court Convictions May Serve as Predicate Offenses Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) Without Violating the Sixth Amendment.**

Recidivist statutes like § 117(a) punish only the *current* offense, not the prior convictions. The existence of a prior conviction is simply a fact establishing the defendant’s status as a repeat offender. *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 623 (1912) (recidivist laws “do not punish the offender for the prior offense, but for the last offense”). *Accord Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673, 678 (1895); *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 451 (1962). Because the predicate conviction is valid under the law that created it—tribal law—its use in a later federal prosecution does not retroactively violate the Sixth Amendment. *See Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 565 (1967) (permitting use of prior convictions for enhancement even if they were obtained under procedures that would not satisfy current standards). The Sixth Amendment concerns only the *trial* of the prior offense; it does not limit Congress’s power to define the elements of a federal felony. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963) (right to counsel attaches at trial, not later proceedings). Where, as here, the tribal court had no constitutional obligation to provide counsel, the resulting conviction is not “uncounseled” in the constitutional sense.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Reliance on *Burgett v. Texas* Is Misplaced.**

In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), this Court held that a prior conviction obtained in violation of *Gideon* could not be used to enhance a state sentence. But *Burgett* presupposed that the prior conviction was *invalid* under the Sixth Amendment. Tribal court convictions are not subject to the Sixth Amendment, *Talton*, 163 U.S. at 382, so they are not “void” or “presumptively unreliable” for Sixth Amendment purposes. *Burgett* does not control. Indeed, *Burgett* itself recognized that the “right to counsel” analysis depends on “whether the prior conviction was obtained in compliance with the Constitution.” 389 U.S. at 114. Where the Constitution does not apply, compliance is measured by the relevant sovereign’s law. *See United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 381 (1886) (Congress has plenary power over Indian affairs, but tribes retain sovereign authority). The Ninth Circuit’s decision thus extends *Burgett* beyond its constitutional foundation.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Undermines Tribal Sovereignty and Congressional Policy.**

Congress, in enacting § 117(a), deliberately included tribal court convictions as predicates, recognizing the tribal justice system’s role in combating domestic violence. The Ninth Circuit’s rule—requiring tribal courts to provide counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment before their convictions may be used—impermissibly intrudes on tribal self-governance. *See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 62 (1978) (citing *Talton*, 163 U.S. at 384) (tribal sovereignty limits federal courts’ power to impose constitutional requirements). This Court has long held that the “people of the States” are not entitled to impose their procedural norms on tribes. *Johnson v. M’Intosh*, 21 U.S. 543, 589 (1823). The Ninth Circuit’s decision violates that principle and frustrates Congress’s goal of holding violent recidivists accountable.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
