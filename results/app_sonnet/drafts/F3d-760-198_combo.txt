# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred by Evaluating Each Incident in Isolation Rather Than Considering the Totality of the Circumstances

The district court dismissed Moll's hostile work environment claim because it isolated each allegation and asked whether that single incident was, standing alone, "sexually offensive." This method of analysis is legally unsound. Courts have long recognized that a course of conduct cannot be fairly assessed by examining its component parts in isolation. In *Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp.*, 370 U.S. 690 (1962), the Supreme Court held that "the character and effect of a conspiracy are not to be judged by dismembering it and viewing its separate parts, but only by looking at it as a whole." *Id.* at 699. The same principle applies here: Moll's environment cannot be understood by parsing Irving's notes from his refusal to let her communicate by phone or email, his denial of her promotion while promoting less senior men, his revocation of her work-from-home privileges while permitting male colleagues to continue working remotely, and her exclusion from office social events. Each of these facts, sex-neutral on its face, becomes probative of a sexually hostile environment when read together with the overtly sexual notes and the hotel-room incident.

This "whole picture" requirement is not novel to sex discrimination. In *Interstate Circuit, Inc. v. United States*, 306 U.S. 208 (1939), the Court likewise refused to evaluate parallel conduct incident-by-incident, instructing that the totality of surrounding circumstances must inform the inference to be drawn. And in the employment context itself, courts examining patterns of discriminatory treatment have refused to compartmentalize individual practices, recognizing instead that a series of facially neutral practices, taken together, may establish an unlawful pattern that no single practice reveals on its own. *See Local 189, United Papermakers & Paperworkers v. United States*, 416 F.2d 980 (5th Cir. 1969) (assessing a seniority system's discriminatory character by its cumulative operation rather than by isolated provisions).

The district court's atomized approach thus contravenes settled principles requiring courts to assess a challenged course of conduct as an integrated whole. Because several of Moll's allegations—the note-writing, the communication restrictions, the promotion denials, the work-from-home revocation, and the social exclusion—occurred or continued within the limitations period, and because those allegations must be read together with the earlier sexual conduct to reveal the true character of Moll's working environment, the hostile work environment claim was improperly dismissed.

## II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact, Including Credibility Questions Raised by Conflicting Non-Party Testimony, Precluded Summary Judgment

The district court further erred in granting partial summary judgment to Verizon because the record contained the kind of factual and credibility disputes that have long been understood to belong to the jury, not the court. On summary judgment, "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts contained in such materials must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). This is especially true where, as here, the case turns on questions of motive and intent—the retaliatory character of Moll's transfer to Syracuse, and Verizon's true reasons for restricting her work arrangements while permitting male colleagues greater latitude. The Supreme Court has cautioned that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles." *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). So too here: whether Verizon's justification for transferring the Buffalo Sales Engineers to Syracuse was pretextual, or whether it was genuinely unrelated to Moll's lawsuit, is a question of motive unsuited to resolution on a paper record. *Cf. NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584 (1941) (recognizing that the timing of an adverse employment action in relation to protected activity is probative circumstantial evidence of unlawful motive, properly weighed by the finder of fact).

Nor should the district court have discounted the shifting testimony of non-party witnesses. Long-settled doctrine holds that credibility determinations, including assessments of why a witness's account changed, are peculiarly within the jury's province and may not be resolved by the court on a cold record. *See Arnstein v. Porter*, 154 F.2d 464 (2d Cir. 1946) (reversing summary judgment where resolution depended on the credibility of witness testimony that could not properly be assessed without a trial). Likewise, in *Colby v. Klune*, 178 F.2d 872 (2d Cir. 1949), Judge Learned Hand emphasized that summary judgment is particularly inappropriate where the ultimate question depends on a party's or witness's state of mind—a determination best made after the finder of fact has observed the witness. The same reasoning forecloses summary judgment based on discounting a non-party witness's later, more complete testimony simply because it conflicts with an earlier, less complete account. Unlike a party's self-serving about-face, a non-party witness has no comparable stake in the litigation's outcome, and the discrepancy itself—rather than being disregarded—is properly tested through cross-examination before the jury, consistent with *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944), which held that even seemingly uncontradicted testimony may be impeached by surrounding circumstances and should not be credited wholesale by the court on summary judgment.

For these reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment below and remand for trial.
