# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park held "immemorially . . . for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to social, civic, and recreational activities benefiting the community. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. As this Court has explained, the State has power to preserve public property under its control for the use to which that property is lawfully dedicated, and it need not open the property to every use that members of the public might wish to make of it merely because it has been dedicated to some limited purposes. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). Just as the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held—in an opinion this Court adopted—that a legislature may regulate the use of public grounds "no more an infringement of the rights of a member of the public than for the owner of a private house to forbid" a particular use of his own property, *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897), Milford may exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. This Court has long recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property for assembly is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). Milford's Community Use Policy operates the same way: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The record shows that the Good News Club's proposed meeting opens and closes in prayer, invites children to accept Christ as their personal savior through a scripted invitation segment, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer. This is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962), and the state may not sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting, *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963) (the state may not establish a "religion of secularism" any more than it may sponsor religious observance—neutrality is the touchstone). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, supervised implicitly by the school's continuing presence on its own property, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. As this Court held in striking down devotional exercises conducted in the classroom, the use of "tax-supported public school buildings" for religious instruction offends the Establishment Clause even absent formal compulsion. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands that "the First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
