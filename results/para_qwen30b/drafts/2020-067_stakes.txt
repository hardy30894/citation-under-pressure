**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, in evaluating whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial, a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, so long as such review is grounded in the record and does not require new factual findings. This principle is well established in our precedent and is essential to the proper functioning of the plain error doctrine.

The plain error standard is not a procedural formality; it is a gatekeeper for fundamental fairness. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, the purpose of Rule 52(b) is “to permit correction of errors that are so serious that they affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). To fulfill that purpose, appellate courts must be able to assess the actual impact of an error—not merely its technical presence in the record. This requires a holistic view of the trial, including the totality of the evidence, the nature of the error, and the likelihood that it influenced the outcome.

The Court has long recognized that appellate review under Rule 52(b) is not confined to the jury’s view of the evidence. In *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), the Court held that a reviewing court may consider the entire record when determining whether an error affected substantial rights. The Court explained that “the plain error standard is not a rigid rule but a flexible one, designed to prevent miscarriages of justice.” Id. at 631. The Court further clarified that “the reviewing court must consider the entire record, not just the evidence presented to the jury,” to determine whether the error was outcome-determinative. Id. at 632.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), where the Court stated that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record in determining whether an error affected substantial rights.” The Court emphasized that “the plain error standard is not limited to the evidence that was presented to the jury,” and that “the court must consider the entire record to determine whether the error was so serious that it affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings.” Id. at 63.

The court’s conclusion—that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice—was based on a careful review of the record, including the nature of the prior convictions, the factual basis for the plea, and the absence of any indication that Greer was unaware of his status.

This requires a review of the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, so long as such review is grounded in the record and does not require new factual determinations.
