**ARGUMENT**

**A Criminal Defendant Lacks Standing to Invoke the Tenth Amendment to Challenge a Federal Statute as Exceeding Enumerated Powers**

The Tenth Amendment provides that “the powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const. amend. X. This amendment is a structural protection of state sovereignty, not a source of individual rights. See *Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority*, 469 U.S. 528, 549 (1985). Accordingly, a private party—including a criminal defendant—cannot challenge a federal statute on the ground that it violates the Tenth Amendment. The Third Circuit correctly held that Bond lacks standing to raise such a claim, and that decision should be affirmed.

**I. The Tenth Amendment Protects State Interests, Not Individual Rights**

The Tenth Amendment confirms that the federal government is one of enumerated powers, with the remainder reserved to the States. It does not create a personal right enforceable by individuals. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-144 (1939), the Supreme Court squarely held that private parties lack standing to assert a Tenth Amendment violation. There, utility companies argued that the TVA’s sale of electricity invaded state sovereignty. The Court rejected the claim, stating that “absent the states or their officers, [the companies] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 144. This holding is binding precedent, not dicta. *See Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (explaining that the Supreme Court’s “alternative ground” on standing in *Tennessee Electric* is not obiter dictum).

Every court of appeals to address the question after *Tennessee Electric* has reached the same conclusion. *See, e.g., United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33-36; *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). These decisions recognize that the Tenth Amendment “is a truism that simply declares the relationship between the national government and the states,” and that “only states, not individuals, may assert a Tenth Amendment claim.” *Hacker*, 565 F.3d at 525 (internal quotation marks omitted). The rationale is that the Tenth Amendment “does not confer any right on an individual that would give him standing to complain about a federal statute that purportedly exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers.” *Brooklyn Legal Servs.*, 462 F.3d at 234.

**II. Bond’s Status as a Criminal Defendant Does Not Confer Standing**

Bond argues that she has standing because she is a defendant convicted under a statute she claims is unconstitutional. But standing requires a “personal injury” that is “fairly traceable to the defendant’s allegedly unlawful conduct.” *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). While Bond suffers the concrete injury of conviction, that injury is not traceable to any violation of *her own* constitutional rights. The Tenth Amendment does not protect her; it protects the States. The State of Pennsylvania has not objected to the federal prosecution, and Bond cannot “borrow” the State’s interest. *See Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35 (“the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes”). To allow Bond to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge would permit any criminal defendant to litigate structural federalism questions that belong exclusively to the States—a result that would flood the federal courts with claims that “are not susceptible to judicial resolution by individuals.” *Hacker*, 565 F.3d at 527.

**III. Bond’s Reliance on Cases Involving Other Constitutional Provisions Is Misplaced**

Bond may point to cases where criminal defendants have challenged statutes under the Commerce Clause or other enumerated powers. But those provisions regulate the *relationship between the federal government and individuals*. The Tenth Amendment, by contrast, “is not a font of individual rights”; it is a “declaration of a commercial relationship between the national government and the states.” *Hacker*, 565 F.3d at 525 (quoting *United States v. Wright*, 128 F.3d 1274, 1276 (8th Cir. 1997)). Indeed, the Supreme Court has never held that an individual may invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge a federal criminal statute. The Court’s modern federalism cases—such as *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), and *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997)—were brought by States, not individuals. Bond’s attempt to assert a Tenth Amendment claim is thus without precedent.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Tenth Amendment reserves certain powers to the States, but it does not create a cause of action for private parties. Bond lacks standing to challenge 18 U.S.C. § 229 on federalism grounds, and the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
