## Argument

The question presented should be answered yes. Rule 23 does not permit certification on speculation that damages might someday be shown on a classwide basis. A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” and determine, by a preponderance of the evidence, that Rule 23’s requirements are actually satisfied. In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305, 307, 320 (3d Cir. 2008). That includes the damages component of predominance where the plaintiffs’ theory depends on common proof. Id. at 323-24. A court “may delve beyond the pleadings,” and an overlap between class certification and the merits “is no reason to decline to resolve relevant disputes when necessary” to decide certification. Id. at 316-17. If the record does not show a viable common method of measuring damages attributable to the alleged classwide injury, Rule 23(b)(3) predominance is not met. Id. at 325-26.

That principle follows from the Supreme Court’s repeated instruction that a class may not be certified unless the evidence supports the Rule 23 findings. General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982), held that a court must be satisfied, after “rigorous analysis,” that the prerequisites of Rule 23 are met. Likewise, Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 350-51 (2011), reiterated that the Rule 23 inquiry “will entail some overlap with the merits,” because a court must be assured that the requirements are actually present. And Comcast’s own theory of injury underscores the point: predominance requires that damages be “capable of measurement on a classwide basis,” not that plaintiffs offer a damages model that may or may not fit the certified liability theory. See Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997); Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 323-24.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in Parko v. Shell Oil Co., 739 F.3d 1083, 1084-86 (7th Cir. 2014), is persuasive on the same point: certification cannot rest on an expert model that does not isolate damages caused by the specific common injury supporting the class. The classwide model must “measure only those damages attributable to that theory,” because otherwise individualized issues overwhelm predominance. Id. at 1085. The Eighth Circuit has likewise held that Rule 23 requires a “showing that damages are susceptible of measurement across the entire class for purposes of Rule 23(b)(3),” and that unsupported assumptions in a damages model defeat certification. In re Zurn Pex Plumbing Products Liability Litigation, 644 F.3d 604, 617-18 (8th Cir. 2011).

Here, the district court certified a class after finding that Dr. McClave’s model provided a common method to quantify damages, but the court of appeals accepted that conclusion without demanding admissible proof that the model measured only injury attributable to the sole remaining theory of liability. That was error. Hydrogen Peroxide requires courts to resolve disputes relevant to certification and to ensure that plaintiffs’ proof is more than “speculation” or a “possibility” of classwide proof. 552 F.3d at 320, 323. A model that aggregates multiple possible causes of price effects, or that fails to isolate damages from the theory actually certified, cannot establish predominance. Id. at 325-26.

The Supreme Court’s decisions in Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27, 32-35 (2013), and Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo, 577 U.S. 442, 453-54 (2016), confirm the rule: a damages methodology must be tied to the plaintiffs’ liability theory, and class treatment is improper where individualized damages questions defeat predominance. Tyson Foods does not relax that requirement; it permits representative proof only when each class member could have relied on the same evidence to prove the same injury. 577 U.S. at 453-54. It does not authorize certification where the plaintiff lacks reliable classwide proof that damages can be measured consistently for the class claim being tried.

The Court should therefore reverse. Rule 23(b)(3) requires proof, not promises. Where the record lacks adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a classwide basis for the certified theory of injury, certification is improper. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 323-26; Wal-Mart Stores, 564 U.S. at 350-51; Comcast Corp., 569 U.S. at 34-35.
