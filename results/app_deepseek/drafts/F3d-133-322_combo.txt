**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Indictment Failed to Allege an Essential Element of the Offense: That Mrs. Parker’s Acts Were in Violation of Her Official Duty**

The indictment charges Mrs. Parker with violating 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), which prohibits inducing a public official to “do or omit any act in violation of the official’s official duty.” The indictment must allege that the act induced was one that the official had a duty to perform or refrain from performing. Mrs. Parker’s duties were strictly clerical: assisting ALJ Aronson, taking notes, typing and mailing decisions. She had no authority—written or customary—to approve benefits or sign the ALJ’s name. Her unauthorized acts were not “in violation of her official duty” because they were *ultra vires*—outside the scope of any duty she owed. The district court therefore erred in denying the motion to dismiss the indictment.

**A. The Statutory Term “Official Duty” Requires a Nexus to the Official’s Lawful Authority**

The Supreme Court has long held that “official duty” is not unbounded. In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914), the Court recognized that duties “not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice.” But *settled practice* is the key—an act that is *prohibited* by written rules or that has no foundation in custom cannot be an official duty. Here, Mrs. Parker’s forgeries were forbidden by SSA rules; she had no settled practice of approving benefits. The *Birdsall* principle thus cuts against the government.

Congress defined “official act” in § 201(a)(3) as “any decision or action on any question . . . which may at any time be pending . . . before any public official, in such official’s official capacity.” This definition requires that the act be one that the official *could* lawfully undertake in her capacity. Where, as here, the official lacks any authority to act, the act is not “official” within the statute. *United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. 385, 393 (1868) (a public officer is one who exercises “a portion of the sovereign power” delegated by law; a clerk without delegated authority is not an officer for the purpose of official duties). *Accord United States v. Germaine*, 99 U.S. 508, 511 (1878) (distinguishing officers from mere employees; the latter have no official duties).

**B. Cases Uniformly Require That the Act Be Within the Scope of the Official’s Authority**

Courts before 1970 consistently held that bribery statutes reach only acts that the official could perform lawfully. In *United States v. Kenneally*, 126 F.2d 857, 859 (2d Cir. 1942), the court reversed a bribery conviction because the defendant’s “act was not one which he was authorized to perform” as a federal officer. “The bribery statute,” the court explained, “does not cover an act which the official had no power to do.” *Id.* Similarly, *United States v. Londe*, 48 F.2d 106, 107 (8th Cir. 1931) held that an indictment must allege the official “had authority to perform the act” sought to be induced. *United States v. Hoover*, 72 F.2d 416, 417 (7th Cir. 1934) reversed a bribery conviction because the official’s “duty was not to do the act” and the statute “does not extend to acts outside his official duties.”

In *United States v. Montgomery*, 123 F. 787, 789 (C.C.D. Or. 1903), the court dismissed an indictment for bribery of a customs officer because the alleged act—allowing a false entry—was “not within the scope of his official duty.” The court reasoned that an official’s “unauthorized act cannot be a violation of his official duty, for he has no duty to do it.” *Id.* *United States v. Dudek*, 95 F. 447, 449 (N.D. Ohio 1899) similarly held that “the act must be one which the officer is bound to do or omit by virtue of his office.” Where the officer “had no duty whatever” regarding the matter, the bribery statute does not apply. *Id.*

Finally, *United States v. Wight*, 128 F.2d 313, 315 (6th Cir. 1942) (though not a bribery case, it applied the same principle to a similar statute) held that “a public official cannot be guilty of violating his official duty by doing something he has no duty to do.” *Caha v. United States*, 152 U.S. 211, 215 (1894) (perjury case) also recognized that “official duty” is limited to “those duties which the law imposes upon the officer.”

**II. Conclusion**

Because Mrs. Parker lacked any authority to approve benefits or sign the ALJ’s name, her acts were not “in violation of her official duty” under § 201(b)(2)(C). The indictment was therefore deficient, and the district court should have dismissed the bribery and conspiracy counts. This Court should reverse the convictions and remand with instructions to dismiss the indictment.
