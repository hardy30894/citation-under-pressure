**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s order retaining this case for trial under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) directly contravenes the plain command of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That statute provides that after coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings, “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.” The word “shall” is mandatory, leaving no room for discretion. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955); *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947); *Duncan v. United States*, 78 U.S. 288, 293 (1870) (when “shall” appears, “the duty is imperative”). The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation—not the transferee district court—is the sole entity authorized to remand. Section 1407(a) thus strips the transferee court of any power to retain a case for trial, as the district court did here.

The district court’s invocation of § 1404(a) cannot override this specific statutory mandate. When a specific statute and a general one conflict, the specific governs. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940); *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 125 (1904). Section 1407(a) is a precise, limited grant of authority for pretrial proceedings only; § 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision. The latter cannot be used to circumvent the former’s explicit remand requirement.

Moreover, § 1404(a) authorizes transfer only “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 342–44 (1960). A district court cannot “transfer” a case to itself; that is not a transfer but a retention. *Hoffman* held that the transferee district must be one where the action “might have been brought” originally. A court already possessing the case cannot satisfy that condition because the action is already there. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 58–59 (1949), similarly recognized that § 1404(a) is a venue statute, not a grant of power to reassign cases in defiance of other statutory commands.

The legislative history of § 1407 confirms that Congress intended to limit multidistrict transfers to pretrial proceedings. The statute’s structure—requiring remand “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings”—reflects a policy choice that plaintiffs retain the right to trial in their chosen forum. *See United States v. National City Lines*, 334 U.S. 573, 588–89 (1948) (venue statutes strictly construed; courts may not expand them by implication). The district court’s self-assignment undermines that choice and violates both the text and purpose of the statute.

No pre-1970 case authority supports the district court’s action. To the contrary, *General Investment Co. v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co.*, 260 U.S. 261, 268 (1922), held that a court may not assume power not expressly granted by statute. And *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), established that when a statute’s language is plain, “the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” The plain text of § 1407(a) commands remand by the Panel. The district court had no authority to retain the case.

Finally, the district court’s order is an erroneous exercise of jurisdiction that warrants correction. Mandamus is appropriate to confine a lower court to its lawful jurisdiction. *Ex parte Fahey*, 332 U.S. 258, 260 (1947). The district court exceeded the jurisdictional limits imposed by § 1407(a). *See also Ex parte Crane*, 30 U.S. 190, 205 (1831) (mandamus lies to correct usurpation of jurisdiction). This Court should vacate the transfer order and direct remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
