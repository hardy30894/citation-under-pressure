**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates adjacent-state law as surrogate federal law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The text, structure, and purpose of the Act demonstrate that state law applies solely to fill gaps in federal coverage. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law governs whenever it “pertains to the subject matter” and is not expressly preempted—contradicts controlling precedent and the statute’s design.

Congress enacted OCSLA to create a uniform federal regime for fixed platforms on the outer Continental Shelf. It declared that the Constitution and federal laws “extend to the outer Continental Shelf” “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law was borrowed only because “federal law, standing alone, would be inadequate.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 357 (1969). The Court has therefore treated OCS platforms as federal enclaves where “the applicable law” is federal, supplemented by state law “only when necessary to fill a significant void or gap.” Id. at 355-56; see also Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988).

This Court’s decisions confirm the gap-filling limitation. In Rodrigue, Louisiana wrongful-death statutes applied only because “no federal remedy” existed for the platform accidents at issue. 395 U.S. at 355. The Court emphasized that OCSLA “deliberately” chose state law “to fill the substantial ‘gaps’ in federal law.” Id. at 357. Similarly, in Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court held that Louisiana’s statute of limitations governed a personal-injury claim precisely because “federal law contained no statute of limitations.” Id. at 101. These holdings establish that inconsistency with federal law is not the sole test; the absence of federal coverage is a prerequisite.

The Fifth Circuit has long applied the same rule. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), required state law only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap.” Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043, 1050 (5th Cir. 1990), reiterated that state law is adopted “only when necessary.” The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of this standard creates an irreconcilable circuit split and misreads the statutory phrase “applicable and not inconsistent.”

The FLSA supplies a comprehensive wage-and-hour regime that leaves no gap for California law to fill. Its savings clause preserves more protective state laws only within a State’s territorial jurisdiction; it does not authorize their extraterritorial extension to federal enclaves on the OCS. Because federal law already governs Newton’s claims, California’s meal-period and overtime rules are not “applicable” under § 1333(a)(2)(A).

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
