**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between federal patent policy and the constitutional balance of state and federal judicial authority. The Supreme Court of Texas correctly concluded that federal courts do *not* possess exclusive jurisdiction over this state-based legal malpractice claim, despite the presence of a substantial patent law issue. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming dismissal on jurisdictional grounds, and the Federal Circuit’s precedents in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), are not binding on this Court and, more importantly, fail to satisfy the Supreme Court’s four-part test for exclusive federal jurisdiction under *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363, 162 L.Ed.2d 257 (2005). This case does not meet the threshold for exclusive federal jurisdiction because the patent issue, while relevant, is not *necessary* to the resolution of the malpractice claim, is not *actually disputed* in a way that implicates federal interests, and would *disturb* the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.

### I. The Patent Issue Is Not Necessary to the Malpractice Claim

Under *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09, 108 S.Ct. 2166, 100 L.Ed.2d 811 (1988), federal jurisdiction exists only when a plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” The malpractice claim here does not depend on the resolution of the experimental use exception. Minton’s claim rests on the *negligence* of his attorneys in failing to raise a defense, not on the *merits* of that defense. The cause of action is grounded in state tort law—specifically, the duty of care owed by an attorney to a client. The outcome of the malpractice suit turns on whether Gunn’s failure to assert the experimental use exception was a proximate cause of Minton’s damages, not on whether the exception would have succeeded in federal court.

As this Court has held, “To prevail on a legal malpractice claim, a plaintiff must show that ... [his attorney’s negligence] proximately caused the plaintiff’s injuries.” *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The causation inquiry is a state-law question, and it is entirely possible—indeed, likely—that Minton could have established causation even if the experimental use exception were legally or factually unviable. The claim does not require a federal court to determine whether the lease was experimental; it requires only that a jury assess whether Gunn’s failure to raise the defense was a substantial factor in Minton’s loss. This is a classic “case within a case” under *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied), where the federal issue is merely *incidental* to the state tort claim.

### II. The Patent Issue Is Not Actually Disputed in a Way That Justifies Federal Jurisdiction

The Federal Circuit’s approach in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* assumes that any patent issue raised in a malpractice suit is “actually disputed” simply because it is raised. But the Supreme Court has made clear that “actual dispute” means more than mere assertion—it requires a genuine conflict over the meaning or application of federal law. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. Here, the experimental use exception was never properly pleaded or briefed in the underlying patent litigation, and the Federal Circuit affirmed the district court’s denial of reconsideration. The issue was not “actually disputed” in the federal forum; it was *abandoned*. Thus, there is no live controversy over the exception’s validity or applicability.

Moreover, the Texas trial court found, based on the record, that there was no evidence that the lease was for experimental purposes. The lease was commercial in nature: it provided for a percentage of Stark’s gross revenues, included performance warranties, and was used to generate business. The fact that Minton may have *believed* the purpose was experimental does not make the issue “actually disputed” in the legal sense. The state court’s determination that the exception was not factually viable is consistent with the record and does not require federal adjudication.

### III. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities

The final prong of *Grable* requires that federal jurisdiction not “disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. The Federal Circuit’s expansive reading of § 1338(a) threatens that balance. Legal malpractice claims are quintessentially state-law matters, governed by state rules of professional conduct, standards of care, and damages. To allow federal courts to preempt such claims whenever a patent issue is *involved* would effectively transfer control of a vast swath of state tort law to the federal judiciary.
