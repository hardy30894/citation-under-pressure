ARGUMENT

A criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge her conviction on the ground that the statute of conviction exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers in violation of the Tenth Amendment. The Third Circuit correctly held that only the States, or their officers, may assert such a claim.

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States or the people those powers not delegated to the United States. U.S. Const. amend. X. This reservation protects the structural allocation of authority between the federal government and the States. Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939). In that case, private utilities challenged federal power sales as an invasion of state authority under the Tenth Amendment. The Court held that the utilities had “no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment” because “[t]here is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states.” Id. at 144.

The same principle governs here. Section 229 implements a valid treaty. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). No State has objected to its application. Bond, a private defendant, therefore cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to contest Congress’s authority to enact the statute. See also Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 101-02 (1998) (jurisdictional issues must precede the merits).

The majority of circuits have applied this rule to deny private-party standing. United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). Although two circuits have permitted such challenges, Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999); Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Department of Energy, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), those decisions cannot be reconciled with Tennessee Electric’s explicit holding that absent state objection, private litigants have no Tenth Amendment standing.

Bond’s conviction rests on conduct that the statute plainly reaches. Allowing her to litigate federalism objections would convert the Tenth Amendment from a shield for state sovereignty into a sword for individual defendants. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
