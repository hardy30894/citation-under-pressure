# ARGUMENT

## I. The Population Reduction Order Reflects the Traditional Equitable Principles That Congress Codified in the PLRA.

The Prison Litigation Reform Act requires that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn," extend "no further than necessary to correct the violation," and represent the "least intrusive means necessary" to do so. These requirements did not invent a new constraint on judicial power; they codified equitable doctrines this Court has long applied when constitutional violations required systemic remedies. As this Court explained in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), courts fashioning relief for constitutional violations are "guided by equitable principles," and "[t]raditionally, equity has been characterized by a practical flexibility in shaping its remedies and by a facility for adjusting and reconciling public and private needs." *Id.* at 300. That flexibility does not license unbounded relief, but it does permit remedies commensurate with the scope of the violation found.

This Court's decision in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), confirms the point. There the Court held that "[t]he essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case. Flexibility rather than rigidity has distinguished it." *Id.* at 329–30. Similarly, in *Porter v. Warner Holding Co.*, 328 U.S. 395 (1946), the Court held that "the comprehensiveness of this equitable jurisdiction is not to be denied or limited in the absence of a clear and valid legislative command," and that once jurisdiction attaches, "all the inherent equitable powers" of the court are available "for the proper and complete exercise of that jurisdiction." *Id.* at 398. The PLRA's "narrowly drawn," "extends no further than necessary," and "least intrusive means" requirements operate within, not against, this tradition: they demand that a remedy be proportionate to the violation, not that it be trivial in relation to a systemic wrong.

## II. The Three-Judge Court's Factual Findings That Overcrowding Was the Primary Cause of the Constitutional Violations Are Entitled to Substantial Deference.

The three-judge district court did not order population reduction in the abstract. After extensive evidentiary proceedings, it found that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the Eighth Amendment violations pervading California's prisons. That finding of causation is a factual determination subject to deferential review. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), "[a] finding is 'clearly erroneous' when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395. Petitioners identify no such definite and firm conviction of error here; they instead ask this Court to substitute its own assessment of causation for that of the tribunal that heard the evidence firsthand over years of litigation. Nothing in the PLRA displaces the ordinary rule that a trial court's findings on causation—the linchpin of the "extends no further than necessary" inquiry—warrant deference.

That deference is especially appropriate because the underlying constitutional guarantee is not static. As the Court recognized in *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), "the basic concept underlying the Eighth Amendment is nothing less than the dignity of man," and the Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Id.* at 100–01. And in *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court observed that the cruel-and-unusual-punishments clause "may be therefore progressive" and enlarges in meaning "as public opinion becomes enlightened by a humane justice." *Id.* at 373, 378. Overcrowding that produces systemic denial of medical and mental health care implicates precisely the dignity interests these cases protect, and the remedy must be adequate to that harm. *See also Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962) (Eighth Amendment binds the States through the Fourteenth Amendment).

## III. Because Less Intrusive Measures Failed for Nearly a Decade, the Population Cap Was the Least Intrusive Means Available.

The PLRA does not require courts to repeat measures already proven ineffective before ordering effective relief. This Court's school-desegregation cases establish that principle. In *Griffin v. County School Board*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), the Court held that where a decade of gradualist remedies had failed, "[t]he time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out," and courts must be free to order relief adequate to end the violation "now." *Id.* at 234. So too here: the district court did not leap to population reduction. It first entered consent decrees, then appointed a Receiver, and only after years of noncompliance concluded that no remedy short of population reduction could cure the violation. Petitioners' own resistance to intermediate remedies cannot now be invoked to condemn the remedy that resistance necessitated. As this Court stated in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), constitutional rights "are not to be sacrificed or yielded" to a State's calculated recalcitrance. *Id.* at 4. The order below is the product of exactly the equitable restraint the PLRA demands, applied only after less intrusive alternatives were exhausted.
