**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires Only That a Defendant Meet the Dusky Standard of Competency to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel.**

This Court has long held that the constitutional floor for competency in criminal proceedings is the standard articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam): whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the general due process baseline for mental competence in criminal proceedings. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), confirmed that a defendant lacks the "capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense" only when he falls below this same threshold. *Id.* at 171. Nothing in *Dusky* or *Drope* suggests that a defendant's mental capacity must be measured on a sliding scale that rises as the stakes of a particular decision increase. See *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) (due process forbids trial of a person who is mentally incompetent, without reference to a graduated scale of competency for different decisions).

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard, drawn from *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny, imposes precisely such a sliding scale, reasoning that "[c]ompetency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial." Pet. App. 14 (quoting *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1983)). That approach has no footing in this Court's decisions. Competence is competence; the *Dusky* standard asks whether the defendant can rationally understand the proceedings and consult with counsel, and that inquiry does not vary depending on whether the defendant chooses to exercise or to relinquish a particular right.

**II. The Ninth Circuit Erred by Conflating the Distinct Inquiries of Competency and the Knowing, Voluntary Character of a Waiver.**

The court of appeals' error stems from its failure to separate two analytically distinct questions: (1) whether the defendant is *competent*, and (2) whether his decision to plead guilty or waive counsel was *knowing and voluntary*. This Court has always treated these as separate inquiries. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court defined waiver of the right to counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464—a standard addressed to the defendant's awareness of the right and the consequences of forgoing it, not to some heightened measure of cognitive function. Likewise, in *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), the Court required that a defendant electing self-representation be "made aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation, so that the record will establish that 'he knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open.'" *Id.* at 835 (quoting *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942)). And in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court held that a guilty plea must be a "voluntary and intelligent choice among the alternative courses of action open to the defendant." *Id.* at 242. Each of these decisions asks whether the record demonstrates *awareness*—not whether the defendant possesses some enhanced psychiatric capacity beyond that required by *Dusky*.

The Ninth Circuit's own authority illustrates the confusion. In *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), this Court remanded because, "though there was a hearing to determine petitioner's competence to stand trial, no such determination was made with respect to his capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel and to conduct his own defense." *Id.* at 150. *Westbrook* held only that a finding of trial competence does not automatically establish that a defendant *knowingly and voluntarily* relinquished the right to counsel—a separate factual inquiry into the validity of the waiver. It did not hold, and this Court has never held, that the defendant's underlying mental *capacity* must satisfy some standard higher than *Dusky*'s "rational understanding" test. The Ninth Circuit's transformation of *Westbrook*'s waiver-validity requirement into a heightened competency threshold cannot be squared with the decision it purports to apply.

**III. A Single Competency Standard Serves Due Process and Preserves Workable Trial Administration.**

Requiring trial courts to apply one competency standard—*Dusky*'s rational-and-factual-understanding test—while separately assessing whether a plea or waiver was knowing and voluntary, respects both the defendant's due process interests and the practical administration of justice. This Court has recognized the value of a "presumption of regularity" attaching to state-court proceedings and judgments, *Parke v. Raley*, 506 U.S. 20, 29 (1992), a presumption undermined by an amorphous, undefined "reasoned choice" inquiry untethered to any fixed clinical standard. The *Dusky* standard, by contrast, is familiar, administrable, and has governed competency determinations in this Court's jurisprudence for over three decades.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
