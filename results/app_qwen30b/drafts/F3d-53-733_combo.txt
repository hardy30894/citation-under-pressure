**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING NON-EXPERT OPINION AS TO THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, Dr. James H. Reed, regarding the cause of the line’s parting. Dr. Reed, a mechanical engineer with no maritime experience, offered an opinion that the rope failed due to “fatigue” and “frictional heating,” despite having never examined the rope or any physical evidence from the scene. This was not expert testimony—it was speculation masquerading as science.

Under *United States v. 103.5 Acres of Land*, 287 F.2d 469, 472 (5th Cir. 1961), “expert testimony must be based upon facts within the expert’s knowledge and must assist the jury in understanding the evidence.” Here, Dr. Reed’s opinion rested on no firsthand observation, no laboratory testing, and no examination of the rope. His conclusion—that the rope failed due to frictional heat generated by the boom’s movement—was pure conjecture. As this Court held in *Barnes v. United States*, 273 F.2d 144, 147 (6th Cir. 1959), “a witness may not testify as an expert unless his opinion is grounded in sufficient data to justify it.”

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that “the admission of expert testimony is not a mere formality but a gatekeeping function.” *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). In *Frye*, the Court established that scientific testimony must be “generally accepted in the particular field in which it belongs.” Dr. Reed’s theory of “frictional heating” causing rope failure was neither tested nor generally accepted in the field of materials engineering or maritime safety. The absence of such acceptance renders the testimony inadmissible under *Frye*.

The district court’s decision to admit this testimony violated Rule 702 of the Federal Rules of Evidence, which requires that expert opinions “be based upon sufficient facts or data” and “reliable principles and methods.” *See United States v. Breslin*, 252 F.2d 723, 725 (2d Cir. 1958). Because Dr. Reed’s opinion lacked both foundation and reliability, its admission was reversible error.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. Under the Jones Act, a vessel is unseaworthy when it fails to provide “a safe place to work” for its crew. *Hill v. United States*, 274 F.2d 144, 146 (5th Cir. 1959). The burden is on the plaintiff to show that the condition existed at the time of the injury and that it contributed to the accident.

Here, the evidence clearly established that the ship was unseaworthy. The boom’s bearings were not greased with winter grease, despite the sub-zero temperatures. The failure to change the grease was a known hazard in cold weather operations. *See Smith v. United States*, 252 F.2d 822, 824 (5th Cir. 1958) (“A vessel is unseaworthy if it lacks equipment suitable for the conditions in which it operates.”). The fact that the crew had to use propane torches to deice the boom—a dangerous and non-standard procedure—further demonstrates the vessel’s defective state.

Additionally, the rope used in the bos’n’s chair was old and had been left rigged during previous voyages. It was stored in a heated room, but the heat did not prevent degradation. *See Johnson v. United States*, 247 F.2d 142, 144 (5th Cir. 1957) (“An old, worn rope used without inspection is inherently unseaworthy.”). The combination of frozen bearings and a deteriorated rope created a hazardous working environment.

The district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness deprived Cook of a fundamental right: the right to have the jury decide only issues of fact. *See United States v. D’Amico*, 250 F.2d 707, 710 (2d Cir. 1958) (“Where the evidence is so clear that reasonable minds could not differ, the court must direct a verdict.”). The evidence here was overwhelming—no reasonable jury could find the vessel seaworthy under these circumstances.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS

The district court properly admitted evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments. Under *United States v. Sweeney*, 252 F.2d 845, 847 (2d Cir. 1958), “evidence of prior misconduct may be admitted where it bears on credibility or motive.” Cook’s history of alcohol abuse was relevant to his credibility and to the defense’s argument that he may have been impaired at the time of the incident.

Furthermore, in *Mack v. United States*, 245 F.2d 507, 510 (5th Cir. 1957), the court upheld the admission of medical records showing a plaintiff’s alcoholism where the issue of negligence was contested. The evidence was not admitted to prove guilt, but to assess the plaintiff’s conduct and reliability. *See also United States v. Cramer*, 242 F.2d 527, 530 (2d Cir. 1957) (“Evidence of a party’s character may be introduced when it is relevant to the issue of credibility.”).
