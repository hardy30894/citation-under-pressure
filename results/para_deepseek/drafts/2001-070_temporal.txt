**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments.” But from the founding era through 1970, this Court consistently held that the death penalty for murder is neither cruel nor unusual. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958) (plurality opinion) (observing that the death penalty “has been employed throughout our history” and “cannot be said to violate the constitutional concept of cruelty”). Execution of a mentally retarded offender is simply a permissible application of the death penalty; it does not run afoul of the Eighth Amendment.

**I. The Death Penalty Is Constitutional for Homicide Offenses.**

This Court has long upheld capital punishment as a legitimate exercise of state sentencing authority. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1879) (rejecting Eighth Amendment challenge to death by shooting); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (holding that the Eighth Amendment “is not intended” to prohibit “all sever punishments” but only those that are “cruel and unusual”). The very premise of the penalty—that the State may take life for the most serious crimes—remains sound. *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 99 (noting that the death penalty “cannot be said to violate the constitutional concept of cruelty”).

**II. Mental Retardation Does Not Categorically Bar Execution.**

Petitioner argues that mental retardation renders execution categorically disproportionate. But the Eighth Amendment does not require that capital sentences turn on intelligence quotients. At common law, only those who lacked the capacity to understand the nature of punishment—i.e., the insane—were exempt from execution. See *Blackstone’s Commentaries* *24–25 (1769) (noting that “a lunatic” cannot be executed because he “is not capable of committing a crime,” but that “an idiot” who knows right from wrong may be held accountable). Mental retardation, unlike insanity, does not necessarily deprive an offender of the ability to comprehend the wrongfulness of his acts or to appreciate the sanction.

This Court’s pre-1970 decisions reinforce that the Eighth Amendment focuses on the *mode* or *gross disproportionality* of punishment, not on the subjective characteristics of the offender. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910), the Court struck down a 15-year sentence of hard labor for falsifying a public document because it was “cruel in its excess.” But *Weems* itself emphasized that the cruel‑and‑unusual inquiry examines whether the punishment is “so proportioned” to the crime as to “shock the sense of justice of the people.” *Id.* at 369. For a capital crime like premeditated murder, the ultimate sanction is not disproportionate. See *Badders v. United States*, 240 U.S. 391, 394 (1916) (upholding death sentence for mail‑related murder); *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 585–86 (1959) (death sentence for kidnapping‑murder not disproportionate under Due Process or Eighth Amendment).

**III. Evolving Standards Do Not Support Petitioner’s Claim.**

*Trop* announced that the Eighth Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” 356 U.S. at 101. But evolving standards—as they existed before 1970—did not demand a categorical exemption for mental retardation. State practices at the time confirmed that many jurisdictions permitted execution of mentally retarded offenders. Cf. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (upholding second attempt at execution because the Constitution does not require “the infliction of pain” to be “free from risk”). If a failed electric chair does not offend the Eighth Amendment, a measured consideration of a defendant’s mental capacity during sentencing likewise does not.

**IV. States Retain Discretion to Weigh Mitigation.**

The Eighth Amendment does not dictate the precise factors a sentencer must consider. That is a matter of state law. See *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 250–51 (1949) (sentencing judge may consider “the defendant’s background and character” without constitutional mandate). Virginia law already permits the sentencer to consider mental retardation as a mitigating circumstance. Nothing in the Eighth Amendment bars a jury—after hearing evidence of a defendant’s deficits—from concluding that the death penalty is nonetheless appropriate. The Virginia Supreme Court’s decision rested on this sound principle: mental retardation does not automatically insulate a murderer from capital punishment.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
