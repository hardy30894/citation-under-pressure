**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) permits the application of state law only when necessary to fill a gap in federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state law may apply whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law already covers the subject. This interpretation contradicts the plain text, structure, and purpose of the OCSLA, as well as this Court’s precedent.

**I. The Text and Structure of the OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Approach**

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision states that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting qualifier. It does not command the wholesale adoption of state law; rather, it permits state law to fill only those areas where federal law is silent. See *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365 (1969) (holding that state law applies under the OCSLA only when “there is no applicable federal law”).

The OCSLA’s legislative history confirms this reading. Congress recognized that federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The Act was intended to supply a “gap-filling” body of law for the Outer Continental Shelf, not to displace federal law where it already exists. *See* S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 2 (1953) (explaining that the OCSLA was designed to “provide the necessary legal framework” for activities on the Shelf). Where federal law is comprehensive, there is no gap to fill.

**II. This Court’s Precedent Requires a Gap-Filling Analysis**

In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), this Court applied the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision to determine whether state wrongful-death law could apply to accidents on fixed drilling platforms. The Court held that state law could apply only because the Death on the High Seas Act—the relevant federal law—did not cover accidents on fixed structures. *Id.* at 360-61. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA “does not make applicable the laws of the adjacent State to the exclusion of all other laws.” *Id.* at 365. Instead, state law is adopted only “to the extent that it is not inconsistent with other Federal laws.” *Id.* at 365-66.

The Court’s reasoning in *Rodrigue* makes clear that the OCSLA does not permit state law to supplement federal law where federal law already provides a comprehensive scheme. The Court distinguished between areas where federal law is “applicable” and those where it is not. *Id.* at 366. Where federal law is applicable, state law is preempted. *Id.*

**III. The Fair Labor Standards Act Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Wage-and-Hour Law**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”), 29 U.S.C. §§ 201-219, is a comprehensive federal statute governing wages, hours, and overtime. It establishes minimum wage, maximum hours, and overtime compensation requirements. The FLSA also contains a savings clause that permits states to enact more protective laws. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). But that savings clause does not authorize the application of state law on the Outer Continental Shelf. The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision is the exclusive mechanism for adopting state law on the Shelf, and it requires a gap in federal law. The FLSA’s savings clause is irrelevant to the OCSLA analysis.

The FLSA leaves no gap for state law to fill. It provides a complete set of rules for wages and hours, including overtime and meal periods. The Department of Labor has issued regulations elaborating on these requirements. See 29 C.F.R. §§ 785.1-785.50. Because the FLSA is a comprehensive scheme, there is no “void or gap” for state law to occupy. *See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (holding that state law applies under the OCSLA only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law).

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Undermine the OCSLA’s Purpose**

The Ninth Circuit’s holding that state law may apply whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law would effectively nullify the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision. Under that interpretation, state law would apply even where federal law already governs the subject, creating a patchwork of conflicting obligations. This would defeat Congress’s intent to create a uniform body of federal law for the Outer Continental Shelf. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365 (emphasizing that the OCSLA was designed to “provide a uniform body of law” for the Shelf).

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA permits the application of state law only when necessary to fill a gap in federal law. Because the FLSA is a comprehensive federal scheme that leaves no gap for state wage-and-hour law, the Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed.
