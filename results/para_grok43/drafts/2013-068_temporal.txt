The Act constitutes a valid regulation of the time, place, and manner of expression. Streets and sidewalks have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions. Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). The State therefore retains authority to impose reasonable restrictions on their use. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941).

The Act is content-neutral. It applies alike to all persons and does not turn on the ideas or information that speakers wish to convey. See Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368 (1931). Its exemptions for clinic employees, law-enforcement officers, and through-travelers are defined by conduct and function, not by viewpoint. Such classifications do not render the statute invalid when they serve the neutral purpose of facilitating safe access to medical facilities. See Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 554-55 (1965).

The fixed thirty-five-foot zones are narrowly drawn to protect the public interest in unobstructed ingress to and egress from reproductive-health facilities. Analogous limitations on picketing and demonstrating immediately adjacent to courthouses have been sustained to safeguard the administration of justice from outside influence. Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 559, 562 (1965). The same principle justifies protecting patients and staff from physical obstruction and harassment at the entrances, exits, and driveways of clinics. The zones leave open ample alternative channels: sidewalk counseling, distribution of literature, display of signs, prayer, and amplified speech remain available immediately outside the marked arcs and at other locations. See Martin v. City of Struthers, 319 U.S. 141, 145-46 (1943); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 160-61 (1939).

Because the Act is content-neutral, serves significant governmental interests, is narrowly tailored, and preserves adequate means of communication, it satisfies the requirements for a permissible time, place, and manner regulation. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
