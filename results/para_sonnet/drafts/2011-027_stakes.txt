# ARGUMENT

## THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR TITLE NAVIGABILITY REQUIRES DETERMINING WHETHER THE RIVER SEGMENT WAS NAVIGABLE AT STATEHOOD

### A. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Fixes Title to Riverbeds at the Moment of Statehood

This Court has long held that title to the beds of navigable waters passes to a new State upon its admission to the Union under the equal-footing doctrine. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845). The doctrine's temporal anchor is statehood itself: title vests, if at all, at that instant and is governed by the condition of the waterway at that time. *Utah Div. of State Lands v. United States*, 482 U.S. 193, 196–97 (1987) (title to submerged lands is governed by federal law and vests in the State "as of the time of her admission into the Union").

This Court reaffirmed the statehood-specific inquiry in *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), the very decision the Montana Supreme Court cited below. There, the Court examined whether the Missouri River, "at the point the dam is located," was navigable "at the time Montana became a State." *Id.* at 551, 556. The inquiry was not whether the river was navigable in some general or contemporary sense, but whether *that segment*, *at that time*, met the navigability-for-title standard first announced in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1871), and refined in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931).

### B. The Segment-Specific, Statehood-Specific Test of *United States v. Utah* Controls

In *United States v. Utah*, this Court squarely held that navigability for title purposes must be assessed "in the condition in which it was at the time of statehood, and not as subsequently modified." 283 U.S. at 87. The Court emphasized that a river need not be navigable "throughout its entire length"; rather, navigability is determined "on a segment-by-segment basis." *Id.* at 77 (assessing navigability of "different sections" of the Colorado River separately). This segment-and-time-specific approach is not incidental — it is the doctrinal core of the test, because it is the condition of the riverbed at the moment sovereignty transfers that determines who owns it.

*Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), confirms the same principle with respect to the Great Salt Lake, holding that the lake's navigability "at the date of Utah's admission to the Union" was the relevant question. *Id.* at 10. The Court did not ask whether the lake was navigable when the litigation arose, or whether it had since become navigable through improvement; it asked what the condition was at statehood.

### C. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Statehood-Specific Test

The trial court and the Montana Supreme Court applied precisely this framework, finding that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable "at the time of statehood" in 1889. This finding was compelled by *Utah*'s holding that post-statehood alterations — whether by dam construction, dredging, or other modification — cannot retroactively divest the State of title vested at admission, nor can subsequent nonuse or changed conditions manufacture title where none existed. 283 U.S. at 87. Conversely, the corollary is equally binding: evidence of present-day navigability, standing alone, cannot substitute for evidence of navigability at the 1889 baseline.

PPL does not dispute that some historical inquiry into 1889 conditions is required. Rather, PPL's true objection—reflected in its evidentiary challenges to the Foley and Roberts affidavits—is to the *quality* of proof the State used to satisfy that historical burden. But this Court has recognized that where the relevant condition existed generations in the past, courts must necessarily rely on the best available historical evidence, including government surveys, contemporaneous accounts, and expert reconstruction. *See Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. at 11 (relying on historical lake-level data and 19th-century survey evidence to establish conditions at statehood). The Montana courts' reliance on 19th-century military and civilian expedition reports, contemporaneous commercial-use records, and historical photographs was entirely consistent with the methodology this Court itself employed in *Utah*.

### D. PPL's Proposed "Present Use" or "Susceptibility Without Historical Anchor" Standard Would Displace the Constitutional Test

PPL's position — that navigability can be assessed by reference to the river's general characteristics without rigorous anchoring to 1889 — would collapse the distinction this Court drew in *The Daniel Ball* between waters that are navigable in fact because they are "used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce," 77 U.S. at 563, and waters that have merely become navigable, or been made to appear navigable, through later human intervention. Allowing present conditions to control would permit the very kind of retroactive title manipulation *Utah* forbade: a State could lose riverbed title through subsequent damming that impedes commerce, or a private party could defeat state title by pointing to modern recreational or industrial use unmoored from the 1889 baseline.

The statehood-specific rule also serves the equal-footing doctrine's core purpose: ensuring parity among the States by applying a uniform, historically fixed test rather than one that varies with contemporary economic or engineering circumstances. *See Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 230 (equal footing requires that new States receive "the same rights, sovereignty and jurisdiction" as the original States over their navigable waters and soils beneath them).

### CONCLUSION

The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires courts to determine, segment by segment, whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable *at the time of statehood*—not merely whether it is navigable today or was navigable at some other point. This rule, announced in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), and applied by this Court to Montana's own rivers in *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), was faithfully applied by the Montana Supreme Court below. The judgment should be affirmed.
