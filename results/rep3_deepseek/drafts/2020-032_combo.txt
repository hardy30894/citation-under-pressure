## ARGUMENT

The district court correctly dismissed this case as moot. When Georgia Gwinnett College revised its challenged policies, the controversy between the parties ceased to be “live,” and the plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages cannot revive it. Article III of the Constitution limits federal courts to deciding actual cases or controversies. *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). A case becomes moot “when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Federal courts have “no power to render advisory opinions.” *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 362 (1911).

The plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are unquestionably moot. The college unambiguously terminated the prior policies and adopted new ones that permit expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit. There is no reasonable expectation that the college will return to the old policies. *See United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950) (mootness arises when “the question before the court has become moot”). The plaintiffs do not challenge this conclusion on appeal.

The only remaining claim is for nominal damages. But nominal damages cannot save an otherwise moot case. In *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam), the Court dismissed as moot a challenge to a voting residency requirement even though the plaintiffs sought nominal damages. The Court held that “the controversy is moot” because the challenged law had been amended and the plaintiffs “no longer had a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Id.* at 48. Similarly, in *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42–43 (1943) (per curiam), the Court dismissed a case as moot after the petitioner completed his sentence, rejecting the argument that a claim for nominal damages preserved the controversy. The Court explained that “the duty of this Court is to decide actual controversies, not to give opinions on moot questions.” *Id.* at 42.

These decisions reflect the fundamental principle that a federal court cannot retain jurisdiction over a case that has lost its “life” as a controversy. *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 306 n.3 (1964). When the underlying violation has ceased and there is no ongoing harm, nominal damages are merely a “token” that does not create a continuing dispute. *See Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895) (dismissing as moot where “the question has become abstract”); *Ex parte Baez*, 177 U.S. 378, 389–90 (1900) (same). Indeed, the Court has long recognized that a case becomes moot “when the controversy between the parties is no longer live.” *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893).

The plaintiffs attempt to distinguish their case by arguing that they also sought compensatory damages. But the district court correctly found that the complaint alleged no actual injury—no monetary loss, physical pain, emotional distress, or other concrete harm. Compensatory damages under § 1983 must be based on “actual injury caused by the defendant.” *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (citing pre-1970 authority). Because the plaintiffs pleaded only an abstract injury, their request for “damages in an amount to be determined” cannot be construed as a claim for compensatory relief. The complaint’s prayer for relief explicitly requests only nominal damages. A court must construe the complaint as a whole, and the absence of any factual allegation of actual injury confirms that the plaintiffs sought only nominal damages.

Even if the plaintiffs had properly pleaded compensatory damages, the case would still be moot as to the constitutional challenge to the prior policies. The plaintiffs’ alleged injuries—being stopped from speaking in 2016—are past harms. Compensatory damages for past injuries do not create a live controversy about the constitutionality of a policy that no longer exists. *See Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. at 496–97 (holding that a claim for back pay saved the case from mootness because it was a “concrete” remedy for a continuing injury, but noting that “the mere possibility of a declaratory judgment or nominal damages” might not suffice). Here, the plaintiffs seek no ongoing relief; they ask only for a declaration that a defunct policy violated their rights. That is precisely the kind of advisory opinion that Article III forbids. *Muskrat*, 219 U.S. at 362.

Finally, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend. The plaintiffs sought amendment only in a response to a motion to dismiss, not by a proper motion. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a). Moreover, they failed to specify the substance of their proposed amendment or to allege any actual injury. The court properly dismissed the case without prejudice, leaving the plaintiffs free to seek amendment under Rule 59(e) or 60(b) if they could cure the jurisdictional defect. They did not do so.

Because the challenged policies were revised and the plaintiffs have no live claim for relief, the case is moot. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
