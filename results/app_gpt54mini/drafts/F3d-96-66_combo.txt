**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because a reasonable jury could find Bora Corp. negligent under § 905(b) for retaining an “active operations” duty and failing to exercise reasonable care in the ship’s ongoing hatch-cover and snatch-block operations. The governing question is whether the ship’s crew, while still controlling the equipment at issue, negligently left a hazardous condition that injured Serbin during cargo operations. Under well-settled maritime principles, a vessel that actively participates in, directs, or retains control over instrumentalities used in the work remains bound to exercise reasonable care. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424 (1939); *Canadian Aviator, Ltd. v. United States*, 324 U.S. 215 (1945).

A shipowner is not relieved of liability merely because longshoremen are performing the cargo work. Maritime law has long imposed a duty of due care where the vessel’s own conduct creates or maintains the risk. In *The Arizona v. Anelich*, 298 U.S. 110 (1936), the Court recognized that a vessel may be responsible where its negligence contributes to a longshoreman’s injury in the course of cargo handling. Likewise, *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), explained that seamen’s work hazards are not limited to the vessel’s employees; rather, maritime liability follows the realities of shipboard work. Although Congress later altered remedies, it did not erase the basic negligence principle that a ship must act reasonably with respect to hazards it creates or controls. *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953); *Kermarec v. Compagnie Générale Transatlantique*, 358 U.S. 625 (1959).

Here, the record supports a finding that the crew, not the stevedore, controlled the snatch blocks. The blocks were fixed to the hatch covers; the crew operated the block-and-pulley system; and “moving the snatch blocks is the responsibility of the crew.” That evidence is sufficient to bring this case within the settled rule that one who retains control over a dangerous instrumentality must use reasonable care in its operation. *Canadian Aviator*, 324 U.S. at 224–25; *Socony-Vacuum Oil*, 305 U.S. at 431–32. A jury could find that leaving a 100-pound block in the down position atop cargo in the wing created an obvious hazard in a place where longshoremen were required to work, and that the crew’s failure to reset it before or during the unloading operation was negligent. *The Arizona v. Anelich*, 298 U.S. at 121–22.

The district court’s summary judgment also overlooked the traditional maritime rule that issues of negligence, reasonable care, and foreseeability are ordinarily for the trier of fact. *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. Goodman*, 275 U.S. 66 (1927), recognized that negligence depends on what a reasonable actor would do under the circumstances; where the facts permit differing inferences, the question is for the jury. See also *Tiller v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co.*, 318 U.S. 54 (1943) (reaffirming the broad fact-bound character of negligence determinations). Serbin testified that the block was in the middle of a work area where the hookup man ordinarily stood, that it impeded removal of the cargo beneath it, and that he moved it to avoid delay. A jury could conclude that Bora should have anticipated precisely this kind of response and that the danger was foreseeable. *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R. Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928), is not to the contrary; it confirms that foreseeable risk is the touchstone, and here the risk to a longshoreman working immediately beside the block was manifest.

Nor can Bora evade liability by pointing to Serbin’s effort to move the block himself. Maritime law has never treated a plaintiff’s decision to continue working in the face of shipboard hazard as a complete bar where the vessel’s negligence remained an operative cause. *Socony-Vacuum Oil*, 305 U.S. at 431–32; *Pope & Talbot*, 346 U.S. at 408–09. Serbin’s attempt to avoid delay and protect the cargo went, at most, to comparative fault or assumption of risk concepts that do not justify removing the case from the jury where the vessel’s own negligence is supported by evidence. *Kermarec*, 358 U.S. at 632; *Tiller*, 318 U.S. at 58–59.

Finally, summary judgment was improper because the ship’s duty depended on factual disputes about control, custom, and the crew’s role in repositioning the blocks. A vessel that undertakes the operational task of opening and securing hatch covers cannot, consistent with maritime due care, leave a dangerous positioning of equipment for the stevedore to discover and correct at its own peril. *Canadian Aviator*, 324 U.S. at 224–25; *Seas Shipping*, 328 U.S. at 94–95. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
