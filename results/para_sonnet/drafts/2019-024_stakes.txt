# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1113(2) REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE KNOWLEDGE, NOT MERE ACCESS TO DISCLOSED INFORMATION

Section 1113(2) bars suit "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The word "actual" is not surplusage. This Court has long recognized that when Congress uses the word "actual" to modify "knowledge," it means to draw a meaningful distinction from constructive knowledge. *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 27 (2001) (contrasting a "discovery rule," which can be triggered by information a plaintiff should have discovered, with an "actual knowledge" requirement). Indeed, the very structure of ERISA confirms this reading: Congress originally enacted § 1113 with *two* separate triggers—actual knowledge, and constructive knowledge based on when a report "was filed with the Secretary" from which the plaintiff "could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976) (repealed 1987). By eliminating the constructive-knowledge trigger in 1987 and leaving only the actual-knowledge provision, Congress made clear that access to information—without more—cannot suffice. See *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) ("Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.").

Respondent and Intel effectively ask this Court to resurrect the constructive-knowledge standard that Congress deliberately repealed, arguing that disclosure alone—regardless of whether the plaintiff read or retained the information—starts the limitations clock. That approach collapses the actual/constructive distinction Congress preserved. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held below, a plaintiff must be "actually aware" of the underlying transaction, not merely furnished the opportunity to become aware of it. Pet. App. 14a.

## II. THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT CONFIRMS THAT "ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE" MEANS SUBJECTIVE AWARENESS

This Court has consistently construed "actual knowledge" requirements throughout federal law to demand genuine subjective awareness, not constructive or imputed knowledge. In the context of the discovery rule under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, this Court held that Congress's use of "known" rather than "should have known" language means the statute runs only from actual discovery. *TRW Inc.*, 534 U.S. at 27. Similarly, when Congress uses "actual knowledge" or "actual notice" as opposed to "should have known," it signals an intent to impose a subjective standard focused on the plaintiff's genuine cognitive state. *Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich LPA*, 559 U.S. 573, 584 (2010) (distinguishing "actual knowledge" from a standard that would encompass negligent failure to know).

Nothing in the statutory text or this Court's precedent supports the inference that a plaintiff who received, but never opened, read, or comprehended, disclosure documents can be charged with "actual knowledge" of their contents. Constructive knowledge—the doctrine that a person is deemed to know what a reasonable person would have learned from available information—is precisely the doctrine Congress excised from § 1113 in 1987.

## III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE ACTUAL-KNOWLEDGE STANDARD TO THE SUMMARY JUDGMENT RECORD

The court below properly held that ERISA plaintiffs bringing claims under § 1104 must have actual knowledge "both that those investments occurred, and that they were imprudent." Pet. App. 21a. This is not a heightened legal-sufficiency requirement; it is simply the factual content of "knowledge of the breach" for an imprudence claim, since the breach *is* the imprudent investment decision. The court's earlier decisions confirm that "knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation" is what triggers the limitations period. *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985). But knowledge of a transaction logically requires the plaintiff to be aware the transaction happened—not merely that Intel made information about the transaction accessible on a website.

Respondent Sulyma submitted sworn testimony that he was "unaware that the monies that [he] had invested through the Intel retirement plans had been invested in hedge funds or private equity" and did "not recall seeing any documents" alerting him to that fact. Pet. App. 22a. That testimony created a genuine dispute of material fact under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56. On summary judgment, courts must view the evidence "in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party." *Curley v. City of North Las Vegas*, 772 F.3d 629, 631 (9th Cir. 2014). Where a plaintiff swears he did not know the contents of a disclosure, a court cannot simply impute that knowledge to him based on the fact that Intel posted the disclosure on a website he could have visited.

## IV. PETITIONER'S RULE WOULD EFFECTIVELY REWRITE § 1113(2) TO MEAN "SHOULD HAVE KNOWN"

Intel's proposed rule—that disclosure of information necessarily constitutes "actual knowledge" regardless of whether the plaintiff read or retained it—asks this Court to adopt precisely the standard Congress rejected. If mere availability of information sufficed, the word "actual" would do no work at all, and § 1113(2) would operate identically to a constructive-knowledge or negligent-discovery rule. This Court does not read statutes to render words superfluous. *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) ("[C]ourt[s] must give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.").

The Ninth Circuit's approach preserves the statutory text without eviscerating fiduciaries' ability to invoke the limitations defense: fiduciaries retain the six-year repose period under § 1113(1), and may still establish actual knowledge through circumstantial evidence—for example, proof that a plaintiff opened, read, and discussed a disclosure—rather than through a per se rule that disclosure equals knowledge. See Pet. App. 15a–16a (surveying circuit precedent permitting actual knowledge to be shown where "plaintiffs were parties to the transaction" or "were specifically informed").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
