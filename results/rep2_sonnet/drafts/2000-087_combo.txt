**ARGUMENT**

**Section 1231(a)(6) Must Be Construed to Authorize Detention Only for the Period Reasonably Necessary to Effect Removal**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not speak in terms of duration. It says an alien "may be detained beyond the removal period." Nothing in that text compels—or even suggests—that Congress meant to authorize detention without temporal limit, potentially for the rest of a person's life. Where a statute is fairly susceptible of two readings, one of which raises grave constitutional doubts and one of which does not, this Court has long adhered to the settled canon that it will construe the statute to avoid the constitutional question. *Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) ("The Court will not pass upon a constitutional question although properly presented by the record, if there is also present some other ground upon which the case may be disposed of."). That canon governs here with particular force because this Court applied precisely the same interpretive method to a related immigration-detention provision in *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957). There the Court read the Attorney General's authority to question a supervised alien narrowly—limiting it to inquiries relevant to the alien's departure—rather than adopt a limitless construction that would have raised serious constitutional problems. The same restraint is due here: § 1231(a)(6) should be read to permit detention only for the period reasonably necessary to bring about removal, not indefinitely.

**Indefinite Civil Detention of a Resident Alien Raises Serious Due Process Concerns**

The Fifth Amendment's guarantee that no "person" shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law is not limited to citizens. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), this Court held that Congress could not authorize imprisonment of Chinese laborers as a mere incident of exclusion proceedings without due process, explaining that no person, alien or citizen, may be "deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law." That principle applies with special force to an alien like Zadvydas, who was lawfully admitted decades ago and has resided continuously in this country. As this Court recognized in *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), "once an alien lawfully enters and resides in this country he becomes invested with the rights guaranteed by the Constitution to all people within our borders." The stakes of unchecked detention following a deportation order are not abstract. In *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), this Court observed that erroneous deportation "may result also in loss of both property and life; or of all that makes life worth living," and required a judicial hearing before a resident could be summarily removed on a disputed claim of alienage. The same solicitude for liberty that animated *Yamataya v. Fisher*, 189 U.S. 86 (1903)—which held that even in deportation proceedings an alien is entitled to be heard before the government may act on the "matters upon which that liberty depends"—confirms that indefinite civil confinement, untethered to any prospect of removal, cannot be squared with the Due Process Clause. Civil detention that has no foreseeable endpoint is not detention "to secure removal"; it is preventive incarceration by another name, imposed without the individualized adversarial procedures that this Court's due process precedents demand before liberty may be so curtailed.

**Shaughnessy v. Mezei Does Not Control, and Carlson v. Landon Confirms Only Temporary Detention Is Permitted**

The government's reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. Mezei was an alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States; the Court's toleration of his continued exclusion rested on the government's plenary power to exclude aliens seeking admission, a power resting on wholly different footing from the power to expel someone already admitted and residing here, as this Court's own precedents distinguishing entrants from excludable aliens make clear. See *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. 590. Zadvydas, by contrast, was admitted as a resident alien in 1956 and has lived in the United States ever since; he stands in the position of the entrant whose liberty interest *Ng Fung Ho* and *Yamataya* protect, not the excluded alien of *Mezei*.

Nor does *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), support open-ended detention. *Carlson* upheld detention without bail of aliens *pending completion of deportation proceedings*, where confinement was inherently temporary and tied to the imminent resolution of a live proceeding and a genuine risk of flight or danger during that finite period. It did not sanction detention after a final order of removal has issued and removal itself has become not merely delayed but not reasonably foreseeable. Once, as here, deportation is no longer achievable within any predictable time because no receiving country will accept the alien, the rationale of *Carlson*—a brief, bounded detention incidental to an ongoing process—provides no warrant for perpetual confinement.

**Conclusion**

Section 1231(a)(6), construed consistently with *Witkovich* and *Ashwander* to avoid the serious due process problems identified in *Wong Wing*, *Yamataya*, *Ng Fung Ho*, and *Kwong Hai Chew*, and distinguishable from *Mezei* and *Carlson*, authorizes detention only for the period reasonably necessary to accomplish an alien's removal—not indefinite detention where removal is not reasonably foreseeable.
