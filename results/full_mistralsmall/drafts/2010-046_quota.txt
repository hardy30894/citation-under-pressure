**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER IS AUTHORIZED BY 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3) AND DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT (PLRA).**

The PLRA’s “three-judge court” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3), expressly authorizes a court to “impose prospective injunctive relief as necessary to correct the constitutional violation.” In *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 514 (2011), the Supreme Court upheld the three-judge court’s population reduction order, holding that it was “necessary to remedy the violation” of prisoners’ Eighth Amendment rights. The Court recognized that “overcrowding [was] the primary cause” of the constitutional violations, *id.* at 520, and that “[r]elief must therefore be directed at the overcrowding problem.” *Id.* at 521. The Court further explained that “[t]he need for a population limit is evident” because “[a]ll other measures will be insufficient” to remedy the violations. *Id.* at 522.

The PLRA does not bar such relief. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) permits prospective injunctive relief “as necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right,” and § 3626(a)(3) specifically authorizes a three-judge court to “impose prospective injunctive relief” to correct constitutional violations. The Supreme Court in *Brown v. Plata* rejected the argument that § 3626(b) (which limits prospective relief) applied to three-judge courts, holding that § 3626(b) “does not apply to three-judge courts.” 563 U.S. at 514. The Court reasoned that § 3626(b) “applies only to single-judge determinations,” *id.*, and that the three-judge court’s order was consistent with § 3626(a)(3). *Id.* at 515.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1163 (9th Cir. 2010), aff’d sub nom. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, likewise upheld the three-judge court’s order. The Ninth Circuit held that the order was “necessary to remedy the constitutional violations” and did not violate the PLRA. *Id.* at 1170. The court explained that the PLRA’s “findings and declarations” do not “limit the authority of a three-judge court” under § 3626(a)(3). *Id.* at 1171 (citing *Porter v. Nussle*, 534 U.S. 516, 524 (2002)).

**II. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT.**

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar the three-judge court’s order. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Supreme Court held that prospective injunctive relief against state officials is not barred by the Eleventh Amendment. The Court in *Brown v. Plata* reaffirmed this principle, holding that the three-judge court’s order was “properly characterized as prospective relief” and did not violate the Eleventh Amendment. 563 U.S. at 518.

The Ninth Circuit likewise rejected the state’s Eleventh Amendment argument in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d at 1172. The court held that the three-judge court’s order was “properly characterized as prospective relief” and did not violate the Eleventh Amendment. *Id.* (citing *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123).

**III. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER IS SUPPORTED BY ADEQUATE FACTUAL FINDINGS.**

The three-judge court’s order is supported by extensive factual findings. The court found that “serious overcrowding in California’s 33 prisons was the ‘primary cause’ for violations of the Eighth Amendment.” *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. at 520. The court further found that “[a]ll other measures will be insufficient” to remedy the violations and that “[t]he need for a population limit is evident.” *Id.* at 521-22.

The Supreme Court in *Brown v. Plata* upheld these findings, holding that they were “not clearly erroneous.” 563 U.S. at 520. The Court explained that the three-judge court’s findings were “thorough and well supported by the evidence.” *Id.* at 521.

**IV. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER IS NOT BARRED BY THE PLRA’S “LEAST RESTRICTIVE MEANS” REQUIREMENT.**

The PLRA does not require the three-judge court to impose the “least restrictive means” of relief. In *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. at 514, the Supreme Court held that § 3626(a)(3) does not incorporate the “least restrictive means” requirement of § 3626(b). The Court explained that § 3626(a)(3) “authorizes a three-judge court to ‘impose prospective injunctive relief as necessary to correct the violation,’” and that this language “does not incorporate the ‘least restrictive means’ requirement.” *Id.* at 515.

The Ninth Circuit likewise rejected this argument in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d at 1171. The court held that the three-judge court’s order was “necessary to remedy the constitutional violations” and did not violate the PLRA’s “least restrictive means” requirement. *Id.*

**V. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SEPARATION OF POWERS DOCTRINE.**

The three-judge court’s order does not violate the separation of powers doctrine. In *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. at 518, the Supreme Court held that the three-judge court’s order did not “invade the province of the Executive Branch.” The Court explained that the order was “properly characterized as prospective relief” and did not “direct the Executive Branch to take any particular action.” *Id.*

The Ninth Circuit likewise rejected this argument in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d at 1172. The court held that the three-judge court’s order was “properly characterized as prospective relief” and did not violate the separation of powers doctrine. *Id.* (citing *Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267, 289-90 (1977)).
