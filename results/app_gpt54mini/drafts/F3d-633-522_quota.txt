## ARGUMENT

### I. DHL’s severance “releases” did not extinguish the workers’ WARN Act claims.

The WARN Act confers a statutory right to notice before a covered mass layoff or plant closing, and that right may not be avoided by private contract unless the purported waiver is knowing, voluntary, and unmistakable. See *Carpenters Dist. Council of New Orleans & Vicinity v. Dillard Dep’t Stores, Inc.*, 15 F.3d 1275, 1283–84 (5th Cir. 1994); *O’Neill v. Sears, Roebuck & Co.*, 108 F. Supp. 2d 443, 446–47 (E.D. Pa. 2000). The releases here were not an informed surrender of WARN claims. They were presented after DHL had already announced the end of its domestic shipping business, after the facilities had begun shedding jobs, and amid time pressure that left employees little meaningful opportunity to assess the legal effect of the documents. The short decision windows, the lack of individualized legal advice, and the fact that the workers were asked to waive claims before they could know the full scope of WARN liability all undermine any finding of a valid relinquishment. See *Melan v. Belle Vernon Area Sch. Dist.*, 311 F.3d 819, 830–31 (3d Cir. 2002); *Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co.*, 415 U.S. 36, 51–52 (1974).

A release cannot waive rights that are not yet meaningfully understood or that arise from a statutory scheme designed to protect workers during sudden displacement. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that a worker’s waiver of important federal rights must be clear and voluntary. *Wright v. Universal Mar. Serv. Corp.*, 525 U.S. 70, 80–81 (1998); *Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc.*, 522 U.S. 422, 427–28 (1998). These principles apply with force here because the release language was buried in a severance package, offered in a crisis, and coupled with a short revocation period that did not supply the deliberation WARN requires. See *Leavitt v. Nw. Bell Tel. Co.*, 921 F.2d 160, 164–65 (8th Cir. 1990).

### II. The district court erred in treating the severance agreements as independent consideration that could buy a WARN waiver.

DHL argues that the workers received severance “beyond anything to which [they were] already or otherwise entitled.” But a payment offered to induce a surrender of statutory rights does not itself establish that the waiver was valid. Consideration is necessary, not sufficient. See *Oubre*, 522 U.S. at 427; *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80. Under ordinary waiver principles, courts must examine the totality of the circumstances, including the employee’s opportunity for deliberation, knowledge of the right being waived, and the absence of coercion. *Melan*, 311 F.3d at 830–31; *Cirillo v. Arco Chem. Co.*, 862 F.2d 448, 451–52 (3d Cir. 1988).

Those factors cut against DHL. Employees were told the company was exiting domestic shipping; they faced imminent displacement; and they were required to choose severance while simultaneously making rebid decisions affecting their employment. That is not the sort of considered, bilateral negotiation that supports forfeiture of federal rights. Cf. *Henn v. Nat’l Geographic Soc’y*, 819 F.2d 824, 828–29 (7th Cir. 1987) (enforcing waiver only where the employee’s decision was knowing and deliberate).

### III. The releases, even if effective as to some claims, did not clearly and unmistakably waive the workers’ WARN claims.

Courts do not presume that a general release covers specific statutory claims unless the language is clear. *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80; *Metzger v. Lands’ End, Inc.*, 669 F.3d 887, 890–91 (7th Cir. 2012). Here, the release listed many causes of action, including the federal WARN Act, but the document was not explained in a way that would allow workers to understand the distinct legal consequences of waiving a claim under a federal notice statute that had not yet been litigated or assessed. The presence of WARN in boilerplate text does not answer whether each signer knowingly waived a claim arising from DHL’s own failure to provide statutory notice before the severance process began. See *Oubre*, 522 U.S. at 427; *Town of Newton v. Rumery*, 480 U.S. 386, 393–94 (1987).

Moreover, WARN rights are not ordinary contract rights. The statute serves an important public purpose by protecting workers and communities from abrupt economic dislocation. See *United Mine Workers of Am. v. Martinka Coal Co.*, 202 F.3d 717, 722 (4th Cir. 2000). Courts should therefore insist on especially clear proof before concluding that employees surrendered the Act’s protections. See *Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody*, 422 U.S. 405, 416–18 (1975) (statutory employment protections are remedial and should not be lightly defeated).

### IV. At a minimum, the class members’ claims were not defeated by the releases as a matter of law.

The record establishes that 506 workers signed one of several forms and that the bargaining units received different deadlines and different severance packages. Those variations matter. They show that the releases were individualized, not uniform in substance or timing, and therefore could not extinguish class claims categorically at the pleading or summary judgment stage. See *In re Cendant Corp. Prides Litig.*, 243 F.3d 722, 736–37 (3d Cir. 2001) (release defenses are fact intensive); *Kirsch v. Fleet St., Ltd.*, 148 F.3d 149, 170–71 (2d Cir. 1998).

Because DHL has not established a valid, knowing, and voluntary waiver of WARN rights, the releases do not bar this suit. The judgment should be reversed.
