# ARGUMENT

## THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD OF COMPETENCE FOR DEFENDANTS WHO SEEK TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL

The Ninth Circuit invented a competency standard found nowhere in this Court's precedents. That court held that a defendant is "competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if he has the capacity for 'reasoned choice' among the alternatives available to him," a standard it deemed higher than "the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense." *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 270 (9th Cir. 1992) (quoting *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975)). This holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's decisions in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), *Drope*, and *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966).

### A. The Competency Standard for Standing Trial Governs Pleading Guilty and Waiving Counsel

In *Dusky*, this Court articulated a single test for competency: whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and has "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." 362 U.S. at 402. This Court reaffirmed that standard in *Drope*, explaining that competence turns on "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding, and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." 420 U.S. at 171 (quoting *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402).

Nothing in *Dusky* or *Drope* suggests that a different, more demanding standard applies once a defendant decides to plead guilty or dispense with counsel rather than proceed to trial. To the contrary, both decisions frame competency as a unitary inquiry into the defendant's mental capacity to understand the proceedings and to function within them—an inquiry that does not vary with the particular procedural choice the defendant makes. A defendant who lacks the competence described in *Dusky* is incompetent for all purposes in the criminal process; a defendant who possesses that competence is competent to make the range of decisions the criminal process demands, including the decision to plead guilty or represent himself.

### B. The Decision to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel Does Not Demand Greater Mental Acuity Than the Decision to Stand Trial

The Ninth Circuit reasoned that waiving counsel and pleading guilty require a "higher level of mental functioning" than standing trial because such waivers relinquish numerous constitutional rights—to counsel, to compulsory process, to confront witnesses, to a jury trial, and against self-incrimination. 972 F.2d at 265 (citing *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969)). But the *quantity* of rights waived does not translate into a heightened *competency* requirement. A defendant who stands trial also makes numerous critical decisions—whether to testify, whether to accept a plea offer, whether to challenge evidence—each implicating constitutional rights. The competency required to make those decisions is the same competency *Dusky* demands: a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to consult with counsel.

This Court's decision in *Westbrook v. Arizona* is illustrative, not to the contrary. There, the Court remanded because the trial court had determined the defendant's competence to stand trial without separately considering whether he was competent to waive counsel. 384 U.S. at 150. But *Westbrook* did not hold that a different or higher standard of competence applies to waiver of counsel; it held only that the trial court must direct its inquiry to the waiver decision itself, using the *same* competency standard, rather than assuming that a general finding of trial competence necessarily resolves the waiver question. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" test transforms *Westbrook*'s procedural requirement—that the *trial court ask the question*—into a substantive requirement that the *answer be judged by a stricter test*. That transformation finds no support in *Westbrook*'s reasoning or holding.

### C. The "Reasoned Choice" Standard Is Unworkable and Untethered to Any Coherent Constitutional Principle

The Ninth Circuit's standard also fails because it provides no discernible content beyond the *Dusky* formulation it purports to supplement. The court below could not explain what capacities beyond rational and factual understanding and ability to consult with counsel the "reasoned choice" standard requires; it simply asserted that competency to waive rights "requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial." 972 F.2d at 270 (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1983); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981)). Due process demands clarity in the standards trial courts must apply, particularly where, as here, a defendant's fundamental rights and, in a capital case, his life are at stake. *Dusky*'s standard supplies that clarity; the Ninth Circuit's freestanding "reasoned choice" test does not.

Moreover, the practical effect of the Ninth Circuit's rule is to permit a defendant to be tried, convicted, and sentenced under the ordinary competency standard, yet to bar that same defendant—found competent to withstand the rigors of a full trial—from making the simpler decision to forgo that trial. This is anomalous. If a defendant is competent to understand the charges, to consult with counsel, and to assist in his defense throughout a trial, he is necessarily competent to make the more limited decision to enter a guilty plea or to proceed without counsel. There is no principled basis in the Due Process Clause for concluding otherwise.

### CONCLUSION

The competency standard articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), and reaffirmed in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), governs a defendant's competence to plead guilty and to waive the right to counsel. The Ninth Circuit's heightened "reasoned choice" standard has no basis in this Court's precedents and should be rejected. The judgment below should be reversed.
