**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989). Congress may delegate authority so long as it provides “an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Supreme Court has not invalidated a congressional delegation on nondelegation grounds since 1935. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 n.7.

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies the “intelligible principle” standard. Congress limited the Attorney General’s authority in multiple ways. First, the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only if he finds it “necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). Second, he must consider three specific factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse; the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and the risk to public health. *Id.* § 811(h)(3); *see id.* § 811(c)(4)-(6). Third, the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit it to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be considered. *Id.* § 811(h)(4). Fourth, temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. *Id.* § 811(h)(2). These constraints provide “meaningful guidance” and satisfy the nondelegation doctrine. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374.

Petitioners argue that delegations authorizing criminal prohibitions require stricter scrutiny. But the Court in *Mistretta* upheld a delegation that empowered the Sentencing Commission to promulgate binding sentencing guidelines, which directly affect criminal penalties. *Id.* at 371-79. The Court did not apply a heightened standard. Moreover, the temporary scheduling power is narrower than the permanent scheduling power, which this Court has previously upheld. *See United States v. Barraza*, 843 F.2d 1251, 1256 (9th Cir. 1988) (upholding permanent scheduling delegation). The temporary provision’s emergency nature and time limits actually *reduce* the risk of arbitrary action.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL PROPERLY DELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DRUG ENFORCEMENT ADMINISTRATION.**

The Controlled Substances Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that the Attorney General may “promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary and appropriate” and may “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” This language is unambiguous. *See United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987) (holding that § 871(a) authorizes subdelegation of temporary scheduling power).

The Attorney General exercised this authority by regulation, delegating to the DEA Administrator “[t]he functions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). This regulation encompasses the temporary scheduling power. The DEA Administrator then followed the statutory procedures: he published notice, considered the required factors, and issued a temporary scheduling order for 4-methylaminorex (Euphoria). 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174 (1987); 52 Fed. Reg. 38,225 (1987). The subdelegation was therefore lawful.

Petitioners’ contrary argument would frustrate Congress’s intent. Congress amended the CSA in 1984 to expedite emergency scheduling precisely because the permanent process was too slow to combat designer drugs. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 98-848, pt. 1, at 10 (1984). Allowing the Attorney General to delegate this power to the DEA—the agency with scientific and investigative expertise—effectuates that purpose. *See United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 545 (9th Cir. 1988) (upholding subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
