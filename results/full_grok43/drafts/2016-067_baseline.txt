The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s],” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a term that “includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] … another.’” Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)). The Act therefore “cover[s] all third persons who regularly collect debts for others.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977). It does not reach a creditor collecting its own debts. See id. at 3–4.

Santander purchased the loans outright. Once title passed, the debts were owed to Santander, not “another.” The complaint therefore fails at the threshold definition. See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1316 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[A] person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition.”). The same conclusion follows from the statutory text: “the word ‘another’ modifies both ‘owed or due’ and ‘asserted to be owed or due,’” so the phrase reaches only debts owed to a third party at the time of collection. Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013); accord Paroline v. United States, 572 U.S. 434, 451 (2014) (clause applicable to one phrase applies to all parallel phrases).

Petitioners’ contrary reading—that default status alone converts a purchaser into a debt collector—finds no support in the statute. The Act distinguishes debt collectors from creditors by whether the collector acts “on behalf of others or for its own account,” not by the debt’s status at acquisition. The exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) presupposes that the person already satisfies the principal definition; it does not create an independent basis for liability. Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1314. Treating every purchaser of defaulted paper as a debt collector would also sweep in ordinary creditors whose “principal purpose” is not debt collection, a result Congress did not intend. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).

Because the complaint alleges only that Santander collected debts it owned, Santander was not acting as a “debt collector” when the challenged communications occurred. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
