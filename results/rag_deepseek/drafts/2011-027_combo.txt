## Argument

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title Only in Riverbeds Beneath Waters That Were Navigable at Statehood, and Only for Discrete Stretches Proved Navigable.**

Montana entered the Union on November 8, 1889. Under the equal footing doctrine, a State acquires title to lands beneath waters that were navigable at the moment of statehood. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1 (1894); *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926). Title is fixed at that moment; later changes cannot create or divest it. The Constitution therefore requires a two-part inquiry: which river stretches are at issue, and whether each such stretch was navigable in 1889.

**II. This Court Has Always Applied a Segment-by-Segment, Time-Specific Test.**

In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 801 (1931), the Court did not treat the Colorado River as an undifferentiated whole. Instead, it decreed that title to different reaches, measured by mile, vested in different owners. The Court held that one stretch of the Colorado River “is not a navigable river and the title to the bed of said last-mentioned stretch of river is vested in the United States.” *Id.* at 802. That disposition is coherent only if each discrete segment is evaluated independently. Likewise, *United States v. West Virginia*, 295 U.S. 463 (1935), analyzed the navigability of the New and Kanawha Rivers as separate waterways in the context of West Virginia’s admission. And *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377 (1941), confirms that navigability is a factual question about a river’s natural condition as a highway for commerce. That factual question cannot be answered for an entire river without considering each of its reaches separately.

The Montana Supreme Court applied precisely this test. The trial court made findings for each dam site on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers, determining whether that particular stretch was navigable in 1889, and imposed damages only for state-owned beds. The state court reviewed those findings and affirmed. That is exactly what the constitutional rule requires.

**III. PPL’s Contrary Arguments Are Foreclosed by This Court’s Precedents.**

PPL apparently argues that navigability for title must be assessed for the entire river system, and that later improvements or changes can be considered. *United States v. Utah* refutes the first proposition: the Court divided the Colorado and San Juan Rivers into reaches and awarded title accordingly, necessarily rejecting any whole-river approach. As for the second, title is fixed at statehood; neither later improvements nor modern commerce can retroactively make a previously nonnavigable riverbed state property.

PPL may invoke cases such as *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222 (1956), *Federal Power Commission v. Union Electric Co.*, 381 U.S. 90 (1965), and *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121 (1967). Those cases concern the federal navigational servitude and federal licensing jurisdiction, not the equal footing title of a State. They say nothing about whether a riverbed passed to Montana at statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly recognized that those federal authorities do not alter the title test. Nor does *Grand River Dam Authority v. Grand-Hydro*, 335 U.S. 359 (1949), which addressed state water rights, support PPL here.

**IV. The State Court’s Evidentiary Approach Was Necessary and Proper.**

PPL’s remaining complaint is that the State’s historical evidence was hearsay and should have been excluded. But navigability in 1889 cannot be proved except through historical records; no living witness can testify from personal knowledge. The Montana Supreme Court held that the State Archivist’s authentication and the ancient-document, public-records, and periodical exceptions made the materials admissible. That ruling mirrors this Court’s own reliance on early exploration and survey records in *United States v. Utah* and *United States v. West Virginia*. The district court was entitled to weigh those historical sources and make factual findings. Its conclusion that certain stretches were navigable at statehood is a classic factfinding that the state court properly affirmed.

**V. The Time-of-Statehood Rule Is a Constitutional Limit, Not a Procedural Preference.**

The question presented asks whether the constitutional test requires a trial court to determine navigability of the relevant stretch at the time of statehood. The answer is yes. The equal footing doctrine grants States title only to lands beneath waters that were navigable at admission. *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57; *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 55. Any other rule would allow States to acquire property retroactively, contrary to the United States’ retained ownership of nonnavigable beds. The Montana courts faithfully followed that constitutional command by examining each segment of the three rivers and determining its navigable character in 1889. The judgment should be affirmed.
