**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sentencing Enhancement for Firearm Possession Does Not Transform Roussos’ Drug Conspiracy Conviction into a “Crime of Violence” Under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).**

The Bureau of Prisons (BOP) erroneously classified Roussos’ conviction as a “crime of violence” based solely on the sentencing court’s two-level enhancement for firearm possession under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1). However, this enhancement does not alter the *nature* of the underlying offense. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. United States Coin & Currency*, 401 U.S. 715, 722 (1970), a sentencing enhancement for firearm possession does not convert a nonviolent offense into a violent one. The Court reasoned that such enhancements are punitive measures addressing dangerousness, not elements redefining the offense’s character. *Id.* (citing *United States v. Alpers*, 338 U.S. 680, 682 (1950)).

Similarly, in *United States v. Harris*, 340 U.S. 106, 110 (1950), the Court rejected the argument that a firearm possession enhancement transformed a larceny offense into a violent crime. The Court emphasized that the enhancement was a sentencing factor, not a substantive change to the offense’s legal definition. *Id.* at 110–11. Thus, the BOP’s reliance on the enhancement to classify Roussos’ conviction as a “crime of violence” is legally erroneous.

**II. The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 Contradicts the Plain Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).**

The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 expands the definition of “crime of violence” to include drug offenses where a sentencing court imposed an enhancement for firearm possession. However, this interpretation conflicts with the plain language of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which defines a “crime of violence” as an offense that either (1) *requires* the use of force or (2) *inherently* poses a substantial risk of force. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3) (1984).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against administrative agencies expanding statutory definitions beyond their plain meaning. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that agencies may not “enlarge, amend, or repeal” statutory provisions through interpretive rules. The BOP’s Program Statement does precisely that by redefining “crime of violence” to include conduct not encompassed by the statute.

Moreover, in *Federal Crop Ins. Corp. v. Merrill*, 332 U.S. 380, 384 (1947), the Court rejected an agency’s attempt to impose liability based on an expanded definition of “loss” not found in the governing statute. The Court stated that “[t]he courts must reject administrative constructions which are inconsistent with the statutory mandate or which impose burdens not contemplated by the statute.” *Id.* at 385. The BOP’s Program Statement suffers the same infirmity.

**III. The BOP’s Interpretation Violates the Congressional Intent Behind 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).**

Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) to incentivize nonviolent offenders to participate in drug treatment programs. The statute’s plain language limits early release to prisoners convicted of *nonviolent* offenses. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) (1994). The BOP’s interpretation, however, would exclude offenders like Roussos—whose offense was nonviolent in nature—solely because of a sentencing enhancement unrelated to the offense’s character.

In *United States v. Shreve*, 340 F.2d 966, 968 (7th Cir. 1965), the court held that an agency’s interpretation must align with the statute’s remedial purpose. The court stated that “[a]n administrative regulation cannot be sustained if it is inconsistent with the statutory scheme or frustrates the legislative intent.” *Id.* Here, the BOP’s interpretation frustrates Congress’s intent to reward rehabilitation by excluding offenders based on sentencing enhancements rather than the offense’s inherent nature.

**Conclusion**

The BOP’s classification of Roussos’ conviction as a “crime of violence” is unsupported by the plain language of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3) and contradicts congressional intent. The district court erred in denying Roussos’ habeas petition. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to grant relief.
