# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Unitary "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard for Polling Is Not Rationally Related to the Purposes of the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act says nothing about employer polling; the practice is entirely a creature of Board policy. Yet this Court has long recognized that an employer confronted with objective evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support faces a genuine dilemma: it must choose between continuing to bargain with a union that may no longer represent its employees and risking liability for withdrawing recognition improvidently. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969) (discussing the tension between an employer's duty to bargain and its right to test a union's continuing majority status). Polling, no less than a Board-conducted election, serves the Act's central purpose of ensuring that a bargaining representative actually represents the employees for whom it purports to speak. *Cf. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (a bargaining order is justified only where it reflects the employees' actual wishes).

The Board's decision to impose on polling the identical evidentiary threshold required for outright withdrawal of recognition—rather than a lesser showing calibrated to the far less drastic nature of a poll—cannot be reconciled with reasoned administrative decisionmaking. An agency rule is entitled to deference only where "there is a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962). The Board's own stated premise—that Board-supervised elections are preferable to polling because they carry procedural safeguards polling lacks—points toward a *lower*, not identical, evidentiary threshold for the comparatively modest step of polling. An agency's reasoning must be internally coherent, for "the orderly functioning of the process of review requires that the grounds upon which the administrative agency acted be clearly disclosed and adequately sustained." *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943). Collapsing three distinct employer options—withdrawal of recognition, a Board election, and an internal poll—into a single undifferentiated standard, without accounting for their plainly different consequences for employees and for the bargaining relationship, is precisely the sort of unreasoned equivalence *Chenery* forbids.

This Court has likewise insisted that the Board's policymaking discretion, though broad, must accommodate the competing statutory interests at stake rather than mechanically apply a single rule across dissimilar circumstances. *See Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945). And the Board's remedial and procedural choices must serve the purposes of the Act, not administrative convenience alone. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941). By declaring elections categorically preferable to polling while nonetheless applying an identical evidentiary predicate to both, the Board has adopted a policy unmoored from its own stated rationale.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt about the Union's continuing majority status cannot stand. Review of Board factfinding is not toothless. Congress intended that reviewing courts "assume more responsibility for the reasonableness and fairness of Labor Board decisions than some courts had shown in the past," and directed that "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). A reviewing court may not treat the Board's chosen inference as conclusive merely because it is *an* available inference; the inference selected must itself be reasonable when the record is viewed as a whole. *Id.* at 490.

The Board's treatment of the record here reflects exactly the one-sided factfinding *Universal Camera* condemns. The ALJ and the Board discounted, one by one, every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown—dismissing employee statements as equivocal, refusing to credit reports of coworker sentiment because the coworkers themselves did not testify, and excluding from the count employees no longer on the payroll—while crediting nothing that corroborated the reasonableness of Allentown's doubt. This selective winnowing mirrors the error this Court identified in *NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 291–92 (1965), where the Board's inference of unlawful motive was not "a reasonable inference from the evidence" but a predetermined conclusion imposed on ambiguous facts. As in *Brown*, the Board here approached the record with a thumb on the scale, construing every ambiguous statement against the employer rather than weighing the evidence even-handedly.

Nor can the Board's approach be defended as ordinary credibility assessment entitled to deference. Where the Board applies a categorical rule—discounting entire classes of evidence without individualized assessment—it has not performed the weighing *Universal Camera* requires; it has instead erected a standard no employer's evidence could realistically satisfy. *See NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963) (Board findings must rest on the actual dynamics of the record). The cumulative, largely uncontradicted testimony here—far exceeding the Board's crabbed count of seven employees—demonstrates that Allentown's doubt was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
