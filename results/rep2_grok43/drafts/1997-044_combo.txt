ARGUMENT

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, like its predecessors, embodies the fundamental policy that a discharge frees the honest debtor from all prior obligations to afford a fresh start. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (discharge gives debtor “a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt”). Exceptions to discharge must therefore be confined to their precise terms. Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915); Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915).

Section 17(a)(2) of the 1898 Act excepted only “liabilities for obtaining money or property by false pretenses or false representations.” 30 Stat. 550. This Court repeatedly held that the exception reaches solely the sum actually obtained by the fraud. Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904); Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877). Additional liabilities, whether imposed by statute or judgment, fall outside the exception. Strang v. Bradner, 114 U.S. 555, 559 (1885).

The same limitation applies to punitive or exemplary damages. In Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473 (1904), the Court explained that the discharge exception for willful and malicious injuries did not extend to every consequence of the tort, but only to the injury itself. Id. at 485. Likewise, McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138, 141 (1916), confined the exception to the debt “created by” the fraud, not to later statutory multipliers.

Friend v. Talcott, 228 U.S. 27 (1913), confirms that a judgment for fraud does not enlarge the nondischargeable debt beyond the amount obtained. The Court there refused to treat the entire judgment as excepted, holding that only the sum traceable to the fraudulent procurement survived discharge. Id. at 38.

The treble damages and fees awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not part of the “money … obtained by” fraud within the meaning of the statute. They are a statutory penalty imposed after the fact. Because the 1898 Act and its judicial construction limited the exception to the sum actually received, the additional award remains dischargeable. Ames v. Moir, 138 U.S. 306, 311 (1891); In re Marshall, 266 F. 665, 667 (D. Mass. 1920).

The courts below therefore erred in holding the entire judgment nondischargeable. The discharge must issue as to all amounts beyond the $31,382.50 actually obtained.
