**ARGUMENT**

I. THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PROVE THE MENS REA REQUIRED UNDER STAPLES V. UNITED STATES, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), FOR THE MACHINE GUN CONVICTION.

The government’s evidence was legally insufficient to establish that Belen Nieves-Castaño possessed the requisite *mens rea* for conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the statute prohibiting unlawful possession of a machine gun. This court must reverse the conviction because the government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm in question was a machine gun—a requirement firmly established by *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602, 114 S. Ct. 1793, 128 L. Ed. 2d 608 (1994).

In *Staples*, the Supreme Court held that the government must prove that the defendant *knew* the firearm was a machine gun. The Court emphasized that “the statute does not define the term ‘machinegun’ in terms of the defendant’s knowledge or intent,” but that “the absence of a mens rea requirement in the statute does not mean that the Government need not prove knowledge.” Id. at 608. The Court concluded that “the Government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machinegun,” because “the statute’s penalty is so severe that it would be unjust to impose it without proof of knowledge.” Id. at 609.

Here, the government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun. The only evidence regarding her knowledge was her statement to an agent that she had “once opened the bag, looked in, and observed that there was a rifle in it, and that she knew it was an AK-47.” This statement, admitted under the hearsay exception for statements made to law enforcement, establishes only that she knew the weapon was an AK-47 rifle—*not* that she knew it was a machine gun.

The distinction is critical. An AK-47 is a semi-automatic rifle, not a machine gun, under federal law. A “machine gun” is defined as “any firearm which can fire automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger.” 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(16). The AK-47, as a semi-automatic weapon, does not meet this definition. The government did not introduce any evidence—expert testimony, forensic analysis, or otherwise—that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon had been modified to fire automatically, or that she was aware of any such modification. Indeed, the government did not even attempt to prove that the weapon was, in fact, a machine gun at the time of the offense.

The government’s failure to prove knowledge of the weapon’s classification as a machine gun renders the conviction legally unsustainable. As the Supreme Court made clear in *Staples*, “the Government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machinegun.” 511 U.S. at 609. Without such proof, the conviction violates due process. The jury was not instructed on the *mens rea* requirement, and the trial court failed to sua sponte correct the omission. This error was not harmless. The government’s entire case rested on Nieves-Castaño’s statement that she knew it was an AK-47—information that, by itself, does not establish knowledge of the weapon’s classification as a machine gun.

This court has previously recognized that “a conviction cannot stand if the government fails to prove the essential elements of the offense, including the required mental state.” *United States v. Rivera-Rivera*, 472 F.3d 1, 10 (1st Cir. 2006). Here, the government failed to prove the *mens rea* element required by *Staples*. The evidence was insufficient as a matter of law to support the conviction under § 922(o).

Accordingly, the conviction for unlawful possession of a machine gun must be reversed. The judgment of the district court should be vacated, and the case remanded for entry of judgment of acquittal on that count.

*Staples*, 511 U.S. at 609, stands for the proposition that “the Government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machinegun.” The government did not do so here. The conviction cannot stand.
