**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental conflict in the interpretation of OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that California’s wage and hour laws apply on the Outer Continental Shelf so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute’s text, disregards binding precedent, and undermines Congress’s intent to create a comprehensive federal regime for offshore labor. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY APPLY STATE LAW—IT REQUIRES A GAP IN FEDERAL LAW

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a broad, open-ended mandate to apply state law whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law ignores the statute’s foundational purpose: to fill gaps in federal law, not to supplant it. The provision states that state laws “are declared to be the law of the United States” *to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations*. (Emphasis added.) This language does not create a default rule of state law application; rather, it functions as a *gap-filling mechanism*, designed to ensure that the OCS is not left without a legal framework when federal law is silent or inadequate.

This reading is confirmed by the legislative history. When Congress enacted the OCSLA in 1953, it explicitly rejected the idea of incorporating all state law into federal jurisdiction. As the Senate Report explained, Congress feared that “federal law, standing alone, would be inadequate because it was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). But Congress did not intend for state law to *supplement* federal law in every instance—it intended for state law to *fill voids* where federal law was absent. The 1975 amendment, which expanded the scope of “state law” to include laws “hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed,” did not alter this purpose. It merely ensured that the OCS would be governed by the same legal regime as deepwater ports, not that state law would automatically displace federal law.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision is not a blank check for state law. In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366 (1969), the Court held that Louisiana’s wrongful death law applied because the federal High Seas Act did not provide a remedy for deaths occurring on fixed platforms—“an obstacle to the application of state law” had been removed. The Court did not apply Louisiana law simply because it was “applicable and not inconsistent”; it applied it because *no federal remedy existed*. The same principle governs here. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive, detailed framework for wage and hour protections, including a savings clause that expressly permits more protective state laws. 29 U.S.C. § 218. But the FLSA is not silent—it is *complete*. It establishes minimum standards, defines overtime, and provides enforcement mechanisms. Where federal law is comprehensive, there is no “gap” to fill, and thus no basis for applying state law under OCSLA.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION CONTRADICTS BINDING PRECEDENT

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case did not hold that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. Rather, it established a clear rule: state law may be applied on the OCS only when federal law is “inadequate or silent,” and “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” is required. Id. at 1036. The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this test in subsequent cases involving OCS claims, including *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), which held that state law applies only when federal law does not provide a remedy. The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of this well-established test—without meaningful justification—creates a circuit split and undermines the uniformity Congress sought to achieve.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has never endorsed the notion that state law automatically applies on the OCS whenever it is not inconsistent with federal law. To the contrary, in *Huson v. United States*, 404 U.S. 94 (1971), the Court declined to apply Louisiana law to a worker’s claim because the FLSA already provided a federal remedy. The Court emphasized that “the federal law is not merely a minimum standard, but a comprehensive scheme.” Id. at 100. The same is true here. The FLSA governs wage and hour claims on the OCS through the OCSLA’s extension of the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), and the Department of Labor’s regulations. These regulations define “workweek,” “hours worked,” and “meal periods” with precision. See 29 C.F.R. § 778.110. To allow California law to override these federal standards—especially when the FLSA already permits more protective state laws—would render the federal scheme superfluous.

### III. THE FLSA’S SAVINGS CLAUSE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE STATE LAW TO SUPPLANT FEDERAL LAW

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that California’s wage and hour laws are “not inconsistent” with the FLSA because the FLSA contains a savings clause is legally flawed. The savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—permits states to enact laws that provide *greater* protections than the FLSA. But it does not permit states to *replace* the FLSA’s comprehensive framework.
