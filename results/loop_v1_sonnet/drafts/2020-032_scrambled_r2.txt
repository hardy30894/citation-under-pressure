# ARGUMENT

## I. A Claim for Nominal Damages Does Not, by Itself, Present the "Definite and Concrete" Controversy That Article III Demands.

The judicial power of the federal courts extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has consistently refused to adjudicate disputes that have lost their live, adversarial character. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911) (holding that the judicial power extends only to "actual controversies arising between adverse litigants, duly instituted in courts of proper jurisdiction," and not to suits designed merely to test the validity of legislation in the abstract). This limitation is not a modern innovation. In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), this Court held that "[t]he duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is to decide actual controversies by a judgment which can be carried into effect, and not to give opinions upon moot questions or abstract propositions, or to declare principles or rules of law which cannot affect the matter in issue in the case before it." *Id.* at 314. Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged provision of the Student Code of Conduct, no live dispute remained over the validity of those provisions; petitioners no longer face, and cannot show any reasonable prospect of again facing, enforcement of rules that no longer exist. A bare demand for a nominal sum, untethered to any pleaded actual injury, does not resurrect that extinguished controversy; it identifies no continuing relationship between the parties and calls for no "judgment which can be carried into effect" beyond an advisory pronouncement. *Id.*

This Court's decision in *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), confirms the point. There, this Court dismissed an appeal because intervening events had made it "impossible for the court to grant any effectual relief whatever," reasoning that a court "will not proceed to a formal judgment" once the underlying controversy has been extinguished by events outside the litigation. *Id.* at 653. Where, as here, the policy that gave rise to the dispute has been repealed and superseded, and where petitioners identify no actual, continuing injury flowing from that repealed policy, there is no controversy left for a court to determine through effectual relief.

## II. Nashville, C. & St. L. Railway v. Wallace Confirms That an "Actual Controversy" Remains the Touchstone Even for Claims Seeking Only Declaratory or Nominal Relief.

This Court's declaratory-judgment jurisprudence likewise requires that the dispute presented remain a genuine, live controversy between adverse parties rather than a request to settle a legal question in the abstract. *Nashville, Chattanooga & St. Louis Railway v. Wallace*, 288 U.S. 249 (1933). A residual request for a token sum, disconnected from any pleaded injury and from any policy still in force, does not supply the concrete adverseness that this requirement contemplates. Petitioners' claim for nominal damages is precisely such a residual, insubstantial vestige of a dispute that has otherwise been resolved by GGC's voluntary repeal of the challenged provisions.

## III. The Voluntary Revision of the Challenged Policy Mooted This Case, and Petitioners Have Identified No Reasonable Expectation That the Same Controversy Will Recur.

This Court has long distinguished between disputes that are "capable of repetition, yet evading review," which survive voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct, and disputes that present no such danger of recurrence. *Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911). This case falls into the latter category. GGC's repeal of the Prior Policies was complete: the offending speech-zone requirement was eliminated and the challenged Student Code provision was removed outright. This Court's decision in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953), teaches that voluntary cessation of a challenged practice may moot a controversy where the party defending the practice demonstrates that the practice is not reasonably likely to recur. *Id.* at 633. That showing is amply supported here, and petitioners identify nothing in the record suggesting GGC intends, or has any reason, to revive the rescinded provisions.

By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court found no mootness because a genuine, substantial monetary dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction project—an interest bearing directly on a real economic stake between the parties. *Id.* at 306 & n.3. Nominal damages, unmoored from any pleaded actual injury, present no comparable stake; they are the mirror image of the substantial cost dispute preserved in *Liner*, not its equivalent. And this Court's practice in *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36 (1950), of vacating judgments that have become moot pending resolution of the litigation confirms that the mere technical survival of a claim on the docket does not, by itself, preserve a live controversy for adjudication. *Id.* at 39.

## Conclusion

Because the Prior Policies no longer exist, because petitioners allege no actual injury, and because a request for a token sum cannot substitute for the "actual controvers[y]" that Article III requires, *Muskrat*, 219 U.S. at 356; *San Pablo*, 149 U.S. at 314, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held this case moot.
