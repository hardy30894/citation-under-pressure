**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) adopts state law as surrogate federal law only to fill gaps in federal law—not whenever state law is merely “applicable and not inconsistent.” The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading rewrites the statute and conflicts with this Court’s precedent.

**I. OCSLA’s Text and Purpose Require a Gap-Filling Role for State Law**

OCSLA extends federal law to the outer Continental Shelf “to the same extent as if [it] were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law is then incorporated only “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting qualifier. It means state law applies only when federal law is incomplete—i.e., when there is a “gap” that state law can fill without conflicting with federal law.

Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The statute thus borrows state law to supply rules where federal law is silent. But where federal law already provides a comprehensive scheme, no gap exists, and state law cannot apply. This Court recognized as much in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), which held that OCSLA “remove[s] any obstacle to the application of state law” only when federal law is unavailable. There, the Death on the High Seas Act did not cover fixed-platform accidents, so state wrongful-death law filled the void. *Id.* at 366. The Court did not suggest that state law could apply whenever it was merely “not inconsistent” with federal law.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s “Significant Void or Gap” Test Is Correct**

The Fifth Circuit has long applied OCSLA to require a “significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be adopted. *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). That test flows directly from the statute’s text and purpose. It ensures that state law does not displace comprehensive federal schemes. This Court has never rejected that approach, and it should endorse it here.

**III. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) establishes a detailed, nationwide framework for minimum wages, overtime, and recordkeeping. *Overnight Motor Transp. Co. v. Missel*, 316 U.S. 572, 577 (1942). It defines “regular rate,” *Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc.*, 323 U.S. 37, 40 (1944); prescribes overtime pay, *Walling v. A. H. Belo Corp.*, 316 U.S. 624, 630 (1942); and governs piece-rate compensation, *Walling v. Youngerman-Reynolds Hardwood Co.*, 325 U.S. 419, 424 (1945). The FLSA’s regulations further elaborate these requirements. *See, e.g., Walling v. Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co.*, 331 U.S. 17, 21 (1947) (approving DOL regulations). The Act covers employees “engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce,” *Warren-Bradshaw Drilling Co. v. Hall*, 317 U.S. 88, 90 (1942), which includes platform workers like Newton. Because the FLSA provides a complete set of wage-and-hour rules, there is no “gap” for state law to fill.

**IV. The FLSA’s Savings Clause Does Not Create a Gap on the OCS**

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws *within the states’ territorial jurisdiction*. But the OCS is not within any state’s territory; it is an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction. *See United States v. Townsley*, 323 U.S. 557, 560 (1945) (federal enclaves are subject to federal law alone). The savings clause does not authorize states to project their laws onto federal territory. To hold otherwise would allow each adjacent state to impose its own wage-and-hour regime on the OCS, undermining the uniformity that OCSLA was designed to achieve. *See 149 Madison Ave. Corp. v. Asselta*, 331 U.S. 199, 204 (1947) (FLSA’s purpose is uniform national standards).

**V. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Render OCSLA’s Limiting Language Superfluous**

If state law applied whenever it was “not inconsistent” with federal law, the phrase “to the extent that” would be meaningless. Congress could simply have said “applicable and not inconsistent state law shall apply.” It did not. This Court must give effect to every word. *Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co.*, 375 U.S. 34, 36 (1963) (per curiam) (statutes should be construed to avoid surplusage). The Ninth Circuit’s reading would also conflict with *Rodrigue*, which applied state law only because federal law was *absent*—not merely because it was not inconsistent.

**Conclusion**

The OCSLA permits state law only when federal law leaves a gap. The FLSA leaves no gap for wage-and-hour claims. The judgment below should be reversed.
