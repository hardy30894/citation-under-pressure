The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment on four of the seven calls placed from the 8047 number. The TCPA makes it unlawful “to make any call . . . using an artificial or prerecorded voice to deliver a message” without the called party’s prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). Liability therefore turns on whether a prerecorded voice was in fact used during the call. See, e.g., Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster, Inc., 569 F.3d 946, 953 (9th Cir. 2009) (holding that the statute requires the actual employment of the prohibited technology to deliver the message).

The undisputed record establishes that the Cisco Dialer plays its prerecorded message only upon detection of a “positive voice.” Calls 2, 3, and 4 reached an answering machine; Call 5 produced a data error. In each instance the device did not detect a positive voice and therefore did not play any prerecorded message. Because no prerecorded voice was used, these four calls fall outside the TCPA’s prohibition.

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that DISH’s admission of using the Cisco Dialer on the 8047 line established a violation “regardless of whether Ybarra triggered it with a positive voice”—rests on an erroneous reading of the statute. The admission concerns only the device’s capability, not its actual operation on any given call. Where the record affirmatively shows that the prerecorded component was never activated, summary judgment for the plaintiff is improper. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986).

DISH concedes liability on the three calls that detected a positive voice. As to the remaining four, however, the judgment must be reversed.
