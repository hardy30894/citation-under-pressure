**ARGUMENT**

I. **The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Improper Vouching and Legal Conclusions from Government Witnesses.**

A new trial is required when the erroneous admission of evidence “affect[s] a substantial right,” Fed. R. Evid. 103(a), and there is a “reasonable possibility that the improperly admitted evidence contributed to the verdict.” *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946); *United States v. Kaplan*, 490 F.3d 110, 122 (2d Cir. 2007). The challenged testimony here went well beyond permissible fact testimony. Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant were allowed to tell the jury, in substance, that Officers Ramos and Rivera were credible, that probable cause existed to arrest and prosecute Appellants, and that evidence favored Appellees’ account. That was error.

First, witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses. The Second Circuit has repeatedly held that credibility determinations belong to the jury, and that a witness may not place the prestige of her office behind another witness’s testimony. *United States v. Arroyo-Angulo*, 580 F.2d 1137, 1146 (2d Cir. 1978); *United States v. Richter*, 826 F.2d 206, 208 (2d Cir. 1987). That rule is especially important when the vouching comes from law-enforcement or prosecutorial officials, whose apparent neutrality can carry undue weight. *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1985). Here, government attorneys and a police lieutenant effectively told the jury that Ramos and Rivera were telling the truth. That is precisely the sort of credibility bolstering the law forbids.

Second, a witness may not offer legal conclusions on issues reserved for the jury. In this Circuit, testimony that “states a legal conclusion” is inadmissible because it usurps the court’s role in instructing the jury and the jury’s role in applying the law to the facts. *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 139-40 (2d Cir. 1988), vacated on other grounds, 490 U.S. 1016 (1989); *Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 363-64 (2d Cir. 1992). The line was crossed here when government witnesses were permitted to opine that probable cause existed and that the officers’ conduct was justified. Those opinions were not mere shorthand descriptions of facts; they were legal judgments on the ultimate issues in the case.

Third, witnesses may not interpret evidence for the jury where jurors are just as capable of drawing their own conclusions. *United States v. Kaplan*, 490 F.3d at 122; *United States v. Grinage*, 390 F.3d 746, 748-50 (2d Cir. 2004). The government witnesses here commented on whether particular evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants’ claims, again telling jurors how to decide disputed facts. The admission of such testimony was prejudicial because it came from officials who appeared disinterested and authoritative.

The error was not harmless. The central issues at trial were whether Ramos and Rivera fabricated their account, whether they had probable cause, and whether Cameron was prosecuted maliciously. The improper testimony went directly to those issues. Where erroneous evidence bears on the key disputed facts and likely influenced the verdict, a new trial is required. *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 765; *Crigger v. Fahnestock & Co.*, 443 F.3d 230, 238-39 (2d Cir. 2006). 

II. **The District Court Properly Denied Judgment as a Matter of Law Because the Photographs Did Not “Blatantly Contradict” Appellees’ Version.**

Judgment as a matter of law is warranted only when “a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis” to find for the nonmovant. Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a); *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000). Appellants invoke *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007), but that case applies only where objective evidence “blatantly contradict[s]” the nonmovant’s account so that no reasonable jury could believe it. The photographs here did not do that. They were consistent with competing inferences and did not conclusively establish that Cameron never reached into the car or that Higgenbottom did not interfere. The District Court correctly left the dispute to the jury. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150; *Scott*, 550 U.S. at 380-81.

III. **The Jury Should Have Been Instructed That Obstructing Governmental Administration Requires a Lawful Arrest.**

Under New York law, obstructing governmental administration requires obstruction of an officer “in effecting an authorized act.” N.Y. Penal Law § 195.05; *People v. Case*, 42 N.Y.2d 98, 101-02, 396 N.Y.S.2d 841, 365 N.E.2d 872 (1977). Because a lawful arrest is an “authorized act,” unlawfulness defeats the charge. *People v. Romeo*, 9 N.Y.3d 51, 54-55, 844 N.Y.S.2d 574, 876 N.E.2d 1065 (2007). The requested instruction correctly stated New York law and was necessary to prevent the jury from finding liability on an unlawful arrest theory. The failure to so instruct was error.

IV. **A Punitive-Damages Instruction Was Warranted.**

Punitive damages are available under § 1983 when a defendant acts with “evil motive or intent” or with “reckless or callous indifference” to federally protected rights. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983); *Lee v. Edwards*, 101 F.3d 805, 809 (2d Cir. 1996). Appellants’ evidence—that the officers knowingly fabricated the encounter and pursued false charges—would have permitted a reasonable jury to find the requisite malice. See *King v. Macri*, 993 F.2d 294, 297-98 (2d Cir. 1993). The punitive-damages instruction should therefore be given on retrial.
