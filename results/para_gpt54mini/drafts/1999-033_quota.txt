**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not require a plaintiff to plead membership in a traditionally defined class to obtain judicial review of arbitrary, irrational government discrimination. This Court has repeatedly recognized that equal protection “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). That principle is not limited to race, alienage, or other suspect classifications; it applies whenever the government intentionally singles out one person for disfavored treatment without a rational basis. *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 112–13 (1949); *Bickel v. Burkhardt*, 632 F.2d 1251, 1255 (5th Cir. 1980).

This Court’s precedents confirm that a “class of one” claim is cognizable. In *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000), the Court held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that she has been “intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that there is no rational basis for the difference in treatment.” *Id.* at 564. The Court did not require class membership, group-based discrimination, or animus toward an identifiable social category. Rather, the constitutional wrong lay in the government’s singling out one homeowner for more onerous treatment than others similarly situated. *Id.* That rule follows naturally from *Cleburne* and *Railway Express*, which both rejected arbitrary distinctions unsupported by legitimate governmental purpose. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 446–50; *Railway Express*, 336 U.S. at 110–15.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary limitation cannot be squared with this Court’s equal protection jurisprudence. The court below treated spiteful or malicious discrimination as the only actionable theory absent class membership. But this Court has long held that irrational singling out itself violates equal protection. In *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission of Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336, 345–46 (1989), the Court invalidated a property tax assessment scheme that produced grossly unequal treatment of comparable properties. The constitutional violation was not tied to group status; it was the “intentional systematic undervaluation” of one taxpayer’s property compared with others. *Id.* at 346. Likewise, in *Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield*, 247 U.S. 350, 352–53 (1918), the Court recognized that purposeful and arbitrary discrimination in taxation would offend equal protection even where no suspect class was involved.

The same principle appears in the Court’s public-employment cases. In *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 602–03 (2008), the Court distinguished the general rule that equal protection protects individuals from arbitrary state action and explained that “class of one” claims are viable outside the discretionary public-employment setting. *Id.* at 603–04. *Engquist* thus confirms—not undermines—that where government action is governed by objective standards, an individual may invoke the Equal Protection Clause without alleging class membership. *Id.* at 605–07.

The government’s position also conflicts with the line of cases allowing equal protection challenges to arbitrary administrative distinctions in contexts far removed from suspect classifications. In *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445–47 (1923), the Court held that equal protection was violated where one taxpayer was deliberately assessed at full value while others were systematically underassessed. In *Armour v. City of Indianapolis*, 566 U.S. 673, 680 (2012), the Court reiterated that the Clause permits review of differential treatment under rational-basis scrutiny, but only where the government’s line-drawing is rational. And in *Morrison v. Board of Education*, 461 F.2d 184, 189–90 (2d Cir. 1972), the court recognized that the Constitution forbids “invidious” selective treatment even absent class-based discrimination. These authorities reflect the settled proposition that equal protection bars arbitrary government favoritism and hostility toward an individual.

Here, Olech alleged precisely the kind of individualized, irrational discrimination the Equal Protection Clause forbids. She claimed that Willowbrook demanded a 33-foot easement from her while requiring only 15-foot easements from other similarly situated property owners, and that the Village’s demand lacked any rational basis. Those allegations fit comfortably within *Olech* itself and require no additional allegation of membership in a class. *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000). To hold otherwise would create an irrational gap in constitutional protection: a municipality could intentionally impose burdensome conditions on a single citizen for no reason at all, so long as the citizen could not identify a group-based animus. The Equal Protection Clause does not tolerate that result. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 446; *Allegheny Pittsburgh*, 488 U.S. at 346; *Sioux City Bridge*, 260 U.S. at 445–47.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed. Under this Court’s precedents, a plaintiff need not plead class membership where she alleges intentional differential treatment from others similarly situated and the absence of any rational basis. That is enough to state an equal protection claim. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564.
