**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Language of the FDCPA Establishes That a Debt Purchaser Collecting for Its Own Account Is a Creditor, Not a Debt Collector**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act defines a “debt collector” as any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The word “another” is the critical term. It means a person other than the collector itself. When Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, it became the owner of those debts. It thereafter collected for its own account, not for “another.” A person collecting its own debts is a creditor, not a debt collector. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (defining “creditor” as “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed”).

This distinction between collecting for oneself and collecting for another is fundamental. The FDCPA was enacted to regulate third-party debt collectors who act as agents for creditors, not creditors who collect their own debts. *See* S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977) (explaining that the term “debt collector” covers “all third persons who regularly collect debts for others”). The statute’s structure confirms this: the definition of “debt collector” in § 1692a(6) contains two principal categories—persons whose principal business is debt collection, and persons who regularly collect debts “owed or due *another*.” The second category plainly requires that the debt be owed to someone else at the time of collection.

**II. The Default Status of a Debt Is Irrelevant to Whether a Purchaser Is a Debt Collector**

Petitioners argue that because the debts were in default when Santander purchased them, Santander must be a debt collector. This argument misreads the statute. The FDCPA does not define “debt collector” by reference to when a debt went into default. Instead, the default status of a debt appears only in an *exclusion* from the definition of debt collector—specifically, § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the term “debt collector” any person collecting a debt “which was not in default at the time it was obtained.” That exclusion applies only to persons who *otherwise* meet the definition of debt collector. It does not create an affirmative rule that purchasing defaulted debt automatically makes one a debt collector. *See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015) (explaining that the exclusions in § 1692a(6)(A)-(F) come into play only if a person first satisfies one of the definitions in the main text).

Petitioners’ reliance on the definition of “creditor” in § 1692a(4) is equally misplaced. That definition excludes from the term “creditor” any person who receives an assignment of a debt in default “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (emphasis added). Santander did not purchase the debts “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection *for another*.” It purchased the debts for its own account. The exclusion therefore does not apply.

**III. The FDCPA’s Purpose Does Not Support Petitioners’ Interpretation**

The FDCPA’s stated purpose is “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Congress did not intend to regulate creditors who collect their own debts, even if those debts were originally owed to another. *See Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (noting that the Act’s definition of “debt collector” includes a person who regularly collects debts owed to “another”). A contrary rule would sweep within the FDCPA every bank, finance company, or other entity that purchases defaulted debt and attempts to collect it—even though such entities are acting as creditors, not third-party collectors. This would expand the statute far beyond its intended scope.

**IV. The Complaint Fails to Allege That Santander Was Collecting for Another**

The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the plaintiffs’ loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, and thereafter attempted to collect on those loans for its own account. It does not allege that Santander was collecting on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto or any other entity at the time of the challenged conduct. Because Santander was collecting its own debts, it was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector. The district court correctly dismissed the complaint for failure to state a claim.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
