Arizona’s rule is consistent with due process. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a State to let a defendant use psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea when the State has chosen to channel mental-health proof into an insanity defense. The Constitution requires that the prosecution prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but it does not prescribe the evidentiary form in which a State must allow a defendant to rebut that proof. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

This Court has long approved state choices to allocate mental-state issues to insanity doctrine. In Davis, the Court held that, once insanity is in issue, the government must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt in federal prosecutions, 160 U.S. at 484-85, but Davis did not constitutionalize a separate diminished-capacity defense. To the contrary, Davis reflects that the law of mental responsibility may be structured by the sovereign that defines the offense. See Salinger v. United States, 272 U.S. 542, 548-49 (1926). That holding is decisive here: if due process permits a State to place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it necessarily permits a State to confine psychiatric proof to that defense rather than permit it also to operate as a partial negation of intent.

Petitioner’s theory would constitutionalize a broader mental-state defense than this Court has ever required. But the Court has repeatedly said that the Constitution does not mandate every rule a defendant might prefer for introducing evidence. In Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), the Court invalidated a state rule that arbitrarily barred a defendant from calling an accomplice witness, because it prevented the accused from presenting “the testimony of witnesses to establish a defense.” Id. at 19. Yet Washington was about a categorical exclusion of a witness, not about a State’s decision to limit the legal use of a category of evidence that is otherwise admitted. The relevant pre-1970 cases do not support the proposition that due process compels admission of expert testimony for whatever inferential purpose the defense desires.

Nor has this Court ever held that due process forbids a State from channeling psychiatric evidence to insanity when that channel still permits the defendant to litigate criminal responsibility. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), addressed competency to stand trial, not evidentiary use at trial. And in Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court required a competency hearing when the evidence raised a bona fide doubt about competence. Those cases protect the ability to stand trial and the fairness of proceedings; they do not create a separate constitutional entitlement to a diminished-capacity defense.

Arizona’s rule also comports with the traditional common-law distinction between justification, excuse, and element-negating proof. The State proved the elements of the homicide offense, including the requisite intent, through ordinary evidence of Clark’s conduct and the circumstances of the shooting. Arizona merely declined to allow expert psychiatric testimony to be used for a purpose the State has chosen not to recognize: partial exculpation short of insanity. That policy choice is constitutionally permissible. See Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946) (recognizing that the “administration of justice” remains a proper concern of the courts); cf. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-06 (1934) (due process is violated only by procedures that offend “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental”).

Finally, petitioner’s rule would unsettle the States’ long-settled authority to define and administer insanity. The Court in Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910), rejected the argument that evidence of mental condition automatically negates criminal liability, and the pre-1970 decisions have never held that a defendant has a standalone due process right to present expert mental-state evidence to dispute intent apart from the insanity defense. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
