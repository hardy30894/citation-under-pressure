**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners’ claim that the law violates their First Amendment rights fails under settled precedent established long before 1970.

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT PERMITS CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATIONS THAT PROTECT THE RIGHT TO ENTER HEALTHCARE FACILITIES.**

The First Amendment does not prohibit reasonable regulations that preserve the public’s access to essential services, including reproductive healthcare. In *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972), the Court held that “the right to speak does not include the right to speak at all times and places or in any manner.” This principle has long been recognized: the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech so long as the regulation is content-neutral, serves a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *See, e.g., Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576–77 (1941) (upholding a permit requirement for parades where the purpose was to “preserve the peace and order of the community”); *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (recognizing that “the right of assembly is not absolute” and may be regulated to prevent disorder).

The Massachusetts buffer zone law satisfies all three prongs of this test. The law is content-neutral: it applies equally to all individuals regardless of the message they convey. It does not distinguish between pro-life and pro-choice speakers, nor does it target any particular viewpoint. *See, e.g., Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 162 (1939) (holding that a law regulating signs is valid if it “does not discriminate between different types of speech”); *Bolton v. North Carolina*, 305 U.S. 281, 285 (1938) (upholding a law regulating public meetings that applied equally to all groups).

The law serves a significant governmental interest: the protection of the right of patients and employees to enter and exit abortion clinics safely and without harassment. This interest is no less compelling than the interest in maintaining public order during parades or meetings. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 577 (recognizing that “the preservation of public order and safety” is a legitimate governmental interest); *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. at 515 (upholding regulation of public meetings to prevent “disorder and violence”).

Finally, the law leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners may speak, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and wear symbolic garments from outside the buffer zone. *See, e.g., Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. at 163 (upholding a law that allowed “the use of loudspeakers” and “the distribution of handbills” as alternative means of expression). The First Circuit correctly found that the law does not “impede their ability to communicate effectively” in any constitutionally significant way. *See, e.g., Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 577 (holding that “the right to speak does not include the right to speak at all times and places”).

**II. THE LAW IS NOT A PRIOR RESTRAINT ON SPEECH.**

The petitioners argue that the buffer zone law constitutes a prior restraint. This argument fails. A prior restraint is a government action that prohibits speech before it occurs. *See, e.g., Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697, 716 (1931) (defining prior restraint as “a censorship of expression before publication”). The Massachusetts law does not prohibit speech; it merely restricts where speech may occur. It does not require any individual to obtain a permit or submit their message for approval. *See, e.g., Grosjean v. American Press Co.*, 297 U.S. 233, 244 (1936) (holding that a law requiring prior approval of newspapers was a prior restraint); *Patterson v. Colorado*, 205 U.S. 454, 462 (1907) (upholding a law that regulated the time and place of speech but did not require prior approval).

The law is not a prior restraint because it does not prevent the petitioners from speaking at all. They may speak from outside the buffer zone, and they may distribute literature and display signs. The law merely prevents them from approaching individuals as they enter or exit the clinic. This is a classic time, place, and manner regulation, not a prior restraint.

**III. THE LAW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT’S DUE PROCESS OR EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSES.**

The petitioners’ claim that the law violates due process or equal protection is equally without merit. The law applies equally to all individuals and does not discriminate on the basis of race, religion, or political belief. *See, e.g., Skinner v. Oklahoma ex rel. Williamson*, 316 U.S. 535, 541 (1942) (holding that “the right to be free from arbitrary governmental action” is protected by the Due Process Clause); *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537, 559 (1896) (upholding a law that applied equally to all races, even if it had discriminatory effects).
