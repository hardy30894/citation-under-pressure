The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law to disputes arising on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state wage and hour laws are adopted as surrogate federal law on the OCS as long as they are "applicable and not inconsistent" with existing federal law, regardless of whether there is a gap in federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the OCS "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. However, this provision must be read in light of the legislative history and the Supreme Court's interpretation of the Act. The OCSLA was enacted to provide a body of law applicable to the seabed, subsoil, and fixed structures on the OCS, Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), and Congress intended for federal law to be the primary source of law on the OCS.

The Supreme Court has recognized that the OCSLA's incorporation of state law is not unlimited. In Rodrigue, the Court held that state law applies on the OCS only when there is no applicable federal law. 395 U.S. at 357-58. The Court's decision in Rodrigue was based on the understanding that the OCSLA was intended to provide a comprehensive federal law framework for the OCS, with state law filling gaps where necessary.

The Fifth Circuit has also interpreted the OCSLA to require a gap in federal law before state law can be applied. In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steamship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the OCS only when there is a "significant void or gap" in federal law. This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue and the legislative history of the OCSLA.

The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour disputes, and it provides a remedy for employees like Newton. See Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel, 316 U.S. 572 (1942); Warren-Bradshaw Drilling Co. v. Hall, 317 U.S. 88 (1942). The FLSA's savings clause allows for more protective state laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but this does not mean that state law can be applied on the OCS in the absence of a gap in federal law.

Other Supreme Court decisions have also recognized the importance of federal law in governing disputes on the OCS. In Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co., 375 U.S. 34 (1963), the Court held that the Longshoremen's and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, as applied through the OCSLA, provides a federal remedy for injuries sustained on the OCS. Similarly, in United States v. Townsley, 323 U.S. 557 (1945), the Court recognized that federal law governs the employment relationships on the OCS.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to apply California's wage and hour laws to Newton's claims without requiring a gap in federal law is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's interpretation of the OCSLA and the Fifth Circuit's decision in Continental Oil. The correct interpretation of the OCSLA is that state law applies on the OCS only when there is a gap in federal law. Since the FLSA provides a comprehensive remedy for Newton's claims, California's wage and hour laws do not apply.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to dismiss Newton's state law claims.
