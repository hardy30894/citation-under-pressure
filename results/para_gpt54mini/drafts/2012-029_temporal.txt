Federal courts do **not** have exclusive jurisdiction merely because a state-law case turns on a patent issue. The patent statutes reach only cases “arising under” the patent laws, and the Supreme Court has long required more than the presence of a federal ingredient. See **Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912)** (“A suit does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy” over federal law); **Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936)** (rejecting an expansive view of federal-question jurisdiction and insisting on a “selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside”).

That settled rule defeats exclusive federal jurisdiction here. A state legal-malpractice claim is created by state law; it does not become a patent case simply because the plaintiff must prove what would have happened in underlying patent litigation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a federal issue embedded in a state claim is not enough unless the federal issue is truly central to the cause of action. See **Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569**; **Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18**. The focus is on the plaintiff’s own claim, not on anticipated defenses or collateral patent questions. **Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569**.

The Court’s patent-jurisdiction decisions point in the same direction. In **Luckett v. Delpark, Inc., 270 U.S. 496, 500-01 (1926)**, the Court held that a suit that merely involved a patent dispute did not necessarily arise under the patent laws where the claim could be resolved on non-patent grounds. Likewise, in **Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co., 185 U.S. 282, 287-88 (1902)**, the Court emphasized that federal jurisdiction depends on the plaintiff’s statement of his own cause of action, not on how a defendant might raise patent issues in response. Those cases reject the notion that patent law is a jurisdictional magnet whenever it appears in the background.

The same principle governs state malpractice suits. A legal-malpractice action asks whether the attorney breached a duty and caused injury under state tort law. Even when the “case within a case” requires examining an underlying patent dispute, the patent question remains evidentiary and subsidiary to the state-law claim. See **The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25-26 (1913)** (looking to the real nature of the controversy and declining to expand federal jurisdiction merely because patent matters were implicated); **Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259-60 (1897)** (holding that jurisdiction turns on whether federal law creates the cause of action or is essential to the plaintiff’s right to relief, not on incidental federal questions).

Here, the right to relief is not created by patent law. Minton seeks damages for attorney negligence. To recover, he must prove duty, breach, and causation under Texas law. The patent issue is only one step in showing causation; it does not transform the entire controversy into a suit “arising under” the patent laws. Under **Shulthis** and **Gully**, that is not enough to trigger exclusive federal jurisdiction. See **Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569**; **Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18**.

Nor does exclusive jurisdiction follow from the fact that the malpractice case may require a court to consider whether a patent defense would have succeeded. Federal jurisdiction is not created by the need to apply federal law as a rule of decision in a state cause of action. See **Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co., 291 U.S. 205, 214-15 (1934)** (holding that federal jurisdiction does not arise simply because federal law is involved in proving an element of a state claim); **T. B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu, 339 F.2d 823, 828 (2d Cir. 1964)** (Friendly, J.) (under the analogous federal-question inquiry, a case does not arise under federal law merely because federal law may be consulted or applied). Those authorities confirm that a federal issue may be relevant without being jurisdictional.

Because Minton’s claim is a state malpractice action, and because the patent question is at most an embedded element of proof, the courts of Texas retained jurisdiction. The federal patent laws do not displace state authority over ordinary attorney-malpractice suits. See **Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569**; **Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18**; **Luckett, 270 U.S. at 500-01**. The judgment dismissing for lack of jurisdiction should therefore be reversed, and the case remanded for resolution on the merits in state court.
