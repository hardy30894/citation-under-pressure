The plain language of 28 USC section 1407(a) mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's clear directive is that the Panel's authority is limited to transferring cases for pretrial purposes, and that the original district court retains jurisdiction over the case for trial. 

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case, affirming the District Court's invocation of section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial, contravenes the statutory mandate of section 1407(a). As Judge Kozinski noted in his dissent, the federal courts have consistently disregarded the statutory language, starting with the Second Circuit's decision in Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir.1971). However, this line of cases is not supported by the statutory text or Supreme Court precedent.

In Ex Parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), the Supreme Court held that section 1404(a) allows a district court to transfer a case to a more convenient forum. However, this decision does not imply that a transferee court under section 1407(a) has the authority to retain a case for trial. In fact, the Supreme Court's decision in Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964), emphasizes the importance of respecting the statutory limits on a district court's transfer authority.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's decisions in Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960), and Kilpatrick v. Texas & Pacific Railway Co., 337 U.S. 75 (1949), establish that a district court's authority to transfer a case is strictly limited by the statutory language. In Hoffman, the Court held that a district court may only transfer a case to a district where it "might have been brought." Similarly, in Kilpatrick, the Court emphasized the importance of respecting the plaintiff's choice of forum.

The decision in Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19 (1960), also supports the petitioner's argument. In that case, the Supreme Court held that a district court has broad discretion to transfer a case under section 1404(a), but this discretion is still limited by the statutory language. The Court's decision in Koehring Co. v. Hyde Construction Co., 382 U.S. 362 (1966), further emphasizes the importance of respecting the statutory limits on a district court's transfer authority.

In light of these Supreme Court decisions, it is clear that the Ninth Circuit's decision in this case was erroneous. The plain language of section 1407(a) requires that the case be remanded to the original district court for trial, and the District Court's invocation of section 1404(a) to retain the case was improper. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to the Northern District of Illinois for trial.

Barge FBL-585, and Koehring Co. v. Hyde Construction Co., all support the petitioner's argument that the case should be remanded to the original district court for trial. Additionally, the Supreme Court's decisions in Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad, 345 U.S. 379 (1953), Herb v. Pitcairn, 325 U.S. 77 (1945), and Missouri Ex Rel. Southern Railway Co. v. Mayfield, 340 U.S. 1 (1950), and Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co., 375 U.S. 71 (1963) further reinforce the importance of respecting the statutory limits on a district court's transfer authority.
