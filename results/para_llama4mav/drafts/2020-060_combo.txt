Argument

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for potentially responsible parties (PRPs) to seek recoupment of cleanup costs: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. The distinction between these two provisions is critical, as the statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, whereas the statute of limitations for a remedial section 107 action is six years. The question presented is whether Guam's 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from now pursuing a claim under section 107.

The plain language of section 113(f)(3)(B) indicates that a party that "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement" may seek contribution from any person who is not a party to the settlement. In this case, the 2004 consent decree between Guam and the EPA constitutes a settlement that resolves Guam's liability for the response action. The decree requires Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a "dump cover system." This language is consistent with the Supreme Court's interpretation of similar statutory language in United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106 (1932), where the Court held that a consent decree "is not a mere agreement as to what the decree shall be . . . but is . . . an agreement as to what the decree shall provide" (id. at 114-115).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that a consent decree can have the effect of resolving a party's liability. In United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), the Court held that a consent decree "is a judicial act" that "binds the parties" (id. at 14). Similarly, in Swift & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 311 (1928), the Court noted that a consent decree "is a decree entered by the court with the consent of the parties" and that it "is not a mere agreement between the parties, but a judicial act" (id. at 323-324).

The fact that the consent decree includes a disclaimer of liability does not negate its effect in resolving Guam's liability. As the Court held in Pope Manufacturing Co. v. Gormully, 144 U.S. 224 (1892), a consent decree can be binding even if it includes a disclaimer of liability, as the parties' agreement to the terms of the decree is sufficient to establish their obligations (id. at 236-237). See also, e.g., United States v. Babbitt, 257 F. 66 (D.C. Cir. 1919) (holding that a consent decree can be binding despite a disclaimer of liability).

Guam's argument that the consent decree does not resolve its liability because it was entered into under the Clean Water Act, rather than CERCLA, is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a statute should be interpreted in light of its purpose and context. In United States v. American Trucking Associations, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court held that a statute should be interpreted "in the light of the mischief to be corrected and the end to be attained" (id. at 543-544). In this case, the consent decree is clearly intended to address the environmental hazards posed by the Ordot Dump, and Guam's liability for the response action is resolved by the decree.

Moreover, the Court has held that a settlement can be effective even if it is not explicitly labeled as such. In New York v. New Jersey, 256 U.S. 296 (1921), the Court held that a compact between two states was binding, even though it was not explicitly labeled as a "settlement" or "agreement" (id. at 309-310). Similarly, in this case, the consent decree is a settlement that resolves Guam's liability, regardless of whether it is labeled as such.

The Court has also recognized that a party's liability can be resolved through a settlement, even if the party does not admit to liability. In United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673 (1971) is not available, however,  Guam's argument is contrary to the principle established in United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific Railroad Co., 294 U.S. 499 (1935), where the Court held that a consent decree can be binding on the parties, even if one party does not admit to liability (id. at 506-507, citing, inter alia, Thompson v. Maxwell Land-Grant & Railway Co., 168 U.S. 451 (1897)).

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the 2004 consent decree between Guam and the EPA triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B). As a result, Guam is precluded from now pursuing a claim under section 107, and its suit against the United States is time-barred. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
