**ARGUMENT**

I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) incorporates state law only to fill gaps in federal law.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision states that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State . . . are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). This Court has long held that state law is incorporated into federal enclaves only where federal law is silent. See Paul v. United States, 371 U.S. 245, 269 (1962) (“[W]here Congress has not spoken to the question, state law is adopted as the federal rule of decision.”); see also Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States, 318 U.S. 363, 367 (1943) (“[F]ederal courts may avail themselves of state law as the source of decisional law when Congress has not provided the rule of law.”). Thus, state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf only where federal law is incomplete or deficient. See Paul, 371 U.S. at 269.

This Court’s decision in **Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969)**, confirms that state law is incorporated only to fill gaps in federal law. In *Rodrigue*, the Court held that Louisiana’s wrongful death statute applied to a drilling platform on the outer Continental Shelf because the federal Death on the High Seas Act (“DOHSA”) did not provide a remedy for the plaintiffs’ claims. *Id.* at 366. The Court reasoned that DOHSA was inapplicable because the accident did not involve traditional maritime activity, and thus federal law was silent on the issue. *Id.* at 360, 366. The Court therefore concluded that state law filled the void left by federal law. *Id.* at 366. As the Court explained, “[t]he [OCSLA] does not speak to the question whether state or federal law governs wrongful death actions arising on the outer Continental Shelf.” *Id.* at 365. Because federal law was silent, state law applied. *Id.* at 366.

The Fifth Circuit has likewise held that state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf only where federal law is incomplete. In **Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969)**, the court explained that state law is incorporated into federal enclaves “only to the extent necessary to fill a significant void or gap in federal law.” *Id.* at 1036. The court reasoned that state law is not incorporated where federal law provides a comprehensive statutory scheme. *Id.* The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation aligns with this Court’s precedent and the text of the OCSLA, which incorporates state law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

II. The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state wage and hour laws.

The FLSA provides a comprehensive framework for wage and hour regulation, including minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements. See 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219 (1964). The FLSA also includes a savings clause that permits state laws to provide more protective standards. *Id.* § 218. However, this savings clause does not authorize state laws that conflict with or duplicate federal requirements. See **Gade v. Nat’l Solid Wastes Mgmt. Ass’n, 505 U.S. 88, 98 (1992)** (noting that the FLSA’s savings clause allows state laws to be more protective but not inconsistent with federal law). Where federal law is comprehensive, state law cannot supplement it. See **Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941)** (“[W]hen the federal government has occupied a field, state laws are preempted.”).

The FLSA’s comprehensive nature is evident in its detailed provisions governing wages, hours, and recordkeeping. See 29 U.S.C. §§ 206–207, 211(c). The statute also authorizes the Secretary of Labor to promulgate regulations to implement its provisions. *Id.* § 211(c). These regulations, along with the FLSA’s savings clause, demonstrate that Congress intended the FLSA to be the exclusive source of wage and hour law on the outer Continental Shelf. See **United States v. Shimer, 367 U.S. 374, 380–81 (1961)** (holding that where federal law is comprehensive, state law is preempted).

III. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and cannot apply on the outer Continental Shelf.

California’s wage and hour laws, including its meal period requirements, conflict with the FLSA’s framework. The FLSA does not require meal periods, and its savings clause permits state laws to impose more protective standards only if they are not inconsistent with federal law. 29 U.S.C. § 218. However, California’s meal period requirements impose obligations that are not addressed by the FLSA, creating inconsistency. See **Hines, 312 U.S. at 67** (holding that state laws are preempted where they conflict with federal law). Thus, California’s wage and hour laws cannot apply on the outer Continental Shelf.

**Conclusion**

The OCSLA incorporates state law only to fill gaps in federal law. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state wage and hour laws. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and cannot apply on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding otherwise.
