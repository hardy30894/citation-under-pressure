The government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, that burden does not shift. In Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391 (1957), the Court explained that the statute of limitations begins to run upon withdrawal, and the prosecution must establish that the conspiracy continued and that the defendant was still a participant within the five-year period. Id. at 397.

This allocation follows from the fundamental rule that the prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the offense. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) ("[T]he Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime"). Membership in a conspiracy during the limitations window is such a fact. See United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910) (a conspiracy "continues up to the time of abandonment or success").

Withdrawal is not an affirmative defense that reallocates the burden. The Court has long held that "the burden of proof rests upon the prosecution to prove the guilt of the accused." Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895). Evidence of withdrawal merely negates the element of continued membership; it does not create a new issue on which the defendant must persuade the factfinder. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (withdrawal requires "affirmative action" but does not relieve the government of proving ongoing participation).

The court of appeals therefore erred in relieving the government of its constitutional obligation. The judgment should be reversed.
