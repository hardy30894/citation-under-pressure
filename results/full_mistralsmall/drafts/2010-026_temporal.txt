**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ninth Circuit erred in expanding Exemption 2 to shield ESQD arc maps because the statutory text and controlling precedent limit the exemption to “internal personnel rules and practices,” not operational safety data.

The Freedom of Information Act’s Exemption 2 permits withholding only “matters that are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2) (1967). This Court has long construed the phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” narrowly, consistent with Congress’s intent to ensure broad public access to government records. In *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352 (1976), the Supreme Court emphasized that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed,” id. at 361, and that Exemption 2 applies only to “trivial administrative matters,” id. at 369. The Court’s reasoning in *Rose*—decided after the Ninth Circuit’s opinion—reinforces that Exemption 2 does not encompass operational safety data like ESQD arc maps, which are not “personnel rules” but rather technical safety standards used to design and secure military facilities.

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981), conflicts with the statutory text and this Court’s precedent. As the Supreme Court explained in *Rose*, Exemption 2 was intended to cover only “routine matters such as parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave,” id. at 363 (quoting S. Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 8 (1965)), not sensitive operational data. The D.C. Circuit’s test—later embraced by the Ninth Circuit—stretches the exemption far beyond its intended scope by allowing agencies to withhold any document that is “predominantly internal” and poses a hypothetical risk of circumvention. This approach improperly transforms Exemption 2 into a broad catch-all for operational secrecy, contrary to Congress’s explicit delineation of FOIA exemptions. *See Adm’r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975) (exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be narrowly construed).

II. The ESQD arc maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices” because they are operational safety tools used to mitigate public risk, not agency employment policies.

The ESQD arc maps at issue are technical documents used by the Navy to design storage facilities and minimize explosive risks to surrounding communities. They are not “personnel rules” governing agency employees; rather, they are operational safety standards akin to engineering specifications. The Supreme Court has cautioned that FOIA exemptions must not be read to shield “operational files” or “investigatory files compiled for law enforcement purposes,” *see Citizens Comm. to Investigate the FBI v. FBI*, 448 F. Supp. 1097, 1102 (D.D.C. 1978) (citing *Soucie v. David*, 448 F.2d 1067, 1078 (D.C. Cir. 1971)), and the same principle applies here. The arc maps are not “internal” in the sense of governing agency personnel; they are external-facing tools used to assess public safety risks.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Hardy* involved a law enforcement manual detailing search and seizure procedures—clearly an “internal personnel practice” under Exemption 2. The ESQD arc maps, by contrast, are not personnel rules but operational safety data. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Rose* underscores this distinction, emphasizing that Exemption 2 applies only to “trivial administrative matters,” not to documents that inform public safety. 425 U.S. at 369.

III. The risk of “circumvention” posited by the Navy is speculative and insufficient to justify withholding the arc maps under Exemption 2.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test requires a showing that disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1073. However, the Supreme Court has rejected speculative claims of harm as a basis for withholding records under FOIA. In *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, the Court rejected the agency’s argument that disclosure of disciplinary records would “frustrate the investigatory process,” noting that such risks must be “real and not merely speculative.” 425 U.S. at 379. Similarly, the D.C. Circuit has held that Exemption 2 requires a “demonstrable risk” of circumvention, not a hypothetical one. *See Schwaner v. Dep’t of Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793, 795 (D.C. Cir. 1990) (quoting *Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1073).

Here, the Navy’s affidavits assert that disclosure of the arc maps could enable adversaries to “reverse engineer” sensitive information about ordnance storage. However, this risk is speculative and unsupported by evidence. The Supreme Court has made clear that FOIA exemptions cannot be invoked based on “vague, unsubstantiated fears of embarrassment or public misunderstanding.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 379. The Navy’s failure to classify the arc maps under Exemption 1—designed specifically for national security concerns—further undermines its claim that disclosure poses a genuine risk. *See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1) (1967)*.

**Conclusion**
The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to shield operational safety data like ESQD arc maps contravenes the statutory text and this Court’s precedent. Exemption 2 applies only to “internal personnel rules and practices,” not to technical safety standards used to protect the public. The speculative risk of circumvention asserted by the Navy is insufficient to justify withholding the records. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and remand with instructions to order disclosure of the ESQD arc maps.
