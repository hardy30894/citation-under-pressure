The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention beyond 90 days. Section 1231(a)(6) says only that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period,” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), but it does not say they may be held forever. When Congress uses ambiguous immigration language, this Court has repeatedly required a narrow construction to avoid serious constitutional doubt. Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948); United States Ex Rel. Stapf v. Corsi, 287 U.S. 129, 135 (1932); Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957). That rule applies here with particular force because indefinite civil detention raises grave due process concerns. Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896); Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 538-42 (1952).

This Court has already recognized that immigration detention statutes are not read to confer boundless confinement absent clear congressional authorization. In United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), the Court construed a supervision statute for aliens under final deportation orders to limit the Attorney General’s inquiry powers, explaining that the statute should not be read to authorize “unbounded” governmental action where a narrower construction would suffice. Id. at 199-205. Likewise, in Fong Haw Tan, the Court refused to adopt a construction that would expand deportation consequences beyond what Congress plainly stated. 333 U.S. at 10. The same interpretive discipline controls here. Section 1231(a)(6) may permit detention for a reasonable time while the Government seeks removal, but it does not plainly permit detention with no end point.

The structure of the immigration laws confirms that Congress knows how to authorize continuing custody when it wants to. The former deportation supervision statute provided that an alien with a final deportation order outstanding for more than six months “shall, pending eventual deportation, be subject to supervision under regulations prescribed by the Attorney General.” Witkovich, 353 U.S. at 195. This Court did not read that statute to permit unlimited restraint. Id. at 199-205. Nor did earlier cases involving deportation and exclusion suggest that indefinite civil custody is the default. In Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193, 196-97 (1958), and Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185, 187-88 (1958), the Court distinguished between exclusion and entry, but it did not suggest that an alien lawfully present in the United States may be imprisoned indefinitely simply because removal proves difficult. Similarly, United States ex rel. Mensevich v. Tod, 264 U.S. 134, 136-37 (1924), and Phillippides v. Day, 283 U.S. 48, 52-53 (1931), involved statutory implementation of deportation authority, not a claim of perpetual detention untethered to a realistic prospect of removal.

The Government will likely rely on Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), but Carlson involved discretionary denial of bail to deportable aliens during pending proceedings, not endless post-order confinement. Id. at 530-31, 538-42. That case upheld detention only as a regulatory incident of an ongoing immigration process, not as punishment without limit. Id. at 543-44. Wong Wing likewise teaches that while Congress may regulate admission and removal, punitive confinement requires constitutional justification. 163 U.S. at 237-38.

Mezei does not compel a different result. In Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 215-16 (1953), the Court upheld exclusion-related detention at the border, in a posture fundamentally different from that of a resident alien who has lived in the country for years and been ordered removed. This Court in Zadvydas correctly recognized that Mezei involved an alien who had not effected entry. 533 U.S. 678, 694-95 (2001). That distinction matters because the Constitution tolerates far less liberty deprivation for a resident alien physically inside the country than for an excluded alien seeking admission. Leng May Ma, 357 U.S. at 188-90; Rogers, 357 U.S. at 196-98.

Finally, the Government’s reading would create the very constitutional infirmity the canon against serious constitutional questions is designed to avoid. This Court has long presumed that Congress does not lightly authorize grave deprivations of liberty. Fong Haw Tan, 333 U.S. at 10; Carlson, 342 U.S. at 537-42. The better reading is the one this Court adopted in Zadvydas: section 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, detention is no longer authorized. 533 U.S. at 689, 699-701. That construction should be affirmed.
