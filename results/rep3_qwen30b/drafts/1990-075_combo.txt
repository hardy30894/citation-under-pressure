**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it permit an impermissible delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). The delegation is fully consistent with the constitutional principle that Congress may delegate authority to administrative agencies so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of that power. This Court has long recognized that Congress may empower executive officers to act in the public interest under broad statutory mandates, provided the standards are not so vague or undefined as to render the delegation a surrender of legislative authority. The statute before us satisfies this test.

**I. Congress Has the Authority to Delegate the Power to Temporarily Schedule Drugs Under an Intelligible Principle**

The Constitution vests legislative power in Congress, but it does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to other branches when it does so with clear standards. In *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the President to fix rates for transportation of goods, holding that “the power to make rules for the government of the country is not necessarily a legislative power.” Id. at 409. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not forbid Congress to delegate legislative power, provided it lays down an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to act is directed to conform.” Id. (emphasis added). This principle has been repeatedly reaffirmed.

In *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), the Court upheld a statute authorizing the Secretary of the Treasury to regulate the importation of certain goods, noting that “the power to make rules for the government of the country is not necessarily a legislative power.” Id. at 692. The Court rejected the argument that such delegation violated Article I, stating that “the Constitution does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to administrative officers when it provides a standard to guide their action.” Id. The Court further observed that “the necessity of the times, and the complexity of modern affairs, require that Congress should be able to delegate authority to officers charged with the execution of the laws.” Id. at 693.

The temporary scheduling provision of the Controlled Substances Act contains precisely such an intelligible principle. Section 811(h) requires the Attorney General to consider only three factors: (1) the history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and (3) the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). These are not vague or amorphous standards. They are objective, fact-based criteria that have been used for decades in administrative law to determine whether a substance poses a threat to public safety. The Court has long recognized that such criteria are sufficient to satisfy the intelligible principle test. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to regulate tobacco, noting that “the statute defined the conditions under which the power might be exercised.” Id. at 128. Similarly, in *United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Ass’n*, 322 U.S. 533 (1944), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to regulate insurance, stating that “the statute provided a standard by which the Secretary could act.” Id. at 550.

Here, Congress has not left the Attorney General free to act at will. The statute mandates that the Attorney General act only when there is an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” and only after notice and the opportunity for comment from the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (4). These procedural safeguards, combined with the three specific factors, constitute a clear and enforceable standard. The Court has repeatedly held that such procedural and substantive limits are sufficient to satisfy the non-delegation doctrine. In *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81 (1921), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to regulate the sale of food, stating that “the statute defined the conditions under which the power might be exercised.” Id. at 88. The same is true here.

**II. The Attorney General May Lawfully Subdelegate Authority to the DEA**

The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is not only permissible but consistent with longstanding administrative practice. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), the Court upheld the delegation of authority to the Secretary of Agriculture to enforce the Clayton Act, noting that “the Secretary may delegate his authority to subordinate officers.” Id. at 129. The Court emphasized that “the power to administer the law may be delegated to officers of the executive department.” Id.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), where the Court upheld the delegation of authority to the Secretary of Agriculture to enforce the Packers and Stockyards Act. The Court stated that “the Secretary may delegate his authority to subordinate officers, provided the delegation is consistent with the statute.” Id. at 195. The Court further held that “the delegation of authority to administrative officers is not prohibited by the Constitution.” Id.

The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is consistent with these precedents. The regulation at issue, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), was issued pursuant to the Attorney General’s authority under 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which permits the Attorney General to “delegate to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice such functions as he may deem appropriate.” The DEA Administrator is a duly appointed officer of the Department of Justice, and the delegation is limited to the specific functions conferred by Congress. The Court has long recognized that such subdelegations are valid so long as the original delegation is constitutional and the subdelegation is within the scope of the authority granted.
