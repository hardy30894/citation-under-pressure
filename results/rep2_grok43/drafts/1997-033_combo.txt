The plain language of section 1407(a) commands that actions transferred for pretrial proceedings "shall" be remanded at the conclusion of those proceedings. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (statutory words "are to be taken in their plain and ordinary sense"). This mandatory directive admits of no exception for self-assignment under section 1404(a). United States v. Missouri Pacific R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929) ("where the language is plain and unambiguous there is no occasion for construction").

Section 1404(a) authorizes transfers only to districts where the action "might have been brought." Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 342-43 (1960). A multidistrict transferee court lacks authority to convert itself into such a district for trial purposes, as the original transfer is limited to pretrial coordination. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 60 (1949) (section 1404(a) "does not enlarge or diminish the venue rights of litigants").

The policy of section 1407 preserves plaintiffs' choice of forum for trial. Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947) (plaintiff's venue selection "should rarely be disturbed"). Allowing a transferee court to retain the case for trial would nullify that choice and the statutory remand obligation. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 622 (1964) (transfers must not "prejudice the rights of the parties").

Mandamus lies to correct a district court's refusal to remand. Roche v. Evaporated Milk Assn., 319 U.S. 21, 26 (1943) (writ available where "there is no other adequate remedy"). The error is clear because section 1404(a) motions cannot override the explicit command of section 1407(a). Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955) (section 1404(a) "is not a mere codification of forum non conveniens" but remains subject to statutory limits).

These authorities confirm that the district court exceeded its authority by assigning the case to itself for trial rather than remanding it.
