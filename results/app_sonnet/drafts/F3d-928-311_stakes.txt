**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court erred in treating Furlow's South Carolina distribution conviction as a "serious drug offense" and a "controlled substance offense."**

Both the ACCA and the career offender provision require a court to determine whether a prior state conviction matches a federal generic offense, not whether the defendant's actual conduct happened to satisfy that offense. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990) (courts must look "only to the statutory definitions—i.e., the elements—of a defendant's prior offenses, and not to the particular facts underlying those convictions"). This categorical approach controls the ACCA's "serious drug offense" definition and the Guidelines' parallel "controlled substance offense" definition, because both provisions ask whether the state offense of conviction necessarily involves "distributing" a controlled substance. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(b).

Under *Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 261 (2013), a state statute may serve as a predicate only if its elements are "the same as, or narrower than, those of the generic offense." Where the state statute "sweeps more broadly" than the generic crime, a conviction under that statute cannot count "even if the defendant actually committed the offense in its generic form." *Id.* at 261. *Mathis v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2251 (2016), reaffirmed that a sentencing court may not consider "how a given defendant actually perpetrated the crime," but only whether the "least of th[e] acts" criminalized by the statute falls within the generic definition. *Moncrieffe v. Holder*, 569 U.S. 184, 190-91 (2013) (predicate analysis focuses on "the minimum conduct criminalized by the state statute").

South Carolina's distribution offense is defined to reach conduct broader than the generic federal offense of "distributing" a controlled substance, because it encompasses transfers accomplished through means—including attempted or constructive delivery—that do not require the completed transfer of an actual, physical substance that generic "distribution" contemplates. Because the state offense's least culpable conduct sweeps beyond the ACCA's and the Guidelines' generic "distributing" element, the district court was required, under *Descamps* and *Mathis*, to determine whether the South Carolina statute is divisible into discrete offenses—some matching the generic crime and some not—and, if divisible, to consult only the limited class of documents approved in *Shepard v. United States*, 544 U.S. 13, 26 (2005) (charging document, plea agreement, plea colloquy, or comparable judicial record), to determine which alternative Furlow's plea necessarily established.

The record here does not establish that the district court undertook this divisibility analysis or examined any *Shepard*-approved document identifying the specific alternative element of conviction. Absent such a showing, the government failed to carry its burden of proving that Furlow's South Carolina conviction rests on the narrower, generic form of the offense. *See Descamps*, 570 U.S. at 269 (sentencing enhancements may not rest on facts neither found by a jury nor admitted by the defendant). The conviction therefore cannot serve as an ACCA or career-offender predicate, and Furlow's sentence should be vacated.

**II. Furlow's Georgia first-degree arson conviction is not a categorical match for the enumerated offense of "arson."**

The ACCA and the Guidelines both list "arson" as an enumerated predicate without further definition. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). Where Congress or the Sentencing Commission "employs an undefined term drawn from the common law," the courts assume it carries its "generic, contemporary meaning." *Taylor*, 495 U.S. at 598. That generic meaning, consistent with the categorical approach reaffirmed in *Mathis*, controls regardless of the label the state legislature attaches to the offense; the comparison is between the elements of the generic crime and the elements of the state statute, not the facts of Furlow's own conduct. *Mathis*, 136 S. Ct. at 2248-49; *Descamps*, 570 U.S. at 261.

Generic arson, at its core, requires the malicious or intentional burning of property belonging to another, or under circumstances presenting a danger to another's person or property. Georgia's first-degree arson statute, by contrast, extends criminal liability to burnings of an offender's own property under specified circumstances—such as burning to defraud an insurer—without requiring the endangerment of another person's property or person that the generic offense demands. Because the Georgia statute's minimum conduct is broader than the generic federal definition, a conviction under it cannot categorically qualify as "arson" for ACCA or Guidelines purposes. *See Moncrieffe*, 569 U.S. at 190-91 (predicate analysis asks whether "the least culpable conduct hypothetically covered by the statute" satisfies the generic offense). Nor did the government demonstrate, through any *Shepard*-approved document, that Furlow's own conviction rested on the narrower subset of Georgia arson conduct—if any—that matches the generic offense. *Shepard*, 544 U.S. at 26.

Under *Gonzales v. Duenas-Alvarez*, 549 U.S. 183, 193 (2007), a defendant challenging a categorical match must show "a realistic probability, not a theoretical possibility," that the state statute reaches conduct beyond the generic offense; Georgia's own arson provisions, criminalizing the burning of one's own insured property, supply exactly that realistic application, not a hypothetical one.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, neither Furlow's South Carolina distribution conviction nor his Georgia first-degree arson conviction qualifies as a predicate under the ACCA or the career offender provision. This Court should vacate Furlow's sentence and remand for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement or the career-offender designation.
