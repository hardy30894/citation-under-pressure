The respondent, Eugene Woodard, has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is inapplicable to clemency proceedings, as they are not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. The Supreme Court has long recognized that clemency is an act of executive discretion, as seen in Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925), where the Court characterized clemency as an act of "mercy" exercised by the executive. This characterization is consistent with the Court's earlier holdings that the executive's power to grant reprieves and pardons is "unlimited" and not subject to judicial control, as stated in United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871).

The creation of a clemency procedure by a state does not create a protected liberty interest. The Court has established that a statutory procedure does not necessarily give rise to a constitutional right, as held in Murray v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. 272, 277 (1856). Furthermore, the Court has characterized a pardon as a "private" act of the executive, not subject to due process requirements, in Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 603-04 (1896). The distinction between the adjudicatory process and clemency proceedings is further underscored by the Court's description of a pardon as "an act of grace" separate from the judicial process, as seen in United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. 150, 161 (1833).

The respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is also without merit. The Ohio clemency procedure does not compel the respondent to waive his Fifth Amendment rights, as he is not required to participate in the clemency interview. According to Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 111-12 (1908), the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated when a person is not compelled to testify against themselves. The respondent is free to assert his Fifth Amendment privilege if he chooses to participate in the clemency interview.

The Court of Appeals' remand for further proceedings is premature and unnecessary. A federal court should not intervene in a state proceeding unless there is a clear constitutional violation, as established in Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496, 500-01 (1941). In this case, there is no such violation, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims was proper.

In conclusion, the respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims should be reinstated.
