**ARGUMENT**

**8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) Does Not Authorize the Indefinite Detention of a Removable Resident Alien**

**I. The canon of constitutional avoidance requires this Court to read an implicit time limit into § 1231(a)(6).**

This Court has long held that where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, one raising grave constitutional doubts and one avoiding them, the latter must be adopted. In *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932), the Court explained that "when the validity of an act of the Congress is drawn in question, and even if a serious doubt of constitutionality is raised, it is a cardinal principle that this Court will first ascertain whether a construction of the statute is fairly possible by which the question may be avoided." This Court applied that very canon to a companion immigration-supervision provision in *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), narrowly construing the Attorney General's authority to question a deportable alien under supervision rather than read the statute to grant "unbounded" authority that would raise serious constitutional problems. The same interpretive discipline governed *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283 (1944), where the Court refused to read wartime detention authority to permit confinement of a person once the statutory purpose justifying detention no longer applied, holding that Congress does not lightly authorize confinement untethered to any temporal or purposive limit.

Section 1231(a)(6) is susceptible of the same limiting construction. Nothing in its text compels indefinite detention; it says only that a removable alien "may be detained beyond the removal period." Under *Crowell*, *Witkovich*, and *Endo*, that silence must be read, not as a grant of perpetual custody, but as authority limited to the period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal, the evident statutory purpose.

**II. Indefinite civil detention of a lawfully admitted resident alien raises grave due process concerns because such an alien is entitled to the full protection of the Constitution.**

The constitutional stakes here are not abstract. This Court has repeatedly held that once an alien has been lawfully admitted and resides within the United States, he is entitled to the same due process protections as a citizen. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886), the Court held that the Constitution's protections are "universal in their application, to all persons within the territorial jurisdiction," without regard to alienage. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), likewise held that Congress could not subject aliens within the United States to punitive confinement without the process the Constitution demands for any person. And in *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922), the Court recognized that a resident alien "may be deprived of his liberty" only through the process the Constitution affords, because deportation proceedings threaten consequences that "may result... in loss of both property and life, or of all that makes life worth living." *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953), confirmed that a lawfully admitted resident alien "is invested with the rights guaranteed by the Constitution to all people within our borders."

Petitioner Zadvydas was lawfully admitted to the United States in 1956 and has resided here ever since; he is squarely within the category of persons these cases protect. Reading § 1231(a)(6) to permit his confinement for the remainder of his life, notwithstanding that removal has become impossible, would strip him of the liberty interest this Court has consistently protected for resident aliens since *Yick Wo* and *Wong Wing*. Freedom from physical restraint is, as those cases establish, the core liberty the Due Process Clause protects, and civil detention untethered to any prospect of removal is functionally punitive confinement without trial.

**III. Neither *Mezei* nor *Carlson* authorizes indefinite detention of a resident alien whose removal is not reasonably foreseeable.**

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), was misplaced. *Mezei* involved an alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States and was therefore treated, for constitutional purposes, as if he were still outside the country. That "entry fiction" has no application to Zadvydas, a resident alien who was lawfully admitted decades ago and whose presence here is fully within the protection of *Kwong Hai Chew* and *Ng Fung Ho*. Nor does *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), rescue the detention here. *Carlson* upheld detention of aliens pending deportation proceedings precisely because that detention was temporally bounded by the pendency of removal itself; it did not sanction confinement after removal had become a practical impossibility. Even *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), which recognized Congress's plenary power to exclude and deport aliens, never suggested that power extends to indefinite civil imprisonment once deportation cannot be accomplished.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, § 1231(a)(6) must be construed to authorize detention only for the period reasonably necessary to secure removal, not indefinitely, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
