# ARGUMENT

## I. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I Fix an Exclusive List of Eligibility Requirements That No State May Supplement

Article I provides that Representatives must be twenty-five years old, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected, U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2, and that Senators must be thirty years old, nine years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State, id. § 3, cl. 3. This Court has already confronted the character of these provisions in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), holding that even Congress itself may not exclude a duly elected Member for failing to meet a qualification beyond those the Constitution enumerates. The Court traced the Convention debates and concluded that the Framers deliberately rejected proposals allowing additional qualifications to be imposed, reasoning that the delegates "adopted the fixed standards" set out in the constitutional text precisely to foreclose manipulation of eligibility for federal office. *Id.* at 539. If Congress—the body directly answerable to the electorate whose representatives are at issue—lacks power to add qualifications, a single State acting alone possesses even less authority to do so, for a state enactment binds only the votes of its own citizens while purporting to redefine the qualifications for a national office.

Amendment 73's Section 3 does precisely what *Powell* forbids. Though styled as a "ballot access" restriction, it operates as an absolute bar: a three-term Representative or two-term Senator "shall not be eligible to appear on the ballot." That is a qualification in substance, whatever label the drafters selected, and the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized as much.

## II. Members of Congress Represent the Nation, Not the States, and Their Qualifications Must Be Uniform

The office of Representative or Senator is a creature of the national government, not an instrumentality of the State from which its occupant is elected. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), this Court held that the federal government, "though limited in its powers, is supreme," id. at 405, and that the States possess no authority to control or burden the exercise of powers the Constitution commits to the national government. That principle applies with full force here: the composition of Congress is a matter of national concern, and allowing each State to impose its own supplemental disqualifications would produce, as the Arkansas Supreme Court itself recognized below, "a hodge-podge of qualifications" destructive of the uniformity the Framers intended.

This Court's election-law decisions confirm that federal elections are a matter of national, not state, sovereignty. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), the Court explained that the right to choose Representatives in Congress is "a right and power conferred by and under the Constitution of the United States," id. at 661-62, independent of state largesse. Similarly, in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court held that the right of qualified voters to select their representatives "is a right secured by the Constitution," id. at 314, one the States administer but do not create. If the very right to vote for Congress flows from the federal Constitution rather than state law, so too must the qualifications of those for whom that vote may be cast.

## III. A State Cannot Use Its Elections Clause Authority to Accomplish What the Qualifications Clauses Forbid

Petitioners' resort to "ballot access" language cannot rescue Section 3. The Elections Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, permits States to regulate the "Times, Places and Manner" of holding elections, but this Court has confined that authority to procedural matters. In *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), the Court described the state power under the Elections Clause as reaching only regulations "relating to notices, registration, supervision of voting, protection of voters ... and making and publication of election returns," id. at 366—not substantive limits on who may hold office. And in *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724 (1974), while upholding certain neutral ballot-access rules, this Court cautioned that such regulations may not be used to disqualify candidates who satisfy the Constitution's own criteria. A permanent, lifetime bar from the ballot after a fixed number of terms is not a procedural regulation of the "manner" of holding elections; it is a substantive disqualification masquerading as one.

## IV. Any Alteration of Congressional Qualifications Must Proceed Through Article V, Not State Initiative

Finally, if the People wish to add term limits to the constitutional qualifications for Congress, the exclusive vehicle is Article V. In *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), this Court held that the method of amending the Constitution "is a Federal function which cannot be performed by a State," derived solely from Article V, and that state procedures cannot substitute for that process. *Dillon v. Gloss*, 256 U.S. 368 (1921), likewise confirms that Article V supplies the sole mechanism for altering the constitutional text. Arkansas may not, through a state initiative, accomplish what only a constitutional amendment ratified under Article V can achieve.

## V. The Controversy Is Justiciable

As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held, the impairment of Respondents' associational and voting rights presented a live controversy ripe for decision, consistent with this Court's holding in *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), that burdens on the right to vote for the candidate of one's choice are cognizable before an election occurs.

For these reasons, Section 3 of Amendment 73 should be held unconstitutional.
