ARGUMENT

I. The Same Statutory Phrase Must Bear the Same Meaning Whether the Board Dismisses for Untimeliness or for Want of Jurisdiction

Section 7702(a)(1) defines a “mixed case” as one in which the employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the [Board]” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” Nothing in that text distinguishes between an appeal rejected because it was filed a day late and one rejected because the Board concluded the underlying separation was voluntary. This Court has long insisted that “there is a natural presumption that identical words used in different parts of the same act are intended to have the same meaning.” Atlantic Cleaners & Dyers, Inc. v. United States, 286 U.S. 427, 433 (1932). The Federal Circuit and Board would nonetheless have the operative phrase—“action which [she] may appeal”—mean one thing when the dismissal is labeled “procedural” and another when it is labeled “jurisdictional.” Atlantic Cleaners forbids exactly that maneuver. A single statutory phrase cannot be read to send one class of pre-merits dismissals to district court and a materially identical class to the Federal Circuit merely because of the label the Board affixes to its own order.

II. Doubts About the Forum for Judicial Review of Agency Action Must Be Resolved in Favor of Access to the Courts

Even if the text were ambiguous, this Court’s settled canon would resolve the ambiguity for Perry. “[O]nly upon a showing of ‘clear and convincing evidence’” of a contrary legislative purpose should courts “restrict access to judicial review.” Rusk v. Cort, 369 U.S. 367, 379–380 (1962). That presumption traces to Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48, 51 (1955), and was reaffirmed in Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 140–141 (1967), where the Court explained that Congress’s “generous review provisions” must be given a “hospitable” interpretation. The same instinct underlies Stark v. Wickard, 321 U.S. 288 (1944), which refused to infer that Congress meant to insulate an agency’s threshold determinations from meaningful judicial scrutiny absent a plain statement to that effect. Nothing in the Civil Service Reform Act clearly and convincingly states that an employee who alleges discrimination loses her district-court forum the moment the Board decides, rightly or wrongly, that it lacks jurisdiction over the very same allegations. Ambiguity should therefore be resolved in favor of the forum Congress designed for discrimination claims—the district court—not against it.

III. A Jurisdictional Dismissal That Turns on the Voluntariness of the Employee’s Separation Cannot Be Severed From the Discrimination Allegations That Produced It

This case illustrates why bifurcating jurisdictional and merits dismissals into separate appellate tracks defeats the statute’s purpose. The Board found it lacked jurisdiction only because it first found that Perry’s settlement and retirement were voluntary—a finding inseparable from his claim that the settlement was coerced by discriminatory and retaliatory pressure. This Court has recognized that jurisdictional facts of this kind cannot be walled off from the merits simply because an agency chooses to characterize its ruling as “jurisdictional.” See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932). Similarly, in Estep v. United States, 327 U.S. 114 (1946), the Court held that even where a statute made a local board’s classification “final,” a reviewing court retained authority to examine whether the board had acted within the “scope of its authority,” because finality provisions do not extend to questions going to the board’s power to act at all. The same logic applies here in reverse: a Board ruling that it lacks power to act, resting entirely on disputed discrimination-related facts, is not meaningfully different from a ruling on the merits, and it should not be shunted to a forum with no competence over Title VII, the ADEA, or the Rehabilitation Act.

That the Federal Circuit lacks such competence is precisely why Congress carved discrimination claims out of its general review jurisdiction in the first place. Denying district-court review of a jurisdictional dismissal premised on discrimination allegations threatens the very rights the statute exists to protect. This Court has cautioned against inferring that Congress meant to leave a statutory right without an effective remedy. See Leedom v. Kyne, 358 U.S. 184, 190 (1958) (“This Court cannot lightly infer that Congress does not intend judicial protection of rights it confers against agency action taken in excess of delegated powers.”). Civil-rights and remedial personnel statutes, no less than the veterans’ reemployment statute construed in Fishgold v. Sullivan Drydock & Repair Corp., 328 U.S. 275, 285 (1946), are “to be liberally construed for the benefit of” the employees they protect. A rule that strips discrimination claimants of their district-court forum whenever the Board frames its dismissal as jurisdictional is the opposite of liberal construction.

IV. A Single, Predictable Rule Avoids Needless Complexity

Finally, sound judicial administration favors a rule fixed at the outset of litigation rather than one contingent on how the Board ultimately rules. Cf. Ashwander v. TVA, 297 U.S. 288, 341 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (courts should avoid constructions that generate unnecessary complications when a sound alternative exists). Requiring parties to guess—before the Board even rules—whether their case will end up in district court or the Federal Circuit invites wasted filings, transfer motions, and exactly the confusion this litigation reflects. A single rule sending all mixed-case dismissals, procedural or jurisdictional, to district court respects both the statutory text and the coherent administration of justice that this Court values in analogous contexts. Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (consistency of agency and judicial treatment lends predictability and persuasive force to legal rules).

For these reasons, the Board’s dismissal of Perry’s mixed case for lack of jurisdiction is reviewable in district court.
