## Argument

I. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73'S TERM LIMITS ON MEMBERS OF CONGRESS ARE UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE THEY ADD QUALIFICATIONS BEYOND THOSE ENUMERATED IN THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for members of Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency, and the Framers' decision to enumerate these qualifications implies that they intended to exclude other qualifications. As the Supreme Court has held, "the States have no power to add qualifications" beyond those specified in the Constitution. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 550 (1969). The Court has consistently applied this principle to strike down state attempts to impose additional qualifications on congressional candidates. See, e.g., id.; U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, 316 Ark. 251, 872 S.W.2d 349 (1994) (relying on Powell).

The term limits imposed by Amendment 73 on members of Congress are, in effect, additional qualifications that are not authorized by the Constitution. By limiting the number of terms a member of Congress may serve, Amendment 73 effectively adds a qualification that is not enumerated in the Constitution. This is precisely the type of state-imposed qualification that the Qualifications Clauses are intended to prevent.

## II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE PROVISIONS OF AMENDMENT 73 ARE NOT SEVERABLE.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 is insufficient to save the provisions related to state officeholders. The ballot title and the Preamble to Amendment 73 presented the term limits for state and federal officeholders as a single, unified package. The voters had no opportunity to vote on the term limits for state officeholders separately from those for federal officeholders. As this Court has held, the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). In this case, it is impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the inclusion of term limits for federal officeholders.

The practice of coupling a legitimate objective (term limits for state officeholders) with one of doubtful legality (term limits for federal officeholders) is not fair to voters and should be discouraged. By presenting the two provisions as a single package, the sponsors of Amendment 73 created a situation in which the voters were not given a meaningful opportunity to vote on the term limits for state officeholders separately. Therefore, the entire Amendment 73 should be held invalid.

## III. THE CIRCUIT COURT CORRECTLY EXERCISED JURISDICTION OVER THE DECLARATORY JUDGMENT ACTION.

The circuit court correctly determined that the matter was justiciable and ripe for adjudication. The appellees had standing to challenge Amendment 73 because they had a legitimate interest in the controversy and were adversely impacted by the Amendment. The uncertainty over the application of Amendment 73 created a case and controversy that was ripe for decision. See UHS of Ark., Inc. v. City of Sherwood, 296 Ark. 97, 752 S.W.2d 36 (1988); Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983).

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
