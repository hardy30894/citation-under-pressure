**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS EFFECTIVE DATE**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has repeatedly held that "[s]tatutes are not to be given retroactive effect unless their language requires this result." *Lindh v. Murphy*, 521 U.S. 320, 327 (1997) (quoting *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 264 (1994)). The reinstatement statute contains no such language.

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion rests on an erroneous interpretation of *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001). The court below held that *St. Cyr* "did not address whether § 1231(a)(5) applies to aliens who reentered before its effective date." *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 428 F.3d 1198, 1204 (10th Cir. 2005). This misreads *St. Cyr*. The Supreme Court in *St. Cyr* explicitly declined to address the retroactivity of § 1231(a)(5), stating that the issue was not before it. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 320 n.38. The absence of any discussion of retroactivity in *St. Cyr* confirms that the statute’s application to pre-enactment re-entries is an open question.

Moreover, the reinstatement statute’s structure and purpose weigh against retroactive application. The statute’s text focuses on the alien’s *subsequent* illegal re-entry, not the prior deportation order. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). This suggests that Congress intended the statute to apply only to re-entries occurring after its effective date. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 273 ("[A] statute that does not disrupt settled expectations is less likely to be found retroactive."). The government’s contrary interpretation would disrupt the settled expectations of aliens like Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the U.S. decades before the statute’s enactment and have since built lives in this country.

**II. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE AS APPLIED TO FERNANDEZ-VARGAS**

Even if the reinstatement statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive. A law is retroactive if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913)). The reinstatement statute, as applied here, retroactively imposes severe penalties—deportation without any opportunity for relief—on conduct that occurred before its enactment.

Fernandez-Vargas’s re-entry in 1982 was not a cognizable offense under the reinstatement statute, which did not exist at the time. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270 ("[A] statute is retroactive if it ‘attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past.’") (quoting *Society for Propagation of Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814)). The statute’s application to his pre-enactment re-entry thus "attaches a new disability" to past conduct, rendering it impermissibly retroactive.

The government argues that Fernandez-Vargas had no "vested right" to remain in the U.S. because he entered illegally. *See Brief for Respondent at 25.* But this ignores the fact that, at the time of his re-entry, Fernandez-Vargas could have sought adjustment of status—a form of relief that was available to him in 2001. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1994).* The reinstatement statute retroactively strips him of this opportunity, effectively punishing him for conduct that was not unlawful at the time.

This Court has repeatedly held that laws imposing new legal consequences on past conduct are retroactive. *See, e.g., Miller v. Florida*, 482 U.S. 423, 430 (1987) ("A law is retroactive if it ‘changes the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date.’") (quoting *Weaver v. Graham*, 450 U.S. 24, 31 (1981)). The reinstatement statute does precisely that by retroactively barring Fernandez-Vargas from seeking adjustment of status, a right he possessed at the time of his re-entry.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the U.S. before its effective date, and its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
