The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. When a defendant withdraws from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, the government must still prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The D.C. Circuit's decision affirming the petitioners' convictions is in line with established precedent, which holds that the government must prove the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy within the statute of limitations period.

In Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391 (1957), the Supreme Court held that the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. The Court emphasized that the mere fact that a conspiracy existed at some point in the past is insufficient to establish liability; rather, the government must show that the conspiracy was ongoing and that the defendant was a part of it during the relevant period. Id. at 396-397.

Similarly, in United States v. Mejia, 82 F.3d 1032 (11th Cir. 1996), the Eleventh Circuit held that the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute of limitations period. The court noted that "the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member of it during that time." Id. at 1038 (citing Grunewald, 353 U.S. at 396-397).

The petitioners' argument that the government failed to prove their involvement in the conspiracy during the relevant period is unavailing. The trial court and the D.C. Circuit correctly applied the relevant case law, including Grunewald and Mejia, in determining that the government met its burden.

Furthermore, the D.C. Circuit's decision is consistent with the principle that the government bears the burden of proof in criminal cases. As the Supreme Court held in In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. at 364. In the context of conspiracy cases, this means that the government must prove the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy during the relevant period.

In conclusion, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit's decision affirming the petitioners' convictions is in line with established precedent, and the government's burden of proof was met in this case. The petitioners' claims should be denied.
