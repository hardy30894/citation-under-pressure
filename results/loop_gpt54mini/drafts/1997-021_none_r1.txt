Petitioner is entitled to relief for two independent reasons: the Board’s polling standard is inconsistent with the Act, and the Board’s application of that standard is unsupported by substantial evidence.

First, the Act does not authorize the Board to foreclose a narrowly tailored employee poll unless the employer already has proof sufficient to withdraw recognition. The National Labor Relations Act protects employees’ freedom of choice and the Board may not adopt a rule that makes a lawful poll practically unavailable. NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940); NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). Congress entrusted the Board with administering the statute, but not with erasing meaningful distinctions the Act leaves in place. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944); NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963).

A poll is not the same thing as withdrawal of recognition. A poll is an inquiry designed to test objective indications of employee sentiment before an employer takes the far more consequential step of ending recognition. The Board’s rule, however, collapses those two inquiries into one. If an employer already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal, there is little reason to permit a poll; if it does not, the Board’s rule forbids the poll. That regime deprives polling of independent utility and cannot be squared with the Act’s basic structure. This Court has not required the Board to embrace rules that rigidly ignore workplace realities or convert discretionary statutory administration into formalism divorced from practical consequences. NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1938); Southern Steamship Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47 (1942).

The better view is that the Board may regulate polling procedures, but it may not insist on a withdrawal-level showing before an employer may even ask employees where they stand. The Act favors stable bargaining relationships, but it also protects employees’ freedom to support or reject representation. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). A confidential, supervised poll serves that statutory balance by allowing an employer to confirm or dispel a genuine doubt before acting. Nothing in the Act compels the Board’s contrary rule.

Second, even under the Board’s own framework, the finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate. Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938). And reviewing courts must consider the whole record, not just the evidence favoring the agency. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951).

The record here showed significant employee disaffection. Before the poll, employees made statements suggesting the union had lost support among the bargaining unit. The company did not rely on rumor alone; it conducted a secret-ballot poll supervised by a neutral priest, and the result was 19 to 13 against the union. Yet the Board discounted this evidence by parsing individual statements in isolation and treating plainly probative evidence as inconclusive by fiat. That is not substantial-evidence review.

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that objective employee statements are relevant to an employer’s good-faith doubt. In NLRB v. Century Cement Mfg. Co., 208 F.2d 84 (2d Cir. 1953), the court held that employee expressions of dissatisfaction bear directly on majority support. In NLRB v. R. H. Oswald Co., 217 F.2d 193 (7th Cir. 1954), the court recognized that employers need not wait for formal decertification when objective evidence indicates loss of support. In NLRB v. Somerville Packing Co., 211 F.2d 574 (1st Cir. 1954), the court required the Board to evaluate employee statements in context rather than dismiss them mechanically. And in Columbia Envelope Co. v. NLRB, 151 F.2d 571, 575 (7th Cir. 1945), the court explained that the Board must address probative evidence rather than ignore it.

The Board’s contrary conclusion cannot be reconciled with the whole record. It discounted comments from employees who were in the unit when the poll was announced, minimized statements of dissatisfaction that obviously reflected anti-union sentiment, and refused to credit firsthand reports of workplace opinion. That approach is especially untenable where the employer’s poll was conducted with safeguards designed to eliminate coercion. The Board’s decision therefore rests on conjecture, not substantial evidence. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951); NLRB v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co., 337 U.S. 656, 659-60 (1949).

Finally, the Board’s order should be set aside because the company’s conduct was orderly and lawful under longstanding labor-law principles. The employees voted in secret; the poll was neutral and noncoercive; and the Board identified no unfair labor practice in the polling procedure itself. In these circumstances, insisting that the employer lacked a reasonable doubt despite strong record evidence to the contrary is arbitrary in substance, even if labeled factfinding. Allen-Bradley Co. v. NLRB, 286 U.S. 254, 260-61 (1932); Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 798-99 (1945).

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Board’s polling standard is not rationally consistent with the Act, and that the finding against Allentown Mack is unsupported by substantial evidence.
