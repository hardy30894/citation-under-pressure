**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT EXPRESSLY PERMITS A COURT TO ORDER POPULATION REDUCTION TO REMEDY EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit a court from ordering population reduction to remedy constitutional violations. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(1)(A), a court may "modify, suspend, or terminate" a consent decree or "issue any other appropriate order" necessary to remedy violations of the Constitution or federal laws. This statutory authority is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), which affirmed that the PLRA does not preclude injunctive relief for constitutional violations. The Court in *Salerno* emphasized that Congress intended to preserve the ability of federal courts to fashion appropriate remedies for constitutional wrongs.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized the broad remedial authority of federal courts in cases involving Eighth Amendment violations. In *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), the Court held that a district court has broad authority to fashion a remedy for conditions that violate the Eighth Amendment, including the power to order the release of prisoners. The Court stated that "the power to fashion a remedy for conditions that violate the Constitution is a power that the federal courts have always possessed." This authority is not diminished by the PLRA; rather, the PLRA provides a framework for ensuring that such remedies are not used to impose excessive or unreasonable conditions.

**II. THE ORDER IS NARROWLY TAILORED TO THE "PRIMARY CAUSE" OF THE CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS FOUND IN THIS CASE.**

The district court’s order to reduce the prison population is narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found in this case. Following a lengthy trial, a special panel of federal judges determined that serious overcrowding in California’s prisons was the "primary cause" of the violations of the Eighth Amendment. This finding is supported by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 (1976), which established that deliberate indifference to serious medical needs violates the Eighth Amendment. The record demonstrates that overcrowding directly contributed to the denial of adequate medical care and other constitutional rights.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 563 U.S. 439 (2011), affirmed that overcrowding necessitated population reduction. The Court noted that the district court found that the state was unable to remedy the constitutional violations through other means, such as construction, and that population reduction was the only effective remedy. The order requiring the release of between 38,000 and 46,000 inmates to bring the population within 137.5 percent of design capacity is a direct response to the finding that overcrowding was the primary cause of the violations. This order is not arbitrary; it is a necessary consequence of the court’s determination that the state’s current practices violate the Constitution.

**III. THE ORDER IS NOT "EXCESSIVE" OR "UNREASONABLE" UNDER THE PLRA.**

Under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(1)(A), a court may not issue an order that is "excessive" or "unreasonable." The order in this case is reasonable and does not impose an excessive burden on the state. The Supreme Court has held that prison regulations are valid if they are reasonably related to legitimate penological interests. *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78 (1987). The population reduction order is a reasonable means of ensuring that the state can provide constitutionally adequate care to its inmates.

The order is also consistent with the standards set forth in the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 563 F.3d 1001 (9th Cir. 2009). In that case, the court affirmed the district court’s authority to appoint a receiver to oversee the provision of health care in the prisons. The court found that the state’s failure to comply with prior orders and its inability to fund the receiver’s plans necessitated the court’s intervention. The population reduction order is a continuation of this intervention, designed to ensure that the state can comply with its constitutional obligations. The order is not excessive because it is limited to the amount necessary to remedy the constitutional violations, and it is reasonable because it is based on the findings of a special panel of federal judges.
