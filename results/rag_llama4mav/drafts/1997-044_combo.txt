The question presented is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The plain language of § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The issue is whether this provision allows debtors to discharge debts arising from their fraudulent conduct.

The lower courts have held that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A), and that the award of treble damages and attorney's fees in this case is therefore nondischargeable. However, the petitioner argues that this interpretation is overly broad and contradicts the "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Bankruptcy Code is intended to provide a "fresh start" for honest debtors. As the Court stated in Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick, 43 U.S. 202 (1844), the discharge in bankruptcy is intended to provide relief to the "honest citizen" who is "relieved from the burden of hopeless insolvency." However, this policy is not absolute, and the Court has also recognized that certain debts are not dischargeable due to their nature or the circumstances under which they were incurred.

In Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1878), the Court held that debts created by "fraud" within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Act are not dischargeable. The Court defined "fraud" as "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong." Id. at 709. This definition is controlling here, as § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge debts obtained by "actual fraud."

The petitioner's argument that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are dischargeable is unpersuasive. The Court has long recognized that statutory damages can be considered part of the debt incurred through fraudulent conduct. In United States v. Johnson, 319 U.S. 302 (1943), the Court held that treble damages awarded under the Emergency Price Control Act were not punitive, but rather a means of compensating the plaintiff for the harm suffered.

Furthermore, the Court has held that debts that are not dischargeable include those that arise from fraudulent conduct. In Moore v. Bay, 284 U.S. 4 (1931), the Court held that a trustee in bankruptcy can recover property transferred by the bankrupt in fraud of creditors. Similarly, in Miller v. Irving Trust Co., 296 U.S. 256 (1935), the Court held that a claim arising from the termination of a lease due to the bankrupt's default was not provable in bankruptcy.

The petitioner's reliance on the "fresh start" policy is misplaced. As the Court stated in Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358 (1964), the "fresh start" policy is not intended to provide a means for debtors to avoid liability for their fraudulent conduct. The Court has also recognized that certain debts, such as those arising from taxes or fiduciary duties, are not dischargeable due to their nature or the circumstances under which they were incurred. See Missouri State Life Insurance v. Jones, 290 U.S. 199 (1933); Bluthenthal v. Jones, 208 U.S. 64 (1908).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that debts resulting from fraud are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The award of treble damages and attorney's fees in this case is therefore nondischargeable. The judgment of the lower courts should be affirmed.
