ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Burnett Gave Habitat Adequate Notice of His Serious Health Condition.

An employee need not recite a diagnosis to trigger an employer's statutory obligations; he need only provide information reasonably sufficient to apprise the employer that a serious health condition may be involved. This principle is not novel. In analyzing an analogous notice requirement under the Longshoremen's and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, the Supreme Court held that formal notice is unnecessary where the employer already possesses actual knowledge of the relevant facts, because the purpose of any notice requirement is to ensure that the responsible party is fairly informed, not to erect a technical trap for the unwary employee. Voehl v. Indemnity Insurance Co. of North America, 288 U.S. 162 (1933). The same logic governs here. Burnett told Polo of his "weak bladder," his intent to see a doctor, his subsequent diagnosis of high PSA levels, his need to see a urologist, his forthcoming prostate biopsy, and — most tellingly — he analogized his own condition to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer. A jury could readily find that this cumulative disclosure "fairly apprised" Habitat of a serious health condition, even without a formal diagnosis being spoken aloud.

Moreover, the knowledge that Habitat's own agents possessed is properly imputed to the corporation itself. It has long been settled that notice to an agent, received in the course of his employment and touching a matter within the scope of his authority, is notice to the principal. American Surety Co. v. Pauly, 170 U.S. 133 (1898). Polo was Burnett's direct supervisor, and Hehr was a supervisor entrusted with receiving medical documentation under Habitat's own internal policy. Whatever Polo or Hehr knew — including the biopsy documentation stating that indications for the examination included "an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)" — is chargeable to Habitat as a matter of law. The district court's contrary conclusion that Burnett "failed to provide notice" as a matter of law ignored this settled agency principle and improperly resolved a disputed factual question against the non-movant.

That error is compounded by the well-established rule governing summary judgment itself. The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts contained in [the record] must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962). Here, the record — Polo's email scheduling Burnett's sick leave for doctor's appointments, the biopsy documentation given to Hehr, and Burnett's own statements comparing his illness to his brother-in-law's cancer — permits the reasonable inference that Habitat possessed sufficient knowledge of Burnett's condition. Summary judgment was improper because that inference, not the employer's preferred characterization of the facts, must control at this stage.

II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Regarding Habitat's Retaliatory Motive Independently Preclude Summary Judgment.

Even setting aside the notice question, the timing and sequence of Habitat's adverse actions raise a triable issue as to motive that summary judgment cannot resolve. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the timing of an employer's adverse action in relation to an employee's protected conduct is itself probative circumstantial evidence of unlawful motive. NLRB v. Link-Belt Co., 311 U.S. 584 (1941) (recognizing that the timing of discharges following protected activity supports an inference of unlawful motive). Here, Burnett disclosed his diagnosis and biopsy documentation to Hehr on January 7, 2004; that same day, Polo issued him a reprimand for "substandard work" after years without complaint. One week later, Polo issued two more reprimands, denied Burnett's requested transfer, called him a "loose cannon," and represented to Burnett that he was being terminated. A jury, not a court on summary judgment, must weigh whether this abrupt escalation reflects legitimate performance concerns or retaliatory animus tied to Burnett's medical disclosures.

The Supreme Court has cautioned against resolving questions of motive and intent through summary adjudication, observing that such issues are ill-suited to disposition without trial "where motive and intent play leading roles, the proof is largely in the hands of the alleged [wrongdoer], and hostile witnesses thicken the plot." Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). This admonition applies with equal force to Habitat's asserted justifications, which rest entirely on Polo's own characterization of events that Burnett disputes. Similarly, the Court has recognized that employer conduct which reasonably tends to discourage the exercise of protected rights may itself permit an inference of unlawful motive without further proof of specific intent. NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221 (1963). Finally, it is the movant, not the non-movant, who bears the burden of demonstrating the absence of a genuine factual dispute. Cities Service Oil Co. v. Dunlap, 308 U.S. 208 (1939). Habitat failed to carry that burden here, and the district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for trial.
