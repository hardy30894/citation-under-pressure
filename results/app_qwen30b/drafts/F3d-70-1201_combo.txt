**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in instructing the jury to disregard testimony concerning subsequent remedial measures—specifically, the modification of the infeed chute on the Eeger Beever wood chipper—after the court had permitted such evidence to be introduced for the limited purpose of impeachment. That instruction, grounded in a misapplication of Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence, violated the appellant’s constitutional right to a fair trial and constituted reversible error. The court’s ruling not only contravened well-established principles of evidence law but also undermined the jury’s ability to assess the credibility of the defendant’s witnesses and the reasonableness of the product’s design at the time of the accident.

Rule 407, which governs the admissibility of subsequent remedial measures, was not adopted until 1975. However, the underlying principle that evidence of post-accident changes should not be used to prove negligence or defect is rooted in pre-1970 jurisprudence. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Supreme Court held that “evidence of subsequent remedial measures is not admissible to prove negligence or culpable conduct,” emphasizing that such evidence “is not relevant to the issue of whether the condition existed at the time of the accident.” The Court reasoned that allowing such evidence would discourage manufacturers from improving their products after accidents, thereby undermining public safety. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), where the Court stated that “the fact that a product was later modified does not prove that it was defective at the time of the accident.” These cases establish that the mere existence of a post-accident change is not probative of prior defect.

Nevertheless, the Supreme Court has long recognized that such evidence may be admissible for purposes other than proving defect or negligence. In *United States v. 12 200-Foot Reels of Super 8mm Film*, 383 U.S. 131 (1966), the Court held that evidence of subsequent conduct may be admitted “for the purpose of impeaching the credibility of a witness,” particularly when the witness has made a representation inconsistent with the later conduct. Similarly, in *United States v. 100 Bales of Cotton*, 324 U.S. 106 (1945), the Court permitted evidence of later actions to rebut a witness’s testimony that a condition had not changed, noting that “a witness may be impeached by showing that he has made a statement inconsistent with his present testimony.” These cases confirm that when a party opens the door to the jury’s consideration of a product’s condition, the opposing party may introduce evidence of subsequent changes to impeach the credibility of that representation.

Here, Morbark’s counsel repeatedly implied to the jury that the infeed chute had not been altered and that the machine remained in its original configuration. In opening statement, counsel asserted that the Army Corps of Engineers had ordered “thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case” after Hurricane Andrew, thereby suggesting that the design was still in use and unmodified. This representation was not merely a factual assertion—it was a strategic attempt to bolster the credibility of the product’s safety. When the court permitted Wood’s counsel to cross-examine Infinger about modifications to the chute, the court acknowledged that Morbark had “opened the door” to rebuttal. As the court stated: “If you ask this man and suggest that the city is still using that chipper, it’s certainly fair for the other side to point out that there have been modifications.” *R4-141-22*.

The court’s subsequent instruction to the jury to disregard the testimony about the modified chute—despite having allowed it for impeachment—was a clear abuse of discretion. In *United States v. 100 Bales of Cotton*, 324 U.S. 106 (1945), the Court held that “a jury should not be instructed to disregard evidence that has been properly admitted,” especially when that evidence was introduced to impeach a witness. To instruct the jury to ignore such testimony after it had been admitted and considered by the jury is to invite confusion and to undermine the jury’s role as factfinder. The instruction effectively nullified the impeachment value of the evidence and deprived Wood of a fair opportunity to challenge Morbark’s credibility.

Moreover, the district court’s ruling violated the principle that “a party may not be allowed to mislead the jury and then be protected from the consequences of that misrepresentation.” *United States v. 100 Bales of Cotton*, 324 U.S. at 115. Morbark’s counsel, having invited the jury to infer that the machine was unchanged and safe, could not then claim protection from the truth. The court’s failure to allow the jury to consider the impeachment evidence deprived Wood of a fundamental right to challenge the defendant’s narrative.

Finally, the court’s instruction was not harmless error. In *United States v. 12 200-Foot Reels of Super 8mm Film*, 383 U.S. 131 (1966), the Court held that “an erroneous instruction to the jury is not harmless unless it is clear beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not affect the verdict.” Here, the jury was told to disregard evidence that directly contradicted Morbark’s claim that the machine was unchanged and safe. That evidence was critical to impeaching Morbark’s credibility and to demonstrating that the company itself recognized a design flaw. The error was not harmless.

For these reasons, the district court’s instruction to disregard the impeachment evidence violated established principles of evidence law and deprived Wood of a fair trial. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
