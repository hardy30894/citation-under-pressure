Petitioner Cohen’s debt for rent overcharges should have been discharged because § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts only debts “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by” actual fraud, not every liability that later follows from a fraudulent act. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court has repeatedly construed discharge exceptions narrowly in light of the Bankruptcy Code’s fresh-start policy. Kawaauhau v. Geiger, 523 U.S. 57, 62 (1998); Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). Congress, however, chose the limiting phrase “to the extent obtained by,” and that language cabins nondischargeability to the value actually acquired through fraud. Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 66 (1995); Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213, 218 (1998).

Under the better reading of the statute, the only nondischargeable debt is the amount fraudulently obtained—the excess rent itself. The Ninth Circuit in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 199 (9th Cir. 1991), correctly focused on the statutory text and held that punitive damages were dischargeable because they were not themselves “obtained by” fraud. That construction preserves the textual limitation Congress wrote into § 523(a)(2)(A). Id. at 198-99. By contrast, decisions extending nondischargeability to all consequential liabilities improperly collapse the words “to the extent obtained by” into a blanket exception for any claim with a fraud predicate. See Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. at 218-19 (describing the competing approaches). The statute does not say “any debt arising from fraud”; it says debt “obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A).

The bankruptcy and lower courts also erred in treating treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs as part of the fraudulently “obtained” debt. A debtor does not “obtain” punitive or multiple damages from a victim at the time of the fraudulent transaction; those amounts are imposed later as a statutory consequence of litigation. In re Levy, 951 F.2d at 199. Section 523(a)(2)(A) therefore makes nondischargeable the fraudulent gain, not every later-added sanction. Id.; see also Toms v. Hart, 897 F.2d 989, 994 (9th Cir. 1990) (limiting § 523(a)(2)(A) to the amount actually obtained by fraud).

This Court’s precedents on scienter do not justify enlarging the debt beyond the amount obtained. While actual fraud may be proven by circumstantial evidence, intent to deceive cannot be inferred to create a broader damages exception than the statute allows. Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. at 70-71; Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. at 291. Cohen’s conduct may have violated rent-control law, but bankruptcy nondischargeability is a federal question governed by the Code’s text, not the breadth of a state consumer-fraud remedy. See Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48, 54-55 (1979). The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act may authorize treble damages and fees, but § 523(a)(2)(A) incorporates only debts “obtained by” fraud. Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. at 223-24.

Nor does policy support denying discharge of the entire award. The Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” is reserved for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. at 286-87. Cohen’s liability for the rent overcharge itself is properly excepted if the Court finds actual fraud; but extending nondischargeability to statutory multiples and fee-shifting awards would transform a narrow fraud exception into a general anti-debt collection device. That is not what Congress enacted. See Kawaauhau v. Geiger, 523 U.S. at 62-63.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only the amount actually obtained by fraud, not treble damages, attorney’s fees, or costs. The judgment should be reversed insofar as it declared those additional sums nondischargeable.
