# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1231(a)(6) Does Not Authorize Indefinite Detention

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute permits the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely—must be answered in the negative. Section 1231(a)(6) provides only that a removable alien "may be detained beyond the removal period," but it does not say for how long. Where a statute's text is ambiguous as to duration, this Court has long declined to read into it a grant of unlimited executive power over liberty. In *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), the predecessor supervision statute similarly provided that an alien "shall, pending eventual deportation, be subject to supervision under regulations prescribed by the Attorney General." This Court construed that open-ended supervisory authority narrowly rather than read it as license for unbounded executive control, precisely because a broader reading would raise serious questions about the scope of the Attorney General's power over aliens who could not be removed. The same interpretive caution applies here. Nothing in the text of § 1231(a)(6) compels—much less requires—a reading that would permit detention to continue in perpetuity merely because removal has not yet, and may never, occur.

## II. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires a Reasonable-Time Limitation

Even if the statutory text were less clear, the canon of constitutional avoidance resolves any doubt against the government's reading. This Court has consistently required that ambiguous immigration statutes be construed to avoid serious constitutional problems rather than to create them. Indefinite civil detention of a person who has been lawfully admitted to the United States implicates the basic liberty protected by the Due Process Clause. As *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), makes clear, the government's power to expel aliens from the country is a distinct power from the power to imprison them, and imprisonment as punishment "could not be inflicted except by judicial sentence." 163 U.S. 228. Post-removal-period detention under § 1231(a)(6) is civil, not punitive, and is justified only by its connection to the goal of accomplishing removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, that connection is severed, and what remains is detention untethered to any statutory purpose—precisely the kind of open-ended confinement that raises the constitutional concerns this Court's precedents instruct should be avoided through construction, not confronted head-on.

## III. Precedent Involving Excludable Aliens Does Not Control the Detention of Admitted Aliens

The government's reliance on cases involving aliens who never effected entry into the United States is misplaced. In *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), and its companion case *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193 (1958), this Court held that aliens paroled pending an admissibility determination remain, for legal purposes, outside the country—their parole "did not alter her status as an excluded alien or otherwise bring her 'within the United States.'" 357 U.S. 185. That "entry fiction" reflects the unique constitutional posture of aliens who have never been admitted. Zadvydas, by contrast, was lawfully admitted as a resident alien decades ago. He stands in a fundamentally different constitutional position than the aliens in *Leng May Ma* and *Quan*, or in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), which likewise "dealt with aliens who had been stopped at the border, and had not effected entry into the United States." An admitted alien's liberty interest is not diminished merely because the government later obtains an order of removal that cannot, in fact, be executed.

## IV. Even Detention Statutes Upheld by This Court Have Been Bounded by Time and Individualized Process

Where this Court has sustained detention of aliens facing deportation, it has done so only in the context of detention *during* deportation proceedings, subject to bail determinations turning on individualized risk. In *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), the Court reviewed detention without bail of an alien facing deportation as a Communist Party member, but even there the inquiry turned on "the likelihood that the alien would appear when ordered to do so under the circumstances as developed in the habeas corpus hearing"—a temporally bounded, individualized assessment tied to the pendency of removal proceedings. That framework cannot be stretched to justify detention that persists indefinitely after the removal period has expired and removal itself has become not merely delayed but unforeseeable.

## V. A Reasonable-Time Construction, With a Presumptively Reasonable Period, Best Reconciles the Statute and the Constitution

The proper construction reads § 1231(a)(6), "in light of the Constitution's demands," to limit "an alien's post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien's removal from the United States," and to preclude "indefinite detention." Once "removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." A six-month presumptive period after which the alien may demand that the government justify continued confinement by evidence of a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future—shrinking as detention lengthens—faithfully implements the statute's purpose of "assuring the alien's presence at the moment of removal" without transforming a removal statute into an instrument of permanent civil confinement.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed, and the statute construed to forbid Zadvydas's continued, indefinite detention.
