# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Preempts OSHA Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Statutory Authority Over the Specific Working Conditions at Issue

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act provides that OSHA's authority does not extend to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies... *exercise* statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1) (emphasis added). This Court has long recognized that the touchstone of preemption under this provision is actual exercise of regulatory authority, not the mere existence of jurisdictional power. See *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Ass'n*, 505 U.S. 88, 96 (1992) (state law preempted when OSHA "has promulgated" standards addressing the working conditions at issue).

The Fifth Circuit's categorical approach—holding that the Coast Guard possesses "exclusive authority over the working conditions of seamen" simply because it has general statutory authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2—inverts this analysis. The court below relied on its prior decisions in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), for the proposition that "OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation." *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826-27. But neither *Clary* nor *Donovan* addressed the critical distinction at issue here: whether the Coast Guard's authority over *uninspected* vessels—vessels for which the Coast Guard has issued only minimal, targeted safety regulations—triggers the same blanket preemption applicable to comprehensively regulated *inspected* vessels.

## II. The Coast Guard's Minimal Regulation of Uninspected Vessels Cannot Displace OSHA's Jurisdiction

The record establishes that Rig 52 was an uninspected vessel. The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are narrow and specific, addressing only discrete safety equipment: fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire flame arrestors, ventilation systems, and emergency signaling devices. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.*, 26.01 *et seq.* These regulations bear no relationship to the comprehensive occupational safety and health standards Congress required the Coast Guard to promulgate for inspected vessels.

Section 4(b)(1)'s preemption analysis must be conducted with respect to the *specific working conditions* at issue, not industry-wide. The statute speaks of preemption "with respect to" particular "working conditions"—not blanket exemption for an entire category of employment. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Where, as here, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over the specific hazard giving rise to the citation—here, blowout-prevention and well-control procedures during drilling operations—no conflict exists between OSHA's citation and any actual Coast Guard regulatory scheme.

The court below acknowledged that the Coast Guard "is no stranger to uninspected vessels" and catalogued the narrow categories of equipment-based regulations applicable to such vessels. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc. v. Herman, No. 99-60124, slip op. at 5-6 (5th Cir. June 2, 2000). Yet nothing in that catalogue addresses the drilling and well-control practices that caused the explosion on Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit's own regulatory summary thus undermines its ultimate holding: the very absence of comprehensive Coast Guard regulation of drilling operations aboard uninspected vessels demonstrates that no "exercise" of authority under Section 4(b)(1) has occurred with respect to the working conditions OSHA sought to regulate here.

## III. Extending Clary and Donovan to Uninspected Vessels Contradicts Congress's Purpose in Enacting the OSH Act

Congress enacted the OSH Act "to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions." 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). This broad remedial purpose counsels against expansive readings of statutory exemptions. The Fifth Circuit's approach would create precisely the regulatory vacuum Congress sought to avoid: workers aboard uninspected vessels would receive neither the comprehensive protection Congress intended the Coast Guard to provide for inspected vessels, nor the fallback protection of OSHA.

The court below invoked *Donovan*'s concern that overlapping regulation might produce "the anomaly... of steaming in and out of OSHA coverage." 720 F.2d at 829. But the opposite anomaly is equally troubling: workers "steaming in and out" of *all* meaningful occupational safety coverage, protected by neither agency, simply because their vessel happens to be uninspected. Congress could not have intended Section 4(b)(1) to create such gaps in protection.

Moreover, *Clary* itself provides no support for the blanket rule the Fifth Circuit derived from it. As the court below conceded, "there is no indication from *Clary* that the barge in that case was inspected." Mallard, slip op. at 4. *Clary*'s holding rested on the specific OSHA regulation at issue there—a "color coding" requirement for a steel deck plate—not on any considered analysis of the inspected/uninspected distinction. 609 F.2d at 1121. Stare decisis principles do not require extending dicta-adjacent holdings to circumstances the original court never considered. See *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399 (1821) (general expressions in an opinion are to be taken in connection with the case in which they are used).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Fifth Circuit and hold that OSHA retains jurisdiction to issue citations for working conditions aboard uninspected vessels where the Coast Guard has not exercised comprehensive regulatory authority over those specific conditions.
