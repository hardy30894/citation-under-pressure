# ARGUMENT

## I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE CONSTITUTION ESTABLISH AN EXCLUSIVE LIST OF ELIGIBILITY REQUIREMENTS FOR MEMBERS OF CONGRESS

Article I, Section 2 provides that Representatives must be twenty-five years old, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the state; Article I, Section 3 imposes parallel requirements for Senators. These provisions do not merely establish minimum floors—they constitute the complete and exclusive roster of qualifications that may be imposed on candidates for federal office.

This Court settled the analogous question for presidential qualifications in **Powell v. McCormack**, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), holding that Congress itself lacked power to add qualifications for its own membership beyond those enumerated in the Constitution. The Court explained that the Framers "adopted the fundamental principle that the people should choose whom they please to govern them," and that allowing additional qualifications would "eas[e] the difficulty of establishing an oligarchy" contrary to the Framers' design. *Id.* at 547. If Congress—the body most directly accountable to the people who elect its members—cannot add qualifications, individual states surely cannot do so unilaterally.

The historical record confirms this exclusivity. As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized below, "the action finally taken by the framers of the constitution, following exhaustive debates, is strong evidence that term limits for senators and representatives was rejected." The Framers considered and rejected proposals for durational residency requirements and rotation-in-office provisions, settling instead on the fixed qualifications ultimately adopted. This deliberate choice reflects a considered judgment that uniform, minimal qualifications should govern access to federal office nationwide.

## II. ALLOWING STATES TO ADD QUALIFICATIONS WOULD FRACTURE THE UNIFORM NATIONAL CHARACTER OF CONGRESS

Members of Congress are not creatures of their individual states alone; they are, as the court below aptly recognized, part of a "national team" whose members "pass laws which affect not only their own state, but all the states." This national character requires uniform qualifications across all fifty states—a requirement incompatible with a patchwork of state-imposed restrictions.

This Court's decision in **U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton** must be understood against the backdrop of **McCulloch v. Maryland**, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), which established that the federal government's authority derives from the people of the United States as a whole, not from the states as sovereign entities acting individually. Chief Justice Marshall's reasoning that the states cannot exercise powers that would allow them "to burden" or control federal instrumentalities applies with equal force here: allowing Arkansas to add qualifications for its congressional delegation would permit a single state to diminish the rights of the entire national electorate to consider candidates otherwise eligible under the Constitution.

The structural argument finds further support in **Powell**, where this Court emphasized that the Qualifications Clauses were designed to prevent "a select group of citizens" from exercising "an eternal and irrevocable veto" over the electorate's choices. 395 U.S. at 541 n.76 (quoting House debate materials). Permitting states to impose de facto disqualifications—whether through direct exclusion or through ballot-access restrictions that achieve the same result—would recreate precisely the danger the Framers sought to eliminate, merely relocating the "select group" from Congress to individual state electorates acting through initiative.

## III. AMENDMENT 73'S BALLOT-ACCESS MECHANISM IS A DISQUALIFICATION IN SUBSTANCE AND CANNOT EVADE CONSTITUTIONAL SCRUTINY

Petitioners' characterization of Section 3 as merely regulating "ballot access" rather than imposing qualifications is a distinction without constitutional significance. The practical effect of denying a candidate placement on the ballot is functionally identical to declaring that candidate ineligible for office. As the Arkansas Supreme Court recognized, "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp"—and it is equally too fine a point to escape constitutional analysis.

This Court's ballot-access jurisprudence confirms that substance controls over form. In **Anderson v. Celebrezze**, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), cited approvingly by the court below, this Court recognized that state regulations governing ballot access implicate fundamental associational and voting rights precisely because such regulations can effectively exclude candidates from meaningful electoral competition. *Id.* at 786-88. A state cannot achieve through the back door of ballot regulation what it is forbidden from achieving through the front door of direct disqualification.

## IV. STATES RETAIN NO RESERVED POWER TO ADD QUALIFICATIONS FOR FEDERAL OFFICE

Petitioners' invocation of the Tenth Amendment misunderstands the nature of state power regarding federal offices. States do not possess inherent authority over the structure of the federal government from which the power to regulate qualifications might be "reserved." As **McCulloch v. Maryland** teaches, the federal government—including the qualifications for its own members—emanates from the people of the United States, not from the states in their independent sovereign capacities.

The Arkansas Supreme Court's own reasoning, though ultimately expressed in the context of severability, illuminates the core constitutional principle: "Uniformity of qualifications is paramount, and individual states are not free to engraft variations. The terms for members of Congress can be limited only by amending the United States Constitution." This is precisely correct. Any change to the qualifications for federal office must proceed through Article V's amendment process, which requires the assent of three-fourths of the states—not through unilateral action by a single state's electorate.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court insofar as it held that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution.
