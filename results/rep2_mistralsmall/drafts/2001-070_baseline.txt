**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF OFFENDERS WITH MENTAL RETARDATION.**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted by reference to “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). In assessing contemporary values, this Court accords “great weight” to the actions of state legislatures and juries. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 312 (2002). The Court has also considered the views of “professional organizations, sentencing juries, and state legislatures.” *Id.* at 316.

Here, the Commonwealth submits that the execution of mentally retarded offenders does not violate the Eighth Amendment. The Court in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), declined to find such executions unconstitutional, and at the time of Atkins’ offense, only two states had categorically prohibited the execution of the mentally retarded. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311. The Court in *Atkins* acknowledged that “much has changed” since *Penry*, including the fact that a majority of states now prohibit such executions. *Id.* at 314-15. However, the Court did not hold that the Eighth Amendment categorically prohibits the execution of mentally retarded offenders. Instead, it remanded for a determination of whether Atkins was in fact mentally retarded. *Id.* at 317-18.

The Court’s decision in *Atkins* does not control this case. The question presented in *Atkins* was whether the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded offenders. The Court answered that question in the affirmative. *Id.* at 321. However, the Court’s holding was limited to the facts of that case, and it did not address the broader question of whether the Eighth Amendment categorically prohibits such executions. *Id.* at 317-18. The Court’s decision in *Atkins* is therefore not controlling in this case, where the question presented is whether the execution of mentally retarded persons is prohibited by the Eighth Amendment.

**II. THE VIRGINIA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED *PENRY* IN AFFIRMING ATKINS’ DEATH SENTENCE.**

The Virginia Supreme Court relied on *Penry* in rejecting Atkins’ contention that he could not be sentenced to death because he is mentally retarded. *Atkins v. Commonwealth*, 260 Va. 375, 380-81, 535 S.E.2d 746, 749 (2000). The Court in *Penry* held that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. 492 U.S. at 335-36. The Court reasoned that “the goals of retribution and deterrence” are not served by executing mentally retarded offenders, but it declined to find such executions unconstitutional. *Id.* at 327-28.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in affirming Atkins’ death sentence. The Court in *Penry* declined to find the execution of mentally retarded offenders unconstitutional, and the Virginia Supreme Court followed that precedent. *Atkins*, 260 Va. at 380-81, 535 S.E.2d at 749. The Court in *Atkins* did not overrule *Penry*, and the Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in affirming Atkins’ death sentence.

**III. THE JURY’S SENTENCING VERDICT WAS SUPPORTED BY THE EVIDENCE AND FREE FROM LEGAL ERROR.**

The jury’s sentencing verdict was supported by the evidence and free from legal error. The jury heard testimony from a forensic psychologist who opined that Atkins was mildly mentally retarded. However, the State presented evidence rebutting that opinion, and the jury was free to credit the State’s evidence over the defense’s evidence. *See Penry*, 492 U.S. at 327-28 (noting that the jury is free to credit the State’s evidence over the defense’s evidence).

The jury’s sentencing verdict was also supported by the evidence of Atkins’ culpability. The jury heard evidence that Atkins abducted, robbed, and murdered Eric Nesbitt. The jury heard evidence that Atkins planned the robbery, drove Nesbitt to an automated teller machine, and shot Nesbitt multiple times. The jury heard evidence that Atkins fled the scene and attempted to cover up his crime. The jury’s sentencing verdict was supported by this evidence and free from legal error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
