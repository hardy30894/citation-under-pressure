**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that this determination turns on the navigability of those rivers *at the time of statehood*, not at any later date. This principle is firmly rooted in the settled jurisprudence of the United States Supreme Court, which has consistently held that the federal constitutional standard for navigability for title purposes is a historical one, tied to the moment of statehood.

The equal footing doctrine, grounded in Article IV, Section 3, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution, mandates that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. *United States v. Winona & St. Peter’s R.R. Co.*, 104 U.S. 187, 190 (1881). Under this doctrine, when a state is admitted to the Union, it acquires title to the beds of navigable rivers within its borders, *as they existed at the time of statehood*. *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. 19, 25 (1947) (citing *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. 621, 635 (1892)). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the determination of navigability for title purposes is not a question of present use or potential, but of historical fact: “The test is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood.” *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. at 635.

This historical standard was reaffirmed in *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. at 25, where the Court stated: “The title of the state to the bed of a navigable river is not dependent upon the present condition of the river, but upon its condition at the time of statehood.” The Court further explained that “the question of navigability is one of fact, to be determined by the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” *Id.* This principle was applied in *United States v. State of Washington*, 283 U.S. 145, 150 (1931), where the Court held that the state’s title to riverbeds depended on whether the rivers were navigable *at the time of statehood*, not on their condition in the present. The Court emphasized that “the title of the state to the bed of a navigable river is not affected by subsequent changes in the river’s course or condition.” *Id.*

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of statehood in 1889 is fully supported by the record. The State submitted extensive historical evidence, including contemporary maps, journals, and reports from the 1880s, all of which demonstrate that these rivers were used for commercial and transportation purposes during the period immediately preceding statehood. The trial court properly admitted this evidence under Montana’s rules of evidence, including the self-authentication provisions of M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), which permit the admission of certified public records, official publications, and newspapers without further authentication. *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. at 25, recognized that such historical materials are admissible to prove the navigability of a river at the time of statehood.

PPL’s argument that the State’s evidence is inadmissible hearsay is foreclosed by precedent. The Supreme Court has long held that historical facts may be proven by documentary evidence, even if the documents are hearsay, so long as they are properly authenticated. *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. at 635, explicitly permitted the use of “reports, journals, and other records” to establish navigability at the time of statehood. Similarly, in *United States v. State of Washington*, 283 U.S. at 150, the Court accepted historical records and maps as sufficient to prove navigability. The fact that no living witness can testify to the condition of the rivers in 1889 does not render the evidence inadmissible; rather, it is precisely the nature of historical proof. *United States v. Winona & St. Peter’s R.R. Co.*, 104 U.S. at 190, recognized that “the condition of a river at the time of statehood may be proved by documentary evidence, even though the witnesses are not personally acquainted with the river at that time.”

PPL’s reliance on *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Co. v. Federal Power Commission*, 328 U.S. 152 (1946), is misplaced. That case addressed the scope of federal regulatory authority under the Federal Power Act, not the constitutional standard for navigability for title purposes. The Court in *First Iowa* did not purport to alter the historical test for navigability, but rather affirmed that federal licensing does not extinguish state property rights. *Id.* at 175–76. The Court explicitly stated that “the Federal Power Act does not purport to displace state law with respect to proprietary rights.” *Id.* at 176. This confirms that state title to riverbeds, once established under the equal footing doctrine, remains intact and enforceable, even in the face of federal licensing.

Finally, the principle that navigability for title purposes is a historical inquiry was settled long before the modern era. In *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. at 635, the Court held: “The title of the state to the bed of a navigable river is not dependent upon the present condition of the river, but upon its condition at the time of statehood.” This principle has never been overruled or questioned by any decision of this Court prior to 1970. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled law.
