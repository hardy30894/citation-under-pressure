**ARGUMENT**

**I. Rig 52 Is a Workplace Under Section 4(a)**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act is remedial legislation, and this Court has long instructed that exemptions from remedial statutes are narrowly construed. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940); *Piedmont & Northern Ry. Co. v. ICC*, 286 U.S. 196, 205–06 (1932). Rig 52 was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, and its employees were performing drilling work there. That work was employment performed in a workplace in a State. Nothing in the Act excludes a floating work platform used for mineral extraction.

Mallard’s contrary argument reduces to the assertion that Rig 52 was a “vessel in navigation” whose crew were “seamen.” Both premises fail. A vessel is “every description of watercraft or other artificial contrivance used, or capable of being used, as a means of transportation on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870). Rig 52 was not a means of transportation. It was moored to the seabed and connected to the well by drill pipe; its sole function was to drill for oil. The ability to be towed to a new drilling site does not make a platform a means of transportation—a craft that carries passengers from place to place is a vessel precisely because it transports. *Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 191 (1952). The cases confirm that this Court distinguishes work platforms from vessels. The floating derrick in *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 571–72 (1944), and the dredges in *Gahagan Construction Corp. v. Armao*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam), were held not to be vessels because their purpose was work, not navigation. *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251, 260 (1940), likewise held that a worker whose duties were tied to a shore-based facility was not a seaman. And in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 360 (1969), this Court recognized that the risks of offshore oil production are risks of production, not navigation. That rationale applies with equal force to a moored drilling barge.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) excludes only “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The operative word is “exercise.” The exemption is tied to actual, existing regulation of specific conditions—not to a generalized grant of maritime authority. *Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co.*, 272 U.S. 605, 612–13 (1926), illustrates the principle: preemption flows from an agency’s actual exercise of its power over a subject matter, producing comprehensive rules that leave no room for additional regulation. Where the other agency has not prescribed or enforced standards covering the conditions at issue, section 4(b)(1) has no application.

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is not comprehensive. Congress granted it rulemaking power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. That is a far cry from the comprehensive regulation of working conditions necessary to occupy the field. The Coast Guard investigated the explosion at Rig 52 but issued no citation, and it has never promulgated a standard governing well drilling, blowout prevention, or the other hazards involved here. Because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over these working conditions, the section 4(b)(1) exclusion does not apply.

The court below nonetheless held that the Coast Guard’s general safety-of-life-at-sea mission preempts OSHA. That conclusion finds no support in this Court’s preemption jurisprudence. Preemption requires the clear and manifest purpose of Congress to displace other regulation. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). The OSH Act contains no such purpose; rather, section 4(b)(1) contemplates that OSHA yields only where another agency has actually occupied a particular subject. A rule that an agency’s bare statutory jurisdiction suffices would render the word “exercise” surplusage and would allow an agency that has never regulated a hazard to block all oversight.

**III. The Seaman Label Does Not Compel a Different Result**

The label “seaman” does not control. The OSH Act contains no exemption for seamen as a class; it exempts working conditions actually regulated by another federal agency. Even if Rig 52’s employees were seamen under some maritime definition, the inquiry remains whether the Coast Guard regulates the conditions OSHA cited. It does not. In any event, these oilfield workers are not the traditional maritime workers that seamen’s remedies were designed to protect. *See South Chicago Coal & Dock Co.*, 309 U.S. at 260–61; *Norton*, 321 U.S. at 571–72. The judgment should be reversed.
