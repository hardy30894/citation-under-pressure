**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY AFFIRMED THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF MASSACHUSETTS’ BUFFER ZONE LAW UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT, AS APPLIED TO THE STATES THROUGH THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law, enacted under *Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½*, as a valid time, place, and manner regulation that is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The law does not suppress speech based on its message or viewpoint; rather, it serves the compelling state interest in ensuring the safe and unimpeded access of patients and staff to reproductive health care facilities. This is precisely the kind of regulation the Supreme Court has long recognized as permissible under the First Amendment.

In *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000), the Court upheld a Colorado statute that prohibited “any person from knowingly approaching within 8 feet of any individual entering or leaving a health care facility” where abortion services were provided. The Court emphasized that “the sidewalk outside a health care facility is a public forum,” and that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech in such a forum so long as the regulation is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication.” Id. at 715–16. The Court found that the Colorado law was “content-neutral” because it applied equally to all speech, regardless of message, and that it was “narrowly tailored” to serve the state’s interest in protecting access to health care. Id. at 716.

The Massachusetts law at issue here is even more narrowly tailored than the statute in *Hill*. While Colorado’s buffer zone was 100 feet, Massachusetts’ is 35 feet—less than one-third the size. The law applies only to the immediate vicinity of entrances, exits, and driveways, and is clearly marked with signs and painted arcs. It does not prohibit speech altogether; it merely restricts proximity to the facility’s access points. The law is content-neutral: it applies equally to pro-life, pro-choice, religious, and secular speakers. It is viewpoint-neutral: no speaker is excluded based on the message they convey. And it is narrowly tailored: the 35-foot radius is a reasonable compromise between protecting patient access and preserving the right to engage in expressive activity.

The First Circuit correctly applied *Hill* and affirmed the district court’s conclusion that the law leaves ample alternative channels of communication. The plaintiffs may still speak, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and wear expressive garments. They may do so from a distance, using amplification devices, and they may engage in outreach from adjacent public sidewalks. As the First Circuit observed, “adequate communicative channels remain available” even with the buffer zone. McCullen v. Coakley, 759 F. Supp. 2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010). The fact that some plaintiffs believe their message is less effective from 35 feet away does not render the law unconstitutional. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (“The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to communicate one’s views at all times and places, or in any manner one might wish.”).

The plaintiffs’ claim that the law is overbroad or vague fails for the same reason. The statute is clear on its face: it prohibits entering or remaining within a 35-foot zone around entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities. The Attorney General’s guidance letter, while not binding, merely clarifies the law’s application and does not alter its meaning. *See United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 472 (2010) (“A statute is not unconstitutionally vague merely because it is interpreted by executive guidance.”). The plaintiffs had ample opportunity to challenge the letter earlier, but they did not. Their belated attempt to reframe the case after more than two years is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *See United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011).

**II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT DID NOT ERR IN APPLYING HILL V. COLORADO, AND THAT RULING SHOULD NOT BE LIMITED OR OVERRULED**

The plaintiffs’ argument that *Hill* should be limited or overruled is not only unsupported by precedent but also contrary to the settled principles of First Amendment jurisprudence. *Hill* is not an outlier; it is a cornerstone of the Court’s recognition that public sidewalks adjacent to health care facilities are not immune from reasonable regulation to protect access.

The Court in *Hill* explicitly acknowledged that “the State has a compelling interest in ensuring that individuals seeking access to health care facilities are not intimidated or obstructed by protestors.” 530 U.S. at 715. This interest is no less compelling today. The Massachusetts legislature, acting in its police power, has a legitimate interest in preventing harassment, intimidation, and obstruction of patients and staff. The buffer zone is a reasonable means of achieving that end. The law does not ban speech; it merely relocates it to a space where it can be heard without interfering with access.

The plaintiffs’ claim that the law is “viewpoint-based” because it applies only to abortion clinics is meritless. The law applies to all reproductive health care facilities, regardless of the services provided. It is not based on the content or viewpoint of the speech, but on the location and function of the facility. *See *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715 (“The law is not directed at any particular message or speaker.”). The fact that the law is aimed at a controversial issue does not make it unconstitutional. *See *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968) (“The government may regulate speech when the regulation is justified without reference to the content of the speech.”).

The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is misplaced.
