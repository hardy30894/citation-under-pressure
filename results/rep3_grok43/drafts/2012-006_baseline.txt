The Due Process Clause places upon the prosecution the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). In a conspiracy prosecution, that burden encompasses proof that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396 (1957) (the statute begins to run from the last overt act); see also United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910).

Once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove continued membership beyond a reasonable doubt. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (affirmative act of withdrawal terminates liability for subsequent acts of co-conspirators); United States v. Borelli, 336 F.2d 376, 388 (2d Cir. 1964) (Friendly, J.) (government bears burden to show defendant “was still a member of the conspiracy” within the limitations period). The same allocation applies under the Fifth Amendment when the limitations defense is raised. See United States v. Grammatikos, 633 F.2d 1015, 1022-23 (2d Cir. 1980).

Placing the ultimate burden of persuasion on the defendant would relieve the government of its constitutional obligation to prove an essential element. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 215 (1977) (State may not shift burden of disproving an element). Because the court of appeals affirmed the convictions without requiring the government to carry that burden once withdrawal was shown, the judgment should be reversed.
