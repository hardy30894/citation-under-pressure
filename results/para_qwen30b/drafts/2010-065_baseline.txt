**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229, and this Court should affirm the Third Circuit’s judgment on that jurisdictional ground alone. Even if standing were established, the statute is constitutional under the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause, and Bond’s claim fails on the merits.

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE TENTH AMENDMENT CONSTITUTIONALITY OF § 229**

The threshold issue is jurisdictional: a party must have standing to invoke federal judicial power. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998). The Supreme Court has made clear that “a litigant must have suffered an injury in fact that is fairly traceable to the challenged action and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision.” *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61, 112 S.Ct. 2130, 119 L.Ed.2d 351 (1992). Here, Bond’s alleged injury—being prosecuted under a federal statute—is not the kind of injury that confers standing to challenge the constitutional limits of federal power.

The Court has repeatedly held that private individuals lack standing to assert the Tenth Amendment’s protection of state sovereignty. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939), the Court rejected a challenge by private utility companies to the federal government’s operation of a public power project, stating: “The sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment. As we have seen, there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. The Court’s reasoning is clear: the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty, not private economic interests. A private party’s mere allegation of being “harmed” by federal overreach does not satisfy the injury-in-fact requirement when the injury is not to the state’s sovereign authority.

This principle has been reaffirmed in subsequent decisions. In *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201, 124 S.Ct. 1628, 158 L.Ed.2d 420 (2004), the Court emphasized that “the Tenth Amendment is not a source of rights for individuals,” but rather “a structural limitation on federal power.” The injury to state sovereignty is not the same as an injury to a private citizen’s rights. Thus, when a private individual is prosecuted under a federal law, even one that may intrude on state regulatory domains, the injury is not the kind of “injury in fact” required for standing. *See also* *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (holding that private parties lack standing to assert Tenth Amendment claims because “the state represents the interests of its citizens in general”).

Bond’s argument that *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* is “dicta” is meritless. The Court’s holding on standing was not incidental—it was the basis for dismissing the entire claim. *See* *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10, 98 S.Ct. 2985, 57 L.Ed.2d 1018 (1978) (holding that a decision on standing, when it resolves the case, is not dictum). Moreover, the Third Circuit correctly recognized that the split among circuits on this issue does not justify ignoring binding precedent. The weight of authority, including the Supreme Court’s own pronouncements, supports the conclusion that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal statutes.

**II. THE STATUTE IS CONSTITUTIONAL UNDER THE TREATY POWER AND THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE**

Even if standing were established, the statute is valid. Congress enacted § 229 pursuant to its authority under the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause. The United States is a party to the 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention, which obligates signatories to “adopt the necessary measures to implement its obligations,” including enacting penal legislation. 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Congress fulfilled this obligation through the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998, which includes § 229 as its criminal enforcement mechanism.

The Supreme Court has long held that treaties are “the supreme Law of the Land” under Article VI of the Constitution. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920). In *Holland*, the Court upheld a federal statute implementing a treaty on migratory bird conservation, rejecting the argument that the statute violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court reasoned that “if a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” Id. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382. The Court emphasized that “there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” Id. at 433, 40 S.Ct. 382.
