# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 355(f) Confers Jurisdiction Over Any Final Decision of the Board, Including a Denial of Reopening

Section 355(f) of the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, incorporated into the Railroad Retirement Act, authorizes judicial review of "any final decision" of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). Nothing in that text confines review to a claimant's *initial* denial of benefits or excludes a decision on a request to reopen. Where Congress uses broad language like "any final decision," courts must give it its ordinary, expansive meaning. *See Bowen v. Michigan Academy of Family Physicians*, 476 U.S. 667, 670 (1986) (recognizing "the strong presumption that Congress intends judicial review of administrative action"). The Board's own regulations confirm that a ruling on a reopening request is treated as a decision with legal consequence: it either reinstates or forecloses a claimant's substantive entitlement to benefits based on an assessment of "new and material evidence" or "administrative error." 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. When the Board denies reopening after a hearing—as it did here—it has rendered a decision that is final in every functional sense contemplated by § 355(f).

## II. Califano v. Sanders Does Not Control Because the Railroad Retirement Act's Text and Structure Differ From the Social Security Act's

The Fifth Circuit's contrary rule traces to *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), which held that a decision declining to reopen a Social Security claim is not a "final decision . . . made after a hearing" reviewable under 42 U.S.C. § 405(g). *Id.* at 108. But *Sanders* rested on statutory language—"after a hearing"—that has no counterpart in § 355(f). This Court has cautioned against transplanting interpretations of one statute's jurisdictional grant onto a differently worded provision without careful textual comparison. *See Your Home Visiting Nurse Servs., Inc. v. Shalala*, 525 U.S. 449, 453 (1999) (finality analysis is statute-specific). The Fifth Circuit and its sister courts in *Harris v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 198 F.3d 139 (4th Cir. 1999), *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972 (10th Cir. 1995), *Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 918 F.2d 567 (6th Cir. 1990), and *Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250 (7th Cir. 1983), imported *Sanders*'s conclusion wholesale without grappling with this textual distinction. Indeed, even under the Social Security framework, this Court has recognized that when the agency reconsiders a claim on the merits rather than declining on purely discretionary grounds, its action becomes reviewable. *See ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers*, 482 U.S. 270, 278 (1987) (distinguishing a "refusal to reopen" that involves no reexamination of the merits from one that does). Here, the Board's hearing officer did not merely decline as a matter of unreviewable discretion; he adjudicated whether Salinas satisfied the regulatory standard for reopening based on new evidence or administrative error—a merits-based determination indistinguishable in kind from an initial benefits ruling.

## III. The Minority Rule Adopted by the D.C., Second, and Eighth Circuits Better Reflects the Statute's Purpose

Three circuits have correctly held that decisions declining to reopen railroad retirement claims are judicially reviewable. In *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), the D.C. Circuit held that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. The court reasoned that Congress's use of the broad term "any final decision" in § 355(f), unaccompanied by the "after a hearing" limitation found in the Social Security Act, compelled a different result than *Sanders*. *Id.* at 504–05. That reasoning tracks the earlier decisions in *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), and *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636 (8th Cir. 1991), both of which likewise construed § 355(f) to permit review of reopening denials. This consistent minority position gives full effect to the statutory text Congress actually enacted, rather than superimposing a limitation borrowed from an analogous but textually distinct scheme.

## IV. Denying Review Would Leave Arbitrary Board Action Unchecked, Contrary to the Presumption Favoring Judicial Review

The presumption favoring judicial review of federal agency action, articulated in *Bowen*, 476 U.S. at 670, and reaffirmed in numerous contexts, exists precisely to prevent agencies from insulating erroneous or arbitrary decisions from any oversight. If the Board's refusal to reopen is unreviewable, claimants like Salinas—who was denied disability benefits based on a purported failure to show "good cause" for an untimely filing—have no recourse to challenge even blatant legal or factual error in that determination, so long as the agency labels its ruling one of "reopening" rather than an initial denial. Nothing in the Railroad Retirement Act's structure suggests Congress intended such a result. To the contrary, Congress modeled § 355(f) on provisions designed to ensure meaningful judicial oversight of the Board's administration of a mandatory federal benefits scheme.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Board's denial of Salinas's request to reopen his 2006 application constitutes a "final decision" reviewable under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
