**ARGUMENT**

**I. Clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest.**

The Due Process Clause does not extend to executive clemency. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464. The Court rejected any claim of a protected expectancy arising from the high rate at which commutations had historically been granted, explaining that “[a] constitutional entitlement cannot ‘be created—as if by estoppel—merely because a wholly and expressly discretionary state privilege has been granted generously in the past.’” *Id.* at 465.

Ohio’s clemency process is no different. The Governor’s power under Article III, § 11 of the Ohio Constitution is “almost unfettered.” Like the Connecticut Board of Pardons, the Ohio Adult Parole Authority exercises “wholly and expressly discretionary” authority. *Dumschat* therefore controls: no life or liberty interest arises from the clemency proceeding itself.

The court of appeals’ reliance on *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), is misplaced. *Evitts* addressed the first appeal as of right, a stage “integral” to the criminal adjudication. *Id.* at 393. Clemency lies at the opposite extreme. As *Dumschat* recognized, it is an act of executive grace, not an adjudicatory stage. *See also Jago v. Van Curen*, 454 U.S. 14, 20-21 (1981) (per curiam) (no protected interest in parole rescission where state law treated release as a matter of grace). Extending *Evitts* to clemency would constitutionalize a process the Court has repeatedly placed beyond routine judicial oversight.

**II. The voluntary interview option imposes no unconstitutional condition on the Fifth Amendment privilege.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled self-incrimination. *United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. 181, 188 (1977). Ohio’s regulation gives the inmate the option—not the obligation—to request a personal interview. “Only the prisoner and the person or persons assigned by the parole board chair shall be present,” and the inmate “remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes.” Nothing in the regulation requires answers to any question or conditions clemency consideration on waiver of the privilege.

Because participation is voluntary, no governmental compulsion exists. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316-17 (1976) (no Fifth Amendment violation where inmate’s silence was not used to impose a penalty). Any hypothetical future use of statements in a subsequent prosecution is speculative and “premature” for adjudication. *Id.* at 324. The court of appeals therefore erred in remanding for further proceedings on an as-yet-unrealized constitutional claim.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
