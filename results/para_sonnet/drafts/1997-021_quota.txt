# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD, WHILE FACIALLY PERMISSIBLE, MUST BE APPLIED IN A MANNER CONSISTENT WITH ITS OWN TERMS

The Board's standard for employer polling derives from the presumption that an incumbent union retains majority support, a presumption this Court has recognized is rebuttable through "objective considerations." *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990); *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). Petitioner does not ask this Court to discard the Board's polling standard outright, though it recognizes that three circuits have found the standard unworkable because it renders polling "useless" as an independent tool—since an employer possessing sufficient evidence to poll would, under the Board's own logic, already possess sufficient evidence simply to withdraw recognition. *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297-99 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982); *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981).

The Board's professed justification for maintaining an identical standard—that polling, like withdrawal, should require "reasonable doubt about a union's majority status"—collapses under its own admission that Board-conducted RM elections are the "preferred" method precisely because they offer procedural protections that unsupervised polling lacks. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989). If RM elections are preferred because of superior safeguards, logic dictates a *lower* threshold for polling, which the Board itself concedes serves the salutary purpose of allowing an employer to "obtain more certain, precise information about the union's support than is provided by its own reasonable doubt." *Id.* at 1063. An agency's rule must not be arbitrary, and this Court has instructed that agency reasoning be internally coherent. *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983). The Board's polling standard, collapsing the poll inquiry into the withdrawal inquiry, fails that basic requirement of rationality.

## II. EVEN UNDER THE BOARD'S OWN STANDARD, THE FACTUAL DETERMINATION THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

This Court reviews Board factual findings for substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). That standard requires more than "a mere scintilla"; it demands evidence a reasonable mind would accept as adequate to support the conclusion. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939). The Board's finding here fails that test.

Of the thirty-two bargaining-unit employees, statements from a substantial subset—not merely the seven credited by the ALJ—reflected antipathy toward continued union representation. The Board arbitrarily discounted probative evidence by applying rigid, formalistic exclusionary rules untethered from the totality-of-circumstances inquiry this Court's precedents demand. *See Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 787-88 (cautioning against Board presumptions that substitute categorical rules for record-based inquiry into employee sentiment).

The Board discredited employee statements made during job interviews on the theory that such statements are inherently unreliable, relying on *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), *enforced*, 590 F.2d 4 (5th Cir. 1978). But a categorical rule discounting all interview statements—regardless of context, spontaneity, or corroboration—improperly forecloses the fact-specific inquiry substantial-evidence review requires. Similarly, the Board's blanket rule that expressions of dissatisfaction with union dues or representation quality can never evidence opposition to continued representation, drawn from *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), *enforced*, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989), ignores that such statements, considered with the totality of the workforce's conduct, were part of a broader mosaic of anti-union sentiment that the Board was obligated to weigh rather than discard by category.

The Board additionally excluded reports by employees—such as the union's own steward—describing sentiment among their coworkers, on the rationale that such secondhand reports are unreliable absent testimony from every individual employee referenced. This evidentiary rule finds no mooring in ordinary principles of substantial-evidence review, which permit reasonable inferences from probative circumstantial evidence. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488. A union steward's candid acknowledgment that "the union would lose" a vote is powerful, admission-like evidence of the very majority-support question at issue, and the Board's characterization of it as merely "off-the-cuff" does not diminish its evidentiary weight under a properly deferential standard.

Considered together—the successor employer's unique posture, in which the thirty-two-member unit had never itself been polled or certified, *cf. Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 41 n.8; the numerous employee statements reflecting dissatisfaction; and the corroborating secondhand reports—the record compels the conclusion that Allentown possessed the requisite objective, good-faith doubt. The Board's contrary finding, resting on serial exclusion of relevant evidence through categorical rules, does not survive scrutiny under *Universal Camera* and *Columbian Enameling*.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals enforcing the Board's order should be reversed.
