The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not create a cause of action for a plaintiff alleging individualized mistreatment without membership in a class or group. The Clause was adopted to prevent “hostile and discriminating legislation” against particular classes. The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 81 (1873). It “is designed to assure that the States treat alike all persons who are similarly situated.” Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303, 307 (1880). Its prohibitions reach only “class legislation” that singles out groups for unequal burdens or benefits. Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27, 32 (1885).

This Court has consistently required plaintiffs to demonstrate that state action discriminates against a defined class. In Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the ordinance was invalidated because it operated “with a mind so unequal and oppressive as to amount to a practical denial by the State of that equal protection of the laws.” Id. at 373. Likewise, in Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Ellis, 165 U.S. 150 (1897), the statute was struck down because it imposed burdens “upon one class and not upon another.” Id. at 155. The same principle controlled Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. Matthews, 174 U.S. 96 (1899), where the Court examined whether the legislation created “arbitrary discriminations between classes.” Id. at 104.

Individualized animus, standing alone, falls outside the Clause’s protection. Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), upheld a regulation that applied uniformly within the affected class even though it left other businesses untouched, emphasizing that equal protection concerns arise only from differential treatment of similarly situated groups. Id. at 110. Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc., 348 U.S. 483 (1955), confirmed that the Clause permits legislative classifications so long as they rest on any conceivable rational basis and do not single out a protected class for hostile treatment. Id. at 489. Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners, 330 U.S. 552 (1947), rejected an equal-protection challenge to a pilotage system that favored family members, holding that the Constitution does not forbid every instance of personal favoritism absent a showing of class-based discrimination. Id. at 556.

Because respondent alleged only that the Village demanded an easement from her that it had not demanded from others, without identifying any class to which she belonged, the complaint fails to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The decision below therefore erred in recognizing a “class of one” theory.
