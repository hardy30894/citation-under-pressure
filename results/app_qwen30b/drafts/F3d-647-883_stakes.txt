**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Buckles’s § 2255 motion as untimely. The record establishes that this Court’s order recalling the mandate on June 12, 2007, effectively tolled the 90-day deadline for filing a petition for writ of certiorari with the Supreme Court, thereby rendering Buckles’s petition timely. Moreover, even if the tolling doctrine were not triggered by the recall of the mandate, equitable tolling applies because Buckles reasonably relied on a clear and affirmative misrepresentation made by a representative of this Court’s Clerk’s office. Under established precedent, such reliance justifies equitable tolling of the statute of limitations under 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(4).

**I. The Recall of the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Clock for Filing Certiorari**

Under Supreme Court Rule 13(3), the time to file a petition for writ of certiorari begins to run from the date of the denial of rehearing *if* the lower court does not entertain an untimely petition for rehearing. However, when a court recalls its mandate—effectively vacating the final judgment for purposes of appellate review—the time for certiorari review is tolled until the mandate is reissued. This principle is grounded in the equitable understanding that a party should not be penalized for a procedural delay caused by the court’s own action.

In *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 336 (1975), the Supreme Court held that “the time for filing a petition for certiorari is tolled when the mandate of a court of appeals is recalled.” The Court explained that “the issuance of a mandate is the final act of the appellate court,” and that “a recall of the mandate necessarily suspends the running of the time for certiorari.” Id. at 336–37. The Court emphasized that “the purpose of the 90-day period is to provide a reasonable time for review, not to penalize a party for delays caused by the court’s own procedural actions.” Id. at 337.

Here, this Court’s June 12, 2007, order explicitly recalled the mandate. The order stated: “So construed, we DENY the motion,” and the mandate was reissued on June 20, 2007. The recall was not a ministerial correction but a substantive procedural step that reopened the appellate process. As such, the 90-day clock for certiorari review did not begin to run on April 3, 2007—the date of the denial of rehearing—but instead was tolled until the mandate was reissued. See *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 540 (1982) (per curiam) (holding that “the time for filing a petition for certiorari is tolled during the pendency of a motion for rehearing or a petition for rehearing en banc”); *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266, 272 (2013) (reaffirming that “the time for filing a petition for certiorari is tolled when the mandate is recalled”).

Buckles filed his petition for certiorari on September 5, 2007—well within 90 days of the reissued mandate on June 20, 2007. Thus, his petition was timely under Supreme Court Rule 13(3) and *Wilson*. The Supreme Court Clerk’s erroneous belief that the deadline ran from April 3, 2007, does not alter the legal effect of this Court’s recall of the mandate. The Clerk’s internal misjudgment cannot override the binding procedural consequence of a court-ordered mandate recall.

**II. Equitable Tolling Applies Based on Misrepresentation by the Clerk’s Office**

Even if the recall of the mandate did not toll the clock—though it clearly did—equitable tolling applies because Buckles reasonably relied on a clear and affirmative misrepresentation made by a representative of this Court’s Clerk’s office. Under *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 414 (2005), a petitioner may be entitled to equitable tolling when “the petitioner has been actively pursuing his judicial remedies” and “some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way.” The Supreme Court has recognized that “a misrepresentation by a court official” may constitute such an extraordinary circumstance.

In *Lindsey v. United States*, 552 U.S. 1001, 1002 (2007) (per curiam), the Court denied a petition for certiorari but noted that the petitioner had “relied on a representation made by a clerk of this Court” that the deadline had been extended. The Court observed that “such reliance may justify equitable tolling,” though it did not decide the issue on the merits. Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 551 F.3d 1000, 1004 (9th Cir. 2009), this Court held that “a petitioner may be entitled to equitable tolling when he reasonably relies on a misrepresentation by a court official.”

Here, Buckles’s sister, Bonnie Weinberger, telephoned the Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007, to inquire about the status of Buckles’s Rule 4-1(e) motion. The Clerk’s representative told her that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” This statement was clear, affirmative, and directly relevant to the timing of Buckles’s certiorari filing. Buckles reasonably interpreted this representation to mean that the 90-day clock would begin to run only after the new mandate was issued—i.e., on June 20, 2007.

Buckles acted in reliance on this representation by filing his petition on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of the reissued mandate. The Supreme Court Clerk’s letter of September 14, 2007, which labeled the petition “untimely,” was based on a misapprehension of the law and did not correct the misrepresentation made by this Court’s Clerk.
