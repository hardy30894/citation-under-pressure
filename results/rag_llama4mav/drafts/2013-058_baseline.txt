The U.S. Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy. The plain language of 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts "retirement funds" that are held in a tax-exempt account. However, an inherited IRA is not truly a "retirement fund" for the beneficiary, as it is subject to mandatory distribution rules that require the beneficiary to withdraw the funds within a certain timeframe, regardless of their age or retirement status.

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not represent "retirement funds" in the hands of the beneficiary, as it is not held for their retirement (Clark v. Rameker, 673 F.3d 706 (7th Cir. 2013)). The court reasoned that the funds in an inherited IRA are not reserved for the beneficiary's retirement, but rather are subject to distribution requirements that may necessitate withdrawal before the beneficiary's retirement.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320 (2005), which held that IRAs are exempt under §522(d)(10)(E) because they are "similar plans or contracts" to those listed in the statute, and are "on account of" age or length of service. In contrast, an inherited IRA is not "on account of" the beneficiary's age or length of service, but rather is subject to distribution requirements based on the original owner's death.

Furthermore, the Bankruptcy Code's exemption provisions are intended to protect debtors' retirement savings, not to shelter assets that can be freely used for current consumption (see Patterson v. Shumate, 504 U.S. 753 (1992)). Allowing debtors to exempt inherited IRAs would effectively permit them to shield assets that are not truly retirement funds, but rather are available for immediate use.

The Fifth Circuit's decision in Chilton v. Prudential Insurance Co. of North America, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), which held that an inherited IRA is exempt under §522(b)(3)(C), is distinguishable. The Seventh Circuit correctly noted that the Chilton court's interpretation would lead to an overly broad reading of the exemption provision, allowing debtors to exempt assets that are not truly retirement funds.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is consistent with the plain language and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code's exemption provisions. An inherited IRA is not a "retirement fund" within the meaning of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and therefore is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy.
