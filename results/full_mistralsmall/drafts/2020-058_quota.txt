**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER § 1255(a) BECAUSE IT IS THE FUNCTIONAL EQUIVALENT OF LAWFUL ENTRY FOLLOWING INSPECTION**

The Third Circuit erred by divorcing “admission” from the statutory reality that TPS recipients are treated as if they were lawfully admitted. Section 1254a(f)(4) expressly provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This is not a hollow fiction; it is a congressionally ordained legal status that Congress itself equated to lawful admission. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”). The Board of Immigration Appeals has recognized that “[a]n adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). The same logic applies to TPS: once granted, the recipient is treated as lawfully admitted for purposes of § 1255.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced. *Hanif* interpreted “admission” in the context of § 1255(b), not § 1255(a). *Id.* at 485. Section 1255(a) contains no such limitation; it simply requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). TPS recipients meet this requirement because they are deemed lawfully admitted. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (“By the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.”).

II. **THE STATUTORY CONTEXT AND STRUCTURE CONFIRM THAT § 1254a(f)(4) SATISFIES § 1255(a)’S ADMISSION REQUIREMENT**

The Third Circuit strained to find a conflict between § 1254a(f)(4) and § 1255(a), but the statutes are harmonious. Section 1255(a) permits adjustment for those “inspected and admitted or paroled,” while § 1254a(f)(4) deems TPS recipients to be in lawful nonimmigrant status. *See Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016) (“Admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.”). The Fifth Circuit correctly noted that “[a]dmission is an occurrence, defined in wholly factual and procedural terms,” while “status . . . describes the type of permission to be present in the United States.” *Id.* TPS recipients are treated as having the latter, which Congress expressly linked to the former.

The Third Circuit also erred by suggesting that § 1255(k) requires a separate admission. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1). That section merely clarifies that an alien must be “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission” at the time of filing. *Id.* TPS recipients are deemed lawfully admitted, so they satisfy this requirement. *See Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam) (“That an alien with Temporary Protected Status has ‘lawful status as a nonimmigrant’ for purposes of adjusting his status does not change § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement that he is eligible for adjustment of status only if he was initially inspected and admitted or paroled.”).

III. **THE PURPOSE OF TPS SUPPORTS TREATING GRANTS AS ADMISSIONS**

TPS was designed to provide temporary relief to aliens unable to return to unsafe home countries. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s interpretation would force TPS recipients to leave the U.S. and reenter legally—a process that is both impractical and contrary to TPS’s humanitarian purpose. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964 (“Forcing TPS recipients to depart the United States and reenter legally would undermine the purpose of the TPS program.”). Congress could not have intended such a result. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 320 (2001) (“It strains credulity to suggest that Congress would have attached such severe consequences to purely technical violations.”).

IV. **AGENCY INTERPRETATIONS AND PRIOR PANEL PRECEDENT SUPPORT PETITIONERS’ POSITION**

The Third Circuit dismissed an INS General Counsel opinion as non-binding, but agency interpretations are entitled to deference under *Auer v. Robbins*, 519 U.S. 452, 461 (1997). The INS concluded that TPS does not constitute an admission, but that interpretation was issued before *St. Cyr* and other precedents clarified the interplay between TPS and § 1255. *See* INS Gen. Counsel Op. No. 91-27 (1991). More recent decisions, including *Ramirez* and *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), have recognized that TPS recipients are treated as lawfully admitted.

Finally, the Third Circuit’s reliance on *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), is misplaced. *Taveras* addressed adjustment in removal proceedings, not the distinct question of whether TPS constitutes an admission. *Id.* at 290. The distinction is critical: TPS recipients are deemed lawfully admitted, so they satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s judgment and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under § 1255(a).
