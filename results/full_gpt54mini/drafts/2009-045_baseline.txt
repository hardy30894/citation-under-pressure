Respondents are correct that a fee enhancement under a federal fee-shifting statute cannot be based solely on counsel’s quality of performance and the results obtained. The Supreme Court’s cases establish that the lodestar figure—reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is the “guiding light” and is presumed to be a reasonable fee. Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 563-64 (1986); Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984); Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 433, 435 (1983). An enhancement may not be justified by factors already subsumed in the lodestar. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900.

Blum squarely rejected the proposition that “the quality of representation” and “results obtained” independently warrant increasing the fee. 465 U.S. at 898-900. The Court explained that these considerations are ordinarily reflected in the reasonable hourly rate and in the number of hours reasonably expended. Id. at 898-99. It therefore held that the district court erred in enhancing the award absent “specific evidence” that the lodestar failed to account for an exceptional case. Id. at 899. The Court’s language was careful: an enhancement may be appropriate only in the “rare case” where the applicant shows that the quality of service was superior to what one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional. Id. at 899-900.

Delaware Valley reaffirmed that rule. In Delaware Valley I, the Court held that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance.” 478 U.S. at 566. The Court emphasized that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar.” Id. at 566. That is because the lodestar already “adequately takes into account” the factors relevant to a reasonable fee. Id. at 565-66. The Court’s repeated insistence on “specific evidence” and “detailed findings” confirms that a court may not rely on a generalized impression of counsel’s excellence or the desirability of the outcome. Id. at 565-68.

Later cases likewise reinforce the same limitation. In City of Burlington v. Dague, the Court held that enhancements for contingency are unavailable under federal fee-shifting statutes because the lodestar is presumed sufficient and adjustments should not be used to improve on that baseline. 505 U.S. 557, 562-67 (1992). Dague reiterated that departures from the lodestar are permissible only in the rarest circumstances and only where the lodestar does not already capture the relevant consideration. Id. at 562-63. And Perdue v. Kenny A., 559 U.S. 542, 552-54 (2010), which ultimately reviewed this very case, confirmed the governing principle: a fee applicant seeking enhancement bears a “strong burden” and must produce “specific evidence” that the lodestar fee would not have been adequate to attract competent counsel. Id. at 552-53. Perdue held that enhancements are permitted only in “rare” and “exceptional” circumstances and that many asserted justifications, including quality and results, are ordinarily subsumed in the lodestar. Id. at 553-54.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision cannot be squared with those precedents. The court affirmed an enhancement based on counsel’s “skill, commitment, dedication, and professionalism,” and on the “exceptional nature of results.” Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1236-42 (11th Cir. 2008). But those are precisely the kinds of factors Blum and Delaware Valley held ordinarily are already reflected in the lodestar. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565-66. A court may not award a multiplier merely because it believes counsel did a superb job. The question is not whether the work was excellent; it is whether the lodestar, despite properly accounting for market rates and hours, is inadequate on the basis of objective evidence. Perdue, 559 U.S. at 553-54.

Petitioners’ rule would convert fee enhancements into a subjective quality bonus, contrary to the Court’s repeated efforts to make fee awards predictable, objective, and reviewable. See Hensley, 461 U.S. at 437; Blum, 465 U.S. at 900; Dague, 505 U.S. at 562-67. Under controlling precedent, superior performance and strong results may justify a higher reasonable hourly rate in the first instance, or an enhancement only if they are shown by specific evidence to be outside what the lodestar already captures. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-99; Perdue, 559 U.S. at 553-54. Because the enhancement below rested solely on quality of performance and results obtained, it cannot stand.
