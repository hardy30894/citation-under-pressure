**Argument**

I. Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination in a limited public forum.

Milford opened its facilities after hours to district residents for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” Once a state creates such a limited public forum, it may reserve the forum for the stated purposes, but it may not exclude speech because of the speaker’s viewpoint. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829-30 (1995); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 392-93 (1993). The Court has repeatedly held that when a forum is open to discussion of a subject, the government may not bar a religious perspective on that subject. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94.

That is exactly what Milford did here. The school allowed groups like the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club to use the facilities for youth development and moral formation, but excluded the Good News Club because its lessons on morals and character were taught from a Christian viewpoint. The Second Circuit erred in treating that as a permissible subject-matter restriction. The Club did not seek access to discuss a different subject; it sought access to speak on the same subject—moral and character development—from a religious perspective. *Rosenberger* squarely rejects the notion that the government may exclude speech because the viewpoint is religious, even if the underlying topic is otherwise permissible. 515 U.S. at 831-32. As the Court explained, once the State permits discussion of a subject, it may not “silenc[e]” one perspective because it is religious. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394.

Milford’s own stated rationale confirms viewpoint discrimination. The school denied access because the Good News Club’s proposed activities were “the equivalent of religious worship” rather than “the expression of religious views or values on a secular subject matter.” That distinction collapses under this Court’s precedents. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), the Court observed that there is “no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’ . . . and become unprotected ‘worship.’” The point is not whether the Club’s speech is religious; it plainly is. The point is that religious speech on otherwise permitted subjects is protected speech. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 833-34; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94.

II. The Establishment Clause did not justify Milford’s exclusion.

Milford contends that permitting the Club to meet after hours would violate the Establishment Clause. This Court has rejected that exact argument in closely analogous school-forum cases. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university’s equal-access policy did not violate the Establishment Clause. 454 U.S. at 272-75. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court again held that allowing a religious group access to school property for a film series on family and child rearing would not violate the Establishment Clause. 508 U.S. at 395. And in *Board of Educ. of Westside Cmty. Schs. (Dist. 66) v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 248-50 (1990), the Court held that permitting a student Bible club to meet under the Equal Access Act did not amount to state endorsement of religion.

Those decisions control here. Milford’s policy operated after hours, was open to many private community groups, and involved no school sponsorship of the Club’s speech. Under *Widmar* and *Mergens*, the school could avoid Establishment Clause concerns by remaining neutral and making clear that private speakers do not speak for the State. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 251. The Establishment Clause does not require the government to suppress private religious expression in a forum it has opened to other community groups. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 845-46. To the contrary, viewpoint discrimination designed to avoid an Establishment Clause objection is impermissible where the objection rests on nothing more than the private religious character of the speech. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 834-35.

Milford’s position is also inconsistent with *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314-15 (1952), which recognized that the Constitution does not require hostility to religion and that accommodation of religious exercise may coexist with governmental neutrality. Excluding the Good News Club was not neutrality; it was exclusion because the Club’s moral instruction was expressly Christian. That is the very sort of discrimination the First Amendment forbids. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953).

III. The judgment should be reversed.

This Court has already made clear that a school district may not create a forum for community expression and then bar one speaker because the speaker discusses otherwise permitted subjects from a religious viewpoint. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 830-31; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94. Nor may the district invoke the Establishment Clause as a talisman to justify that discrimination. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273-75; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 248-53. Milford violated the Good News Club’s free speech rights, and its asserted Establishment Clause concern does not save the exclusion.
