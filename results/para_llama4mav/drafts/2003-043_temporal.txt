The government's argument that assessing the partnership is sufficient to extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from individual partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, an "individual" is a distinct "taxpayer" from a "partnership." Galletti v. United States (In re Galletti), 298 F.3d 1107 (9th Cir. 2002).

The regulation interpreting § 6203 requires that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This implies that each taxpayer, including individual partners, must be identified and assessed separately. The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), supports this interpretation. In Coson, the court affirmed the district court's holding that an assessment against a partnership did not create a valid lien against an individual partner's property because the partner had not been assessed individually. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Furthermore, the district court's decision in Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D.Cal.1959), aff'd 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960), is distinguishable from the present case. In Young, the court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. However, the court also acknowledged that the government would have had to either assess the tax against the partner individually or bring a court action to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050. This suggests that an assessment against the partnership is not sufficient to extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from individual partners.

In the present case, the IRS assessed the tax against the partnership but not against the individual partners. Under the reasoning of Coson and Young, this assessment is insufficient to extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from the individual partners. Therefore, the IRS's claims against the respondents are barred by the statute of limitations, and the bankruptcy court's disallowance of the claims should be affirmed.
