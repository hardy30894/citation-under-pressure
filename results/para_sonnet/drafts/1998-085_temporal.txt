# ARGUMENT

## I. Congress Legislated Against a Settled Common-Law Background Requiring Egregious Misconduct as a Precondition to Punitive Damages

When Congress used the terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" in 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), it did not write on a blank slate. This Court has long instructed that "where Congress borrows terms of art in which are accumulated the legal tradition and meaning of centuries of practice, it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). The common-law doctrine of exemplary damages is precisely such a tradition, and it has never permitted punitive relief on the bare showing of a culpable state of mind sufficient to establish liability itself.

This Court's own nineteenth-century decisions confirm that exemplary damages were reserved for conduct marked by aggravation beyond the wrong needed to sustain the underlying cause of action. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852), the Court recognized the practice of allowing juries, in actions where the injury was "wanton or malicious, or gross and outrageous," to award damages "having in view the enormity of [the] offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." *Id.* at 371. The doctrine was never understood to attach to ordinary intentional wrongdoing; it was confined to conduct exhibiting a heightened degree of blameworthiness.

This Court reaffirmed that limitation in *Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), explaining that exemplary damages are appropriate only where the defendant's conduct rises to the level of "wanton disregard" of the rights of others—not where the defendant merely commits an unlawful act with knowledge of its unlawfulness. And in *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), the Court again treated exemplary damages as depending on "actual malice" or conduct "so reckless" as to warrant an inference equivalent to malice—an inquiry distinct from, and additional to, whatever intent suffices for liability. See also *New York, Lake Erie & Western Railroad Co. v. Winter's Administrator*, 143 U.S. 60 (1892) (exemplary damages confined to conduct that is "willful" or exhibits "reckless indifference" of an aggravated character); *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512 (1885) (sustaining punitive damages statute directed at "willful" and egregious disregard of duty).

The consistent thread running through these decisions is that the common law did not treat "malice" as a synonym for intentional discrimination or even for knowing violation of a legal duty. It treated malice, and its "reckless indifference" analogue, as shorthand for conduct that is independently egregious—conduct that exceeds the baseline wrongfulness already captured by the elements of liability. See *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886) (exemplary damages permitted only where the wrong is aggravated by "evil motive" or like circumstances of outrage).

## II. Section 1981a's Reference to "Malice" and "Reckless Indifference" Must Be Read to Carry This Traditional, Egregiousness-Laden Meaning

Nothing in the text of § 1981a signals a departure from this inherited framework. To the contrary, by borrowing the very vocabulary—"malice," "reckless indifference"—that this Court's decisions had already invested with a specialized, aggravated meaning, Congress must be taken to have adopted that meaning rather than inventing a new and diluted one. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263. Had Congress intended punitive damages to follow automatically from proof of the discriminatory intent already required to establish liability under Title VII, it had no need to superimpose a separate mental-state requirement at all; the liability finding alone would have sufficed. The separate statutory requirement is meaningful only if it demands something beyond what a plaintiff must already prove to win her case—precisely the "something more" that *Day v. Woodworth*, *Arms*, *Winter's Administrator*, and *Prentice* required at common law.

To collapse that distinction—treating "malice or reckless indifference" as established by the same evidence that proves discriminatory intent under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973)—would convert every successful disparate-treatment verdict into a candidate for punitive damages. That result cannot be squared with the common-law tradition Congress imported into the statute, a tradition in which exemplary damages were the exception reserved for outrageous conduct, not the invariable companion of liability.

## III. Under the Correct Standard, Respondent's Conduct Does Not Support Punitive Damages

Measured against this inherited standard, the record here reveals, at most, a disputed personnel decision resolved against petitioner by a reasonable jury. It does not reveal the kind of wanton, outrageous conduct that *Day v. Woodworth* and its progeny identify as the predicate for punishment beyond compensation. Absent proof of that independent egregiousness, the courts below correctly declined to submit punitive damages to the jury.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
