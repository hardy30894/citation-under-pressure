**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Held That “Actual Knowledge” Under ERISA § 1113(2) Requires Knowledge That The Alleged Breach Occurred And That It Was A Violation Of ERISA.

The text of ERISA § 1113(2) provides that an action “may not be commenced more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this provision to require that a plaintiff have actual knowledge both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was imprudent—i.e., a breach of ERISA’s fiduciary duties. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 909 F.3d 1059, 1065 (9th Cir. 2018), rev’d on other grounds, 140 S. Ct. 722 (2020). This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance that ERISA’s limitations provisions are to be read in light of the statute’s remedial purposes. See, e.g., Tibble v. Edison Int’l, 575 U.S. 523, 530 (2015) (noting that ERISA is “a comprehensive statute designed to promote the interests of employees and their beneficiaries in employee benefit plans”).

The Ninth Circuit’s construction also aligns with this Court’s observation that “actual knowledge” is not satisfied by constructive knowledge or by the mere availability of information. See, e.g., Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985) (holding that actual knowledge is not triggered by knowledge of the law but by knowledge of the transaction constituting the breach). The Ninth Circuit’s approach ensures that the limitations period does not begin to run merely because a plaintiff received disclosures but did not read or recall them. See Sulyma, 909 F.3d at 1065-66.

II. The Ninth Circuit Properly Rejected Intel’s Argument That Disclosure Alone Satisfies The “Actual Knowledge” Requirement.

Intel contends that disclosure of investment decisions through fund fact sheets and other documents is sufficient to trigger the three-year limitations period. This argument misreads the statute. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, “actual knowledge” requires more than the mere receipt of information; it requires that the plaintiff actually knew of the breach and understood it to be a violation of ERISA. See id. at 1065. The Ninth Circuit’s holding is consistent with the principle that limitations periods should not begin to run where a plaintiff lacks the requisite mental state. See, e.g., Merck & Co. v. Reynolds, 559 U.S. 633, 648 (2010) (noting that limitations periods typically do not begin to run until a plaintiff has “discovered” the injury).

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also avoids rendering the “actual knowledge” standard superfluous. As the Court has cautioned, statutory interpretations that render language meaningless are disfavored. See, e.g., Carcieri v. Salazar, 555 U.S. 379, 387 (2009). If disclosure alone were sufficient, the “actual knowledge” requirement would be redundant of the constructive knowledge standard that Congress repealed in 1987. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330.

III. The Ninth Circuit’s Holding Does Not Impede Fiduciaries’ Ability To Comply With ERISA’s Disclosure Requirements.

Intel argues that the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation will discourage fiduciaries from making disclosures or encourage beneficiaries to ignore them. This concern is overstated. ERISA already imposes affirmative duties on fiduciaries to provide plan information, see 29 U.S.C. § 1024(b)(4), and beneficiaries remain free to bring claims within the six-year period if they do not discover the breach earlier. See 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1). The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not alter these obligations; it merely ensures that the limitations period does not begin to run before a plaintiff actually knows of the breach.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s approach is consistent with the principle that limitations periods should not penalize plaintiffs for failing to act on information they did not actually process. See, e.g., Rotella v. Wood, 528 U.S. 549, 556 (2000) (holding that the discovery rule applies where a plaintiff could not have discovered the fraud despite due diligence). The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation prevents fiduciaries from using disclosure as a litigation tactic to cut off meritorious claims before plaintiffs have the opportunity to understand and act on the information provided.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires that a plaintiff know both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was imprudent. This interpretation is consistent with the statute’s text, structure, and remedial purpose. The Court should affirm the judgment below.
