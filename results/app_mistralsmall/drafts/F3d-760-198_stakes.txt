**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BY FAILING TO CONSIDER THE TOTALITY OF THE CIRCUMSTANCES**

A hostile work environment claim under Title VII requires proof that the workplace was “permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult, that was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment.” *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The district court erred by limiting its analysis to only those acts that were “sexually offensive,” disregarding facially sex-neutral conduct that, when viewed cumulatively, contributed to a hostile environment. *See Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002) (holding that a hostile work environment claim may be supported by facially sex-neutral incidents if they are part of a pattern of sex-based discrimination).

The district court’s error was twofold. First, it improperly parsed Moll’s allegations, separating out conduct it deemed “sexually offensive” from other discriminatory acts. This approach contravenes the principle that courts must evaluate all incidents “in their totality.” *See id.* at 377. Second, the court failed to recognize that facially neutral acts—such as Irving’s refusal to permit remote work, denial of vacation, exclusion from social events, and disparate promotion treatment—can constitute evidence of a hostile environment when viewed as part of a pattern of sex-based discrimination. *See id.* at 375-76.

The Second Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that the “totality of the circumstances” standard is not a mere checklist but a holistic inquiry. *See, e.g., Tomka v. Seiler Corp.*, 66 F.3d 1295, 1305 (2d Cir. 1995). The district court’s refusal to consider Moll’s non-sexually offensive allegations in their entirety deprived her of a fair assessment of her hostile work environment claim. This error warrants vacatur of the summary judgment ruling.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON MOLL’S RETALIATION CLAIM**

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under Title VII, a plaintiff must show: (1) participation in a protected activity; (2) adverse employment action; and (3) a causal connection between the two. *Gordon v. N.Y. Hosp. Med. Ctr. of Queens*, 633 F.3d 72, 80 (2d Cir. 2011). The district court granted summary judgment on Moll’s retaliation claim, erroneously concluding that the transfer to Syracuse was not an adverse action.

The transfer was undeniably adverse. It forced Moll to relocate, disrupted her work arrangements (including the ability to work from home), and occurred in close temporal proximity to her EEOC charge. *See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006) (holding that an actionable retaliation claim may arise from any employer action that “might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination”). The district court’s conclusion to the contrary was error.

Moreover, the record contains evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer causation. Moll’s transfer followed her EEOC charge by mere months, and Verizon’s proffered reasons for the transfer—consolidating Sales Engineers in one office—were undermined by evidence that male colleagues retained remote work privileges. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) (holding that a jury may reject a defendant’s proffered reasons for an adverse action if they are pretextual).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING MOLL’S MOTION TO COMPEL PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS**

The district court denied Moll’s motion to compel discovery of documents related to Verizon’s promotion and pay practices, citing lack of relevance. This was an abuse of discretion. Under Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, relevant information is “anything that bears on, or that logically tends to aid, a claim or defense.” *O’Connor v. Chase Manhattan Auto Fin. Corp.*, 201 F.R.D. 37, 40 (S.D.N.Y. 2001). Evidence of Verizon’s promotion and pay disparities among similarly situated employees was plainly relevant to Moll’s equal pay and disparate treatment claims.

The district court’s ruling ignored the broad scope of discovery permitted under Rule 26. *See Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 507 (1947) (holding that the scope of discovery is “as broad as the relevance standard” allows). The court’s refusal to compel production of these documents deprived Moll of critical evidence to support her claims.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated in its entirety, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
