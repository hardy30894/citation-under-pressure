**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of § 1333(a)(2)(A) of the OCSLA in light of settled principles of statutory construction and the well-established doctrine of federal preemption. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California’s wage and hour laws may be applied on the outer Continental Shelf (OCS) simply because they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, without requiring a showing of a gap or void in federal coverage. This interpretation disregards the fundamental principle that federal law governs the OCS as a matter of exclusive federal jurisdiction, and that state law may only supplement federal law when federal law is silent or inadequate. The correct interpretation of the OCSLA, grounded in the text, legislative history, and Supreme Court precedent, is that state law applies only when federal law leaves a gap—*not* whenever state law is merely not inconsistent with federal law.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State, now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed are declared to be the law of the United States for that portion of the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf... to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted.” (Emphasis added.) The phrase “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent” does not mean “whenever they are not inconsistent.” Rather, it reflects a narrow, conditional assimilation of state law—only where federal law fails to provide a complete regulatory scheme. This construction is compelled by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), which held that state law may be applied on the OCS only when federal law does not provide a remedy. In *Rodrigue*, the Court emphasized that the High Seas Act, which provided a federal remedy for deaths on the high seas, was inapplicable because the accidents occurred on fixed platforms, not on navigable waters. The Court concluded that “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” was “remove[d],” *id.* at 366, because the federal remedy was unavailable. This confirms that state law is not automatically applicable merely because it is not inconsistent—it is applicable only when federal law does not cover the claim.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary interpretation, which permits state law to apply whenever it is “not inconsistent,” would render the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision superfluous. If state law could be applied whenever it is not inconsistent with federal law, then the OCSLA would have no need to require a gap. But the legislative history of the OCSLA makes clear that Congress intended state law to fill gaps, not to compete with federal law. As the Court noted in *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988), Congress feared that federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” This concern led Congress to adopt a hybrid system: federal law governs the OCS, but state law may be assimilated only where federal law is silent. The 1975 amendment to the OCSLA, which expanded the scope of “applicable” state law to include laws “hereafter adopted,” did not alter this fundamental principle. It merely ensured that new state laws could be incorporated, so long as they were not inconsistent with federal law and filled a gap.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with the doctrine of preemption established in *Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel*, 316 U.S. 572 (1942). In *Missel*, the Court held that the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) “is a comprehensive statute” that “was intended to be the sole source of regulation” for wage and hour matters in interstate commerce. *Id.* at 578. The Court emphasized that “the Act was designed to be a complete code,” and that “the purpose of Congress was to establish a uniform national standard.” *Id.* at 580. This principle applies with equal force on the OCS, where Congress has declared federal law to be the governing law. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, which permits more protective state laws, does not override the principle that federal law is the primary source of regulation. As the Court stated in *Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc.*, 323 U.S. 37, 42 (1944), “Congress intended the Act to be the exclusive source of regulation in the field.” The FLSA’s comprehensive nature means that state law cannot be applied as a parallel or supplementary source unless federal law is silent.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also undermines the principle of federal supremacy. In *Walling v. A. H. Belo Corp.*, 316 U.S. 624, 630 (1942), the Court held that “the Act was intended to be the exclusive source of regulation” and that “the application of state law would be inconsistent with the purpose of the Act.” The Court rejected the idea that state law could be applied to supplement federal law in the same field. Similarly, in *Walling v. Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co.*, 331 U.S. 17, 20 (1947), the Court emphasized that “the Act was intended to be a complete code,” and that “the application of state law would be inconsistent with the purpose of the Act.” These cases establish that state law may not be applied in a field where federal law is comprehensive and exclusive.
