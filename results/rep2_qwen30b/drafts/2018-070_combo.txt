**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret a statutory scheme that Congress designed to be exclusive and comprehensive. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits state wage and hour laws to apply on the Outer Continental Shelf so long as they are “not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision and undermines the foundational principle that federal law governs the OCS as a matter of exclusive jurisdiction. The correct interpretation, grounded in the text, structure, and pre-1970 precedent, is that state law applies only when federal law is silent or inadequate—i.e., when a “gap” exists. The OCSLA does not authorize the automatic assimilation of state law as “surrogate federal law” merely because it is not inconsistent with federal law. To the contrary, the legislative history and settled precedent make clear that Congress intended federal law to be the primary and exclusive source of legal authority on the OCS, with state law serving only as a fallback.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that state laws “are declared to be the law of the United States” on the OCS “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” This language, far from being a blank check for state law to supplement federal law, reflects a carefully calibrated balance. Congress did not intend to create a dual system of overlapping legal regimes. Rather, it sought to ensure that the OCS would be governed by a coherent body of law, with federal law as the default and state law as a limited supplement. The phrase “to the extent that they are applicable” does not mean “whenever they are not inconsistent.” It means “whenever federal law does not already govern.” As this Court held in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947), “the mere fact that a state law is not in conflict with federal law does not make it applicable where federal law is already in force.” The OCSLA’s structure confirms this: Congress declared that federal law governs the OCS “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This is not a mere analogy—it is a declaration of federal supremacy.

The legislative history of the OCSLA, enacted in 1953, confirms that Congress intended federal law to be the primary governing authority. As the Senate Report explained, Congress rejected the idea of extending maritime law to the OCS because it “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). Congress feared that federal law alone would be “inadequate” to govern the complex activities on the OCS, but only because it was not intended to be a complete code. The solution was not to allow state law to fill every gap, but to incorporate only those state laws that were “applicable” in the sense of being necessary to fill a void. The Senate Report explicitly stated that the purpose of the Act was “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as drilling platforms.” *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). That body of law was to be federal, with state law as a supplement only where federal law was silent.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 25 (1947), where this Court held that federal law governs the seabed of the outer Continental Shelf “as a matter of exclusive jurisdiction.” The Court emphasized that “the United States has exclusive jurisdiction over the submerged lands of the outer Continental Shelf,” and that “no state may assert jurisdiction over the seabed.” Id. at 24. This exclusive federal jurisdiction is not diminished by the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision. On the contrary, the provision operates within the framework of federal supremacy. As this Court held in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. at 305, “where federal law is in force, state law cannot be applied.” The OCSLA does not alter this principle.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case did not hold that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent with federal law. It held that state law applies only when federal law is “inadequate” or “silent,” and that “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” is a prerequisite. Id. at 1036. The court in *Continental Oil* recognized that “the federal law is not a complete code,” but that “the state law may be applied only to the extent that it is necessary to fill a gap.” Id. This is consistent with the principle that federal law governs unless it is silent. The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of this “gap” requirement contradicts both the text and the intent of the OCSLA.

Moreover, the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), is directly on point. There, the Court held that federal law governs the outer Continental Shelf, and that state law cannot be applied unless federal law is “inadequate.” Id. at 25. The Court emphasized that “the United States has exclusive jurisdiction over the submerged lands of the outer Continental Shelf,” and that “no state may assert jurisdiction over the seabed.” Id. at 24. This exclusive federal jurisdiction is not altered by the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision. The provision does not create a new jurisdictional regime; it merely defines the scope of federal law.
