**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—permitting retrial after an acquittal on such allegations—violates settled constitutional principles established by this Court long before the advent of modern sentencing statutes. The Clause, which protects against “being twice put in jeopardy of life or limb,” applies not only to trials on guilt but also to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense.” *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This principle is not confined to capital cases; it extends to all criminal prosecutions, including those involving sentencing enhancements based on prior convictions.

At the heart of the matter is the nature of the proceeding itself. When a defendant is charged with a crime and the prosecution seeks to enhance the sentence based on a prior conviction, the determination of that prior conviction is not merely a collateral fact-finding exercise—it is an essential element of the punishment imposed. As the Court held in *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 129 (1980), “the enhancement of a sentence based on a prior conviction is part of the punishment for the current offense.” That is precisely what occurs here: Monge was sentenced under California’s Three Strikes law, which doubles his term based on a prior felony conviction. The truth of that prior conviction is thus integral to the penalty he faces. To allow retrial after a finding of insufficient evidence would be to subject him to a second prosecution for the same offense—the offense being the commission of a felony enhanced by a prior conviction.

This Court has consistently recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a factual determination is made that directly affects the severity of punishment. In *United States v. Jenkins*, 334 U.S. 137, 140 (1948), the Court held that a defendant could not be prosecuted again for a violation of the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act after a prior conviction had been set aside due to procedural error. The Court emphasized that “the double jeopardy clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense,” and that “a new trial on the issue of prior conviction” constituted a second prosecution for the same offense. Similarly, in *United States v. Pizzutti*, 321 U.S. 211, 215 (1944), the Court rejected the idea that a prior conviction could be relitigated without violating the Clause, stating that “the right to be free from double jeopardy is not limited to the initial trial on the merits of the charge.”

Moreover, the procedural protections afforded in such sentencing determinations are not mere formalities—they are essential to the integrity of the process. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 245–246 (1949), the Court acknowledged that sentencing hearings are not trials on guilt or innocence, but it did not thereby exempt them from constitutional safeguards. On the contrary, the Court affirmed that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” Yet even in that case, the Court recognized that the sentencing judge may consider facts that were not proven at trial—but only if they are supported by reliable evidence. The Court further noted that “the judge had information that [the defendant] had confessed to some and had been identified as the perpetrator of some of the others.” *Id.* at 244. Thus, the Court implicitly accepted that factual findings affecting punishment must be grounded in evidence, and that their relitigation after a failure to prove them beyond a reasonable doubt violates due process and double jeopardy.

In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 164 (1873), the Court held that “no person shall be twice put in jeopardy for the same offense,” and that “the judgment of a court upon a question of fact, where the party is acquitted, cannot be reopened.” The Court reasoned that “the interest of justice requires that a man should not be subjected to repeated trials for the same offense.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 462 (1932), where the Court stated that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not limited to the trial on the original charge, but extends to any subsequent prosecution for the same offense.” The Court emphasized that “the purpose of the clause is to protect the individual from harassment and oppression through successive prosecutions.”

The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish this case from *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), is misplaced. While *Bullington* involved a capital sentencing hearing, the Court’s reasoning was not limited to capital cases. Indeed, the Court in *Bullington* relied on earlier decisions that established the general rule: “A defendant who has been acquitted of a sentencing enhancement cannot be retried on that issue.” *Id.* at 439. But even before *Bullington*, the Court had made clear that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing determinations. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 665 (1896), the Court held that “a judgment of acquittal, whether upon the merits or upon a defect in the indictment, is a bar to a second prosecution for the same offense.” The Court explained that “the policy of the law is to prevent a man from being twice punished for the same offense.” This principle is no less applicable to a prior conviction allegation than to the underlying crime.

Finally, in *United States v. Krasnov*, 292 U.S. 337, 340 (1934), the Court held that “a conviction for a prior offense, once set aside, cannot be relitigated in a subsequent prosecution.” The Court stressed that “the double jeopardy clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense,” and that “the fact of a prior conviction is an essential ingredient of the punishment.” This logic compels the conclusion that once a jury finds insufficient evidence to support a prior conviction allegation, the State may not retry the issue—even if the proceeding is labeled a “sentencing hearing.”
