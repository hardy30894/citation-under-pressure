The government’s burden at trial is to prove the charged conspiracy during the limitations period. But withdrawal before the limitations period does not transform that burden into proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member during the relevant time. Rather, once the evidence shows the defendant’s affirmative withdrawal, he is not criminally responsible for later acts of the conspiracy. Under settled pre-1970 precedent, withdrawal cuts off continuing liability; it is not an element the government must negate in every case.

In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), this Court held that conspiracy is a continuing offense, but also recognized that a conspirator may withdraw. The Court explained that a defendant remains liable so long as he has not dissociated himself from the unlawful agreement. Id. at 369-70. Hyde thus treats withdrawal as a defense to ongoing responsibility, not as an additional element of the offense that the prosecution must prove anew after the fact. See also United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910) (conspiracy continues until accomplished or abandoned, but participation may cease when a conspirator “reaches the conclusion to withdraw”).

That understanding is reinforced by Bannon v. United States, 156 U.S. 464 (1895). There, the Court recognized that criminal liability tracks the defendant’s actual participation in the unlawful scheme. Id. at 472-73. When a defendant has ended his participation before the charged period, the government cannot simply rely on the existence of the conspiracy itself to establish his guilt during the later period. The inquiry is whether he was still a member when the offense continued, not whether the conspiracy once existed.

The burden allocation on affirmative defenses likewise supports petitioner. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court held that the prosecution bears the ultimate burden to prove guilt, but may rely on presumptions and admissions unless the defendant comes forward with evidence of a distinct defense. Id. at 487-88. Withdrawal is of that character. It is a fact uniquely within the defendant’s knowledge and control: when he communicated dissociation, whether he acted inconsistently with the conspiracy, and whether he severed ties before the limitations period ran. Once the defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove the opposite only if it seeks to rely on post-withdrawal participation; but the Constitution does not require the government to disprove withdrawal in the first instance beyond a reasonable doubt. Cf. Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 88-92 (1934) (permitting burden shifting on facts peculiarly within defendant’s knowledge where the ultimate burden of guilt remains with the prosecution).

Nor does Greenleaf’s evidence doctrine compel petitioner’s position. This Court has long distinguished between the prosecution’s burden to prove the offense and a defendant’s burden to establish an independent excuse or exemption. See, e.g., Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469-70 (1943); Morrison, 291 U.S. at 88-92. Withdrawal is not part of the charged agreement; it is a severance from it. The defendant who says he left the conspiracy before the limitations period must produce evidence of that withdrawal. If he does, the government then proceeds on its evidence of continued membership. But nothing in pre-1970 law requires the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt, as an initial matter, that the accused remained in the conspiracy during the limitations period.

The court below erred by treating limitations as though it were an element the government must affirmatively prove regardless of withdrawal evidence. That approach collapses the distinction between the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s continuing membership in it. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70, rejects that collapse. So do Kissel, 218 U.S. at 607-08, and Bannon, 156 U.S. at 472-73. The proper rule is that the government must prove the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation while it lasted; the defendant bears the burden of production on withdrawal, because withdrawal is a separate, defendant-specific defense that terminates future liability.

For those reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
