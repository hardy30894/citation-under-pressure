**ARGUMENT**

I. THE GOVERNMENT’S PHYSICAL PRESENCE ON TUTHILL’S PROPERTY, IN EXCESS OF THE SCOPE OF THE EASEMENT, CONSTITUTES A PER SE PHYSICAL TAKING UNDER THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause provides that private property shall not “be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical occupation of property by the government, even if pursuant to an easement, constitutes a per se taking if the government exceeds the scope of that easement. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982) (“[W]hen the 'character of the governmental action' is a permanent physical occupation of property, our cases uniformly have found a taking to the extent of the occupation, without regard to whether the action achieves an important public benefit or has only minimal economic impact on the owner.”). Here, the government’s installation and maintenance of fiber optic cables far exceeding the BPA’s current operational needs, coupled with the leasing of excess capacity to third-party telecommunications companies, constitutes a physical occupation beyond the scope of the 1953 easement.

The easement granted to the BPA in 1953 permitted the installation and maintenance of “signal lines” necessary for the operation of electrical power transmission lines. The government does not dispute that the fiber optic cables installed in the 1990s exceed the BPA’s current operational needs. *See* Appellee’s Br. at 12 (“The BPA currently needs only a fraction of the thirty-six fibers crossing Tuthill’s property to maintain the power lines.”). Moreover, the government concedes that the BPA leases the excess capacity to unrelated third parties. *Id.* at 13. This unauthorized use of Tuthill’s property—installing and maintaining cables far beyond the BPA’s current needs and permitting third-party use—exceeds the scope of the easement and constitutes a physical occupation.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946), is misplaced. In *Causby*, the Supreme Court held that frequent low flights by military aircraft over a landowner’s property constituted a taking because the flights effectively deprived the landowner of the use of the airspace. *Id.* at 266. Unlike *Causby*, where the government’s actions interfered with the landowner’s use of the property, here the government has affirmatively occupied Tuthill’s property with physical infrastructure that exceeds the scope of the easement. *See also Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 178 (1979) (holding that the government’s imposition of a public right of access to a private marina constituted a taking).

The Court of Federal Claims erred in concluding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings analysis. *See* Tuthill, slip op. at 6. The scope of the easement defines the extent of the government’s lawful authority to use Tuthill’s property. When the government exceeds that scope, it effectuates a taking. *See Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435-36 (holding that a minor but permanent physical occupation of property, even if authorized by statute, constitutes a taking). The government’s installation of fiber optic cables far beyond its current needs, and its leasing of excess capacity to third parties, is not merely an overuse of the easement—it is a physical occupation of Tuthill’s property without compensation.

II. THE GOVERNMENT’S ACTIONS CONSTITUTE A TAKING EVEN IF THEY ARE TEMPORARY OR INTERMITTENT.

Tuthill alternatively argues that, if the government’s actions are not deemed a permanent physical occupation, they constitute a temporary physical taking. The Supreme Court has recognized that even temporary physical occupations may give rise to a taking claim. *See First English Evangelical Lutheran Church v. County of Los Angeles*, 482 U.S. 304, 318 (1987) (“[A] temporary taking must be compensated just as any other.”). Here, the government’s physical presence on Tuthill’s property—installing and maintaining fiber optic cables and permitting third-party use—has persisted for decades and continues to this day. This prolonged and unauthorized use of Tuthill’s property constitutes a taking, regardless of whether it is characterized as permanent or temporary.

III. THE GOVERNMENT’S MISUSE OF THE EASEMENT DOES NOT PRECLUDE A TAKING CLAIM.

The government argues that Tuthill’s takings claim fails because the government’s actions do not constitute a trespass. *See* Appellee’s Br. at 18. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that a physical occupation, even if authorized by an easement, constitutes a taking if it exceeds the scope of that easement. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435-36. The government’s misuse of the easement—installing and maintaining fiber optic cables far beyond its current needs and leasing excess capacity to third parties—is not a mere trespass; it is a taking of Tuthill’s property without just compensation.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tuthill respectfully submits that the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment in favor of the government. The government’s physical occupation of Tuthill’s property, in excess of the scope of the easement, constitutes a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the Court of Federal Claims should be reversed, and this case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
