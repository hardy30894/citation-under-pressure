**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s ADA and MHRA Claims Because a Reasonable Jury Could Conclude That Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled.**

The ADA prohibits discrimination against a “qualified individual with a disability” and defines a “disability” to include a “physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more of the major life activities of such individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(A). The MHRA contains an identical definition. Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010(16). The “regarded as” prong of the disability definition provides that an individual is disabled if he is “regarded as having” such an impairment. 42 U.S.C. § 12102(3)(A). To prevail under this theory, a plaintiff must show that the employer “mistook a physical or mental impairment for a substantially limiting disability.” *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999).

Here, Pine Lawn terminated Epps after Chief Hardy reviewed medical reports indicating that Epps suffered from degenerative disk disease and would likely need to seek a different occupation. Hardy’s memoranda to the Mayor and Board of Aldermen repeatedly referenced Dr. Mirkin’s opinion that Epps could not perform the duties of a patrol officer and would need a different occupation. A reasonable jury could infer from this evidence that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in the major life activity of working. *See, e.g., Weber v. Strippit, Inc.*, 186 F.3d 907, 915 (8th Cir. 1999) (holding that an employer’s belief that an employee could not perform a broad class of jobs could support a “regarded as” claim).

Pine Lawn’s reliance on *Hathaway v. Runyon*, 23 F.3d 264 (8th Cir. 1994), is misplaced. In *Hathaway*, the court held that an employer’s belief that an employee could not perform a single, particular job did not establish a “regarded as” disability. *Id.* at 266. Here, by contrast, Dr. Mirkin’s opinion—that Epps would need to seek a different occupation—suggests a substantial limitation on Epps’s ability to work in a broad range of jobs, not merely his position as a patrol officer. *See also Pack v. Kmart Corp.*, 166 F.3d 1300, 1305 (10th Cir. 1999) (holding that an employer’s belief that an employee could not perform a class of jobs could support a “regarded as” claim).

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s Workers’ Compensation Retaliation Claim Because a Reasonable Jury Could Conclude That Pine Lawn Terminated Him in Retaliation for Filing Workers’ Compensation Claims.**

Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 prohibits employers from discharging or discriminating against an employee for exercising his right to file a workers’ compensation claim. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, Epps must show that (1) he engaged in statutorily protected activity; (2) he was discharged; and (3) there was a causal connection between the protected activity and the discharge. *Johnson v. Mo. Dep’t of Soc. Servs.*, 968 S.W.2d 287, 290 (Mo. Ct. App. 1998).

Here, Epps engaged in statutorily protected activity by filing workers’ compensation claims for his August 1, 1998, and April 13, 1999, injuries. Pine Lawn terminated him on March 21, 2000. A reasonable jury could infer a causal connection between Epps’s workers’ compensation claims and his termination based on Chief Hardy’s memoranda, which referenced the number of claims Epps filed and the extensive sick time he used. *See, e.g., Johnson*, 968 S.W.2d at 290 (holding that temporal proximity between protected activity and discharge, combined with other evidence, can support an inference of retaliation).

Pine Lawn’s reliance on *Holloway v. Union Pac. R.R. Co.*, 950 S.W.2d 49 (Mo. Ct. App. 1997), is unavailing. In *Holloway*, the court held that an employer’s knowledge of an employee’s workers’ compensation claim alone was insufficient to establish a causal connection. *Id.* at 52. Here, by contrast, Chief Hardy’s memoranda specifically referenced the number of claims Epps filed, which could support an inference of retaliation. *See also Johnson*, 968 S.W.2d at 290.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s ADA, MHRA, and workers’ compensation retaliation claims. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
