**ARGUMENT**

I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to an alien whose illegal reentry preceded the statute’s effective date.

The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (2000), was enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 305(a)(3), 110 Stat. 3009-546, 3009-598, and took effect April 1, 1997. Its text reaches “an alien [who] has reentered the United States illegally” after having been removed. Because the statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment,” Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994), the presumption against retroactivity requires that it be read to govern only post-enactment reentries. See also Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer, 520 U.S. 939, 946 (1997) (same presumption governs statutes that “create[] a new cause of action”).

This Court has applied the presumption to immigration provisions that enlarge the consequences of past conduct. In INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the Court held that IIRIRA’s restrictions on discretionary relief did not apply to convictions entered before the statute’s effective date, because “aliens [had] likely relied upon” the prior regime. Id. at 323. The same reliance interest exists here: an alien who reentered before 1997 could reasonably expect to remain eligible for adjustment of status under the law then in force. Nothing in the text of § 241(a)(5) supplies the “clear intent” required to overcome the presumption. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270.

II. Application of the reinstatement bar to pre-enactment reentries is impermissibly retroactive.

Even if the statute were construed to reach pre-1997 reentries, that construction would be impermissible. Retroactive effect exists when a statute “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability.” Id. at 269 (quoting Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)). Section 241(a)(5) does all three: it converts a prior removal order into a perpetual bar to any relief under the INA and eliminates the opportunity to seek adjustment of status that existed at the time of reentry.

Petitioner’s 1982 reentry gave rise to no “protectable expectation” only because the Tenth Circuit applied the new statute to conduct completed fifteen years earlier. That application “attach[es] a new disability” to the completed act of reentry, Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269, and therefore cannot be given retroactive effect absent explicit congressional direction. Because Congress supplied none, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
