ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. Competency to stand trial is governed by a single constitutional standard: whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard applies equally to the decisions to plead guilty and to waive counsel.

This Court has never adopted a distinct, higher competency test for those decisions. In Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975), the Court reiterated that the Dusky formulation measures the capacity “to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” The same language appears in Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 388 (1966). Nothing in those opinions suggests that a defendant who satisfies Dusky may nevertheless be deemed incompetent to enter a plea or to proceed pro se.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation finds no support in this Court’s precedents. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), requires that a waiver of counsel be “intelligent and competent,” but that inquiry concerns whether the waiver is knowing and voluntary, not whether a separate, elevated level of mental functioning is constitutionally mandated. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), likewise treats the competence of a self-representation waiver as governed by the Dusky standard. Once a defendant is found competent under Dusky, due process is satisfied; any additional requirement that the defendant demonstrate a “reasoned choice” among strategic alternatives is a matter of state law or evidentiary sufficiency, not federal constitutional command.

The record here shows that two psychiatrists examined Moran shortly before the plea hearing and concluded he possessed the capacity to understand the proceedings and assist counsel. Under Dusky, that determination was sufficient. The state courts therefore committed no constitutional error in accepting Moran’s guilty pleas and waiver of counsel without applying a heightened standard. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
