**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from its after-hours facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. By opening its property to a broad array of community organizations for expressive activities, the District created a public forum from which it may not exclude speech based on its religious viewpoint.

**I. The District Created a Public Forum by Opening Its Facilities to Diverse Community Groups**

When the government opens its property for expressive use by the public, it creates a public forum subject to the same constitutional protections as traditional public fora. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks “have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public” for assembly and discussion). The record shows that the District permitted the Southern Harmonizers—a musical group—to perform, and allowed a lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics. These uses were expressive and secular, but the District’s policy did not limit the forum to any particular subject matter. By admitting such groups, the District “opened the door” to the community for expressive activities. Once a forum is opened, the government may not “discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint.” *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951).

**II. The Exclusion of Religious Speech Is Impermissible Content Discrimination**

The District denied Lamb’s Chapel access solely because the proposed film series was “church related” and “religious.” This is a textbook example of content-based discrimination. In *Niemotko*, the Court struck down a city’s denial of a permit for a religious meeting in a park when permits had been granted to other groups. The Court held that “the right to use the parks was granted to some and denied to others solely on the basis of the content of their speech.” *Id.* at 273. Similarly, in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69–70 (1953), the Court invalidated an ordinance that prohibited religious meetings in a park while permitting other assemblies, stating that “to allow a city to forbid a religious meeting in a park while permitting other meetings would be a denial of equal protection.” The same principle applies here: the District allowed secular expressive uses but barred religious ones. That is viewpoint discrimination, plain and simple.

The District’s claim that its facilities are a “limited public forum” not opened to religious groups is circular. A forum’s boundaries cannot be defined by the very exclusion challenged. The District cannot avoid constitutional scrutiny by simply declaring that religious speech is never permitted. *See Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940) (the First Amendment “embraces the right to disseminate information concerning religion” and “the right to speak freely”). The District’s rule—Rule No. 7—is a prior restraint on religious speech, and “any system of prior restraints of expression comes to this Court bearing a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity.” *Bantam Books, Inc. v. Sullivan*, 372 U.S. 58, 70 (1963).

**III. The Establishment Clause Does Not Require Exclusion of Religious Speech**

The District may argue that allowing religious use would violate the Establishment Clause. But the Clause “requires the state to be a neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers; it does not require the state to be their adversary.” *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947). Neutrality means equal access, not hostility. In *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952), the Court upheld a released-time program that allowed students to leave school for religious instruction, emphasizing that “the First Amendment does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State.” Permitting Lamb’s Chapel to use school facilities on the same terms as other community groups would not establish religion; it would simply treat religious speech on an equal footing with secular speech. The District’s blanket exclusion “exhibits a hostility to religion which has no place in our constitutional traditions.” *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 211–12 (1948) (Jackson, J., concurring).

**Conclusion**

The District’s policy discriminates against religious speech in a public forum, violating the Free Speech Clause. The Establishment Clause does not compel such discrimination. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
