ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC on the Grounds that the Release Bars Bancorp's Claims

The district court incorrectly concluded that the release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, bars its claims against the FDIC. The release only covers claims "arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." The claims asserted by Bancorp in the instant action, however, relate to the FDIC's actions as receiver, including its payment of post-insolvency interest to itself, its sale of Bank assets, and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These claims do not "arise from or with respect to" the OCC's decision to close the Bank.

The interpretation of a release is a matter of state law, and under New York law, a release will not be read to cover claims that are not explicitly mentioned unless it is clear that such was the parties' intent. See Mangini v. McClurg, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969) ("A release may not be read to cover matters which the parties did not desire or intend to dispose of."); see also Gordon v. Bialystoker Ctr. & Bikur Cholim, Inc., 45 N.Y.2d 692, 697 (1978) (holding that a release should be interpreted in light of the parties' intent). The language of the release here does not explicitly mention the FDIC's actions as receiver, and there is no evidence that Bancorp intended to release the FDIC from claims related to its receivership.

Furthermore, the release was executed as part of a settlement between the FDIC and Joseph Chuang, and Bancorp was not a party to that action. Under these circumstances, it is unlikely that Bancorp intended to release the FDIC from claims that were not at issue in that litigation. See In re Schaefer, 18 F.3d 130, 134 (2d Cir. 1994) (noting that a release should be interpreted in light of the context in which it was executed).

The district court's conclusion that the release bars Bancorp's claims is also inconsistent with the principle that a release should be construed narrowly. See Wells v. Shearson Lehman/Am. Express, Inc., 72 N.Y.2d 11, 19 (1988) ("A release is a contract, and its construction is governed by contract law."); see also Stone v. Nat'l Bank & Trust Co., 188 A.D.2d 865, 867 (N.Y. App. Div. 1992) (holding that a release should be construed narrowly to avoid unintended consequences).

II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC on the Grounds that the Statute of Limitations Bars Bancorp's Claims

The district court also erred in concluding that Bancorp's claims are barred by New York's six-year statute of limitations. Under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), a claim for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, or corporate waste must be brought within six years of the accrual of the claim.

The accrual date for Bancorp's claims is a matter of dispute. The FDIC argues that the claims accrued when the FDIC took the actions complained of, while Bancorp contends that the claims did not accrue until the receivership was terminated on November 1, 1995. The district court erred in adopting the FDIC's position.

Under New York law, a claim for breach of fiduciary duty accrues when the fiduciary openly repudiates its obligation or the plaintiff otherwise becomes aware of the breach. See Westchester Religious Inst. v. Kamerman, 262 A.D.2d 131, 131 (N.Y. App. Div. 1999); see also Kaufman v. Cohen, 307 A.D.2d 113, 122 (N.Y. App. Div. 2003) (holding that a claim for breach of fiduciary duty accrues when the plaintiff discovers or should have discovered the breach). In this case, Bancorp's claims relate to the FDIC's actions as receiver, and the FDIC did not openly repudiate its obligations as receiver until it terminated the receivership.

Furthermore, the FDIC's actions as receiver were not fully known to Bancorp until the receivership was terminated and an accounting was provided. See FDIC v. Providence Coll., 115 F.3d 136, 141 (2d Cir. 1997) (noting that a receiver's duties include providing an accounting to the parties interested in the receivership estate). Under these circumstances, it is reasonable to conclude that Bancorp's claims did not accrue until the receivership was terminated.

The district court's conclusion that the statute of limitations bars Bancorp's claims is also inconsistent with the principle that a statute of limitations should not be applied to bar a claim that was not reasonably discoverable by the plaintiff. See Corcoran v. N.Y. Power Auth., 202 F.3d 530, 544 (2d Cir. 1999) (noting that a statute of limitations may be tolled if the plaintiff was unable to discover the claim due to circumstances beyond its control); see also Merine v. Prudential-Bache Util. Fund, Inc., 859 F. Supp. 715, 720 (S.D.N.Y. 1994) (holding that a statute of limitations may be tolled if the plaintiff was unaware of the claim due to the defendant's concealment).

In conclusion, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to the FDIC on the grounds that the release and statute of limitations bar Bancorp's claims. The release does not cover Bancorp's claims, and the statute of limitations did not begin to run until the receivership was terminated on November 1, 1995. Therefore, the judgment of the district court should be reversed.
