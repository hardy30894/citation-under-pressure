The plain language of 28 USC section 1407(a) mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's clear directive is reinforced by a long line of cases emphasizing the importance of adhering to the statutory text. See, e.g., United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) ("There is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes.").

The District of Arizona's invocation of section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial contravenes the statutory scheme established by section 1407(a). As the court noted in Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949), section 1404(a) is a general transfer provision that must be read in conjunction with other statutory provisions governing the transfer of cases. In this case, the specific mandate of section 1407(a) takes precedence over the general transfer authority granted by section 1404(a).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in the instant case, as well as the Second Circuit's holding in a prior case, and other cases that have permitted transferee courts to invoke section 1404(a) to retain cases for trial, are inconsistent with the statutory text and the principles of statutory construction. Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204 (1932), held that a specific statutory provision takes precedence over a more general one. The court stated that "where there is, in the same statute, a particular enactment, and also a general one, which, in its most comprehensive sense, would include what is embraced in the former, the particular enactment must be operative, and the general enactment must be taken to affect only such cases within its general language as are not within the provisions of the particular enactment." Id. at 208.

Furthermore, the practice of allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial under section 1404(a) undermines the policy choice reflected in section 1407(a), which is to limit the transfer of cases to pretrial proceedings and preserve the plaintiff's traditional right to select the forum for trial. See, e.g., Baltimore & Ohio R. Co. v. Kepner, 314 U.S. 44, 52-53 (1941) (emphasizing the importance of the plaintiff's choice of forum).

The Supreme Court has consistently held that statutory provisions should be interpreted in a manner that gives effect to their plain meaning. See, e.g., Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain...the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms."). In this case, the plain meaning of section 1407(a) requires remand to the original district at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings.

Other cases have also emphasized the importance of adhering to the statutory text and respecting the policy choices reflected in the statute. See, e.g., United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. 76, 95-96 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) ("The intention of the legislature is to be collected from the words they employ."); United States v. Dickerson, 310 U.S. 554 (1940); FTC v. Bunte Bros., 312 U.S. 349, 352-53 (1941) (noting that "the plain words of the statute" should be given effect). In Dickerson, the Court stated that "the function of interpretation is to construe the language so as to give effect to the intent of Congress." 310 U.S. at 562.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the District of Arizona erred in invoking section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial, and the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of that decision was incorrect. The plain language of section 1407(a) and the principles of statutory construction require that the case be remanded to the original district for trial.
