# ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit's construction of "actual knowledge" in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) inverts the presumption of knowledge that has long governed limitations statutes and rewards willful inattention. This Court should reverse.

## I. The Plain Text of Section 1113(2) Forecloses a Subjective-Recall Requirement.

"[T]he meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Congress's word "actual" modifies "knowledge," not "recollection." A participant who receives, opens, and has the opportunity to read a disclosure possesses knowledge of its contents at that moment; that he later forgets having read it does not retroactively erase the knowledge the disclosure conferred. Reading "actual knowledge" to require a plaintiff's present, litigation-time recollection would produce the very "absurd or futile results" this Court has cautioned against when construing statutory text. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress could not have intended a limitations trigger dependent on the vagaries of memory tested years later at deposition.

## II. Settled Principles of Trust and Contract Law Impute Knowledge of Disclosed Information to the Recipient.

This Court has long held that a person who receives a document defining his rights and obligations cannot avoid its legal effect merely by professing ignorance of its contents. In *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. 45, 50 (1875), the Court explained that "[i]t will not do for a man to enter into a contract, and, when called upon to respond to its obligations, to say that he did not read it when he signed it, or did not know what it contained." The same logic governs here. Intel furnished Sulyma, as a plan participant, with fund fact sheets, summary plan descriptions, and annual disclosures describing the very asset allocations he now challenges. Under *Upton*, Sulyma cannot manufacture a triable issue by asserting that he never read, or does not recall reading, materials Intel undisputedly provided him. ERISA's fiduciary provisions were built on this common-law trust foundation, under which a trustee's duty to inform is satisfied—and the beneficiary charged with knowledge—upon delivery of a full and accurate accounting.

## III. This Court's Limitations Jurisprudence Confirms That Ignorance Unaccompanied by Concealment Does Not Extend a Statute of Repose.

Where Congress enacts a limitations period, this Court has refused to graft onto it open-ended tolling based on subjective ignorance absent proof that the defendant concealed the relevant facts. In *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 140 (1879), the Court held that "[c]oncealment by mere silence is not enough. There must be some trick or contrivance intended to exclude suspicion and prevent inquiry." Sulyma does not allege that Intel concealed anything; the Ninth Circuit itself found that Intel "disclosed these investment decisions ... through various documents." A rule extending limitations indefinitely whenever a plaintiff simply declines to read what he is given, with no concealment by the defendant, cannot be reconciled with *Wood*. Nor can it be reconciled with *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874), and *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435 (1918), both of which tied any extension of a limitations period to the defendant's fraudulent concealment, not the plaintiff's inattention. Even the discovery rule this Court has read into federal limitations statutes generally exists to prevent a wrongdoer from profiting by his own concealment, not to reward a beneficiary's disregard of information placed before him. *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 396 (1946) ("This equitable doctrine is read into every federal statute of limitation."). Here there is no wrongdoer to be denied an unjust advantage; it is Sulyma, not Intel, who seeks to benefit from a failure to read.

Finally, the certainty that repose provisions supply would be destroyed by the Ninth Circuit's rule. As this Court has observed, "[s]tatutes of limitation find their justification in necessity and convenience rather than in logic. They represent expedients, rather than principles." *Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson*, 325 U.S. 304, 314 (1945). Congress adopted a three-year actual-knowledge bar, distinct from the six-year outer limit, to give fiduciaries the "necessity and convenience" of repose once they have made full disclosure. A rule permitting plaintiffs to defeat that repose through a bare assertion of non-recollection—despite undisputed evidence of receipt—would convert every three-year bar into the six-year bar Congress separately provided, rendering the shorter period surplusage, a result this Court's interpretive principles forbid. *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
