**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT ESQD ARC MAPS ARE “INTERNAL PERSONNEL RULES AND PRACTICES” UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 2.

FOIA Exemption 2 permits withholding only “matters that are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit’s expansive interpretation of this exemption conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976) (quoting *S.Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 3* (1965)). The Court has emphasized that Exemption 2 is limited to “routine matters of no genuine and significant public interest,” *id.* at 363 (quoting *S.Rep. No. 813 at 8*), and does not extend to technical safety data used to protect the public.

The ESQD arc maps at issue here are not “internal personnel rules and practices.” They are operational safety tools used to design storage facilities and mitigate risks to surrounding communities. *See* Joint Appendix at 45 (Commander Whitbred’s affidavit stating ESQD arcs are “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy”). The Supreme Court has cautioned that Exemption 2 does not shield “operational files” or “matters of public concern.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363-64 (distinguishing Exemption 2 from broader exemptions like Exemption 7). The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced, as *Hardy* involved internal law enforcement manuals—not public safety data. *Id.* at 655.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test, which the Ninth Circuit adopted, has no basis in the statute or Supreme Court precedent. *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1072-74 (D.C. Cir. 1981). The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to expand Exemption 2 beyond its plain text. *See, e.g., Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361 (exemptions must be “explicitly exclusive”); *Adm’r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975) (exemptions are “narrowly construed”).

II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT DISCLOSURE OF ESQD ARC MAPS “PRESENTS A RISK OF CIRCUMVENTING AGENCY REGULATION.”

The Ninth Circuit’s “risk of circumvention” test improperly expands Exemption 2 beyond its statutory scope. The Supreme Court has held that Exemption 2 applies only to “trivial matters” of no public significance. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363. The risk of circumvention must relate to “internal personnel rules,” not operational safety data. *See id.* at 369 (Exemption 2 protects only “routine matters”).

The Navy’s claimed risk—that disclosure could aid an attack—is speculative and unsupported by evidence. The Supreme Court has rejected similar “vague and unsubstantiated” claims of harm. *See, e.g., United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 10 (1953) (requiring specific evidence of harm for privilege claims). The Navy has not classified the ESQD arc maps under Exemption 1, further undermining its national security rationale. *See id.* at 7 (government cannot withhold unclassified documents under a generalized secrecy claim).

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION OF EXEMPTION 2 CONFLICTS WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT.

This Court has long held that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed. *See, e.g., United States v. Murdock*, 284 U.S. 141, 148 (1931) (exemptions to disclosure are strictly limited). The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test disregards this principle. *Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1072-74.

The Supreme Court has also cautioned against conflating Exemption 2 with other exemptions. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363-64. The Ninth Circuit’s approach effectively merges Exemption 2 with Exemption 7(F), which applies only to law enforcement materials. *See id.* at 369 (Exemption 2 does not cover “law enforcement” materials unless they are “routine”).

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION THREATENS THE PUBLIC’S RIGHT TO INFORMATION ABOUT COMMUNITY SAFETY.

The ESQD arc maps are critical to public understanding of risks posed by Navy operations. The Supreme Court has recognized that FOIA serves “the public’s right to know.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 372. The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines this principle by allowing agencies to withhold safety data under an overbroad interpretation of Exemption 2.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that ESQD arc maps are exempt under FOIA Exemption 2. The decision conflicts with Supreme Court precedent and threatens the public’s right to access safety-related information. This Court should reverse.
