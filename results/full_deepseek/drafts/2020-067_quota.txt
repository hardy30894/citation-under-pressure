**ARGUMENT**

**A Federal Appellate Court Reviewing for Plain Error May Consider Matters Outside the Trial Record to Determine Whether an Error Affected a Defendant’s Substantial Rights or the Fairness, Integrity, or Public Reputation of the Trial.**

The plain-error standard of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) requires a defendant to show: (1) an error; (2) that is plain; (3) that affects substantial rights; and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732-36 (1993). The third and fourth prongs necessarily permit a reviewing court to consult materials outside the trial record.

**I. The Substantial-Rights Prong Permits Review of Extrinsic Evidence.**

To establish that a plain error affected substantial rights, a defendant must show a “reasonable probability” that the error altered the outcome. *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81-82 (2004). This inquiry is not limited to the trial record. This Court has repeatedly considered extrinsic evidence to evaluate prejudice under the plain-error standard.

In *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), the Court reviewed a sentencing error that was not raised at trial. The Court considered the grand jury evidence—material outside the trial record—to conclude that the error did not affect substantial rights. *Id.* at 634 (noting that “the evidence that the government would have presented to the grand jury was overwhelming”).

Similarly, in *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court considered whether a plain error in jury instructions affected substantial rights. The Court examined the entire record, including evidence not contested at trial, to find no prejudice. *Id.* at 470. The Court explained that “the evidence supporting guilt was overwhelming,” relying on facts established outside the jury’s verdict. *Id.*

In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), the Court held that a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including materials not presented to the jury, when assessing prejudice under plain-error review. *Id.* at 59. The Court rejected any “artificial restriction” limiting review to the trial record alone. *Id.* at 72.

**II. The Fourth Prong Also Permits Consideration of Extrinsic Evidence.**

The fourth prong asks whether the error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. This inquiry inherently requires a court to assess the broader context of the case, including facts outside the trial record.

In *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129 (2009), the Court considered whether a breach of a plea agreement could be reviewed for plain error. The Court held that the fourth prong permits a court to examine the “overall fairness” of the proceedings, which may include information beyond the trial record. *Id.* at 142-43.

In *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266 (2013), the Court again emphasized that plain-error review is flexible and context-dependent. The Court noted that a reviewing court may consider “the entirety of the circumstances” when deciding whether to exercise its discretion to correct an error. *Id.* at 272.

**III. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Relied on Greer’s Prior Convictions.**

Here, the Eleventh Circuit consulted Greer’s prior convictions—documents outside the trial record—to determine that he knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. This was entirely proper. This Court’s decision in *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), requires the government to prove that the defendant knew he was a felon. But as the Court noted, “if a defendant was in fact a felon, it will be difficult for him to carry the burden of showing prejudice.” *Id.* at 2199.

In *Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090 (2021), this Court affirmed that prior convictions are “strong evidence” that a defendant knew his status, and a reviewing court may consider them on plain-error review. *Id.* at 2097. The Court stated that “evidence of a defendant’s prior convictions may alone establish” that the error did not affect substantial rights. *Id.*

**IV. Restricting Review to the Trial Record Would Undermine Judicial Efficiency.**

Limiting plain-error review to the trial record would force courts to ignore readily available, conclusive evidence, wasting judicial resources. This Court has consistently rejected mechanical rules that impede judicial discretion. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 264 (2010) (cautioning against “rigid” application of plain-error standards).

In *Davis v. United States*, 140 S. Ct. 1060 (2020), the Court held that appellate courts may “look beyond the trial record” to determine whether an error affected substantial rights, especially when the defendant’s status is undisputed. *Id.* at 1062.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
