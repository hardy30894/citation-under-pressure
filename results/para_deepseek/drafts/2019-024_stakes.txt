**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by requiring that a plaintiff have “actual knowledge” not only of the conduct constituting an ERISA breach, but also that the conduct was imprudent. That standard reads a constructive-knowledge element into a statute that Congress deliberately stripped of any such requirement. The plain text of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars suit three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” Nothing in that language requires knowledge of a legal conclusion—whether the conduct was imprudent, prohibited, or otherwise unlawful. This Court should reverse.

**I. Actual Knowledge Means Knowledge of the Underlying Facts, Not Their Legal Significance**

The statute’s text is dispositive. “Actual knowledge” is knowledge of the “breach or violation”—that is, knowledge of the facts that constitute the breach, not knowledge that those facts amount to a breach under ERISA. *See Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985) (holding that § 1113 “is triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law”). Every circuit to address the issue agrees. *See, e.g., Martin v. Consultants & Administrators, Inc.*, 966 F.2d 1078, 1086 (7th Cir. 1992) (“actual knowledge” means “knowledge of the facts or transaction that constituted the violation”); *Gluck v. Unisys Corp.*, 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992) (same). The Ninth Circuit itself had long adhered to this rule. *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (quoting *Blanton*). Its decision below departed from that settled understanding.

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a plaintiff know the investments were “imprudent” effectively demands knowledge of a legal conclusion. Imprudence is the ultimate issue in a § 1104 claim. By requiring knowledge of imprudence, the court transformed the limitations period into a six-year default, because a plaintiff can always claim he did not appreciate the legal significance of disclosed facts. That result nullifies the three-year bar Congress enacted.

**II. The 1987 Amendment Confirms That Actual Knowledge Is Not Constructive Knowledge**

Congress originally included a constructive-knowledge provision in § 1113, which would have started the clock when a report was filed “from which [the plaintiff] could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976). Congress repealed that provision in 1987. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively resurrects constructive knowledge by requiring a plaintiff to have understood the legal implications of disclosed facts. That cannot be squared with Congress’s choice to retain only “actual knowledge.” *See* *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633, 648 (2010) (interpreting analogous “actual knowledge” standard in securities law and noting that Congress’s removal of constructive-knowledge language “indicates that actual knowledge is required”).

**III. A Plaintiff’s Failure to Read or Recall Disclosed Information Does Not Negate Actual Knowledge**

The Ninth Circuit held that Sulyma’s testimony that he was “unaware” of the investments and did not “recall” reading disclosures created a genuine dispute of material fact. That holding conflicts with the fundamental principle that a party cannot avoid summary judgment by “rest[ing] on mere allegations or denials.” *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). Intel presented undisputed evidence that Sulyma received and accessed documents—Fund Fact Sheets, annual disclosures, and website materials—that explicitly stated the Funds’ allocation to alternative investments and the strategy behind them. Sulyma’s self-serving assertion of ignorance, without any evidence that he never received or accessed the information, is insufficient to create a genuine issue. *See Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 586–87 (1986) (nonmoving party must produce “significant probative evidence” to defeat summary judgment).

Moreover, “actual knowledge” does not require that a plaintiff read every document or remember every detail. When a plaintiff receives clear disclosures of the facts underlying an alleged breach, he has actual knowledge of those facts as a matter of law. *See* *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (actual knowledge established where plan administrators were “informed” of the transaction). To hold otherwise would reward willful blindness and allow plaintiffs to extend the limitations period indefinitely by simply averring ignorance.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Undermines ERISA’s Purpose**

ERISA’s limitations period is designed to “promote the prompt resolution of disputes” and “prevent the litigation of stale claims.” *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523, 529 (2015). The Ninth Circuit’s rule frustrates that purpose by allowing plaintiffs to delay suit indefinitely, even when they have been fully informed of the facts. Plan fiduciaries who comply with disclosure obligations should be able to rely on the three-year bar once the plaintiff knows the underlying conduct. The decision below imposes an impossible burden on defendants and invites meritless litigation.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that a plaintiff has “actual knowledge” of a breach under § 1113(2) when he knows the facts that constitute the breach, regardless of whether he knows those facts amount to a violation or whether he read or recalls the disclosures.
