## ARGUMENT

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the School District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s access to school facilities did not violate the First Amendment. The District opened its facilities to a wide range of community groups, thereby creating a limited public forum. Once it did so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel based solely on the religious viewpoint of its proposed films.

### I. The District Created a Limited Public Forum

A limited public forum arises when the government intentionally opens its property for expressive activity by certain speakers or on certain subjects. *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983). The record here demonstrates that the District permitted numerous outside organizations to use its facilities for presentations on family values, child-rearing, and character development—the very subjects of Lamb’s Chapel’s film series. The District allowed, for example, lectures on psychology and the unknown, musical performances, and community meetings. By regularly opening its doors to such groups, the District “created a forum generally open for use by” the community. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981).

The Court of Appeals improperly focused on whether the District had previously admitted groups “akin to Lamb’s Chapel.” That narrow inquiry ignores the broader principle: once a forum is opened to speech on a given subject, the government may not discriminate against particular viewpoints within that subject. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Here, the District allowed secular groups to discuss family values. It cannot close the forum to a religious group that wishes to discuss the same topic from a faith-based perspective.

### II. The Exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel Constitutes Viewpoint Discrimination

Even in a limited public forum, restrictions on speech must be “reasonable and not based on viewpoint.” *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46. The District’s rule—barring all “religious purposes”—is not viewpoint-neutral. It singles out religious speech for disfavored treatment. This Court has repeatedly held that the First Amendment prohibits the government from “regulating speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction.” *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995).

The District’s policy is not a mere content-based restriction; it is a viewpoint-based restriction. The District welcomed presentations on family values from a secular perspective but excluded Lamb’s Chapel’s presentation on the same subject simply because it was grounded in Christian teachings. The film series was “designed to share some practical insights about the family.” The District’s own denial letter stated that “[t]his film does appear to be church related and therefore your request must be refused.” That is the precise definition of viewpoint discrimination.

This Court has rejected similar attempts to exclude religious speech from otherwise open forums. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a state university could not deny student religious groups access to facilities generally open to other student groups. 454 U.S. at 277. Although the instant case involves community groups rather than students, the principle is the same: “the University’s institutional interest in promoting a particular type of viewpoint” cannot justify discrimination. *Id.* at 276. The District’s desire to avoid any appearance of endorsing religion is not a valid basis for silencing religious speech in a forum otherwise open to secular speech on the same topic.

### III. The District’s Establishment Clause Concern Does Not Justify Viewpoint Discrimination

The District argues that permitting religious groups to use school facilities would violate the Establishment Clause. But this Court has made clear that “an equal access policy” does not constitute an establishment of religion. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271. The Court in *Widmar* explained that allowing religious groups to use facilities on the same terms as other groups “would not be inconsistent with the Establishment Clause’s prohibition against the ‘sponsorship or financial support’ of religion.” *Id.* at 274-75 (quoting *Committee for Public Education & Religious Liberty v. Nyquist*, 413 U.S. 756, 771 (1973)). The same reasoning applies here. The District would not be sponsoring or endorsing Lamb’s Chapel’s films by allowing them in the same way it allows secular films. Any benefit to religion would be “incidental” and “no more an establishment of religion than the marginal benefit to religious groups that this Court has held does not violate the Establishment Clause.” *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (plurality opinion).

The Court of Appeals relied on the fact that the District had not previously opened its facilities to religious groups. But that is a self-fulfilling prophecy: the District’s policy prevented any such use. The critical question is what the District *did* open its facilities to—and it opened them to a broad array of groups discussing family and moral issues. Having done so, the District could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel because of its religious viewpoint.

### Conclusion

The School District created a limited public forum for community speech on family and child-rearing topics. It then excluded Lamb’s Chapel solely because of the religious perspective of its films. That is viewpoint discrimination in violation of the Free Speech Clause. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
