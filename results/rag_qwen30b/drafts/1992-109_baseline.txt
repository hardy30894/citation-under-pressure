**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the record in this case raised a good faith doubt about Richard Allan Moran’s competency to make such a momentous decision, and that the trial court therefore had a constitutional duty to conduct a hearing to assess his capacity for reasoned choice. The State’s contrary argument—that the standard for competency to stand trial is sufficient—misreads the law and undermines the integrity of the criminal justice system.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights, such as the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial, demands “utmost solicitude” from the courts. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973) (citing *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969)). This heightened scrutiny is not merely procedural—it is rooted in the constitutional guarantee of due process. When a defendant waives counsel and pleads guilty, he surrenders a constellation of rights essential to a fair trial. *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981). Such a waiver cannot be presumed valid; instead, it must be “knowing, intelligent, and voluntary.” *McMann v. Richardson*, 397 U.S. 759, 770, 90 S.Ct. 1441, 1448, 25 L.Ed.2d 763 (1970).

The critical distinction lies in the standard of competency required. A defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him, and is capable of assisting counsel.” *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975) (quoting *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387, 86 S.Ct. 836, 843, 15 L.Ed.2d 815 (1966)). This standard, known as the *Dusky* standard, focuses on the defendant’s ability to comprehend the charges and assist in his defense. But competency to waive constitutional rights demands more. As this Court has held, a defendant must possess the “capacity for reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him.” *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987) (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)); see also *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. This higher threshold reflects the gravity of decisions to forgo counsel and plead guilty—choices that can result in the loss of life.

The record here demonstrates that the trial court failed to apply this higher standard. Moran had recently attempted suicide, was in a state of profound psychological distress, and was taking four different medications, including Inderal, Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Vistaril—drugs that affect cognition, motor function, and emotional regulation. *See* Ninth Cir. Op. at 972 F.2d 263, 267. His responses during the plea colloquy were monosyllabic and unresponsive, suggesting a diminished capacity for rational deliberation. Yet the court made no inquiry into the effects of his medication or his mental state beyond a single, perfunctory question. *Id.* at 267. The court accepted his waiver of counsel and guilty pleas without any meaningful assessment of whether he could make a reasoned choice among the alternatives—such as retaining counsel, seeking mitigation, or contesting the charges.

The State argues that the postconviction hearing, which concluded Moran was competent to stand trial, satisfies due process. But this argument fails. The state court relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were designed to assess competency to stand trial—not the higher standard of reasoned choice. *Id.* at 268. The psychiatrists did not evaluate Moran’s ability to weigh the consequences of waiving counsel or pleading guilty. Their reports, which focused on Moran’s ability to understand the charges and assist counsel, are irrelevant to the question of whether he could make a reasoned choice. *Id.* at 269. The state court’s failure to apply the correct legal standard renders its finding constitutionally inadequate. *See* *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315, 83 S.Ct. 745, 758, 9 L.Ed.2d 770 (1963) (a federal court may not assume a state court applied the correct legal standard when the record suggests otherwise).

The State also contends that Moran’s own statements—expressing remorse and a desire to prevent mitigation—demonstrate his intent and thus his competence. But intent is not the same as capacity. A defendant may sincerely wish to waive rights without being able to understand the consequences of that decision. *See* *Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637, 647, 96 S.Ct. 2253, 2259, 49 L.Ed.2d 108 (1976) (a guilty plea must be “voluntary and intelligent,” not merely “willing”). The fact that Moran wanted to prevent mitigation does not prove he understood the full scope of what he was giving up.
