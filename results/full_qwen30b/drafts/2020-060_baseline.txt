**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal issue: whether Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered its right to seek contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that it did. The district court erred in holding otherwise, and its decision conflicts with binding precedent and the plain text of CERCLA. We respectfully ask this Court to reverse.

### I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)

CERCLA’s structure is clear: a party that has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action” in an “administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B). 42 U.S.C. § 113(f)(3)(B). The statute does not require an admission of liability; it requires only that the party “resolved” its liability for a response action. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the 2004 Consent Decree satisfied this standard.

The Consent Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system to prevent leachate from entering the Lonfit River. Consent Decree at 5–12, J.A. 94–101. These obligations were not merely administrative; they constituted a full response action under CERCLA. The Act defines “remedial action” to include “the collection of leachate and runoff,” 42 U.S.C. § 101(24), and the cover system was precisely such a remedial measure. The EPA’s own 1988 Record of Decision had already designated the Navy as a potentially responsible party (PRP), and the Clean Water Act complaint explicitly alleged that the discharge of untreated leachate violated the Act. CWA Compl. ¶ 21, J.A. 85. Thus, the response action at issue was not a mere regulatory compliance measure—it was a full-scale remediation effort.

The Consent Decree’s terms demonstrate that Guam “resolved” its liability. It agreed to perform the very response action that CERCLA requires of PRPs. As the D.C. Circuit observed, “a party that agrees to perform a response action in a settlement has resolved its liability for that action.” *Guam v. United States*, 947 F.3d 1, 10 (D.C. Cir. 2020). The fact that the decree was entered without a finding of liability does not alter this conclusion. *See Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1118, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (“Parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.”). The disclaimer of liability in the Consent Decree—“without any finding or admission of liability”—is a standard clause in such agreements and does not negate the substantive resolution of liability. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (disclaimer of liability not dispositive where party assumed remedial obligations).

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that the decree “did not resolve Guam’s liability”—rests on a misreading of the statute. It held that because the decree did not “resolve” liability in the sense of a judicial adjudication, it could not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). But CERCLA does not require a judicial finding. *See Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167 (“Congress created a new cause of action for contribution, not by reference to common law, but by statute.”). The statute’s plain language—“resolved its liability”—refers to the *effect* of the settlement, not the *form* of the agreement. The D.C. Circuit correctly applied this principle.

### II. The Statute of Limitations for Contribution Claims Began to Run When the Consent Decree Was Entered

Section 113(g)(3) provides that a contribution claim must be filed within three years of the “entry of a settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 113(g)(3). The Consent Decree was entered in 2004. Guam’s 2017 complaint was therefore barred by the statute of limitations. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 64 (1998) (“The statute of limitations is a jurisdictional bar.”).

Guam argues that the statute of limitations did not begin to run because the “response action” was not complete. But the statute does not require completion of the response action. It requires only that the settlement “resolve” liability for some or all of the response action. *See Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167. The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the entry of the Consent Decree triggered the three-year clock. *Guam*, 947 F.3d at 11. The fact that cleanup continues does not extend the limitations period. *See United States v. A & M Battery Co.*, 928 F.2d 1478, 1483 (10th Cir. 1991) (statute of limitations begins when settlement is entered, not when cleanup is complete).

### III. The District Court’s Error Creates a Dangerous Loophole

The district court’s interpretation would allow a PRP to avoid the three-year statute of limitations by simply refusing to admit liability in a settlement. That would undermine CERCLA’s purpose of encouraging settlements and ensuring timely resolution of liability. *See Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167 (“Congress intended to provide a mechanism for PRPs to settle with the government and then seek contribution from other PRPs.”).
