**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after having withdrawn from it—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of criminal law: the prosecution must prove every element of a crime, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. The lower court’s contrary holding, which shifts the burden of persuasion to the defendant to prove withdrawal, violates the Fifth and Sixth Amendments and contravenes settled precedent.

The government’s burden to prove conspiracy membership beyond a reasonable doubt is not diminished by a defendant’s alleged withdrawal. In *McDaniel v. Traylor*, 212 U.S. 428, 434 (1909), this Court held that “the essence of the suit is the alleged fraudulent combination and conspiracy,” and that “the value of the matter in dispute” in such cases “was the aggregate amount of all the claims so allowed against the estate.” This principle underscores that a conspiracy is not a mere aggregation of isolated acts, but a continuing agreement whose existence and scope must be proven by the government. The government cannot rely on the defendant’s silence or absence to establish the conspiracy’s continuity; it must affirmatively prove that the defendant was a party to the agreement during the relevant period.

The burden of persuasion on the government is not altered by the defendant’s conduct prior to the limitations period. In *Haas v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 462, 470 (1910), this Court emphasized that “the introduction of copies of the New York indictments” did not “so conclusively destroy the evidence afforded by copies of the District of Columbia indictments as to leave no testimony upon which the commissioner might, upon the whole case, decide that there was proof of probable cause.” This principle applies with equal force in the context of conspiracy: the government must provide sufficient evidence to support a finding of membership, even if the defendant’s conduct prior to the limitations period is relevant. The mere fact that a defendant may have ceased participation does not relieve the government of its obligation to prove ongoing membership.

The government’s burden is not satisfied by mere inference or speculation. In *Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp.*, 340 U.S. 558, 565 (1951), the Court held that “the judgment is prima facie evidence of the conspiracy and also of the performance of such acts in accomplishing it as the jury in the criminal case, in rendering a verdict of guilty, necessarily found to have occurred.” This language confirms that the government must prove not only the existence of a conspiracy but also the defendant’s participation in the acts that constitute it. The jury’s verdict in a criminal case is not a substitute for the government’s burden in a civil or collateral proceeding; rather, it is evidence that must be evaluated under the standard of proof. The government cannot shift the burden to the defendant to disprove participation.

The defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy is not a defense that can be invoked without proof. In *Howard v. Fleming*, 191 U.S. 126, 132 (1903), this Court upheld a trial court’s instruction that “the guilt of the accused must be shown beyond a reasonable doubt, and that on a failure in this respect it was the duty to acquit.” The Court further noted that “the omission in a state trial of any reference to the presumption of innocence does not invalidate the proceedings.” This reaffirms that the burden of proof remains with the prosecution throughout the trial, including in cases involving conspiracy. The defendant is not required to prove his innocence or his withdrawal; the government must prove his guilt.

The lower court’s decision effectively creates a new rule: that a defendant who withdraws from a conspiracy must prove his withdrawal to avoid liability. This is contrary to *In Re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95, 100 (1895), where this Court held that “the empanelling and swearing of the jury and the statement of his case by the district attorney put the prisoner in jeopardy in respect of all the offences charged in the consolidated indictment.” The Court rejected the notion that a defendant could be tried on one count after being placed in jeopardy on multiple counts. Similarly, a defendant cannot be held liable for conspiracy after withdrawal unless the government proves his continued membership.

The government’s burden is not reduced by the passage of time or by the defendant’s absence. In *First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co.*, 391 U.S. 253, 260 (1968), the Court held that “petitioner’s total failure by that date to produce any evidence tending to show Cities’ participation in a conspiracy to boycott him, despite considerable discovery, demonstrated that additional discovery would be merely a fishing expedition.” This principle applies here: the government must produce evidence of ongoing participation, not rely on the defendant’s failure to respond. The burden remains on the prosecution.

The lower court’s decision also undermines the principle that a conspiracy is a continuing offense. In *Partmar Corp. v. Paramount Pictures Theatres Corp.*, 347 U.S. 89, 92 (1954), the Court held that “the judgment in the case of United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc., 334 U.S. 131, which, it argues, would provide, under § 5 of the Clayton Act, 38 Stat. 731, 15 U.S.C. § 16, prima facie evidence of the conspiracy on which the counterclaims were based.” This confirms that the government must prove the existence and scope of a conspiracy, including the defendant’s role in it, at the time of the alleged offense.

Finally, the government’s burden is not satisfied by the mere existence of a prior agreement. In *Hyde v. Shine*, 199 U.S. 62, 67 (1905), the Court held that “the Supreme Court of the District of Columbia cannot take cognizance of the case under the new code which took effect upon that date,” and that jurisdiction must be determined by the law in effect at the time of the offense. This principle applies to conspiracy: the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period, not merely that he was once a member.
