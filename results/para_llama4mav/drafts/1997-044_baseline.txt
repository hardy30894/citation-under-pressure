The Third Circuit erred in holding that the treble damages award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is nondischargeable under ?523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. Although the court correctly determined that Cohen's debt to the respondents was nondischargeable due to his fraudulent conduct, it misinterpreted the scope of ?523(a)(2)(A) by including non-compensatory damages within its ambit.

The language of ?523(a)(2)(A) specifically excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The phrase "to the extent obtained by" is crucial in limiting the nondischargeable debt to the amount actually procured through fraudulent means. As the Ninth Circuit held in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 201-02 (9th Cir. 1991), this language indicates that only the amount of debt directly resulting from the debtor's fraud is nondischargeable.

In contrast, the Eleventh Circuit's decision in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), relied upon by the Third Circuit, focused on the broad definition of "debt" under the Bankruptcy Code, concluding that it encompasses both compensatory and punitive damages. However, this interpretation ignores the limiting phrase "to the extent obtained by" in ?523(a)(2)(A), which ties the nondischargeable debt to the amount obtained through fraud.

The Supreme Court's decision in Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), while establishing the preponderance of the evidence standard for ?523(a)(2) claims, does not support the Third Circuit's expansive reading of the provision. Instead, it emphasizes the importance of proving that the debt was obtained through fraudulent means.

In this case, the treble damages award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act exceeds the amount of debt obtained by Cohen's fraud. While the compensatory damages representing the excessive rents charged to the respondents are nondischargeable, the additional treble damages are punitive in nature and not directly related to the amount obtained by Cohen's fraudulent conduct.

Therefore, this Court should reverse the Third Circuit's decision and hold that only the compensatory damages are nondischargeable under ?523(a)(2)(A), while the treble damages award is dischargeable. This interpretation is consistent with the language and purpose of ?523(a)(2)(A), as well as the principles established in In re Levy and Grogan v. Garner.
