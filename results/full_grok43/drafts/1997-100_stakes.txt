**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that the Clause applies to a capital sentencing proceeding that “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 439. The same analysis governs here.

California law requires the prosecution to prove a prior serious felony allegation to the trier of fact. Penal Code § 1025; *People v. Wiley*, 9 Cal. 4th 580, 589 (1995). The State must establish the allegation “beyond a reasonable doubt.” *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The defendant enjoys the rights to confront and cross-examine witnesses, to present evidence, and to remain silent. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996); *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974). These procedures are “comparable to those required in a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439.

When the Court of Appeal held the evidence insufficient, that determination operated as an acquittal of the allegation. *See Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 211 (1984) (holding that a finding of insufficient evidence at a capital sentencing proceeding “is an ‘acquittal’” that bars retrial). Retrial would therefore place Monge twice in jeopardy for the same offense.

The distinction drawn by the court below between capital and noncapital proceedings is untenable. *Bullington* turned on the procedural character of the hearing, not on the penalty at stake. *See also* *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 134 (1980) (recognizing that double-jeopardy principles can constrain sentencing determinations that resemble trials). Because the California proceeding possessed those trial-like characteristics, the Clause applies and precludes retrial.
