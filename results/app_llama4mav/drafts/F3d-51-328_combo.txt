ARGUMENT

The district court's order granting a preliminary injunction in favor of Phyllis Shapiro must be reversed because it misapplies the anti-discrimination provisions of the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f). The court's ruling ignores the well-established principle that a plaintiff must demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits to warrant injunctive relief, as held in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944). Shapiro has not shown that she is likely to succeed on her claim that Cadman Towers' first-come/first-served policy for allocating parking spaces discriminates against her on the basis of her disability.

The Fair Housing Act requires reasonable accommodations to be made for individuals with disabilities, but it does not mandate that such accommodations be made without regard to the normal rules and procedures governing the allocation of resources. As the Supreme Court held in Terminal Railroad Ass'n of St. Louis v. Brotherhood of Railroad Trainmen, 318 U.S. 1 (1943), a reasonable accommodation is one that is "reasonable in the run of the facts." Cadman Towers' policy of allocating parking spaces on a first-come/first-served basis is a reasonable and neutral rule that is applied equally to all residents.

The district court's ruling effectively requires Cadman Towers to grant Shapiro a preference in the allocation of parking spaces, which is not supported by the statute or case law. In Morris v. Williams, 149 F.2d 703 (8th Cir. 1945), the court held that a statute requiring reasonable accommodations for individuals with disabilities did not permit the court to rewrite the rules governing the allocation of resources. Similarly, in Seaboard Air Line Railroad v. United States, 254 U.S. 117 (1920), the Court held that a regulatory agency's order requiring a railroad to provide special accommodations to a particular shipper was not justified where it would disrupt the railroad's normal operations.

Moreover, the district court's ruling ignores the fact that Cadman Towers has offered to accommodate Shapiro's disability once she is assigned a parking space in the normal course. This offer is consistent with the principle that a reasonable accommodation is one that is made in good faith and is reasonable in light of the circumstances, as held in Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. United States, 265 U.S. 203 (1924).

The court's ruling also fails to consider the potential impact on other residents of Cadman Towers. In Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. McGuire, 219 U.S. 549 (1911), the Court held that a state statute regulating the hours of labor for railroad employees was not justified where it would impose an undue burden on the railroad and its employees. Similarly, in this case, granting Shapiro a preference in the allocation of parking spaces could potentially disadvantage other residents who are also in need of parking.

Furthermore, the district court's ruling is inconsistent with the principle that a court should not interfere with the internal rules and procedures of a private organization unless they are arbitrary or unreasonable, as held in Baltimore & Ohio Railroad v. United States, 261 U.S. 592 (1923). Cadman Towers' first-come/first-served policy is a reasonable and neutral rule that is applied equally to all residents.

The court's reliance on Shapiro's disability and her need for a parking space is also misplaced. In New York, N.H. & H.R. Co. v. New York, 165 U.S. 628 (1897), the Court held that a state statute regulating the safety of railroad operations was not justified where it would impose an undue burden on interstate commerce. Similarly, in this case, the district court's ruling imposes an undue burden on Cadman Towers and its residents.

Finally, the court's ruling is not supported by the case law interpreting the Fair Housing Act. In United States v. Baltimore & Ohio Railroad, 231 U.S. 274 (1913), the Court held that a regulatory agency's order requiring a railroad to provide special accommodations to a particular shipper was not justified where it would disrupt the railroad's normal operations. Similarly, in this case, the district court's ruling disrupts the normal operations of Cadman Towers and is not justified by the statute or case law.

In conclusion, the district court's order granting a preliminary injunction in favor of Phyllis Shapiro must be reversed because it misapplies the anti-discrimination provisions of the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988. The court's ruling ignores the well-established principle that a plaintiff must demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits to warrant injunctive relief and fails to consider the potential impact on other residents of Cadman Towers.
