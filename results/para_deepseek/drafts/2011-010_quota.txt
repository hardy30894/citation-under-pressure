**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends workers’ compensation coverage only to injuries that occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text, structure, and purpose of OCSLA compel this conclusion, and the Ninth Circuit’s contrary “substantial nexus” test cannot be reconciled with this Court’s precedent or the uniform interpretation of every other court to address the issue.

**I. The Text of OCSLA Requires That the Injury Occur on the Outer Continental Shelf**

Section 1333(b) provides LHWCA coverage for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies the injury, not merely the employment. This Court has recognized that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). In a footnote directly addressing § 1333(b), the Court explained that “only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court thus confirmed that § 1333(b) retains a situs requirement, with the employee status requirement added as an additional condition.

The Ninth Circuit dismissed this language as “unconsidered dictum,” but this Court’s considered statements about the meaning of a statute are entitled to great weight. *See United States v. Montero-Camargo*, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc). Moreover, the Court’s observation that OCSLA coverage “would be determined principally by locale” is consistent with the statute’s structure. *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s Situs-of-Injury Test Is Correct**

The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, squarely held that OCSLA requires the injury to occur on the outer Continental Shelf. *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). In *Mills*, a welder injured during the onshore construction of a platform destined for the outer Continental Shelf was denied OCSLA benefits because “the injury did not occur on the Outer Continental Shelf.” *Id.* at 357, 362. The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this rule. *See Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); *Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc.*, 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001); *Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc.*, 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998).

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is unpersuasive. *Curtis* adopted a “but for” test that would extend OCSLA coverage to any injury causally related to offshore operations, regardless of where it occurred. *Id.* at 809-11. But that approach reads the phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” out of the statute. If Congress had intended coverage for all injuries “resulting from” offshore operations, it would not have included the geographic modifier. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b).

**III. This Court’s Precedent Supports a Situs Requirement**

In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), this Court considered whether a worker on a platform in state waters was entitled to LHWCA benefits. The Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA. . . . [T]hat statute draws a clear line.” *Id.* at 425. The Court further noted that OCSLA’s “geographic limitation” is “explicit.” *Id.* This language confirms that OCSLA coverage turns on the location of the injury, not merely the nature of the employment.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Tallentire*’s footnote as “unconsidered” is misplaced. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against disregarding its dicta. *See Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (general expressions in opinions must be taken in connection with the case in which they are used). But even if the footnote were dicta, it accurately reflects the statute’s plain meaning.

**IV. The Purpose of OCSLA Supports a Situs Requirement**

OCSLA was enacted to extend federal law to the outer Continental Shelf, an area beyond state jurisdiction. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1332(1) (declaring policy to “provide for the development of the outer Continental Shelf”). The workers’ compensation provision was designed to ensure that workers on the Shelf received benefits, not to create a nationwide compensation scheme for all oil-and-gas workers. *See Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test would extend OCSLA coverage to any worker whose job is even tangentially related to offshore operations, regardless of where the injury occurs. That result is inconsistent with the statute’s limited geographic scope.

**V. The LHWCA Claim Was Properly Denied**

The LHWCA requires both maritime employment and a maritime situs. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Benefits Review Board correctly found that La Conchita was not a maritime situs. The facility is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, has no direct access to any pier or dock, and is three miles from the nearest pier. *See Motoviloff v. Director, Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs*, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982). Its primary purpose is processing crude oil, a non-maritime activity. *Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 422-24. The scrap-metal consolidation that led to Valladolid’s death was incidental and did not transform the facility into a maritime situs. *See Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978) (requiring that the site be “used as an integral part of longshoring operations”).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. OCSLA requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf, and the LHWCA claim was properly denied for lack of a maritime situs.
