# ARGUMENT

## THE BOARD'S DENIAL OF A REQUEST TO REOPEN A PRIOR BENEFITS CLAIM IS A "FINAL DECISION" SUBJECT TO JUDICIAL REVIEW UNDER 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)

### A. The Plain Text of Section 355(f) Compels Review of Reopening Denials

Section 355(f) of the Railroad Retirement Act provides that "[a]ny claimant... may obtain a review of any final decision of the Board" in the federal courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) (incorporated by 45 U.S.C. § 231g). The statute does not distinguish between decisions on initial applications and decisions on reopening requests. Both constitute "decisions" of the Board, and once the administrative process concludes, both are "final."

This Court has recognized that similar language in the analogous Social Security context—which explicitly excludes Board reopening decisions from judicial review through a specific regulatory carve-out—demonstrates that reopening decisions would otherwise fall within the ordinary meaning of "final decision." *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 108 (1977) (holding that Social Security's reopening denials are unreviewable only because the Social Security regulations "are not 'final decision[s]'" under that Act's specific implementing regulations). Critically, the Railroad Retirement Act contains no comparable regulatory exclusion. The Board's own regulations treat a decision on a reopening request as a "decision," full stop—one that resolves whether a claimant is entitled to benefits. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. When the Board denies reopening, that denial finally and conclusively determines that the claimant will receive no benefits under the reopened application. There is nothing tentative or interlocutory about it.

### B. Roberts and the Fifth Circuit Majority Approach Rest on a Mistaken Extension of Califano

The Fifth Circuit's decision below rests entirely on *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which imported *Califano*'s Social Security reasoning wholesale into the Railroad Retirement Act context without accounting for critical statutory differences. *Roberts* joined a group of circuits— the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth—in concluding that "the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals is limited to the review of Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted," and that reopening denials fall outside this category. *Id.* at 140; *see also Harris v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999); *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995); *Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); *Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983).

But this reasoning conflates two distinct statutory schemes. In *Califano*, this Court's holding depended on specific Social Security regulations that separately defined which decisions were "final decisions made after a hearing" and expressly relegated reopening determinations to a different regulatory category. 430 U.S. at 107–08. The Railroad Retirement Act has no such regulatory architecture. The Fifth Circuit and its sister circuits erred by assuming that *Califano*'s outcome—rather than its statute-specific reasoning—controlled a materially different statutory and regulatory scheme.

### C. The D.C. Circuit and Second and Eighth Circuit Approach Correctly Interprets the Statute

The D.C. Circuit's decision in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), correctly held that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." *Id.* at 502. The *Stovic* court properly recognized that the Railroad Retirement Act's structure—unlike the Social Security Act's—provides no textual or regulatory basis for treating reopening denials as categorically unreviewable. *Id.* at 504.

This approach also finds support in the Second and Eighth Circuits. *See Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966); *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991). These courts recognized what the majority approach obscures: a decision refusing to reopen a benefits determination is the final word on whether the claimant receives benefits under that application. To hold otherwise permits the Board to insulate an entire category of substantive benefits decisions from any judicial oversight whatsoever—a result inconsistent with the Act's evident purpose of ensuring meaningful review of Board action affecting claimants' entitlement to disability and retirement benefits.

### D. Denying Review Produces Anomalous and Unjust Results

The practical consequences of the majority rule illustrate its error. Under that rule, a claimant like Petitioner Salinas—who presents new and material evidence, or demonstrates administrative error, sufficient to warrant reopening under the Board's own regulations, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2—has no recourse if the Board wrongly refuses to reopen, even where the Board's refusal itself violates its own regulatory standards. The Board becomes the sole and unreviewable arbiter of whether its own regulatory reopening criteria have been satisfied. This cannot be squared with the basic principle, reiterated by this Court, that "judicial review of a final agency action by an aggrieved person will not be cut off unless there is persuasive reason to believe that such was the purpose of Congress." *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 140 (1967). Nothing in the Railroad Retirement Act's text, structure, or history supplies such a reason.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that a Board decision denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a "final decision" under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), reverse the judgment of the Fifth Circuit, and remand for further proceedings.
