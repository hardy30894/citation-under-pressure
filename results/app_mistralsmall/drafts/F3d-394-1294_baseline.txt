**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TAX COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MRS. DAVIS’S INTEREST IN THE AMENDED TRUST DOES NOT QUALIFY AS A QUALIFYING INCOME INTEREST FOR LIFE UNDER I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).**

The Internal Revenue Code permits a marital deduction for property passing to a surviving spouse if the spouse receives a “qualifying income interest for life.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). A qualifying income interest exists where the surviving spouse is “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” *Id.* The Tax Court erred in concluding that Mrs. Davis’s interest under the Amendment failed to meet this standard.

The Amendment explicitly provides that Mrs. Davis, as surviving spouse, is entitled to “all of the net income from the trust estate” during her lifetime, payable in quarterly or more frequent installments. This language mirrors the statutory requirement. *See Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-5(f)(1) (stating that income must be payable “at least annually or at more frequent intervals”).* The Tax Court’s reliance on *Estate of Nicholson v. Commissioner*, 94 T.C. 666 (1990), is misplaced. In *Nicholson*, the trustee was given discretion to distribute income “as the trustee deems advisable,” which the court found insufficient to guarantee the spouse’s right to income. *Id.* at 672. Here, by contrast, the trustee’s discretion is expressly limited to determining the *timing* and *amount* of distributions necessary for Mrs. Davis’s “health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare,” in accordance with her accustomed manner of living. This does not negate her entitlement to the income; it merely ensures that distributions are tailored to her needs. *See Estate of Clayton v. Commissioner*, 97 T.C. 327, 335 (1991) (holding that a spouse’s right to income is not defeated by a trustee’s discretion to determine the *amount* of distributions, so long as the spouse is entitled to receive the income).

The Commissioner further argues that the trustee’s discretion to consider Mrs. Davis’s other resources under Paragraph 4 of the Amendment renders her interest non-qualifying. However, this provision does not grant the trustee discretion to *withhold* income but merely permits consideration of other resources in determining the *amount* of distributions. *See Estate of Vissering v. Commissioner*, 99 T.C. 606, 614 (1992) (finding that a similar provision did not defeat the spouse’s qualifying income interest). The statutory requirement is satisfied so long as the spouse is entitled to receive the income, not that she must receive it regardless of other resources. *See Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-5(f)(1) (requiring only that income be payable to the spouse, not that it must be paid in all events).*

**II. THE TAX COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT NO PERSON HAS A POWER TO APPOINT ANY PART OF THE TRUST PROPERTY TO ANY PERSON OTHER THAN THE SURVIVING SPOUSE UNDER I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).**

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) further requires that “no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.” The Tax Court concluded that Mrs. Davis’s power to invade principal under Paragraph 5 of the Amendment violated this requirement because it permitted distributions to her beyond income. However, this interpretation conflates the spouse’s power to *receive* distributions with a third party’s power to *appoint* property to someone other than the spouse.

The statute prohibits powers that allow a third party to divert trust property to others. *See Estate of Spencer v. United States*, 58 F.3d 160, 163 (6th Cir. 1995) (holding that a power exercisable by a third party to distribute trust assets to persons other than the surviving spouse disqualifies the marital deduction). Here, the power to invade principal is vested solely in Mrs. Davis, the surviving spouse, and may only be exercised for her benefit. *See Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-5(g) (stating that a power exercisable by the surviving spouse alone does not disqualify the marital deduction).* The Tax Court’s reliance on *Estate of Spencer* is misplaced, as that case involved a third-party power of appointment. The Amendment’s invasion power is personal to Mrs. Davis and does not permit appointments to others.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Tax Court erred in denying the marital deduction under I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7). The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
