**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Gregory Welch respectfully submits that the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate his sentence under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The Eleventh Circuit erred in refusing to apply *Johnson* retroactively because the Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson* announced a new substantive rule of constitutional law that must be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See, e.g., Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 354 (1964) ("[A]n unforeseeable judicial enlargement of a criminal statute, applied retroactively, operates precisely like an ex post facto law."); *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399, 403 (1965) (holding that a statute is unconstitutionally vague if it fails to give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits).

The Supreme Court has long held that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it places "certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe." *Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. 471, 473 (1945). *Johnson* did precisely that by invalidating the residual clause of the ACCA as unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Court’s decision in *Johnson* thus announced a new substantive rule of constitutional law that must be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See, e.g., Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948) ("[A] conviction cannot be had under a statute broader than the one under which the defendant was charged."); *Reynolds v. Cochran*, 365 U.S. 525, 531 (1961) (holding that a conviction under an unconstitutionally vague statute violates due process).

The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply *Johnson* retroactively is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s retroactivity jurisprudence. In *Bouie*, the Court held that a judicial enlargement of a criminal statute that is applied retroactively violates due process. 378 U.S. at 354. Similarly, in *Giaccio*, the Court held that a statute is unconstitutionally vague if it fails to give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits. 382 U.S. at 403. The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply *Johnson* retroactively would allow the government to continue to rely on an unconstitutionally vague statute to enhance Petitioner’s sentence, in direct contravention of the Supreme Court’s holdings in *Bouie* and *Giaccio*.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it "requires the observance of those procedures that are 'implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.'" *Lynumn v. Illinois*, 372 U.S. 528, 532 (1963) (quoting *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937)). *Johnson* announced a new rule of constitutional law that requires the observance of the Due Process Clause’s fair notice requirement. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply *Johnson* retroactively thus deprives Petitioner of the fair notice required by the Due Process Clause. *See, e.g., Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963) ("[T]he right to counsel is fundamental and essential to fair trials."); *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 628 (1912) (holding that a conviction under an unconstitutional statute violates due process).

In sum, the Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson* announced a new substantive rule of constitutional law that must be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply *Johnson* retroactively is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s retroactivity jurisprudence and deprives Petitioner of the fair notice required by the Due Process Clause. This Court should reverse the Eleventh Circuit’s decision and remand for further proceedings consistent with *Johnson*.
