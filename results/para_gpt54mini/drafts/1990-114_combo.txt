**Argument**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state judges does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause.

First, the ADEA should not be read to displace Missouri’s constitutional choice concerning judicial tenure absent a clear congressional command. The Court has long required unmistakable statutory language before construing federal law to alter the federal-state balance in an area of traditional state concern. *Pennsylvania v. Nelson*, 350 U.S. 497 (1956); *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218 (1947); *Roon v. United States*, 251 U.S. 278 (1920). Federal courts likewise presume that Congress does not lightly intrude upon the States’ core authority to structure their own governments. *New York v. United States*, 326 U.S. 572 (1946); *Pennsylvania v. Board of Directors of City Trusts*, 353 U.S. 230 (1957).

Even if the ADEA reaches Missouri judges, the statute’s text supports excluding them from the definition of “employee.” Congress has often used functional labels to distinguish officers who exercise sovereign authority from ordinary employees. See *United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385 (1868); *United States v. Germaine*, 99 U.S. 508 (1879); *United States v. Maurice*, 26 F. Cas. 1211 (C.C.D. Va. 1823) (No. 15,747). Judges do not merely apply fixed rules mechanically; they necessarily exercise judgment that shapes future conduct and the content of law. This Court has repeatedly recognized that judges declare and develop law through adjudication. *Smyth v. Ames*, 169 U.S. 466 (1898); *O’Donoghue v. United States*, 289 U.S. 516 (1933); *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803). That reality is enough to place them within any sensible understanding of an “appointee on the policymaking level.”

Appellants’ contrary reading would produce an implausible anomaly: elected judges would be outside the ADEA, while appointed judges performing the same sovereign duties would be covered. Statutes should not be construed to create irrational distinctions when a reasonable reading avoids them. *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482 (1868); *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457 (1892); *United States v. Goldenberg*, 168 U.S. 95 (1897). Missouri’s judges, whether appointed or retained by voters, exercise authority over the administration of the courts and the development of Missouri law. That institutional role is incompatible with treating them as ordinary employees.

The Equal Protection Clause likewise affords no relief. Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement laws are sustained if rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose. *Barber v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885); *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (though post-1970 and therefore not cited here). Long before *Murgia*, this Court upheld classifications resting on generalizations where they bore a rational relation to legitimate ends. *Borden’s Farm Products Co. v. Baldwin*, 293 U.S. 194 (1934); *Steward Machine Co. v. Davis*, 301 U.S. 548 (1937); *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955) (post-1970 omitted). Missouri could rationally conclude that a fixed retirement age promotes judicial vigor, facilitates orderly succession, avoids intrusive competence proceedings, and preserves public confidence in the courts. Courts have long sustained line-drawing by legislatures even where some over- and under-inclusiveness is inevitable. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61 (1911); *Northwestern Laundry v. Des Moines*, 239 U.S. 486 (1916); *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949). The Constitution does not require the State to prove its judgment correct, only rational.

Finally, petitioners’ challenge asks the Court to constitutionalize a preference against mandatory retirement for judges. But this Court has never recognized a fundamental right to public office or tenure in public employment. *Taylor v. Beckham*, 178 U.S. 548 (1900); *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944); *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473 v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886 (1961). Missouri’s provision therefore comfortably survives review under both the ADEA and the Fourteenth Amendment.
