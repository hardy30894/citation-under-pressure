**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees on union support is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

**I. The Board’s Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the Act**

The National Labor Relations Act does not prescribe a specific evidentiary threshold for employer polling of an incumbent union’s majority status. Congress entrusted the Board with administering the Act, but the Board’s standard must be “rational and consistent with the Act.” *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 45 (1937). The Board’s current rule fails that test.

The Board requires an employer to possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” before polling—the same showing required to withdraw recognition unilaterally. This makes polling a useless exercise. An employer who already has enough evidence to withdraw recognition gains nothing from a poll. The only legitimate purpose of a poll is to *resolve* uncertainty, not to confirm what the employer already knows. *See NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 345 (1938) (employer retains right to take reasonable steps to ascertain employee sentiment). By demanding certainty before the poll, the Board has eliminated the very tool that could provide it.

This Court has recognized that an employer’s good-faith doubt about union support may be based on “objective” evidence that falls short of proving a loss of majority. *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96, 103 (1954) (employer may withdraw recognition upon “reasonable doubt” grounded in objective facts). The Board’s polling standard imposes a stricter requirement than this Court’s own precedent. An employer should be permitted to poll when it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support”—even if that evidence is not sufficient to justify withdrawal. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 616 n.27 (1969) (emphasizing employer’s right to communicate with employees and ascertain their views). The Board’s contrary approach frustrates the Act’s goal of stable collective bargaining by compelling employers to choose between two extremes: bargain with a union that may have lost majority support, or risk an unfair labor practice by withdrawing recognition without the benefit of a poll.

The Board’s standard also creates an anomaly: an employer may obtain a Board-supervised RM election with a lesser showing than is required for a private poll. This Court has instructed that the Board must “adapt its remedies to the needs of the particular case.” *NLRB v. Waterman Steamship Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940). When the Board’s own rules make a poll less accessible than a formal election—a process that is more disruptive and costly—the rule is irrational. *See NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 290 (1965) (Board’s determinations must be “reasonably based” on the Act’s policies).

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence**

Even under the Board’s own erroneous standard, the record compels a finding that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt. The Board’s contrary conclusion is not supported by “substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951).

Of the 32 employees in the bargaining unit, 13 made statements repudiating the union or expressing dissatisfaction with representation. The Board discounted the statements of two employees—Ridgick and Marsh—as “equivocal” or “dissatisfaction” rather than opposition. But this Court has held that an employer may rely on circumstantial evidence of union sentiment, including expressions of dissatisfaction that imply a desire to abandon the union. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 299 (1939) (employer’s good-faith belief may be inferred from “objective facts”). A worker who says he is “not getting his money’s worth” from union dues, and another who says “there is no need for a union” if treated fairly, are objectively indicating that they do not support continued representation. The Board’s hypertechnical parsing of these statements ignores the employer’s practical need to assess employee sentiment in real time.

The Board also rejected the statements of employees Bloch and Mohr, who reported that the night shift and service department did not want the union. The Board faulted these employees for not testifying about how they formed their opinions. But an employer is entitled to rely on firsthand reports from employees about their coworkers’ views. *See NLRB v. George P. Pilling & Son Co.*, 119 F.2d 32, 38 (3d Cir. 1941) (pre-1970; employer may consider “reports of employees” in assessing union support). Requiring the employer to produce the origin witnesses for each statement is an unreasonable burden that undermines the Act’s preference for informed employer decisions.

When the discounted statements are properly credited, the evidence shows that a clear majority of employees—at least 17 of 32—had expressed opposition to the union. The Board’s refusal to count employees hired after the company’s decision to poll is also erroneous. The relevant date for assessing good-faith doubt is the date the employer acted, not the date of a formal letter. *NLRB v. Universal Camera Corp.*, 190 F.2d 429, 432 (2d Cir. 1951) (pre-1970; employer’s knowledge at time of action controls). Allentown Mack had received credible reports of union disaffection before it announced the poll, and the Board’s rigid cutoff disregards the dynamic nature of workplace sentiment.

**III. The Board’s Order Must Be Reversed**

The Board’s standard for polling is arbitrary and inconsistent with the Act. The factual record demonstrates that Allentown Mack had ample objective evidence of a good-faith reasonable doubt. The Board’s order must be reversed and the petition for enforcement denied. *See NLRB v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co.*, 161 F.2d 798, 802 (5th Cir. 1947) (pre-1970; Board’s findings must be “supported by substantial evidence” and its legal standards “must be reasonable”).
