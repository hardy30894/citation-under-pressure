**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), provided the conviction is valid under the applicable constitutional standards governing tribal sovereignty and due process. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant, Jr.*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), and its subsequent refusal to rehear the matter en banc, rest on a fundamental misreading of the scope of the Sixth Amendment and a disregard for the settled principles of federal Indian law. The Court should reverse, for the following reasons.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Thus Cannot Render an Uncounseled Tribal Conviction Inadmissible as a Predicate Offense**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies only to federal and state criminal proceedings. It does not extend to Indian tribal courts, which are sovereign entities exercising inherent authority over their members. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), reaffirmed that “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders” is “the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty.” This principle was long established before 1970. In *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 384 (1886), the Court held that Congress may “regulate the relations between the Government and the Indians,” and that tribal courts are “independent tribunals” with authority to adjudicate crimes committed by tribal members. The Court further emphasized that “the rights of the Indians are not to be measured by the standards of the common law or the Constitution of the United States,” *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 45 (1913), and that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (citing earlier precedent); see also *United States v. Candelaria*, 271 U.S. 432, 437 (1926) (holding that tribal courts are not bound by the Fifth Amendment’s grand jury requirement).

Because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, an uncounseled conviction in such a forum cannot violate the Amendment. The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that such a conviction is “constitutionally infirm” because it lacks counsel is therefore legally unsound. *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), which the panel relied upon, misapplied the Sixth Amendment to tribal proceedings and has long been criticized as inconsistent with the Court’s doctrine of tribal sovereignty. See *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. at 45; *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. at 384.

**II. The Use of Valid Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Is Consistent with Due Process and the Principles of Comity**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires only that tribal court proceedings afford “fundamental fairness,” not that they mirror federal or state procedures. *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. at 45. The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1302, explicitly recognizes that tribal courts are not bound by the full panoply of constitutional rights, but instead are required to provide “procedural safeguards” that are “equivalent to those guaranteed by the Bill of Rights.” This standard is not coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, but rather reflects Congress’s recognition of tribal self-governance. *United States v. Candelaria*, 271 U.S. at 437; *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. at 45.

The Court has long upheld the validity of tribal court judgments even when they do not include the full panoply of federal constitutional protections. In *United States v. Sandoval*, the Court affirmed the validity of a tribal court conviction for murder, despite the absence of a grand jury indictment, holding that “the rights of the Indians are not to be measured by the standards of the common law or the Constitution of the United States.” 231 U.S. at 45. Similarly, in *United States v. Candelaria*, the Court upheld a tribal court conviction for assault, noting that “the tribal courts are not bound by the constitutional provisions applicable to the federal courts.” 271 U.S. at 437. These decisions establish that tribal court convictions, even when uncounseled, may be used in federal prosecutions if they are valid under tribal law and due process.

**III. The Use of Tribal Convictions as Predicate Offenses Does Not Violate the Principle of Reliability Under *In re Winship***

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), to elevate the burden of proof for predicate offenses is misplaced. *Winship* held that the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt, but it did not purport to apply to the use of prior convictions as elements of a recidivist statute. The Court has consistently held that prior convictions may be used to enhance punishment or establish recidivism under a lower burden of proof. *United States v. Borden*, 347 U.S. 514, 517 (1954) (holding that prior convictions may be used to enhance punishment under the Federal Kidnapping Act); *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939) (upholding use of prior convictions in sentencing under the Sherman Act). The Court has never held that a prior conviction must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt when used as a predicate offense in a recidivist statute.
