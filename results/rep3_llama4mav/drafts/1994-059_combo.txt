The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish exclusive qualifications for members of the House of Representatives and Senate, respectively. The Constitution's text and historical context demonstrate that the Framers intended to preclude states from adding qualifications beyond those enumerated.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member who met the constitutional qualifications, emphasizing that the Constitution sets forth the exclusive qualifications for congressional office. Similarly, in United States v. Smith, 286 U.S. 6 (1932), the Court stated that the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses "prescribe the only qualifications" for congressional office.

The historical context of the Constitution's drafting and ratification supports this interpretation. As James Madison explained in The Federalist No. 52, the Qualifications Clauses were intended to ensure uniformity in the qualifications for congressional office across the states. Allowing states to add qualifications would undermine this uniformity and create disparate standards for congressional office.

The Court has consistently applied this principle in various contexts. In Ex parte Dailey, 93 U.S. 376 (1876), the Court held that a state's attempt to impose additional qualifications for congressional office was unconstitutional. Similarly, in State ex rel. Wettengel v. Zimmerman, 249 Wis. 237, 24 N.W.2d 504 (1946), cert. denied, 330 U.S. 841 (1947), the Wisconsin Supreme Court struck down a state law that imposed term limits on congressional candidates, citing the exclusivity of the Qualifications Clauses.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the Constitution's provisions regarding congressional qualifications are not subject to state modification. In McPherson v. Blacker, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), the Court held that the Constitution's provisions regarding the manner of appointing presidential electors were exclusive, and states could not alter them. Similarly, in Burroughs v. United States, 290 U.S. 534 (1934), the Court upheld federal regulation of congressional elections, emphasizing the federal government's authority over congressional matters.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 cannot save the provision limiting congressional terms. As this Court held in Connolly v. Union Sewer Pipe Co., 184 U.S. 540 (1902), a severability clause is ineffective when the valid and invalid provisions are inextricably linked. Here, the ballot title and preamble to Amendment 73 presented term limits for state and federal officials as a single, unified package, making it impossible to discern whether voters would have approved term limits for state officials alone.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Amendment 73's provision limiting congressional terms is unconstitutional. The Qualifications Clauses are exclusive, and states may not add qualifications beyond those enumerated. The severability clause cannot salvage the invalid provision, as it is inextricably linked to the valid provisions.

The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.
