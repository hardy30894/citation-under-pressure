## ARGUMENT

### I. The Indictment Failed to Allege an Essential Element of the Offense Because Mrs. Parker’s Unauthorized Acts Were Not “Official Acts” Within the Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C)

The district court erred in denying the motion to dismiss the indictment. To sustain a conviction under § 201(b)(2)(C), the government must prove that the defendant committed an “official act” as defined by 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). *United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers*, 526 U.S. 398, 404 (1999). That definition requires a “decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity, or in such official’s place of trust or profit.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). Mrs. Parker’s unauthorized acts—forging signatures and approving benefits she had no power to grant—were not acts undertaken in her “official capacity” or in her “place of trust or profit.”

The statutory language demands a nexus between the act and the official’s lawful duties. *United States v. Biaggi*, 909 F.2d 662, 682 (2d Cir. 1990) (“[T]he ‘official act’ must be a decision or action on a matter that is within the official’s authority.”). Here, Mrs. Parker’s duties as a clerk were limited to assisting ALJ Aronson, recording hearings, and typing opinions. She was expressly *not* authorized to approve benefits or sign the ALJ’s name. Acts that are wholly outside the scope of an official’s authority cannot constitute “official acts” under the bribery statute. *United States v. Margiotta*, 688 F.2d 108, 135 (2d Cir. 1982) (holding that an “official act” must be “within the scope of the official’s duties as defined by law or settled practice”).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), is misplaced. *Birdsall* recognized that duties may be established by “settled practice,” but only where the official had some colorable authority to act. *Id.* at 231. Here, there was no settled practice permitting a clerk to forge signatures or approve benefits. Such acts were not merely unauthorized; they were ultra vires. *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983), is similarly inapposite because the defendant in that case—a prison guard—had authority over inmates; his facilitation of an escape was within the broad scope of his supervisory duties. Mrs. Parker had no authority whatsoever over benefits decisions.

This Court must construe criminal statutes strictly in favor of the defendant. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 347 (1971). Any ambiguity in the definition of “official act” must be resolved against the government. *United States v. Brown*, 663 F.2d 229, 234 (D.C. Cir. 1981). Because the indictment did not allege that Mrs. Parker’s acts were within her official duties, it failed to state an essential element of the offense. *United States v. Standefer*, 610 F.2d 1076, 1090 (3d Cir. 1979) (reversing bribery conviction where official act was not within the scope of the defendant’s authority).

### II. The Indictment’s Deficiency Is Fatal as to Both Appellants

Mr. Parker’s conspiracy and bribery convictions depend entirely on the premise that Mrs. Parker committed an “official act.” *United States v. Podell*, 572 F.2d 31, 35 (2d Cir. 1978) (conspiracy to commit bribery requires agreement to commit an official act). Because the indictment fails to allege that predicate, Mr. Parker’s convictions must also be reversed. *United States v. Isaacs*, 493 F.2d 1124, 1148 (7th Cir. 1974) (indictment’s failure to allege an official act requires dismissal of all related counts).

### III. Conclusion

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying the motion to dismiss the indictment. The convictions of both appellants must be reversed.
