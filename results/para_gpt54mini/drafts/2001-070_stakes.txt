**Argument**

The Eighth Amendment does not require a categorical exemption from capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders. In **Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989)**, this Court held that the Constitution did not then bar the execution of mentally retarded defendants as a class. Penry recognized that mental retardation is relevant to culpability and must be considered in sentencing, but it rejected the proposition that such defendants are constitutionally ineligible for the death penalty. Id. at 340. That holding controls unless and until this Court concludes, under its own Eighth Amendment methodology, that national standards have so shifted as to make execution of the mentally retarded inconsistent with “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” **Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958)**.

Petitioner cannot show that the Constitution now commands the rule he seeks. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Eighth Amendment inquiry is objective and grounded in the judgments of legislatures and sentencing bodies. **Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976)**; **Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 369 (1989)**. In assessing whether a punishment has become unconstitutional, the Court looks first to objective indicia of consensus, especially state legislation and actual sentencing practice. **Penry, 492 U.S. at 331**; **Stanford, 492 U.S. at 373**. As Penry held, the existence of disagreement among the States is highly significant and ordinarily defeats a claim that the Constitution itself has already settled the matter. 492 U.S. at 334. At the time of Atkins’s sentencing and the Virginia Supreme Court’s decision, no national consensus had emerged sufficient to overrule Penry’s holding. The Constitution does not permit this Court to announce a categorical exemption untethered to objective indicia of consensus.

Nor does the Constitution require the Court to treat mental retardation as a per se bar because such defendants may have diminished blameworthiness. This Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment permits individualized capital sentencing and allows the sentencer to give mitigating effect to mental impairments. **Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978)**; **Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 113-15 (1982)**. Penry itself explained that evidence of mental retardation may be relevant to culpability, but it concluded that those considerations are adequately addressed through individualized sentencing rather than a categorical constitutional rule. **Penry, 492 U.S. at 338-40**. Virginia’s capital sentencing scheme comports with that principle because it permits the jury to consider mitigating evidence and to decide whether death is warranted in light of all the circumstances. See **Buchanan v. Angelone, 522 U.S. 269, 276-77 (1998)**.

The State also has substantial penological interests in preserving the death penalty for offenders who commit the most serious murders, even if they are mentally retarded. In **Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 183 (1976)**, this Court identified retribution and deterrence as the principal legitimate purposes of capital punishment. The Court has never held that mental retardation eliminates those interests as a matter of law. To the contrary, the seriousness of the crime and the offender’s demonstrated dangerousness remain constitutionally relevant. **Tuilaepa v. California, 512 U.S. 967, 972-73 (1994)**.

Petitioner’s reliance on the risks of inaccuracy in the diagnosis of mental retardation does not support a constitutional ban. This Court has never constitutionalized a medical classification as a complete exemption from punishment, and Penry recognized that the line between mental retardation and other forms of intellectual impairment may not always be bright. **Penry, 492 U.S. at 338**. Those line-drawing difficulties counsel restraint, not judicial creation of a categorical bar with no direct textual basis in the Eighth Amendment. See **Stanford, 492 U.S. at 378-79**.

In short, the Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry. The Eighth Amendment permits consideration of mental retardation in mitigation, but it does not itself forbid execution of mentally retarded offenders as a class. Because the constitutional rule petitioner seeks is unsupported by controlling precedent and by the objective standards that govern Eighth Amendment analysis, the judgment below should be affirmed.
