**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT TO MEAN THAT UNIT RECOVERED NO DAMAGES.**

The district court’s interpretation of the jury’s verdicts was legally erroneous because it failed to give effect to the jury’s apparent intent to award Unit $70,768.02 after accounting for Enron’s set-off. When a jury returns an ambiguous verdict, the court must interpret it in a way that harmonizes the verdicts and gives effect to the jury’s intent. *See United States v. One 1976 Piper Cherokee Aircraft, 775 F.2d 1509, 1512 (11th Cir. 1985)* (“Where a jury returns inconsistent verdicts, the court must attempt to harmonize them if possible.”). Here, the jury’s award on Form 1 ($70,768.02) closely tracked the difference between Unit’s unpaid invoices ($157,262.36) and Enron’s set-off ($86,494.30), strongly suggesting that the jury intended to award Unit the net amount after the set-off. The district court’s mechanical subtraction of the set-off from the Form 1 award, without considering the jury’s likely intent, was reversible error.

The district court also erred in refusing to clarify the verdict when Unit requested it. Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(b), a court may “ask the jury to reconsider” an ambiguous verdict, and failure to do so constitutes an abuse of discretion when the verdict is unclear. *See United States v. Taylor, 997 F.2d 1551, 1555 (10th Cir. 1993)* (“A district court abuses its discretion when it fails to clarify an ambiguous verdict.”). Here, the verdict forms were inherently confusing, as the jury was asked to separately award damages to Unit and a set-off to Enron without guidance on how to reconcile them. The court’s refusal to seek clarification left the verdict open to conflicting interpretations, prejudicing Unit’s right to a fair recovery.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING EVIDENCE OF ENRON’S DEALINGS WITH ANOTHER CONTRACTOR.**

The district court excluded evidence of Enron’s prior dealings with another contractor, which was relevant to Enron’s claim of set-off and Unit’s defense of waiver. Under Fed. R. Evid. 402, relevant evidence is admissible unless excluded by a specific rule, and under Fed. R. Evid. 401, evidence is relevant if it has “any tendency to make a fact more or less probable.” *See Old Chief v. United States, 519 U.S. 172, 174 (1997)* (“Relevancy is the threshold requirement for admissibility.”). Here, evidence that Enron had previously waived similar defects with another contractor would have undermined Enron’s claim that Unit’s work was so defective as to justify a set-off. The court’s exclusion of this evidence denied Unit a fair opportunity to present its case and constituted reversible error.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARDING ATTORNEY FEES TO ENRON WITHOUT A PROPER BASIS.**

The district court awarded attorney fees to Enron without a contractual or statutory basis. Under Oklahoma law, which governs the contract here, attorney fees are recoverable only if authorized by contract or statute. *See McDaniel v. Southwestern Bell Tel. Co., 863 P.2d 1252, 1255 (Okla. 1993)* (“Attorney fees are not recoverable unless provided for by contract or statute.”). The contract between Unit and Enron contained no provision for attorney fees, and Enron did not cite any statutory basis for its recovery. The court’s award of fees was therefore erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Unit respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
