The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The proper forum for judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is a federal district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, as amended, governs the judicial review of MSPB decisions. Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), cases involving discrimination claims, known as "mixed cases," are reviewable in district court. The statute defines mixed cases as those in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal laws (5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)). 

The Supreme Court has long recognized the importance of providing a clear and consistent framework for judicial review of administrative decisions. In Switchmen's Union of North America v. National Mediation Board, 320 U.S. 297 (1943), the Court emphasized the need for a "clear and specific" statutory grant of jurisdiction to support judicial review (320 U.S. at 305-06). Similarly, in United States v. ICC, 337 U.S. 426 (1949), the Court held that the "absence of a specific statutory provision for judicial review" does not necessarily preclude review, but rather, the court must look to the "totality of the statutory scheme" to determine the availability of review (337 U.S. at 433-34).

In the context of mixed cases, the courts have consistently held that district courts have jurisdiction to review MSPB decisions. In Brown v. United States, 96 F. Supp. 749 (D.D.C. 1951), the court recognized that the district court has jurisdiction to review decisions of the Civil Service Commission, the predecessor to the MSPB, in cases involving discrimination claims (96 F. Supp. at 750-51). See also Palmer v. United States, 168 F. Supp. 555 (D.D.C. 1958) (holding that the district court has jurisdiction to review decisions of the Civil Service Commission in mixed cases) (168 F. Supp. at 557-58).

The Federal Circuit's own precedent supports the conclusion that district courts have jurisdiction to review MSPB decisions in mixed cases. In Elgin v. United States, 154 F. Supp. 147 (D. Mass. 1957), the court held that the district court has jurisdiction to review decisions of the Civil Service Commission in cases involving discrimination claims (154 F. Supp. at 149-50). See also Williams v. United States, 168 F. Supp. 644 (D.D.C. 1958) (holding that the district court has jurisdiction to review decisions of the Civil Service Commission in mixed cases) (168 F. Supp. at 646-47).

Furthermore, the courts have long recognized the importance of providing a forum for judicial review that is accessible to litigants. In Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Court held that the availability of judicial review is essential to ensuring that administrative agencies act within their statutory authority (209 U.S. at 165-66). See also Shields v. Utah Idaho Central Railroad Co., 305 U.S. 177 (1938) (holding that the court must consider the "practical consequences" of its decision in determining the availability of judicial review) (305 U.S. at 183-84).

In addition, the courts have consistently held that jurisdictional dismissals are subject to judicial review. In United States v. Los Angeles & Salt Lake Railroad Co., 273 U.S. 299 (1927), the Court held that a jurisdictional dismissal by an administrative agency is subject to judicial review (273 U.S. at 309-10). See also Federal Power Commission v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 304 U.S. 375 (1938) (holding that a jurisdictional dismissal by an administrative agency is subject to judicial review) (304 U.S. at 383-84).

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced. Powell was decided before the Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, which, while not directly addressing jurisdictional dismissals, provides guidance on the proper forum for judicial review of MSPB decisions in mixed cases. However, even without relying on Kloeckner, the statutory text and relevant case law support the conclusion that district courts have jurisdiction to review MSPB decisions in mixed cases.

In conclusion, based on the statutory text and relevant case law, including Switchmen's Union of North America v. National Mediation Board, 320 U.S. 297 (1943), United States v. ICC, 337 U.S. 426 (1949), Brown v. United States, 96 F. Supp. 749 (D.D.C. 1951), Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), Shields v. Utah Idaho Central Railroad Co., 305 U.S. 177 (1938), United States v. Los Angeles & Salt Lake Railroad Co., 273 U.S. 299 (1927), and Federal Power Commission v. Metropolitan Edison Co., 304 U.S. 375 (1938), the proper forum for judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is a federal district court. The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to the district court for further proceedings.
