# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge the Full Debt Arising From Fraud, Including Treble Damages, Attorney's Fees, and Costs

The text, history, and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code compel the conclusion that a debt "for money, property, services . . . obtained by . . . actual fraud" under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) includes all liability arising from the fraud, not merely the amount fraudulently obtained in the first instance.

### A. The Statutory Term "Debt" Has Long Been Construed to Encompass the Full Extent of a Fraudulent Creditor's Liability

This Court has consistently read the discharge provisions of the bankruptcy laws in light of their remedial purpose of relieving the "honest but unfortunate debtor," while withholding that relief from wrongdoers. In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704 (1878), the Court examined the predecessor fraud exception and explained that the term "fraud" in the bankruptcy discharge context means "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong." 95 U.S. at 709. That construction was necessary, the Court held, because "such a construction of the statute is consonant with equity, and consistent with the object and intention of Congress in enacting a general law by which the honest citizen may be relieved from the burden of hopeless insolvency." *Id.* The corollary is equally clear: where fraud in fact is proven, the debtor forfeits the benefit of discharge as to the debt that fraud created—not merely as to some artificially limited portion of it.

This Court's decision in *Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick*, 43 U.S. 202 (1844), likewise demonstrates that Congress intends categorical exceptions from discharge to reach the entirety of the excepted obligation. The Court there examined whether a fiduciary debt fell within the bankrupt court's jurisdiction at all, recognizing that if such a debt was outside the discharge provisions, "a discharge can in no respect affect the interest of the fiduciary creditor." *Id.* at 207. The same logic applies here: once a debt is correctly categorized as one "obtained by actual fraud," the discharge simply does not reach it, in whole or in part.

### B. The "Debt" Arising From Fraud Is Not Limited to the Value of the Property Fraudulently Obtained

The Third Circuit correctly recognized that a "debt" under the Bankruptcy Code is a broad concept encompassing the full liability flowing from the underlying wrong, not an artificially narrowed subset of that liability. This Court's precedent supports that understanding. In *Miller v. Irving Trust Co.*, 296 U.S. 256 (1935), the Court construed the term "claim" and "debt" under the Bankruptcy Act by looking to the full scope of liability arising from the underlying transaction, rather than parsing the claim into discrete components. Similarly, in *Moore v. Bay*, 284 U.S. 4 (1931), the Court held that the trustee's rights and the treatment of claims under the Bankruptcy Act must be governed by the Act's own terms "since it is superior to all state laws upon the subject," 284 U.S. at 5, confirming that federal bankruptcy law—not artificial state-law labels—controls the classification of what is owed.

That principle disposes of Cohen's contention that treble damages are somehow severable "punishment" rather than part of the "debt" arising from his fraud. As the court below correctly held, because the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A) presents a question of federal law, state-law characterizations of a damages remedy as "punitive" or "compensatory" cannot control its dischargeability. This mirrors the reasoning of *Molzof v. United States*, cited approvingly by the Third Circuit, but is equally consistent with this Court's approach in cases such as *Bruning v. United States*, 376 U.S. 358 (1964), where the Court held that a debtor's discharge did not extinguish interest that accrued on a nondischargeable tax obligation after the bankruptcy petition was filed, because the discharge does not sever component parts of a debt that is otherwise excepted from discharge. If post-petition interest on a nondischargeable tax debt survives discharge, so too must the full measure of damages—compensatory and enhanced alike—flowing from a debtor's fraud.

### C. Permitting Discharge of the Treble-Damages Portion of a Fraud Judgment Would Undermine the Bankruptcy Code's Protection of Defrauded Creditors

The bankruptcy discharge has never been understood as a shield for fraudfeasors. In *Bluthenthal v. Jones*, 208 U.S. 64 (1908), and *Scott v. Ellery*, 142 U.S. 381 (1892), this Court demonstrated the care with which it has policed the boundaries of discharge to ensure that creditors' rights are not extinguished by operation of law without due recognition of the equities specific to fraud and related misconduct. *Scott* explained that a creditor who proves a debt in bankruptcy is bound by the outcome of the discharge proceeding, *id.* at 386, underscoring that Congress calibrated the discharge mechanism with precision—precision that would be undone if courts artificially truncate the "debt" excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A) to the bare principal sum obtained by fraud.

Finally, *Missouri State Life Insurance v. Jones*, 290 U.S. 199 (1933), confirms that damages provisions attached by law to an underlying claim—there, statutory penalties and attorney's fees tied to an insurance recovery—are treated as part of the amount in controversy arising from that claim, not as a separate and severable category. The treble-damages award here, mandated by the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act as a direct legal consequence of Cohen's fraudulent overcharges, is analytically indistinguishable: it is part of the debt "for money . . . obtained by actual fraud," and therefore nondischargeable in its entirety.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
