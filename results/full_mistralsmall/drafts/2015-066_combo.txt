**ARGUMENT**

I. The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts, and an uncounseled conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right is fundamental and applies to all criminal prosecutions, regardless of the forum. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963) (“The right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, and yet be discarded in this.”); *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal prosecutions but extend to state proceedings as well. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344; *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942) (holding that the Sixth Amendment applies to the states via the Fourteenth Amendment).

Tribal courts are not exempt from these constitutional guarantees. While tribal sovereignty is a recognized principle of federal law, see *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), that sovereignty does not permit tribal courts to disregard the Bill of Rights where federal law has been extended to apply in Indian country. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 382 (1896) (holding that the Fifth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts); *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 383-84 (1886) (noting that tribal sovereignty is subject to congressional plenary power). However, where Congress has acted to extend constitutional protections to tribal proceedings—such as through the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303—the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel must be honored. *See* *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 58 (1978) (recognizing that the ICRA imposes certain constitutional-like limitations on tribal courts).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), and its progeny, correctly held that uncounseled tribal court convictions cannot serve as predicate offenses for federal recidivist statutes because they violate the Sixth Amendment. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394-95. This rule is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding recognition that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is essential to the fairness of criminal proceedings. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344; *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 68. The fact that tribal courts may not be bound by the Sixth Amendment in the same manner as federal or state courts does not permit them to impose convictions that would be unconstitutional in those forums. *See* *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222, 224 (1980) (per curiam) (holding that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could not be used to enhance a sentence under a recidivist statute).

II. The use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) violates the due process and reliability principles underlying the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is not merely a procedural formality; it is a structural guarantee designed to ensure the reliability of criminal convictions. *United States v. Ash*, 413 U.S. 300, 309 (1973) (“The Sixth Amendment right to counsel exists to ensure that criminal trials are conducted fairly and reliably.”). An uncounseled conviction, by contrast, is presumptively unreliable because the absence of counsel undermines the adversarial process. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344; *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 68. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the right to counsel is essential to the fairness of criminal proceedings, and its violation taints the resulting conviction. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 462 (1938) (“The right to counsel is fundamental in a criminal prosecution.”).

The use of an uncounseled conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) compounds this unreliability. Section 117(a) criminalizes conduct based on prior convictions, making the uncounseled tribal court convictions an essential element of the federal offense. *See* *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 476 (2000) (holding that any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt). The government’s burden to prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), cannot be satisfied by an uncounseled conviction that is itself constitutionally infirm. *See* *Baldasar*, 446 U.S. at 224 (holding that an uncounseled conviction could not be used to enhance a sentence under a recidivist statute).

III. The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not conflict with *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), because *Nichols* addressed sentencing enhancements, not the use of prior convictions as essential elements of an offense.

The government relies on *Nichols* to argue that uncounseled convictions may be used for sentencing purposes, but *Nichols* does not extend to the use of prior convictions as essential elements of a federal offense. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47 (holding that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines). The Court in *Nichols* emphasized that sentencing courts may consider prior convictions under a preponderance of the evidence standard, *id.* at 748, and that the Sentencing Guidelines provide mechanisms to challenge the reliability of prior convictions, *id.* at 747-48. However, *Nichols* did not address the use of prior convictions as essential elements of an offense, which must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case is consistent with the Supreme Court’s recognition that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is essential to the reliability of criminal convictions. The use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment and the due process principles underlying it. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
