**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE THEY IMPRISON THE FIRST AMENDMENT RIGHTS OF LOBBYISTS BY REQUIRING THE DISCLOSURE OF SPEECH AND EXPENDITURES WITHOUT A NECESSARY AND PROPORTIONAL RELATIONSHIP TO A SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST.

The First Amendment protects not only the content of speech but also the right to engage in anonymous political expression. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 14–15, 96 S.Ct. 612, 626–27, 46 L.Ed.2d 659 (1976). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to speak anonymously is a fundamental aspect of the First Amendment.” *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n*, 514 U.S. 334, 347, 115 S.Ct. 1511, 1520, 131 L.Ed.2d 426 (1995). Florida’s disclosure regime, which mandates the public reporting of all lobbying expenditures—including those for food, entertainment, travel, and communications—violates this principle by compelling disclosure of the very means by which political expression is facilitated. The statute does not merely require disclosure of who paid for lobbying efforts; it compels the disclosure of *how* and *why* those efforts were made, thereby chilling protected speech.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that disclosure requirements must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest and must not burden more speech than necessary. *Riley v. National Federation of the Blind of North Carolina, Inc.*, 487 U.S. 781, 795, 108 S.Ct. 2667, 2677, 101 L.Ed.2d 669 (1988). In *Buckley*, the Court invalidated a disclosure requirement that compelled candidates to report contributions exceeding $100, finding that such a requirement “imposes a significant burden on the right of political expression” and “is not narrowly tailored to serve the government’s interest.” *Buckley*, 424 U.S. at 25, 96 S.Ct. at 632. Similarly, in *McIntyre*, the Court struck down an Ohio law requiring the disclosure of the names of individuals who circulated campaign literature, holding that “the State’s interest in disclosure does not justify the suppression of anonymous political speech.” *McIntyre*, 514 U.S. at 357, 115 S.Ct. at 1525.

Here, Florida’s disclosure mandate is neither narrowly tailored nor proportionate. The statute requires disclosure of expenditures in categories such as “entertainment” and “food and beverages,” which are often essential to building relationships and facilitating dialogue—core components of political expression. The government’s interest in transparency is not sufficient to justify the compelled disclosure of such intimate and expressive conduct. *See also Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 345, 130 S.Ct. 876, 899, 175 L.Ed.2d 753 (2010) (noting that “the First Amendment protects political speech, and that includes speech about candidates and issues, even when funded by corporations”).

Moreover, the statute’s broad definition of “lobbying expenditures” creates a chilling effect on protected activity. The Supreme Court has held that “a statute that is overbroad in its application violates the First Amendment.” *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 472, 130 S.Ct. 1577, 1586, 176 L.Ed.2d 435 (2010). Florida’s requirement that *all* expenditures—regardless of their connection to lobbying—be disclosed, including those made by principals, creates a “fear of reprisal” that deters lawful political expression. *See Abbott Lab. v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 152–53, 87 S.Ct. 1507, 1517–18, 18 L.Ed.2d 681 (1967). The League’s members face the choice between engaging in protected speech or risking civil penalties for non-compliance, a dilemma that renders the statute unconstitutionally overbroad.

II. THE BAN ON CONTINGENCY-FEE LOBBYING VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT BY DIRECTLY RESTRICTING THE CONTENT AND FORM OF POLITICAL SPEECH.

The prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying—whereby a lobbyist receives compensation only if a desired legislative outcome is achieved—constitutes a content-based restriction on political speech. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791, 109 S.Ct. 2746, 2753, 105 L.Ed.2d 661 (1989) (holding that content-based restrictions are subject to strict scrutiny). The government may not regulate speech based on its content unless it demonstrates a compelling interest and that the regulation is narrowly tailored to achieve that interest.

Florida’s justification—that contingency fees create a “conflict of interest” or “incentive to corrupt”—is speculative and fails to meet the strict scrutiny standard. *See *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 383, 112 S.Ct. 2538, 2543, 120 L.Ed.2d 305 (1992) (holding that content-based laws must be “necessary to serve a compelling state interest”).
