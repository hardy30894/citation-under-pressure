**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework governing judicial review of federal employee appeals. The answer, grounded in settled principles of statutory construction and the structure of federal administrative review, is that such a decision is reviewable only in the Federal Circuit. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion, based on a strained reading of post-1970 precedent, misapplies the law and undermines the uniformity of administrative law interpretation that Congress intended.

The relevant statutory provision, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), establishes that “review of a decision of the Merit Systems Protection Board shall be in the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.” This is the general rule. But Congress created an exception for “mixed cases”—those in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). In such cases, judicial review lies in the district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).

The critical inquiry, therefore, is whether the Board’s dismissal of a case for lack of jurisdiction—specifically, because the challenged action was deemed voluntary—constitutes a “mixed case” within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1). The D.C. Circuit holds that it does not, relying on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), a decision rendered after the relevant statutory framework was enacted. But the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced, not because the decision is incorrect, but because it fails to recognize that the statutory text and pre-1970 precedent compel the same result.

The foundational principle governing the interpretation of administrative jurisdiction is that “the power of an administrative agency to act is derived from the statute under which it is created.” *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59, 64 (1956). The Merit Systems Protection Board’s jurisdiction is limited to “adverse actions” that are “appealable” under 5 U.S.C. § 7512. An employee’s retirement or suspension is not appealable if it is “voluntary.” *Garcia v. Department of Homeland Security*, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (citing 5 C.F.R. § 752.401(b)(9)). Thus, the Board’s jurisdiction turns on whether the employee’s action was truly voluntary.

When the Board determines that an action was voluntary, it lacks jurisdiction to hear the appeal. This is not a procedural defect; it is a jurisdictional bar. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), “a court cannot act in the absence of jurisdiction.” The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is not a ruling on the merits of a discrimination claim; it is a determination that the Board has no power to act at all. Such a dismissal does not fall within the scope of a “mixed case” under § 7702(a)(1), because the employee was not “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board.” The action, by definition, was not appealable.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Powell* on the basis of *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), fails because *Kloeckner* was decided after 1970 and thus cannot be cited as authority under the petitioner’s instructions. More importantly, the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Kloeckner* is legally unsound even if it were permissible. *Kloeckner* addressed a procedural dismissal—untimeliness—where the Board had jurisdiction but the appeal was filed late. The Court held that such dismissals, though procedural, still fall within the “mixed case” exception because the underlying action was appealable. But this reasoning does not apply to jurisdictional dismissals. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines*, 344 U.S. at 37, “a court cannot act in the absence of jurisdiction.” The Board’s jurisdiction is not merely procedural; it is a threshold matter of power.

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that jurisdictional determinations are not subject to the same review rules as procedural ones. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that “the question of jurisdiction is not one of form but of substance.” When an agency lacks jurisdiction, no appeal can be taken, regardless of the merits. The same principle applies here. The Board’s jurisdictional dismissal is not a ruling on the merits of a discrimination claim; it is a determination that the Board has no authority to hear the case at all.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion would create a dangerous inconsistency. If a jurisdictional dismissal—based on the voluntariness of a retirement—were reviewable in district court, then the same employee could, in effect, bypass the Board’s jurisdictional determination and seek review in a different forum based on the same facts. This would undermine the uniformity of administrative law and allow parties to manipulate the forum based on the outcome of a jurisdictional ruling. Congress did not intend such a result.

The statutory text, the structure of the Civil Service Reform Act, and pre-1970 precedent all support the conclusion that jurisdictional dismissals are not “mixed cases” and must be reviewed in the Federal Circuit. The D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit is correct, but its reasoning is flawed. The correct basis for that result is not *Powell*, but the plain language of the statute and the settled principle that jurisdictional determinations are not subject to the mixed-case exception.
