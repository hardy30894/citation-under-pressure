**ARGUMENT**

**I. Ohio’s Clemency Proceedings Do Not Implicate a Protected Life or Liberty Interest Under the Due Process Clause.**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Woodard has no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process itself. This Court’s decision in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), compels that conclusion. In *Dumschat*, this Court held that a convicted murderer had no liberty interest in commutation of his life sentence arising from state clemency procedures, explaining that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464. Because Ohio’s clemency statute vests “almost unfettered discretion” in the Governor, *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994), no state-created liberty interest exists. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465.

The court of appeals’ alternative holding—that Woodard retains a separate, predeprivation life and liberty interest in clemency because it is an “integral part” of Ohio’s adjudicatory system—contravenes *Dumschat* and the historical understanding of executive clemency. Clemency is “an act of grace” flowing from the executive, not part of the judicial process. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). Unlike the appellate procedure at issue in *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), clemency is not “an integral part of the … system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.” *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). Rather, clemency operates after adjudication is complete, serving distinct executive interests such as mercy and public policy. To constitutionalize clemency would “create disincentives for States to codify” such procedures, *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 483 (1995), and intrude upon the executive’s traditional prerogative.

**II. Ohio’s Optional Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination in any “criminal case.” *Lefkowitz v. Turley*, 414 U.S. 70, 77 (1973). Ohio’s clemency procedure does not compel Woodard to speak. The regulation provides that the prisoner “may” request a personal interview, and nothing in the procedure authorizes the parole board to compel answers to any question. On its face, the interview is voluntary, and Woodard “remains free to say as much or as little … as he wishes.” *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976). The mere possibility that a voluntary statement could later be used against Woodard in some hypothetical future proceeding does not render the procedure coercive. *Cf. Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 427 (1984) (no compulsion where defendant “could have chosen to remain silent”).

Woodard’s claim of an “unconstitutional condition” fails because the state has not conditioned any benefit on waiver of his Fifth Amendment rights. He is not required to participate in the interview to receive clemency; he may submit written materials instead. The choice to participate is entirely his. Because no compulsion exists, the Fifth Amendment is not implicated. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 288-89 (1998) (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment). The district court properly dismissed this claim as premature and speculative. *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 324.
