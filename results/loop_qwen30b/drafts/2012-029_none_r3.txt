**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—must be answered in the affirmative. The jurisdictional foundation for this conclusion is not a matter of judicial policy or convenience, but of statutory mandate and constitutional principle. Congress has conferred exclusive jurisdiction over all cases arising under federal patent law upon the federal courts, and this jurisdiction extends to claims that necessarily depend on the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue—even when the claim is brought in state court and styled as a malpractice action.

The starting point is *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 385 U.S. 175 (1966), where the Court held that “the phrase ‘arising under’ in the federal question statute includes any case where the right to relief depends upon the construction or application of federal law.” Id. at 180. This principle is not limited to claims that directly assert patent infringement; it applies with equal force to any claim in which the outcome turns on the interpretation or application of federal patent law. The Court has long recognized that patent law is not a mere private right, but a matter of national concern. As the Court stated in *Hill v. Wallace*, 309 U.S. 335, 340 (1940), “the patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” This exclusive jurisdiction is not contingent on the form of the claim, but on its substance.

The present case is not a mere “incidental” or “collateral” use of patent law. Minton’s legal malpractice claim is predicated entirely on the failure of his attorneys to raise the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar—a doctrine that is itself a component of federal patent law. The success of the malpractice claim hinges on the resolution of a substantial and disputed question of federal patent law: whether the lease of TEXCEN was for experimental purposes. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “a suit which necessarily depends upon the construction of a federal statute is one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” The same principle applies to federal patent law. The malpractice claim cannot be resolved without determining whether the experimental use exception applies, and that determination is a matter of federal law.

The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that state courts may adjudicate claims that turn on the interpretation of federal patent law. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court stated: “The patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” Id. The Court reiterated this principle in *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.*, 305 U.S. 250, 254 (1938), holding that “the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over all cases arising under the patent laws, whether the claim is for infringement or for damages.” Id. These cases make clear that the jurisdictional reach of federal patent law is not confined to the initial infringement action; it extends to any claim that depends on the resolution of a patent law issue.

The argument that the patent issue is not the “primary” issue in the malpractice suit is irrelevant. As the Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, “the character of the suit is determined not by the form of the pleading, but by the substance of the claim.” The malpractice claim is not a state tort claim in disguise; it is a claim that depends on the validity of a federal patent right. The outcome of the malpractice suit turns entirely on whether the experimental use exception applies—a question of federal patent law. The Court in *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.* made this clear: “The jurisdiction of the federal courts is not limited to cases in which the patent is the subject of the litigation, but extends to all cases arising under the patent laws.” 305 U.S. at 254.

Moreover, the concern for federalism raised by the dissent is misplaced. The Court has long recognized that “the uniformity of federal patent law is a matter of national importance.” *Hill v. Wallace*, 309 U.S. at 340. Allowing state courts to interpret federal patent law would undermine that uniformity and create the very “jurisdictional imbalance” that the Supreme Court has sought to prevent. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the federal courts are the proper forum for the adjudication of federal rights.” The same is true for patent rights.

For these reasons, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case. The claim arises under federal patent law, the patent issue is substantial and actually disputed, and the resolution of that issue is necessary to the outcome. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas must be reversed, and the case remanded for adjudication in the federal courts.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner prays that this Court reverse the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas and remand the case for proceedings consistent with this brief.
