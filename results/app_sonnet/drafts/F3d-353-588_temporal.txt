**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps’s Disability Discrimination Claims Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether Pine Lawn Regarded Epps as Disabled.**

Summary judgment is improper whenever the record permits more than one reasonable inference on a material fact, and courts must resolve doubtful inferences in favor of the non-movant. *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (“the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts contained in [the] materials must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion”). This caution is heightened where the dispute turns on a decisionmaker’s state of mind. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment should be used “sparingly” in cases where “motive and intent play leading roles”). Likewise, where the evidentiary record requires the court to weigh conflicting characterizations of the same facts, summary disposition is disfavored. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (summary judgment inappropriate where the record leaves “grave doubt” requiring assessment of competing inferences).

Here, Chief Hardy’s own contemporaneous memoranda are the best evidence of Pine Lawn’s perception of Epps. Hardy recommended termination twice, both times invoking Dr. Mirkin’s notation that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different occupation,” and ultimately concluding that Epps “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” That language permits—indeed, invites—the inference that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in his ability to work generally, not merely restricted from the specific tasks of a patrol officer. A reasonable jury could find that Hardy’s repeated citation to Epps’s permanent unsuitability, combined with his simultaneous reliance on Epps’s workers’ compensation history and sick-leave record, reflects a broad-based judgment about Epps’s employability rather than a narrow, task-specific limitation. Under *Poller* and *Diebold*, that inference belonged to the jury, not the court on summary judgment. The district court’s contrary conclusion—that no reasonable factfinder could find Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled—improperly resolved a credibility and inference question that *Sartor* forbids resolving on a paper record.

**II. Sovereign Immunity Does Not Bar Epps’s Statutory Retaliation Claim.**

The Missouri workers’ compensation retaliation statute is remedial legislation, enacted to protect employees who exercise a statutory right from employer reprisal. Remedial statutes are not to be read narrowly or technically but construed to accomplish their evident purpose. *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967) (remedial statutes must be “construed not technically and restrictively, but flexibly to effectuate” their remedial aims). A construction of Missouri’s sovereign immunity doctrine that swallows the retaliation statute whenever the employer happens to be a municipality would defeat the very purpose the legislature sought to serve, since municipal police departments are among the largest employers subject to the workers’ compensation scheme. Nothing in the retaliation statute exempts public employers, and immunity doctrines developed at common law to protect discretionary governmental functions should not be extended by implication to override a specific, later-enacted statutory cause of action. See *Tcherepnin*, 389 U.S. at 336.

Moreover, Missouri’s own municipal immunity jurisprudence has long distinguished between a city’s “governmental” functions, which are immunized, and its “proprietary” or administrative functions, which are not. *Auslander v. City of St. Louis*, 97 S.W.2d 895, 897–98 (Mo. 1936); *Cassidy v. City of St. Joseph*, 247 S.W. 906, 908 (Mo. 1923). The decision to hire, discipline, or terminate an individual officer is an administrative personnel function, not an exercise of the core governmental power to provide police protection to the public. Retaliatory termination for filing a workers’ compensation claim falls on the administrative side of that line, and immunity principles crafted to shield the *provision* of police services should not be stretched to shield discrete, retaliatory personnel decisions made in the *management* of the police force.

**III. Sovereign Immunity Does Not Bar Epps’s Claim for Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.**

The same governmental/proprietary distinction defeats Pine Lawn’s immunity defense to Epps’s intentional tort claim. *Auslander*, 97 S.W.2d at 897–98. Immunity historically protected municipalities from liability for the negligent performance of core public functions, not from liability for intentional, retaliatory conduct directed at a single employee. Because Hardy’s campaign to terminate Epps—built on repeated reliance on Epps’s protected workers’ compensation filings and his medical condition—was an administrative act outside the scope of any core governmental function, the same reasoning that defeats immunity for the retaliation claim defeats it here. *Cassidy*, 247 S. W. at 908.

For these reasons, the district court’s judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
