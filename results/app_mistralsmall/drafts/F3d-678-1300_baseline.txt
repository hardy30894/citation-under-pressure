**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING THE ASSERTED CLAIMS INVALID UNDER 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)**

A. **The Leader2Leader® Product Did Not Anticipate the Asserted Claims**

To establish anticipation under § 102(b), a product must be “identical in all material respects” to the claimed invention. *Net MoneyIN, Inc. v. VeriSign, Inc.*, 545 F.3d 1359, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2008). The district court erred in concluding that Leader2Leader® anticipated the asserted claims because it failed to require that the product perform all the claimed functions.

The asserted claims require, inter alia, “dynamically associating metadata” with data and “dynamically updating the stored metadata” when a user moves between environments. ’761 patent, col.21 ll.38-58. The record evidence shows that Leader2Leader® lacked this functionality. McKibben testified that Leader2Leader® did not “dynamically update” metadata as claimed, and the district court’s contrary finding was based on an erroneous construction of “metadata.” *See Leader Techs., Inc. v. Facebook, Inc.*, 770 F.Supp.2d 686, 717 (D. Del. 2011).

Moreover, the district court improperly relied on Leader’s white paper to find anticipation. A product must be “on sale” or “in public use” in the form it is claimed. *Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998). The white paper described a proposed system, not the Leader2Leader® product itself. The district court’s reliance on it was error.

B. **The Leader2Leader® Product Was Not “On Sale” or “In Public Use” Prior to December 10, 2002**

The on-sale bar applies only if the invention was “the subject of a commercial offer for sale” and “ready for patenting.” *Pfaff*, 525 U.S. at 67. The district court erred in finding that Leader’s January 2002 white paper constituted an invalidating offer for sale.

First, the white paper was not an offer to sell the claimed invention. It described a proposed system and offered software licenses, but it did not identify the specific claims of the ’761 patent. *See id.* at 67-68 (requiring the invention to be the subject of the sale).

Second, the Leader2Leader® product was not “ready for patenting” before December 10, 2002. The record shows that the product lacked the claimed metadata functionality. *See id.* at 67 (requiring the invention to be “sufficiently definite and tested to warrant the conclusion that it would work for its intended purpose”).

The district court also erred in finding that Leader2Leader® was in “public use.” Public use requires that the invention be “in the public domain and ready for use.” *In re Hall*, 781 F.2d 897, 899 (Fed. Cir. 1986). The record shows that Leader2Leader® was demonstrated only to potential customers under confidentiality agreements. *See id.* (holding that demonstrations under confidentiality do not constitute public use).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING LEADER’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW**

A party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law if the evidence permits only one reasonable conclusion. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000). Here, the evidence showed that Leader2Leader® did not anticipate the asserted claims, and no reasonable jury could have found otherwise.

The district court’s denial of Leader’s motion was based on an erroneous construction of “metadata” and an improper reliance on the white paper. These errors deprived Leader of a fair trial. *See A.C. Aukerman Co. v. R.L. Chaides Constr. Co.*, 960 F.2d 1020, 1038 (Fed. Cir. 1992) (en banc) (holding that a party is entitled to JMOL if the evidence is legally insufficient).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
