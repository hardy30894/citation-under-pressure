**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BOP’S INTERPRETATION OF “NONVIOLENT OFFENSE” IN PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 VIOLATES THE CONGRESSIONAL COMMAND OF 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) BY EXPANDING THE TERM “CRIME OF VIOLENCE” BEYOND ITS STATUTORY MEANING.

The core issue before this Court is whether the Bureau of Prisons, in denying Victor M. Roussos early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), acted in accordance with the law. The statute authorizes early release for inmates who complete a drug treatment program, but only if their offense is not a “nonviolent offense.” The BOP, however, has interpreted this statutory term through its own Program Statement 5162.02, which deems a drug conspiracy conviction a “crime of violence” if the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a firearm during the offense—regardless of whether the weapon was actually used, threatened, or even connected to the commission of the crime. This interpretation is not only inconsistent with the plain language of the statute, but also violates well-established principles of statutory construction and administrative law.

The statute itself does not define “nonviolent offense,” but it does define “crime of violence” in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which provides that such an offense must either: (A) have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, or (B) by its nature involve a substantial risk that physical force may be used in the course of committing the offense. This definition is narrow and precise. It does not permit the Bureau to convert a nonviolent drug conspiracy into a “crime of violence” simply because a firearm was found in the defendant’s home after the fact, especially when the weapon played no role in the offense.

The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, which deems a § 846 conviction a crime of violence if a weapon enhancement was applied, exceeds the scope of the statute and violates the principle that “an administrative agency may not interpret a statute in a manner inconsistent with its plain meaning.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The Court in *American Trucking* held that “the meaning of a statute is not to be altered by administrative interpretation which is contrary to its language.” Here, the BOP’s interpretation is contrary to the statutory language: the enhancement under the Sentencing Guidelines was based on possession of a firearm during a drug offense, not on the use or threat of force. The mere presence of a firearm in a residence, without any connection to the commission of the crime, does not satisfy either prong of § 924(c)(3). As the Court stated in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), “the meaning of a statute must be determined from its language, not from the consequences of its application.”

Moreover, the BOP’s rule-making exceeds its statutory authority. The Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq., requires that agency regulations be “in accordance with the law.” *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82 (1932). The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, by creating a per se rule that any drug offense with a weapon enhancement is a “crime of violence,” effectively rewrites the statute. This is impermissible. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, the Court emphasized that “an agency cannot by regulation expand the scope of a statute beyond its express terms.” 310 U.S. at 543. The BOP’s rule does precisely that: it transforms a nonviolent drug conspiracy into a violent offense based on a post hoc enhancement unrelated to the actual conduct of the crime.

The BOP’s interpretation also conflicts with the principle that “penal statutes are to be construed strictly against the government.” *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939). Where a statute creates a benefit—such as early release under § 3621(e)(2)(B)—the courts must interpret it in favor of the individual. *United States v. Borden Co.* reaffirmed that “the rule of strict construction applies with particular force when the statute confers a benefit.” 308 U.S. at 198. The BOP’s expansive reading of “crime of violence” undermines this principle by denying a benefit to an inmate who has fully complied with the treatment program and who, by all accounts, committed no act of violence.

The BOP’s reliance on the Sentencing Guidelines is misplaced. The Guidelines are advisory, not binding, and do not define the meaning of “crime of violence” for purposes of § 3621(e)(2)(B). *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The Court in *Gypsum* held that “the Sentencing Guidelines are not a substitute for statutory interpretation.” The BOP cannot use the Guidelines to expand the scope of a statutory term that Congress has defined elsewhere. The Court in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.* made clear that “an agency cannot use a guideline to override a statutory definition.” 310 U.S. at 543.

Furthermore, the BOP’s rule is arbitrary and capricious. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382, established that “an agency’s action must be based on a rational connection to the facts.” The BOP’s rule lacks such a connection. The firearm was discovered in Roussos’ vacation home, not at the scene of the crime, and was not used or threatened. The enhancement was based on mere possession, not on any actual or threatened use of force.
