**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling is Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s standard for allowing employers to poll employees regarding union support is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Under *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27 (1987), an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. An employer may overcome this presumption through “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” *Id.* at 37-41. The Supreme Court later confirmed that an employer may withdraw recognition or conduct an election upon such a showing. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990).

However, the Board’s polling standard is identical to the standard for withdrawing recognition. This creates an illogical anomaly. The Board has stated that Board-conducted elections are the “preferred method” for testing employee support and that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling.” *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1059-62 (1989). Yet, the Board permits an employer to conduct a poll—lacking the procedural safeguards of an election—only when the employer already has “sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition from the union.” *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984). If polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with “sincere doubts,” the evidentiary standard should be lower than that for withdrawing recognition. *Id.* at 1299. The Board’s rigid application of the same standard to both polling and withdrawal renders the polling option practically useless, contrary to the statutory goal of balancing employer interests with stable collective-bargaining relationships.

**II. The Board’s Factual Determination is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a “reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Sixth Circuit has held that an employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.” *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 868 (6th Cir. 1982). The record here contains overwhelming evidence of such a loss.

The Board refused to count the anti-union statements of Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack because they were hired after the date of the refusal letter. However, the Board’s standard requires an assessment of the employer’s state of mind on that date. The Board also discounted the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, finding them “equivocal” or merely expressions of dissatisfaction. Yet, the Board’s own precedent holds that an employer may not rely on anti-union sentiments expressed during a job interview where the employer has indicated a non-union stance, as such statements are “unlikely to be sincere.” *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978). Furthermore, the Board ignored the statements of Kermit Bloch, who reported that the entire night shift opposed the union, and Ron Mohr, the union steward, who stated the union would lose a vote. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements, without any finding of unreliability, is arbitrary and capricious. The presumption of continuing majority status is weaker here because the bargaining unit was a subset of a previously certified unit that had never been sampled. *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 36-41. Consequently, the Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.
