**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tribal Court of Appeals’ Summary Dismissal Constitutes Exhaustion of Tribal Remedies, Vesting the Federal Court with Subject Matter Jurisdiction.**

Under 25 U.S.C. § 1303, federal habeas relief is available to any person detained pursuant to an action of an Indian tribe who alleges that the tribe violated rights secured by the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”). Though the statute does not expressly require exhaustion, the Ninth Circuit has long required tribal remedies to be exhausted before a federal court will entertain an ICRA habeas petition. *See O’Neal v. Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe*, 482 F.2d 1140, 1144 (9th Cir. 1973). Exhaustion is satisfied, however, when “the tribal courts have been afforded a full and fair opportunity to determine the federal constitutional issues.” *Id.* at 1145.

Selam satisfied this requirement. He appealed both convictions to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals and raised numerous grounds, including the denial of compulsory process. The tribal appellate court held an appeal that it summarily dismissed without a hearing and without addressing Selam’s claims on the merits. Where a tribal appellate court has been given a complete opportunity to review a claim but declines to reach its merits through summary disposition, requiring further exhaustion would render the remedy illusory. *See Moore v. Nelson*, 611 F.2d 434, 437 (9th Cir. 1980) (requiring exhaustion only where meaningful relief remains available). Because no further tribal review was available to Selam, the district court had jurisdiction to consider his petition.

**II. Selam Was Denied His Right to Compulsory Process Under ICRA.**

ICRA provides that no tribe shall deny any person “the right . . . to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). The right to compulsory process is fundamental to the fair trial guaranteed by ICRA and mirrors the Sixth Amendment right applied to the states. *See Settler v. Lameer*, 507 F.2d 231, 237 (9th Cir. 1974) (recognizing that ICRA was intended to impose upon tribes “the same limitations as the Bill of Rights”). The Supreme Court has described compulsory process as one of the “safeguards” of a fair trial, *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 289 (1973), and has held that the Sixth Amendment guarantees not merely the formal right to subpoena witnesses, but the effective assistance of the court in compelling testimony favorable to the defense, *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 18–19 (1967).

Selam provided his appointed spokesperson with the names of five potential character witnesses. Although the spokesperson located three of them, two declined to testify voluntarily because they believed their testimony would harm Selam. Selam was not assisted in obtaining their appearance, and no defense witness—besides Selam himself—testified at either trial. The record shows the trial judge advised Selam that he could subpoena witnesses, but when a lay defendant is represented by appointed counsel and is told that witnesses are available but unwilling to testify, the court's abstract advice to “subpoena someone” does not satisfy the governmental responsibility to secure attendance. As *Washington* makes clear, the defendant’s right to compulsory process imposes an affirmative duty on the court to process and honor requests. *Washington*, 388 U.S. at 19.

The convictions rested on testimonial evidence regarding the two victims. The first conviction was based entirely on the victim’s in-court testimony and Investigator Clements’ repetition of the victim’s out-of-court accusation. The second conviction rested solely on the hearsay statements of a five-year-old who never testified. The defense’s inability to present character witnesses undermines confidence in the reliability of these judgments. This is precisely the adversarial imbalance the compulsory-process guarantee was designed to prevent.

**III. Selam’s Confrontation Clause Claim Was Not Waived.**

Selam raised his Confrontation Clause claim for the first time in his federal habeas petition. The district court, adopting the magistrate’s findings, held the claim waived because Selam had not raised it before the tribal appellate court. This was error for two independent reasons.

First, the claim is not waived because it is subsumed within his compulsory-process claim. The Confrontation Clause of ICRA parallels the Sixth Amendment and protects a defendant’s right “to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). Where, as here, a defendant is prevented from calling defense witnesses and is simultaneously confronted with testimonial hearsay from absent accusers, the compulsory-process and confrontation claims are inseparable. *See California v. Green*, 399 U.S. 149, 160 (1970) (recognizing the Confrontation Clause was “designed to prevent . . . conviction on the basis of ex parte testimony”). Vallery, the claimed failure to raise the precise label of the claim should not bar relief when the underlying evidentiary defect—trial conducted without meaningful adversarial testing of accusations—was squarely presented to the tribal court.

Second, the tribal appellate court’s disposition—a denial of hearing and a one-sentence dismissal of Selam’s appeal without written findings or a reasoned opinion—did not afford Selam a full and fair opportunity to present any claim. See *O’Neal*, 482 F.2d at 1145. A de facto waiver rule operates harshly when the expert tribunal itself provides no process for developing the record or articulating its reasoning. If the tribal appellate court both refuses to hear appeals on the merits and provides no reasoned decision, a federal habeas court should not presume deliberate abandonment of unraised claims. The record is silent as to any opportunity Selam had to amend or supplement his tribal appeal. Given the lay nature of tribal court proceedings and the absence of any waiver colloquy, the district court abused its discretion in applying a strict-contemporaneous-objection rule. *Cf. United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 731 (1993) (waiver requires “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s denial of habeas relief should be reversed and the case remanded for a merits determination of Selam’s Confrontation Clause claim. Alternatively, the matter should be remanded for an evidentiary development of the compulsory-process claim.
