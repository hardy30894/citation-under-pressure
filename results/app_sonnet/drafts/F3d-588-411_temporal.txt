**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Supplementing the Jury Instructions with an Overbroad Definition of "Shielding."**

Congress did not define "shielding from detection" in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), and because the statute is penal, any judicial gloss placed on that term must be construed narrowly rather than expansively. It is a "presupposition of our law . . . that legislatures and not courts should define criminal activity," and "when choice has to be made between two readings of what conduct Congress has made a crime, it is appropriate, before we choose the harsher alternative, to require that Congress should have spoken in language that is clear and definite." United States v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp., 344 U.S. 218, 221-22 (1952). Where ambiguity exists in the reach of a criminal statute, "the ambiguity should be resolved in favor of lenity." Bell v. United States, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955).

That principle carries special force here because the jury itself signaled confusion, sending a note asking the court to define "concealing" and "shielding." A trial judge confronted with such a request bears a heightened obligation to answer correctly, for "particularly in a criminal trial, the judge's last word is apt to be the decisive word," and "when a jury makes explicit its difficulties a trial judge should clear them away with concrete accuracy." Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 612-13 (1946). An instruction that resolves jury confusion by broadening, rather than narrowing, the scope of the offense compounds rather than cures the problem the jury identified.

The definition given below did precisely that. It allowed the jury to convict Ye of "shielding from detection" based on ordinary acts of harboring or employing illegal aliens—providing housing, transportation, and wages—without requiring the jury to find that Ye acted with the specific purpose of preventing government agents from discovering those aliens' unlawful status. But criminal liability has traditionally required proof that the defendant intended the wrongful result of his conduct, not merely that he engaged in acts that happen to have that incidental effect. "The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Where a term in a criminal statute is capable of a broad, undifferentiated reading that would criminalize innocent or merely negligent conduct, courts have narrowed that term by requiring proof of specific intent, precisely to avoid sweeping in conduct Congress never meant to punish. Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101-04 (1945) (construing "willfully" to require specific intent to avoid indefiniteness in the reach of a criminal statute). The district court's instruction did the opposite: it broadened "shielding" so that virtually any act facilitating an illegal alien's continued presence—regardless of purpose—could satisfy the element. That was error, and it was not harmless, because the definition went to the heart of the only contested element of the felony count.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to Prevent the Government from Detecting the Illegal Aliens.**

Even under a proper instruction, a conviction cannot stand unless the record, viewed in the light most favorable to the government, contains substantial evidence supporting every element the jury was required to find. Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) ("the verdict of the jury must be sustained if there is substantial evidence, taking the view most favorable to the Government, to support it"). The reviewing court's task is to determine "whether upon the evidence, giving full play to the right of the jury to determine credibility, weigh the evidence, and draw justifiable inferences of fact, a reasonable mind might fairly conclude guilt beyond a reasonable doubt." Curley v. United States, 160 F.2d 229, 232 (D.C. Cir. 1947).

Measured against that standard, the government's proof at most established that Ye knowingly hired and housed illegal aliens—conduct squarely covered by the separate harboring and employment counts—not that he acted with the specific intent to prevent immigration authorities from detecting them. Ye did not conceal the aliens' presence from government agents; to the contrary, when investigators visited Buffet City in April and August 2005, they freely observed the Hispanic and Chinese workers on the premises, and Ye candidly told agents he lacked I-9 forms for them and that he was leasing an apartment where they lived. Such openness is inconsistent with an intent to "shield" anyone "from detection." That Ye failed to complete paperwork, paid wages in cash, or encouraged workers to obtain documents does not, without more, permit the inference—required under Morissette's mens rea principle—that he acted for the purpose of thwarting government discovery, as opposed to the purpose (established independently by the harboring and employment counts) of continuing to use undocumented labor. Because the evidence, even favorably construed, shows at most knowing employment and harboring rather than a purposeful scheme to defeat detection, no "reasonable mind might fairly conclude guilt beyond a reasonable doubt" on the shielding element. Curley, 160 F.2d at 232.

For these reasons, Ye's conviction on Count One should be reversed.
