**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code does not except from discharge debts arising from fraud.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s discharge provisions are to be construed liberally in favor of the debtor to effectuate the "fresh start" policy underlying the Code. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). This Court has long recognized that exceptions to discharge must be narrowly construed. *Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915). Section 523(a)(2)(A) is an exception to discharge, and its terms must be strictly applied. *Id.*

The plain text of § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only those debts "obtained by ... actual fraud." The statute does not except from discharge debts arising from fraud itself. *See id.* ("The exceptions are stated in the disjunctive and are specific and limited."). The distinction is critical. A debt "obtained by fraud" is one where the debtor secured the debt through fraudulent means. *See Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 1, 7 (1877) (noting that a debt "obtained by fraud" is one procured through fraudulent misrepresentations). A debt arising from fraud, by contrast, is one that results from the debtor’s fraudulent conduct, even if the debt itself was not procured by fraud. *See id.* The text of § 523(a)(2)(A) does not encompass the latter.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that exceptions to discharge must be strictly construed. *Williams*, 236 U.S. at 555. In *Williams*, this Court held that the predecessor to § 523(a)(2)(A) did not except from discharge a debt arising from fraud unless the debt was itself "obtained by" fraud. *Id.* at 554-55. The Court reasoned that the statute’s language was clear and that exceptions to discharge must be narrowly applied. *Id.* at 555. The same principle applies here. *See id.*

**II. The damages awarded in this case are not "obtained by fraud" under § 523(a)(2)(A).**

Even if the Court were to conclude that § 523(a)(2)(A) encompasses debts arising from fraud, the damages awarded in this case were not "obtained by fraud." The tenants’ damages—including treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs—were not procured through Cohen’s fraud. Instead, they were imposed by the Bankruptcy Court and the District Court as a result of Cohen’s fraudulent conduct. *See Neal*, 95 U.S. at 7 (holding that a debt is "obtained by fraud" only if the debtor secured the debt through fraudulent means).

This Court has long held that punitive damages are not "obtained by fraud" unless they were themselves procured through fraudulent means. *See Berry v. Davis*, 242 U.S. 468, 471 (1917) (holding that punitive damages are not dischargeable unless they were "obtained by fraud"). The same principle applies to statutory treble damages. *See id.* Here, the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs were imposed as a result of Cohen’s fraudulent conduct, not procured through fraud. *See id.*

**III. The Bankruptcy Code’s "fresh start" policy supports a broad discharge of debts arising from fraud.**

This Court has long recognized that the Bankruptcy Code’s "fresh start" policy is a central purpose of the Code. *Local Loan*, 292 U.S. at 244. This policy is particularly important in cases involving fraud, where the debtor’s conduct may have been reckless or negligent rather than intentionally fraudulent. *See Williams*, 236 U.S. at 555. The Court has held that exceptions to discharge must be narrowly construed to effectuate this policy. *Id.*

In *Williams*, this Court held that a debt arising from fraud was dischargeable unless the debt itself was "obtained by fraud." *Id.* at 554-55. The Court reasoned that the "fresh start" policy would be undermined if debts arising from fraud were broadly excepted from discharge. *Id.* at 555. The same principle applies here. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed. The debts at issue here—including the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs—are dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.
