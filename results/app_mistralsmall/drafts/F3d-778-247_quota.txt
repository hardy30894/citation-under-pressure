**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SEARCH WARRANT LACKED THE REQUIRED NEXUS BETWEEN THE PLACE TO BE SEARCHED AND THE CRIME UNDER INVESTIGATION.**

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant particularly describe “the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. This nexus requirement ensures that the scope of a search is “reasonably related to the objectives of the authorized search.” *Maryland v. Garrison*, 480 U.S. 79, 84 (1987). Here, the warrant application failed to establish a sufficient nexus between Joubert’s parents’ home and the alleged crimes.

The affidavit relied on generalized assertions that offenders “often maintain possession” of evidence, but did not tie that tendency to Joubert’s parents’ home. *See United States v. Fuccillo*, 808 F.3d 116, 127 (1st Cir. 2015) (finding insufficient nexus where affidavit did not link defendant’s residence to crime). The officer’s belief that evidence “may confirm or dispel” Joubert’s background or relationships was speculative and insufficient to establish probable cause. *See Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983) (probable cause requires “a substantial basis” for concluding evidence will be found).

**II. THE ADMISSION OF UNCHARGED CHILD MOLESTATION TESTIMONY VIOLATED RULE 404(B) AND DUE PROCESS.**

The district court erred in admitting testimony about uncharged acts under Rule 404(b). *See Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 691 (1988) (Rule 404(b) requires proof that the other act occurred and was committed by the defendant). The government failed to present clear and convincing evidence that Joubert committed the prior acts. *See United States v. Decicco*, 868 F.3d 83, 90 (1st Cir. 2017) (standard for Rule 404(b) evidence).

Moreover, the testimony was unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. *See Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180 (1997) (balancing probative value against unfair prejudice). The admission of this evidence violated due process by allowing the jury to convict based on propensity reasoning. *See Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 70 (1991).

**III. THE FEDERAL STATUTES UNDER WHICH JOUBERT WAS CONVICTED EXCEED CONGRESS’S COMMERCE CLAUSE POWER.**

Joubert’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) cannot stand because these statutes regulate purely intrastate conduct. *See United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 617 (2000) (Commerce Clause does not permit federal regulation of intrastate criminal conduct). The government failed to show a sufficient nexus between Joubert’s conduct and interstate commerce. *See Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 17 (2005) (distinguishing economic activity with substantial effect on interstate commerce).

**IV. JOUBERT’S 480-MONTH SENTENCE WAS SUBSTANTIVELY UNREASONABLE.**

A sentence is substantively unreasonable if it “cannot be located within the range of permissible decisions.” *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 92 (1st Cir. 2008). Joubert’s sentence was 20 years above the Guidelines range and disproportionate to the offense conduct. *See Rita v. United States*, 551 U.S. 338, 351 (2007) (appellate review ensures sentences are reasonable). The district court improperly relied on uncharged conduct to justify the sentence. *See United States v. Politano*, 522 F.3d 67, 72 (1st Cir. 2008) (sentencing court must consider § 3553(a) factors).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Joubert’s conviction and sentence should be reversed.
