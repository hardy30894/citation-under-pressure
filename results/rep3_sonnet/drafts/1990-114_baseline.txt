# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA BECAUSE STATES RETAIN AUTHORITY TO DEFINE THE QUALIFICATIONS OF THEIR JUDGES ABSENT A CLEAR CONGRESSIONAL STATEMENT TO THE CONTRARY

This Court has long recognized that "if Congress intends to alter the usual constitutional balance between the States and the Federal Government, it must make its intention to do so unmistakably clear in the language of the statute." *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985). That principle applies with special force here, where Missouri's mandatory retirement provision governs the qualifications of state judges—a matter that goes to the heart of state sovereignty. As this Court has explained, "through the structure of its government, and the character of those who exercise government authority, a State defines itself as a sovereign." A State's power to establish qualifications for those who make and interpret its laws is not merely one power among many; it is essential to the State's separate and independent existence.

The ADEA's definition of "employee" excludes "any person elected to public office," "any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer's personal staff," and "an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Because Missouri's appointed judges plainly fall within the "policymaking level" exception, no conflict with the ADEA exists, and this Court need not resolve any difficult constitutional question of federal power over the structure of state government.

Judges are quintessential policymakers. As the Eighth Circuit correctly recognized, "'[p]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Missouri judges do not merely apply settled rules mechanically to undisputed facts; they exercise the same "discretion" and "judgment" that characterizes policymaking in the executive and legislative branches. Missouri's judges, moreover, exercise substantial administrative and supervisory authority—promulgating rules of professional conduct, supervising circuit administration, and resolving intra-judicial disputes. This authority confirms that Missouri's judiciary, no less than its executive agencies, sets policy for the State.

Petitioners' contrary reading would produce an implausible result: appointed state judges would receive no protection under any of the ADEA's exemptions, while elected judges would be wholly exempt. Congress cannot be presumed to have intended so incongruous a scheme, particularly in an area—the structure and composition of a state judiciary—that touches the core of state sovereignty. Given the "background principle" that States retain the power to prescribe the qualifications of those who exercise their governmental authority, this Court should not construe the ADEA to strip Missouri of that power absent the requisite plain statement. Because no such plain statement exists, the mandatory retirement provision does not conflict with the ADEA.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES RATIONAL-BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and this Court has repeatedly held that state action classifying on the basis of age is subject only to rational-basis scrutiny. *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). In *Murgia*, this Court sustained a mandatory retirement age of fifty for state police officers, holding that a State "does not deny equal protection of the laws by excluding [some] individuals" through a classification that need only be "rationally related to furthering a legitimate state interest." *Id.* at 314-15. The same deferential standard governs here, and Missouri's provision easily satisfies it.

This Court has made clear that under rational-basis review, "a State does not violate the Equal Protection Clause merely because the classifications made by its laws are imperfect." *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471, 485 (1970). A classification "must be upheld against equal protection challenge if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis for the classification." *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). Missouri need not prove that every judge over seventy suffers diminished capacity; it need only demonstrate a rational connection between age and the State's legitimate interests in the competence and vigor of its judiciary.

Missouri's interest in mandatory judicial retirement is unmistakably legitimate. As the Seventh Circuit has explained, "the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode," and mandatory retirement thereby "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges." *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). A fixed retirement age likewise spares the State the institutional difficulty of individualized competency evaluations, avoiding, as the Third Circuit observed, "the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges." *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). And Missouri may rationally conclude, as the Massachusetts high court reasoned in an analogous case, that a fixed age limit "eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment" of case-by-case removal proceedings, while helping "to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary." *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

Petitioners may disagree with the wisdom of a fixed age of seventy, and they may point to individual judges whose faculties remain unimpaired. But rational-basis review does not permit courts to substitute their own judgment for the State's reasonable, if imperfect, line-drawing. As this Court held in *Murgia*, the relevant question is not whether the State's chosen age is the wisest possible policy, but whether the classification rationally furthers a legitimate objective. 427 U.S. at 316. Because mandatory retirement at seventy is rationally related to Missouri's legitimate interest in a competent, vigorous judiciary administered without the delay and indignity of individualized fitness determinations, the provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
