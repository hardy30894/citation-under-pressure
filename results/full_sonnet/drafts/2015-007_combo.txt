# ARGUMENT

## I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Determine Mental Retardation or to Return a Unanimous Sentencing Recommendation.

The Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee, as historically understood, protects the process of adjudicating guilt—not the discretionary judgment of what punishment should follow conviction. This Court has long distinguished the "narrow issue of guilt" from the individualized inquiry that attends sentencing. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949). In *Williams*, this Court held that due process did not require a sentencing judge to be bound by the confrontation and evidentiary rules applicable at trial, explaining that "modern concepts individualizing punishment have made it all the more necessary that a sentencing judge not be denied an opportunity to obtain pertinent information." *Id.* If the Constitution permits a judge alone to weigh mitigating and aggravating facts at sentencing without the procedural trappings of a criminal trial, it follows that Florida's scheme—in which the jury renders an advisory recommendation to guide, but not bind, that same judicial sentencing function—withstands scrutiny.

Nor does the common-law jury-unanimity rule, which this Court traced in *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930), to "trial by jury as understood and applied at common law," extend beyond the determination of guilt. In *Patton*, the essential elements of jury trial identified as constitutionally fixed—twelve jurors, judicial supervision, and unanimity—were discussed exclusively in the context of verdicts of guilt or innocence, not discretionary sentencing recommendations. *Id.* Where this Court has required unanimity in the sentencing context, it has done so as a matter of statutory construction, not constitutional command. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948), the Court required unanimity for a federal capital jury's qualification of its verdict because the governing statute made the jury's own verdict dispositive of sentence. Florida's scheme differs fundamentally: the jury's recommendation is advisory only, and the judge—not the jury—imposes the sentence. Because the jury does not itself return the operative "verdict" of death, the unanimity principle invoked in *Andres* has no purchase here.

This distinction between the guilt-adjudication function protected by the Sixth Amendment and the moral, discretionary judgment involved in capital sentencing is confirmed by *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510, 519 (1968). There, this Court recognized that a capital sentencing jury is asked to express "the conscience of the community on the ultimate question of life or death"—a description that underscores the qualitatively different, discretionary character of the sentencing decision as compared to the historically rigid fact-finding function of a guilt-phase jury. Similarly, in *Singer v. United States*, 380 U.S. 24, 36 (1965), this Court emphasized that the jury-trial guarantee exists to protect defendants against oppressive prosecution, not to dictate the precise procedural mechanics by which sentencing determinations are reached. And while *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968), held the jury-trial right "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" and applicable to the States, that holding, too, was confined to the adjudication of guilt for serious offenses—not the discretionary calculus of sentencing.

Finally, the determination whether a defendant is mentally retarded is a mitigating consideration bearing on the appropriateness of punishment, precisely the sort of individualized judgment that *Williams* commits to the sentencer's discretion rather than to rigid procedural rule. Florida's decision to allow the sentencing judge, rather than the advisory jury, to resolve that question is consistent with the flexible, non-uniform sentencing procedures that the Constitution tolerates among the several States. *See Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967) (States retain broad latitude to structure evidentiary and procedural rules governing punishment).

## II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Mandate Any Particular Jury Procedure So Long As the Punishment Itself Is Not Inherently Cruel.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment addresses the *nature* of the punishment imposed, not the procedural mechanism by which a sentencer arrives at that punishment. This Court has explained that the Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). That inquiry focuses on whether a punishment is disproportionate or inherently inhumane—not on whether a jury's sentencing recommendation was unanimous. Indeed, in *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947), this Court framed the Eighth Amendment concern as whether a State's method "forbids the infliction of unnecessary pain in the execution of the death sentence," a substantive inquiry wholly distinct from jury voting procedure.

That the Eighth Amendment now applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962), does not alter this substantive focus. Robinson addressed whether criminalizing a status was itself cruel and unusual—not whether the decision-making procedure attending punishment satisfied some independent constitutional standard. Because Florida's death sentence for Hurst reflects a proportionate punishment for a heinous, premeditated robbery-murder, and because the mental retardation inquiry was fully available to Hurst as mitigation before the sentencer, no Eighth Amendment violation arises merely from the jury's non-unanimous advisory vote or from the allocation of the mental-retardation determination to the judge.

For these reasons, Florida's capital sentencing scheme comports with both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
