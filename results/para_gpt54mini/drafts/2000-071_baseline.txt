**ARGUMENT**

I. Milford did not violate the Club’s free speech rights.

Milford’s facilities policy created a limited public forum for specified community uses, not an open forum for all comers. In such a forum, the government may reserve access for the purposes for which it was created, so long as its restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s policy permitted use for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but excluded “religious purposes.” That line properly preserved the school’s limited forum for nonreligious community uses. See *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 390-91 (1993).

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford did not engage in viewpoint discrimination. The Club did not seek to discuss morals or character from a religious viewpoint; it sought to conduct meetings that included “singing songs, hearing a Bible lesson and memorizing scripture,” along with prayer, Bible stories, and invitations to salvation. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 508-13 (2d Cir. 2000). That is religious instruction and worship, not merely a viewpoint on an otherwise permitted subject. A school district “may not exclude speech where its distinction is not content-based but viewpoint-based,” *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 828-30 (1995), but Milford’s policy did not target a particular religious perspective within a secular subject; it excluded use for religious purposes altogether. See *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94.

The Court’s precedents confirm that the Constitution does not require a school to open its property to religious exercise merely because it allows analogous secular activities. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), the Court distinguished protected speech from worship in explaining that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” do not lose First Amendment protection simply because they are religious. But *Widmar* involved a university that had opened a forum for student use and then excluded religious worship on the basis of content; here, Milford limited its forum from the outset to nonreligious community uses. See *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. Likewise, *Lamb’s Chapel* held that a school district violated the Free Speech Clause when it excluded religious films addressing family values and child rearing “from a religious perspective,” because the district had opened the forum for discussions on those subjects. 508 U.S. at 393-94. Milford did not exclude the Club from discussing morals as such; it excluded the Club’s religious program.

II. Milford’s exclusion was justified to avoid an Establishment Clause violation.

Even if Milford’s denial implicated free speech, the school was justified in refusing access because it reasonably concluded that admitting the Club would create an Establishment Clause problem. Public schools may act to avoid governmental endorsement of religion, particularly where young children are involved and school property, personnel, and authority would be used to facilitate religious activity. *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971); *Edwards v. Aguillard*, 482 U.S. 578, 583-84 (1987). Milford reasonably believed that weekly after-school meetings featuring Bible lessons, prayer, scripture memorization, and invitations to “believe on the Lord Jesus” would appear to be school-sponsored religious instruction. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 508-13.

The Court has repeatedly recognized that a school’s concern about Establishment Clause endorsement is entitled to serious weight in the elementary school setting. In *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592-93 (1992), the Court emphasized the coercive pressures created by religious exercises in public-school settings. And in *Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308-12 (2000), the Court held that student-led prayer at school events could violate the Establishment Clause because it bore the imprint of school sponsorship. Milford’s concern was at least as substantial here: the Club sought access to a public elementary school for highly devotional activities with young children, after hours but on school premises and under a community use policy administered by school officials. See *Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 223-24 (1997).

Nor was Milford required to risk constitutional violation in order to avoid a speech claim. Where two plausible constructions exist, the school may choose the one that preserves constitutional compliance. *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Fla. Gulf Coast Bldg. & Constr. Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988). Milford did exactly that. Its decision was a permissible effort to keep school facilities from becoming a venue for religious worship and instruction that, in the school’s judgment, would be attributable to the public school itself.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
