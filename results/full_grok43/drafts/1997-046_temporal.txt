The petitioner submits that the judgment below must be reversed. Clemency is a matter of executive grace, not a proceeding to which the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment attaches. As this Court long ago declared, a pardon "is an act of grace, proceeding from the power intrusted with the execution of the laws." United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). The same principle governs commutations and reprieves. Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 310 (1855). Because the power "is not part of the judicial function," its exercise "is rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." See Biddle v. Perovich, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927). No life or liberty interest therefore arises from the clemency process itself.

The lower court's contrary conclusion rests on the premise that clemency has become "an integral part" of the adjudicatory system. That premise finds no support in the decisions of this Court. The Constitution separates the pardoning power from the judicial power, and the separation remains complete. See Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). A state may regulate the manner of applying for clemency without thereby converting the governor's discretionary act into a judicial proceeding entitled to the protections of due process. See Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 601 (1896).

The same separation defeats the Fifth Amendment claim. The regulation at issue permits, but does not require, an interview. A prisoner who elects to participate does so voluntarily. This Court has consistently held that the privilege against self-incrimination "is a prohibition of the use of the process of the law to compel the person to testify," not a bar to voluntary statements. Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892). Where the state "does not compel" the witness to speak, no constitutional violation occurs. See Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 91 (1908). The mere possibility that statements made in a voluntary interview might later be used does not transform an optional procedure into compulsion. See Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910).

Because the clemency interview is neither mandatory nor coercive, it imposes no unconstitutional condition. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
