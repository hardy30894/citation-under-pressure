The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and infringes upon the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). In Tennessee Electric, the Court held that private parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim, stating that "absent the states or their officers, (the utility companies) have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144. Although some circuit courts have permitted private parties to bring Tenth Amendment challenges, the majority of circuits have followed Tennessee Electric, denying standing to private parties. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir.2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir.2009); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir.2006).

The rationale behind denying standing to private parties is that the Tenth Amendment is a structural provision that protects the states' sovereignty, and private parties are not the proper representatives of the states' interests. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir.2005). As the First Circuit Court explained, "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." Id. at 35.

In this case, Bond, as a private party, lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The State of Pennsylvania, not Bond, is the proper party to raise any concerns about the federal government's overreach into areas traditionally regulated by the states.

Furthermore, even if Bond had standing, her challenge to the statute would fail on the merits. The statute was enacted pursuant to the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18; art. II, § 2, cl. 2, to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Treaty Power is a valid source of congressional authority, and that treaty-implementing legislation is constitutional if it is "necessary and proper" to execute the treaty obligations. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920).

In conclusion, Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The judgment of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
