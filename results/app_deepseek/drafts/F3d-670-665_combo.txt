## ARGUMENT

The district court erred by imposing a separate reasonable-diligence requirement on a habeas petitioner who presents a credible claim of actual innocence. This Court should hold that a credible showing of actual innocence alone warrants equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations, because the fundamental miscarriage of justice exception has always focused on the reliability of the conviction, not on the petitioner’s diligence.

### I. The Actual Innocence Exception Does Not Require a Separate Diligence Showing

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal habeas corpus exists to remedy convictions obtained in violation of fundamental constitutional rights. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 401–02 (1963) (habeas review available to correct “grievous wrong” even after procedural default). When a petitioner presents credible evidence of actual innocence, the conviction itself becomes constitutionally suspect, and procedural bars must yield. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (habeas court must consider claims that “render the conviction void”); *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 463 (1953) (habeas review extends to “fundamental fairness” of trial). A conviction based on perjured testimony—as Perkins’s evidence suggests—violates due process. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (knowing use of false testimony “deprives the defendant of his liberty without due process of law”).

The actual innocence exception is rooted in equity, not in a mechanical checklist. *Ex parte Hull*, 312 U.S. 546, 549 (1941) (habeas corpus is “a remedy for the violation of constitutional rights”). Equity has never required a petitioner to show diligence separate from the merits of the claim when the underlying injustice is manifest. *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946) (equitable tolling applies where “the defendant’s fraudulent conduct” conceals the cause of action, without requiring independent diligence); *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874) (equity tolls limitations where fraud prevents discovery, and “the party is not bound to use diligence” until fraud is known). Similarly, a judgment obtained by fraud may be set aside regardless of the prevailing party’s diligence. *Hazel-Atlas Glass Co. v. Hartford-Empire Co.*, 322 U.S. 238, 245 (1944) (equity will “relieve a party from a judgment obtained by fraud”).

Requiring a separate diligence showing conflicts with the purpose of the actual innocence exception: to prevent the incarceration of an innocent person. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 462 (1938) (courts must “protect the fundamental rights of the accused”); *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937) (due process protects “fundamental principles of liberty and justice”). The exception exists precisely because procedural defaults should not bar relief when the conviction is unreliable. *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1, 8 (1963) (abuse-of-writ doctrine does not bar claims where “the ends of justice” require review). A credible claim of actual innocence is itself the ultimate showing of diligence—the petitioner has uncovered evidence that the state’s case was false.

### II. Even If Diligence Were Required, Perkins Acted Reasonably

Perkins obtained three affidavits between 1997 and 2002 that directly implicated the prosecution’s sole eyewitness in the murder. He filed his habeas petition in 2008, within a reasonable time after the last affidavit. The district court’s contrary finding ignored that Perkins, an incarcerated layman, lacked legal assistance and faced substantial obstacles in gathering evidence. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 420 (habeas courts consider “the circumstances of the prisoner’s confinement”). The affidavits were not “substantially available” at trial; they were newly discovered and pointed to Jones’s guilt, not merely to a theory of framing. The district court’s diligence analysis improperly substituted its own view of the evidence for Perkins’s credible showing.

### Conclusion

This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to consider Perkins’s actual innocence claim on the merits, without requiring a separate showing of reasonable diligence. A credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, warrants equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations.
