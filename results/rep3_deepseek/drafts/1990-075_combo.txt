## ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), constitutes a valid delegation of legislative power under the “intelligible principle” standard, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration is expressly authorized by statute.

### I. Congress Provided an Intelligible Principle Guiding the Attorney General’s Temporary Scheduling Authority

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, so long as Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This test has been applied with great flexibility; only twice has the Court found a delegation unconstitutional, and both cases involved statutes that lacked any meaningful standards. *See Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935) (no policy or criterion governed President’s authority to prohibit interstate transport of oil); *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935) (no standard for approving codes of fair competition). Here, by contrast, § 811(h) cabins the Attorney General’s discretion in multiple ways.

First, temporary scheduling may occur only when the Attorney General finds that a substance poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This phrase supplies a concrete, justiciable standard. *Cf. Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 427 (1944) (upholding delegation requiring price controls to be “fair and equitable” because “[t]he standards . . . are as definite as the circumstances permit”). Second, the Attorney General must consider three specific factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of such abuse, and the risk to public health. § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)). Third, he must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit his findings to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments he must consider. § 811(h)(4). Fourth, the temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. § 811(h)(2). These procedural and substantive limits are far more specific than those in many delegations the Court has upheld. *E.g., Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upheld delegation to Federal Home Loan Bank Board to regulate savings and loan associations based on “well-known and generally acceptable standards” of banking).

That § 811(h) authorizes the creation of criminal offenses does not alter the analysis. Congress may delegate authority to define criminal conduct so long as it supplies adequate standards. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to issue regulations governing national forests, violations of which were punishable criminally, because “Congress has [not] delegated legislative power” but rather “has made . . . adoption of . . . administrative rules and regulations the means of carrying into operation [a] general policy.” Likewise, in *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496-97 (1904), the Court upheld a delegation to the Treasury Department to set standards for imported tea, with criminal penalties for violations, because Congress had “submitted to [the Secretary] the duty of determining . . . the standard of quality.” The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision similarly implements Congress’s clear policy of preventing “imminent hazard[s] to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), and the Attorney General merely applies that policy to specific substances.

The delegation here is narrower than the one upheld in *Yakus*, which gave an administrative agency the power to fix prices that would be “generally fair and equitable.” 321 U.S. at 420. And unlike the National Industrial Recovery Act in *Panama Refining*—which “declared no policy as to the transportation of the excess production” and “did not qualify [the President’s] authority,” 293 U.S. at 415—§ 811(h) declares a policy (protecting the public from imminent hazards) and qualifies the Attorney General’s authority through specific factors, deadlines, and interagency consultation.

### II. The Attorney General Lawfully Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA

The Controlled Substances Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions. Section 871(a) provides that the Attorney General may “authorize the performance of any of the functions vested in him by this subchapter . . . by any officer, employee, or agency of the Department of Justice.” Pursuant to this authority, the Attorney General promulgated 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator “[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970.” Such subdelegation is routine and consistent with established principles of administrative law. *See Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 365 (1942) (holding that an “officer with authority to delegate” may assign functions to subordinates, “since otherwise the mere multiplication of duties would make government impossible”). Indeed, “Congress must be deemed to have contemplated” that the Attorney General would employ subordinates to carry out the detailed tasks of scheduling, just as it did in *Grimaud*, where the Secretary of Agriculture delegated rulemaking authority to forest supervisors. 220 U.S. at 517. Because § 871(a) plainly authorizes delegation, the Attorney General’s subdelegation is valid.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
