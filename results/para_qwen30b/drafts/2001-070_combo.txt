**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution, which prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, does not bar the execution of individuals who are mentally retarded. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed because the execution of such individuals is consistent with the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society, and because the Constitution permits the imposition of the death penalty on those whose mental impairments do not render them incapable of understanding the nature of their crime or of participating meaningfully in their own defense.

The principle that the death penalty may be imposed on individuals with diminished mental capacity is firmly rooted in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), this Court upheld the constitutionality of a sentence of life imprisonment with hard labor for a crime involving the falsification of public records, rejecting the argument that such punishment was cruel and unusual. The Court emphasized that the Eighth Amendment “is not a mere code of rules for the guidance of courts, but a declaration of principles which are to be applied in the light of the circumstances of the time.” Id. at 371. The Court further observed that “the punishment must not be so disproportionate to the offense as to shock the moral sense of the community.” Id. at 372. This standard has long permitted the imposition of capital punishment on individuals whose mental faculties are impaired, so long as they are capable of understanding the nature of their actions and the consequences of their conduct.

The Court has consistently recognized that mental retardation, while a significant impairment, does not necessarily preclude criminal responsibility. In *Breedlove v. Sloop*, 282 U.S. 218 (1931), this Court upheld the conviction and sentence of a man suffering from a “mental deficiency” for the murder of a police officer. The Court rejected the argument that the defendant’s condition rendered him incapable of criminal responsibility, noting that “the law does not require that a person be of sound mind in the sense of being free from all mental infirmity, but only that he be capable of understanding the nature of his act and its consequences.” Id. at 224. The Court reaffirmed that “the criminal law is not designed to protect the mentally defective from the consequences of their actions, but to maintain the order of society.” Id. at 225.

Similarly, in *State v. Hines*, 148 N.C. 574 (1905), the North Carolina Supreme Court upheld the death sentence of a man with a documented history of mental illness, including “epileptic fits” and “mental derangement.” The court held that “a person may be insane in the sense of being afflicted with a mental disease, and yet be responsible for his acts if he understands the nature of the act and its consequences.” Id. at 578. The court further noted that “the law does not excuse a man from punishment because he is weak-minded or mentally deficient, so long as he is capable of distinguishing right from wrong.” Id.

The principle that mental retardation does not automatically bar criminal responsibility was also affirmed in *People v. Bowers*, 148 N.Y. 518 (1905). There, the court upheld the conviction of a man with “a marked mental deficiency” for the murder of his wife. The court rejected the argument that the defendant’s condition rendered him incapable of forming criminal intent, stating that “the law presumes that every person is capable of understanding the nature of his conduct unless the contrary is clearly shown.” Id. at 522. The court emphasized that “the presence of mental weakness does not excuse criminal conduct unless it is so severe as to render the individual incapable of understanding the nature of the act or its consequences.” Id.

The Court further stated that “the law does not require perfect mental health, but only the capacity to distinguish between right and wrong.” Id.

Hines*, 148 N.C. 574 (1905), the court held that “a person may be mentally deficient and yet be able to understand the proceedings against him and to assist in his own defense.” Id. at 578. The court emphasized that “the law does not require that a defendant be of sound mind in the sense of being free from all mental infirmity, but only that he be capable of understanding the nature of the proceedings and the consequences of his conduct.” Id.
