# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST AND IS THEREFORE A CONSTITUTIONAL DELEGATION OF AUTHORITY

Article I vests "[a]ll legislative Powers" in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but this Court has long recognized that Congress may enlist the assistance of coordinate branches so long as it "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This Court reaffirmed that standard just two years before this case arose, explaining that the nondelegation inquiry is guided by "a practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989).

Section 811(h) readily satisfies this standard. Congress specified the precise goal the Attorney General must pursue—avoiding "an imminent hazard to the public safety," 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1)—and enumerated the specific factors he must consider in making that determination: "the history and current pattern of abuse" of the substance, "the scope, duration, and significance of abuse," and "what, if any, risk there is to the public health." *Id.* § 811(c)(4)-(6); *see* Pet. App. (citing same). Congress further cabined the delegated authority by requiring publication of notice in the Federal Register, consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and a strict durational limit of one year, extendable by only six months. *Id.* § 811(h)(2), (4). These are precisely the sort of statutory constraints—stated goals, enumerated factors, and temporal limits—that this Court found sufficient in *Mistretta* to sustain the Sentencing Commission's authority to promulgate binding Guidelines. 488 U.S. at 374-79.

Petitioners' reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those decisions—the only two in this Court's history invalidating a delegation—involved statutes that, in the Court's words, failed to "declare any policy" or establish "criterion to govern" executive action. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415. Here, by contrast, Congress specified the precise standard ("imminent hazard to the public safety"), the exact factors bearing on that standard, and rigorous temporal and procedural boundaries. Section 811(h) bears no resemblance to the standardless delegations condemned in 1935 and everything in common with the numerous delegations this Court has sustained in the decades since. *See Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation to fix commodity prices that were "fair and equitable"); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation governed by "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the regulated field).

The rapidity of change in the field being regulated further supports the delegation. As this Court recognized in *Yakus*, Congress may grant broader discretion where "the entire economy" or a rapidly evolving problem "makes it necessary" to depart from rigid legislative specification. 321 U.S. at 424. Designer drug manufacturers exploit the very lag time inherent in ordinary rulemaking; Congress created the temporary scheduling mechanism for the narrow, time-limited purpose of closing that gap, precisely the kind of "technical and complex" area in which delegation is most justified. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372.

Nor does the criminal consequence attached to scheduling alter the analysis. This Court has never held that delegations touching criminal enforcement demand a heightened intelligible-principle standard. To the contrary, Congress may delegate the authority to define the factual predicate that triggers a preexisting criminal statute so long as intelligible standards constrain that determination. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911) (upholding criminal penalties for violation of Forest Service regulations promulgated pursuant to delegated authority). Section 811(h) delegates only the technical, factual determination whether a substance poses an imminent public-safety hazard under enumerated statutory criteria—not the underlying decision to criminalize drug manufacture, which Congress made itself in 21 U.S.C. § 841.

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL WAS AUTHORIZED TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act. Section 871(a) provides that the Attorney General "may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This broad, unqualified grant of subdelegation authority contains no textual exception for temporary scheduling under § 811(h), and petitioners identify none. Where Congress uses expansive language authorizing delegation of "any" function, courts should not read in limitations Congress did not enact. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-73 (deferring to Congress's chosen mechanism for administering complex regulatory schemes).

The Attorney General's 1987 amendment to 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended," was a straightforward and proper exercise of the authority Congress conferred in § 871(a). The DEA is the component of the Department of Justice charged with enforcing the Nation's drug laws, and vesting scheduling authority in the agency with the relevant scientific and law-enforcement expertise reflects precisely the kind of practical administrative arrangement this Court has repeatedly sanctioned. *See Fahey*, 332 U.S. at 250 (delegation appropriate where agency possesses relevant technical expertise).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
