**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Constitution Prohibits Indefinite Detention of a Removable Alien**

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause, made applicable to the States by the Fourteenth Amendment, “prohibits the infliction of punishment without due process of law.” *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 500 (1954). This fundamental right precludes the government from confining an individual without a fixed end or a reasonable justification. *Korematsu v. United States*, 323 U.S. 214, 216 (1944). The writ of habeas corpus, the “great writ of liberty,” cannot be suspended except in cases of rebellion or invasion, implying that detention must be for a specific, lawful purpose and duration. *Id.* Consequently, the government may not detain a removable alien indefinitely simply because removal is difficult; such detention would amount to punishment without due process.

**II. The Post-Removal-Period Statute Must Be Read in Light of Constitutional Limits**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), must be construed in a manner consistent with the Constitution. *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953). In *Mezei*, the Court held that the detention of an alien is “not punitive but is in connection with and in furtherance of the deportation proceedings.” *Id.* Accordingly, the statute authorizes detention only for a “reasonable time” necessary to effectuate removal. *Id.* at 213. If the statute were interpreted to permit indefinite detention, it would effectively authorize punishment without due process, a result the Court has consistently rejected. *Klein v. United States*, 80 U.S. 145, 162 (1871). Where a statute is general in its terms and is susceptible of two constructions, one of which will sustain the act and the other will defeat it, the former must be adopted. *Id.*

**III. The Facts Demonstrate the “Reasonable Time” Has Expired**

Here, Zadvydas has been detained for over three years after the expiration of the removal period. No country has accepted him, and his removal is impossible. The “reasonable time” limitation has long since expired. The government has failed to rebut the presumption that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. Therefore, continued detention is unconstitutional.
