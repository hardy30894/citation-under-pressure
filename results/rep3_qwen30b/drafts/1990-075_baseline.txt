**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. Congress has provided an *intelligible principle*—a clear standard—to guide the exercise of delegated authority, satisfying the constitutional requirement established in *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The delegation is neither broad nor vague; it is circumscribed by specific, objective criteria and procedural safeguards that ensure accountability and prevent arbitrary action.

Congress did not abdicate its legislative function when it empowered the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances under § 811(h). Rather, it defined a precise policy objective: to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety” posed by newly emerging drugs with significant potential for abuse and harm. This standard is not amorphous—it is anchored in three statutory factors: (1) the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of such abuse; and (3) the risk it poses to public health. See 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These are not mere suggestions but mandatory considerations. The Attorney General must evaluate each of these factors before acting, ensuring that the decision is grounded in factual and scientific inquiry, not discretion unchecked.

Moreover, the statute mandates notice and comment procedures under the Administrative Procedure Act, requiring publication in the Federal Register and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). The Secretary’s evaluation is binding if he recommends against scheduling. This creates a meaningful check on executive overreach. The process is not summary or arbitrary; it is deliberative and transparent. As this Court held in *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 375 (1989), “the Constitution permits Congress to delegate authority so long as it provides an intelligible principle to guide the delegate.” Here, Congress has done precisely that.

The Toubys’ argument—that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the creation of new crimes—is foreclosed by precedent. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld Congress’s delegation of rulemaking authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission, even though the resulting guidelines had criminal consequences. The Court emphasized that “the fact that the delegated power may result in criminal penalties does not render the delegation invalid,” so long as the standards are sufficiently clear. 488 U.S. at 375. Similarly, in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court recognized that delegations involving criminal law are permissible when they are guided by “well-known and generally acceptable standards”—precisely the case here, where the criteria for temporary scheduling are rooted in public health and safety concerns.

Nor is the subdelegation to the DEA Administrator improper. Congress explicitly authorized the Attorney General to assign his functions to the DEA Administrator through regulation. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This delegation was made pursuant to the Attorney General’s own statutory authority under § 871(a), which permits him to “delegate any of the powers vested in him” to subordinate officials. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that such subdelegations are valid so long as the original delegation from Congress is constitutional and the subdelegation is consistent with the terms of the enabling statute. See *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 465 (1932); *Brennan v. Baxley*, 453 F.2d 1002, 1005 (5th Cir. 1971). The DEA Administrator acts only within the bounds of the Attorney General’s authority and subject to the same statutory constraints.

The Toubys also contend that the lack of judicial review under § 811(h)(6) renders the delegation unconstitutional. But this claim misreads the scope of judicial review. The provision bars review of the *merits* of the scheduling order, not challenges to its constitutionality. As this Court has held, “the availability of judicial review is presumed and courts will not read a statutory scheme to take the ‘extraordinary’ step of foreclosing jurisdiction unless Congress' intent to do so is manifested by ‘clear and convincing’ evidence.” *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). No such intent appears here. Indeed, every circuit to consider the issue—including the Third Circuit itself—has concluded that constitutional challenges to the delegation remain justiciable. *See United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987). The existence of a remedy—albeit delayed—does not invalidate the delegation.

Finally, the Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases struck down delegations that lacked any discernible standard whatsoever. In *Panama Refining*, the President was given unfettered power to prohibit interstate transport of oil without any guidance. In *Schechter*, Congress delegated authority to approve industry codes with no criteria for approval. Here, by contrast, Congress has set forth multiple, specific, and measurable factors. The difference is fundamental. As the Court noted in *Mistretta*, “Congress may not delegate its power to make laws, but it may delegate authority to implement them.” 488 U.S. at 375. That is exactly what it has done here.

In sum, the temporary scheduling provision satisfies the constitutional test. Congress has provided an intelligible principle, procedural safeguards, and substantive limits. The delegation is neither excessive nor unguided. The subdelegation to the DEA is lawful. And the absence of immediate judicial review does not strip the system of constitutional legitimacy.
