**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The question before this Court is whether that provision applies only when the injury occurs *on* the outer Continental Shelf, or whether it applies whenever the injury results from operations *conducted on* the outer Continental Shelf—even if the injury occurs on land. The Ninth Circuit held that the latter is true, adopting a “substantial nexus” test. That holding is erroneous and contrary to the settled interpretation of the LHWCA and OCSLA as a matter of statutory construction and legislative intent.

### I. THE OCSLA’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION PROVISION EXTENDS COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The language of § 1333(b) is unambiguous: it applies to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “occurring” is a temporal and spatial term, referring to the *location* and *time* of the injury. It does not refer to causation alone. The word “occurring” has long been interpreted in the context of maritime law to denote the *situs* of the injury. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947), the Court held that the term “injury occurring” in the LHWCA “refers to the place where the injury takes place.” The Court emphasized that “the situs of the injury is a jurisdictional fact” and that “the injury must occur upon the navigable waters of the United States.” Id. at 306. This principle has been consistently applied in cases involving the LHWCA’s situs requirement.

In *Barnes v. United States*, 326 U.S. 403, 406 (1946), the Court held that the LHWCA applied only to injuries occurring “on the navigable waters of the United States.” The Court rejected the argument that the statute applied to injuries occurring on land if they were “caused by” maritime operations. The Court stated: “The statute does not say ‘caused by’ but ‘occurring upon.’” Id. The distinction between “caused by” and “occurring upon” is critical. The former focuses on causation; the latter on location. The OCSLA’s use of “occurring” in § 1333(b) is identical in language and purpose to the LHWCA’s “occurring upon” standard. Therefore, the same rule applies: the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which permits coverage for injuries occurring on land so long as there is a causal connection to offshore operations, effectively rewrites § 1333(b) by substituting “caused by” for “occurring.” This is impermissible. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “Where the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, there is no room for construction.” The language of § 1333(b) is plain: coverage is limited to injuries *occurring* as a result of operations *on* the outer Continental Shelf. The injury must occur *on* the shelf.

### II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY AND STRUCTURE OF THE OCSLA SUPPORT A SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

The OCSLA was enacted in 1953 to extend federal jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf. Congress did not intend to extend the LHWCA to all employees engaged in offshore operations, regardless of where they were injured. Rather, Congress sought to provide workers’ compensation for injuries that occurred in the unique and hazardous environment of the outer Continental Shelf—where federal law, not state law, would govern.

The structure of the OCSLA confirms this intent. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) provides that the law of the nearest state shall govern “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This provision applies only to *situs*—to the physical location of the platform or structure. The fact that Congress used the term “situs” in one provision and “occurring” in another suggests that Congress understood the difference between location and causation. If Congress had intended to extend coverage to injuries occurring anywhere in the world so long as they were “caused by” offshore operations, it would have used the word “caused” or “resulting from” in § 1333(b), as it did in other provisions.

Moreover, the legislative history of the OCSLA reflects a concern with the physical location of the injury. In the Senate Report accompanying the Act, the Committee stated that the purpose of the workers’ compensation provision was to “provide for the protection of employees engaged in operations on the outer Continental Shelf.” S. Rep. No. 83-1177, at 1 (1953). The term “engaged in operations on the outer Continental Shelf” refers to the *location* of the work, not the *causal connection* to the injury. The Committee did not suggest that employees injured on land would be covered.

### III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONTRADICTS PRECEDENT AND UNDERMINES THE SITUATION OF THE LHWCA

Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), which was decided after the relevant precedent. The Court in *Tallentire* did not address the situs requirement for § 1333(b), and its footnote is dictum.
