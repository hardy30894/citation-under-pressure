**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION PROVIDE THE EXCLUSIVE, FIXED, AND UNIFORM STANDARDS FOR ELIGIBILITY TO CONGRESS, AND STATES MAY NOT ALTER THEM.**

The Constitution establishes the qualifications for membership in Congress with precision and finality. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state when elected. Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 provides that a Senator must be at least thirty years old, a citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the state when elected. These provisions are not suggestions; they are the exclusive gateways to federal office.

This Court has long recognized that the Constitution’s enumeration of qualifications precludes state additions. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969), the Court held that the Constitution’s age, citizenship, and residency requirements are “the exclusive qualifications” for membership in the House. The Court reasoned that the Framers intended “the Constitution to be the exclusive source of qualifications” for Congress, id. at 540, and that “the people of the several states” should not be permitted to “add to those qualifications,” id. at 541. The same logic applies to the Senate. *See* *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 798 (1995) (holding that the Qualifications Clauses are “uniform” and “national in character”).

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does not merely regulate ballot access; it imposes a substantive disqualification. By barring any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, the Amendment effectively prevents those individuals from being elected. This Court has squarely rejected the argument that a state may achieve indirectly what it cannot do directly. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 829 (holding that “the Constitution prohibits States from imposing any qualification for congressional service beyond those enumerated in the Constitution”). The Amendment’s ballot-access restriction is a qualification in disguise, and it is unconstitutional.

**II. THE FRAMERS’ DESIGN AND THE STRUCTURE OF THE CONSTITUTION CONFIRM THAT STATES LACK THE POWER TO ADD CONGRESSIONAL QUALIFICATIONS.**

The Framers deliberately rejected proposals to include state-imposed term limits in the Constitution. During the Constitutional Convention, the delegates considered and defeated a motion to limit the eligibility of members of Congress to a fixed number of years. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 800-01. Instead, they chose to rely on the electoral process—the “frequent recurrence of elections,” *The Federalist* No. 53 (J. Cooke ed. 1961)—to ensure accountability. The Constitution’s structure reflects a fundamental choice: the people, not the states, should decide who represents them in the national legislature.

Moreover, the Constitution’s Supremacy Clause, Art. VI, cl. 2, establishes that federal law is the supreme law of the land. State-imposed qualifications would undermine the uniformity that the Framers deemed essential. As this Court observed in *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 803, “the Constitution’s vision of a uniform national system of representation” would be “frustrated” if states could impose additional qualifications. The result would be a patchwork of eligibility rules, with some states barring candidates whom other states would welcome. That outcome is incompatible with the Constitution’s design.

**III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73’S CONGRESSIONAL TERM LIMITS ARE UNCONSTITUTIONAL AND THAT THE ENTIRE AMENDMENT MUST FALL.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the Qualifications Clauses. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill*, 316 Ark. 251, 265, 872 S.W.2d 349, 356 (1994). That holding is correct and should be affirmed. The court further held that Sections 1 and 2—which impose term limits on state officials—are not severable from Section 3. *Id.* at 268, 872 S.W.2d at 358. The court found that the Amendment was presented to voters as an “all or nothing” package, and that the sponsors’ reliance on a severability clause could not salvage the unconstitutional portion. *Id.* at 267, 872 S.W.2d at 357. This Court should defer to the state court’s interpretation of its own constitution and its determination that the Amendment’s provisions are inextricably linked.

**CONCLUSION**

The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. States may not add to them, whether by direct disqualification or by indirect ballot-access restrictions. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
