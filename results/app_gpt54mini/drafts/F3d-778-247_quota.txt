**Argument**

**I. The warrant was unsupported by probable cause because the affidavit did not establish a nexus between the suspected evidence and Joubert’s parents’ home.**  
The Fourth Amendment requires a warrant to be supported by probable cause, including a fair probability that evidence of wrongdoing will be found in the place to be searched. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238–39 (1983). A search warrant may not rest on generalized suspicion or “boilerplate” assumptions untethered to the particular premises. *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897, 923 (1984); *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294, 307 (1967). This Court has required a “nexus” between the items sought and the location searched. *United States v. Ribeiro*, 397 F.3d 43, 48 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Feliz*, 182 F.3d 82, 87–88 (1st Cir. 1999). The affidavit here did not supply that nexus. It showed only that Joubert had been under investigation, that he allegedly possessed or destroyed a computer tower, and that he later lived at his parents’ house. But no witness observed the relevant evidence inside that home, and the affiant’s conclusion that molesters “often maintain possession” of records was a generalization, not a particularized showing. *United States v. Zayas-Díaz*, 95 F.3d 105, 110 (1st Cir. 1996); *United States v. Ribeiro*, 397 F.3d at 48. Because probable cause must be grounded in facts, not inferences disconnected from the residence to be searched, the warrant should have been suppressed. *Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238.

**II. The district court abused its discretion by admitting testimony of uncharged child molestation.**  
Evidence of other acts is inadmissible to prove propensity. Fed. R. Evid. 404(b); *Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 685 (1988). Even where offered for a permissible purpose, the district court must still exclude it if its unfair prejudice substantially outweighs its probative value. Fed. R. Evid. 403; *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180–81 (1997). The First Circuit has repeatedly held that prior-bad-acts evidence is admissible only for a genuine non-propensity purpose and only when the trial court carefully weighs prejudice. *United States v. Rodríguez-Estrada*, 877 F.2d 153, 155–56 (1st Cir. 1989); *United States v. Varoudakis*, 233 F.3d 113, 121–23 (1st Cir. 2000). The challenged testimony concerning uncharged molestation did not prove any element of the charged § 2251 and § 2252A offenses; it served chiefly to portray Joubert as a sexual predator, inviting the jury to convict on character rather than proof. *United States v. Gilbert*, 229 F.3d 15, 20–21 (1st Cir. 2000); *United States v. Morena*, 547 F.3d 191, 194–96 (3d Cir. 2008). That is precisely the kind of prejudice Rule 403 guards against. *Old Chief*, 519 U.S. at 180–83.

**III. Congress exceeded its Commerce Clause power as applied to Joubert’s purely local conduct.**  
Although *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 17 (2005), permits regulation of activities that substantially affect interstate commerce in the aggregate, that principle does not erase constitutional limits. The Commerce Clause reaches noneconomic, intrastate conduct only when Congress rationally concludes that the class of activity substantially affects interstate commerce. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559–67 (1995); *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 610–19 (2000). This Court has upheld child-pornography statutes because Congress included jurisdictional elements tied to interstate commerce. *United States v. Robinson*, 137 F.3d 652, 656–58 (1st Cir. 1998); *United States v. Morales-De Jesús*, 372 F.3d 6, 17–20 (1st Cir. 2004). But as applied here, Joubert’s conduct was local: the charged exploitation involved a single child in New Hampshire, and the Government’s proof did not show that his conduct itself crossed state lines. A federal prosecution cannot be sustained merely because the instrumentality happened to be a computer or tape later found to have some attenuated connection to interstate commerce. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 564–68; *Morrison*, 529 U.S. at 617–19. The Constitution does not permit Congress to convert ordinary local sex offenses into federal crimes absent a real commerce nexus. *United States v. Kebodeaux*, 570 U.S. 387, 398–99 (2013).

**IV. The 480-month sentence was substantively unreasonable.**  
Appellate review for reasonableness requires a sentence to be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 50 (2007); *Kimbrough v. United States*, 552 U.S. 85, 101 (2007). A district court abuses its discretion when it gives inadequate weight to mitigating factors or treats the Guidelines as effectively mandatory. *Gall*, 552 U.S. at 51–52; *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 92–93 (1st Cir. 2008). Here, the court imposed a 40-year term that functionally equaled a life sentence without adequately accounting for the non-contact nature of the child-pornography count, the age of the conduct, and the cumulative severity created by grouping overlapping offenses. *United States v. Prosperi*, 686 F.3d 32, 43–46 (1st Cir. 2012); *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d at 96–97. A sentence that is greater than necessary to satisfy deterrence, incapacitation, and just punishment is substantively unreasonable. *Gall*, 552 U.S. at 50; *Kimbrough*, 552 U.S. at 111. The judgment should therefore be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing.

**Conclusion**  
For these reasons, the convictions and sentence should be reversed or vacated, and the case remanded for appropriate relief.
