**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Petitioner lacks a constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in Ohio’s clemency procedure itself. This Court has consistently held that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). The Due Process Clause does not create a substantive right to clemency; it is a matter of grace, not entitlement. *See id.* at 465 (holding that a state’s clemency procedures do not create a liberty interest subject to due process protection). Ohio’s governor enjoys “almost unfettered discretion” in granting clemency, and the state legislature may not prescribe substantive limitations on that discretion. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994). Because clemency is purely discretionary, no state-created liberty interest exists. *See Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976) (no liberty interest in discretionary prison transfers).

The court of appeals’ alternative holding—that minimal due process attaches to clemency because it is “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating guilt or innocence”—is unsupported. *Dumschat* rejected that very reasoning. Justice Stevens’ dissent in *Dumschat* urged that clemency is “a regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process,” 452 U.S. at 471, but the majority declined to constitutionalize it. *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), involved a first appeal as of right, a stage firmly within the adjudicatory process. Clemency occurs after final adjudication and is “a separate, executive act.” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. This Court has never extended *Evitts* beyond direct appeals, and it should not do so here. To hold otherwise would “create disincentives for States to codify prison management procedures,” *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 482 (1995), and chill the offering of clemency interviews—a practice that benefits inmates.

**II. The Voluntary Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

Petitioner’s Fifth Amendment claim is not ripe for review. Ohio’s clemency procedure does not compel any inmate to speak. The regulation provides that only upon the prisoner’s request will a personal interview occur, and the prisoner “remain[s] free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes.” *Woodard v. Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 107 F.3d 1178, 1190 (6th Cir. 1997) (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). There is no threat of penalty for remaining silent. *See Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 427 (1984) (holding that a witness’s choice to speak is not compelled unless the state threatens severe consequences). The mere possibility that a future state court might admit volunteered statements into evidence does not constitute present compulsion. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976) (holding anticipatory Fifth Amendment claims “premature” where no actual incrimination risk existed).

Petitioner’s argument that he faces a “Hobson’s choice” between participating and asserting his Fifth Amendment rights is speculative. He has not applied for clemency, and no interview has occurred. *See id.* (finding no ripe controversy where the alleged risk of self-incrimination was “purely academic”). The Court should not instruct federal courts to engage in hypothetical inquiries into the “nature of the APA’s clemency procedures” or the “risk of self-incrimination ex ante.” Such a remand would be unprecedented and would invite unnecessary interference with state executive functions.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s dismissal of the complaint should be affirmed.
