ARGUMENT

A defendant who establishes withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period commences is entitled to acquittal unless the government proves beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member during the actionable period. This allocation follows from settled principles governing the continuing nature of conspiracy and the government's burden on every element.

Conspiracy is "a partnership in criminal purposes" that endures only so long as the parties adhere to it. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910). Once a conspirator "has committed an overt act" and then "has withdrawn," the statute begins to run as to him. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). The government must therefore show that the accused "continued to be a member" within the limitations window. Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401 (1912).

Withdrawal is "an affirmative defense" that, when raised, requires the prosecution to prove continued participation. Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 217 (1946). The Court has long held that "the burden rests upon the prosecution to prove every ingredient of the offense." Christoffel v. United States, 338 U.S. 84, 89 (1949). Membership during the limitations period is such an ingredient. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 397 (1957) (quoting the rule that the government must establish "that the conspiracy still subsisted" within five years of indictment).

The same rule applies when the defense rests on the running of limitations. "The plea of the statute of limitations is a plea of not guilty" that puts the government to its proof. United States v. Cook, 17 Wall. 168, 178 (1873). In conspiracy prosecutions the government must therefore "prove that the offense was committed within the period of limitations." Bridges v. United States, 346 U.S. 209, 223 (1953).

These authorities establish that once withdrawal is shown, the government cannot rely on earlier acts alone. It must adduce evidence that the defendant "knowingly" remained a participant "during the period charged." Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 334 (1957). Any other rule would relieve the prosecution of its constitutional obligation to prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970) (tracing the rule to pre-1970 precedent).

The court of appeals therefore erred in placing on petitioners the burden to disprove continued membership. The judgment should be reversed.
