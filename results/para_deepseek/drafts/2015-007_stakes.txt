**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment**

The Sixth Amendment requires that any fact that increases the maximum penalty must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000). In *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002), this Court held that aggravating factors in capital cases are the functional equivalent of elements, and thus must be found by a jury. But *Ring* expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme from Arizona’s. In Arizona, the judge alone determined aggravators; in Florida, the jury renders an advisory verdict, and the judge makes the ultimate findings. *Id.* at 597 n.4. This Court has repeatedly upheld Florida’s hybrid scheme. *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 459 (1984) (jury’s advisory role does not violate Sixth Amendment); *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640–41 (1989) (per curiam) (same). *Ring* did not overrule those precedents; it merely held that the jury must find aggravators where the judge does so alone. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 597 n.4.

Hurst argues that the jury’s recommendation must be unanimous. But the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimity in advisory verdicts. *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 410 (1972) (plurality opinion) (non-unanimous jury verdicts in state criminal cases do not violate the Sixth Amendment). Florida’s jury serves as a “conscience of the community,” not as the final arbiter of facts. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 464. The judge independently weighs aggravators and mitigators, and the jury’s non-unanimous recommendation does not deprive Hurst of any right to have a jury find the facts necessary for death. *See* *Hildwin*, 490 U.S. at 641.

**II. Florida’s Procedure for Determining Mental Retardation Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment**

In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded but left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Florida has done so by requiring the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence, with the judge making the ultimate determination. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4). This Court has never required a jury to decide mental retardation. *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6, 7 (2005) (per curiam) (States may assign the determination to the judge). Hurst received a full evidentiary hearing, presented expert testimony, and the trial court found he was not mentally retarded. That finding is entitled to deference. *See* *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 424 (1986) (Powell, J., concurring) (state procedures for competency determinations are presumptively valid).

Hurst’s claim that the jury should have been allowed to decide mental retardation as a bar to death is foreclosed by *Atkins* itself, which did not mandate jury involvement. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. The Eighth Amendment requires only “heightened reliability” in capital sentencing, *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), and Florida’s procedure—with a judge making findings after a hearing—satisfies that standard. *See* *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 195 (1976) (plurality opinion) (States have discretion in designing capital procedures).

**III. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition on Arbitrariness**

Hurst argues that Florida’s lack of jury unanimity renders the death penalty arbitrary. But this Court has upheld Florida’s scheme against Eighth Amendment challenges. *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 252 (1976) (plurality opinion) (Florida’s guided discretion statute is constitutional). The judge’s independent review ensures that death is imposed only when aggravators outweigh mitigators. *Id.* at 253. The fact that Florida is an “outlier” on unanimity does not make its scheme unconstitutional. *See* *Kansas v. Marsh*, 548 U.S. 163, 174 (2006) (States may adopt different procedures so long as they are not “cruel and unusual”). The Eighth Amendment does not require a state to adopt the most common practice; it requires only that the scheme “genuinely narrow” the class of death-eligible defendants. *Lowenfield v. Phelps*, 484 U.S. 231, 244 (1988). Florida’s scheme does so by requiring the judge to find at least one statutory aggravator.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
