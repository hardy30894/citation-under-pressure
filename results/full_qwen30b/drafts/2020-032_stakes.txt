**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) “Freedom of Expression Policy” and the removal of the challenged provision of its Student Code of Conduct rendered Appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, and that nominal damages alone cannot revive a moot controversy. This Court has long recognized that a live controversy is required to maintain standing under Article III, and that a voluntary cessation of allegedly unlawful conduct, especially when accompanied by a permanent policy change, extinguishes the need for judicial intervention. Here, GGC’s comprehensive revision of its speech policies—eliminating the two designated speech zones and abolishing the permit requirement for expressive activity—effectively ended the alleged constitutional violation. The district court and the Eleventh Circuit properly applied binding precedent to conclude that Appellants’ claims are moot, and that nominal damages cannot save a case that has otherwise lost its justiciability.

The doctrine of mootness is rooted in Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement. As this Court has repeatedly held, “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). When a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct, the case may become moot unless there is a “reasonable expectation” that the wrong will recur. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976). In this case, GGC did not merely suspend its policy—it permanently revised it. The new policy permits students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances, and removes the provision that previously authorized enforcement of the speech zone restrictions. This is not a temporary or conditional change; it is a permanent, unambiguous abandonment of the challenged regime. The district court correctly concluded that there is “no reasonable basis to expect that [GGC] will return to [the Prior Policies].” *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, No. 1:16-cv-04658-ELR, 2018 WL 6572558, at *5 (N.D. Ga. Dec. 13, 2018). The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, applying the three-part test from *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), which requires a showing of (1) a voluntary cessation, (2) a permanent change in policy, and (3) no reasonable expectation of recurrence. All three prongs are satisfied here.

Appellants contend that their claims remain justiciable because they seek nominal damages. But this Court has made clear that nominal damages alone cannot preserve standing where the underlying claim has become moot. In *Flanigan’s*, this Court held that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” 868 F.3d at 1258. The Court emphasized that “nominal damages are not a substitute for a live controversy” and that “the mere possibility of a future violation, without more, is insufficient to sustain standing.” Id. at 1259. The same principle applies here. Appellants do not allege any ongoing or imminent threat of enforcement of the prior policy. Uzuegbunam has graduated, and neither he nor Bradford has attempted to engage in expressive activity on campus since the incident in 2016. Their desire to speak is not a present intent to act, but a hypothetical future interest that cannot sustain jurisdiction. *See* *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992) (standing requires a concrete and particularized injury that is actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical).

Appellants’ attempt to distinguish *Flanigan’s* by asserting an “as-applied” challenge is unavailing. The Eleventh Circuit correctly noted that Appellants failed to raise this argument below, and that even if they had, the distinction is legally irrelevant. *Flanigan’s* explicitly rejected the notion that an as-applied challenge can avoid mootness when the challenged conduct has ceased and the policy has been permanently revised. 868 F.3d at 1258. The Court stated: “The fact that a plaintiff challenges a law as applied to him does not prevent the case from becoming moot if the challenged conduct has ceased and the law has been changed.” Id. The same is true here: the conduct at issue—police intervention based on the prior policy—has ceased, and the policy has been replaced. The “as-applied” nature of the claim does not resurrect a dead controversy.

Finally, Appellants argue that the district court abused its discretion by denying their request to amend the complaint to seek compensatory damages. But the request was procedurally improper. Appellants sought leave to amend in their response to a motion to dismiss, not in a separate motion as required by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (“[W]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.”). Moreover, they failed to specify the substance of the proposed amendment or attach a proposed pleading, as required by *Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018). The district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to consider a request made in the wrong procedural posture. Even if the court had considered it, the proposed amendment would not have cured the mootness problem, as the underlying injury—enforcement of the prior policy—has ceased.
