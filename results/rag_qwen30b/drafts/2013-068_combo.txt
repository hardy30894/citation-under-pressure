**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law as a valid time, place, and manner regulation under the First Amendment, as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest in ensuring safe and unimpeded access to reproductive health care facilities, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. The petitioners’ challenge fails under settled precedent, which affirms that the government may regulate expressive activity in public forums—such as sidewalks adjacent to clinics—so long as the regulation is justified by a substantial interest and does not unduly burden speech.

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT PERMITS REASONABLE REGULATIONS OF PUBLIC FORUMS TO PROTECT ACCESS TO HEALTHCARE FACILITIES**

The sidewalks and public ways adjacent to abortion clinics are quintessential public forums, where the public has long exercised the right to speak, assemble, and petition. *Amalgamated Food Employees Union Local 590 v. Logan Valley Plaza, Inc.*, 391 U.S. 308, 314 (1968). This Court has repeatedly recognized that such spaces are “quintessential” public forums, where the government may regulate conduct to prevent obstruction, ensure public safety, and protect the rights of others. *Id.* at 315. The Massachusetts law does not ban speech; it merely restricts the physical proximity of speakers to clinic entrances, exits, and driveways—areas where patients and staff must pass. This is a classic time, place, and manner regulation, justified by the state’s compelling interest in protecting the free and unimpeded ingress and egress of individuals seeking medical care.

The state’s interest in ensuring safe access to health care facilities is both substantial and legitimate. *See* *Patterson v. Colorado Ex Rel. Attorney General of Colo.*, 205 U.S. 454, 462 (1907) (recognizing that the Fourteenth Amendment protects the rights of citizens to free speech and press as attributes of national citizenship, and that states may not abridge those rights). The Massachusetts legislature reasonably concluded that the presence of persistent, close-proximity counseling—particularly when combined with loudspeakers, large signs, and confrontational messages—creates a coercive atmosphere that may deter individuals from entering clinics. The law is designed to prevent such intimidation and to ensure that patients may enter and exit clinics without fear or harassment. This interest is no less compelling than the state’s interest in protecting access to courthouses, which this Court has long upheld. *See* *Amalgamated Food Employees*, 391 U.S. at 316 (upholding regulation of picketing near courthouses to prevent obstruction of free ingress and egress).

**II. THE LAW IS CONTENT-NEUTRAL AND NARROWLY TAILORED TO SERVE A SIGNIFICANT GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST**

The Massachusetts buffer zone law is content-neutral and viewpoint-neutral. It applies equally to all individuals regardless of the message they seek to convey. The law prohibits no speech, only the physical proximity of speakers to clinic entrances. This is precisely the type of regulation this Court has upheld in similar contexts. *See* *Bi-Metallic Investment Co. v. State Board of Equalization*, 239 U.S. 441, 445 (1915) (holding that a state may regulate property valuation without violating the Fourteenth Amendment so long as the regulation is not arbitrary or discriminatory). The law’s neutrality is further confirmed by the fact that it applies to all persons, including those engaged in pro-life counseling, pro-choice advocacy, and even law enforcement or medical personnel.

The law is also narrowly tailored. It creates a fixed 35-foot buffer zone—less than half the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which the First Circuit correctly cited as precedent. The buffer zone is not so broad as to eliminate all meaningful communication. As the First Circuit observed, the plaintiffs retain ample alternative channels: oral speech, distribution of literature, display of signs and symbols, prayer, and wearing of expressive garments. *See* *Amalgamated Food Employees*, 391 U.S. at 315 (recognizing that reasonable regulation of public forums does not violate the First Amendment if alternative channels remain open). The fact that some individuals may be less effective at a distance does not render the law unconstitutional. *See* *Selover, Bates & Co. v. Walsh*, 226 U.S. 112, 138 (1912) (upholding a statute that required notice and redemption periods, even where it affected contractual rights, because it was within the legislature’s power and not arbitrary).

**III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RE-LITIGATION OF THE FACIAL CHALLENGE**

The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *See* *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). This doctrine prevents relitigation of issues already decided in the same case. *Id.* The First Circuit previously upheld the law in *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009), and the Supreme Court denied certiorari. *McCullen v. Coakley*, — U.S. —, 130 S.Ct. 1881, 176 L.Ed.2d 362 (2010). The petitioners have not shown any of the narrow exceptions to the doctrine—no change in controlling legal authority, no new evidence, and no “blatant error” that would result in a miscarriage of justice. *Id.* at 14.
