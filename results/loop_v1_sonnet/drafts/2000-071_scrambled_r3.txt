# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

Streets and parks have been held to belong to the public since time out of mind for purposes of assembly and communication, but a school building stands on a different footing. *See Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to "social, civic and recreational" activities benefiting the community. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. This Court has recognized that the government, in its capacity as property owner, may control the use of its property according to the purpose for which that property is lawfully dedicated, just as a private owner may regulate the use of his own land. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). A government need not open its property to every use citizens might wish to make of it merely because it has opened that property for some limited purposes. *Id.* This authority to condition access to public property according to its dedicated purpose was recognized as early as *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897), where the Court sustained a municipal ordinance restricting public speaking on the Boston Common, holding that the legislature's power to control the use of public grounds is analogous to the power an owner has over the use of his own land. Milford may likewise exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. This Court has long recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property for assembly is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not employed as a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (sustaining a licensing scheme governing the use of public streets for parades and processions). Milford's Community Use Policy operates the same way: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. In *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), the Court struck down school-sponsored Bible readings and prayer recitations conducted on school premises during the school day, explaining that the government must maintain a position of neutrality, "neither aiding nor opposing religion." *Id.* at 222.

The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, and directed at the same young children who attend that school during the day. As the Court held in barring a program of religious instruction conducted by outside instructors within public school classrooms during the school day, the use of the public school system to aid religious groups in dispensing their doctrines to students offends the Establishment Clause even where attendance is not formally compelled. *See Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 209–10 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands that "[g]overnment in our democracy, state and national, must be neutral in matters of religious theory, doctrine, and practice." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Schempp* and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
