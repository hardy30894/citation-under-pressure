**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sentencing Enhancement for Firearm Possession Does Not Transform Roussos’ Drug Conspiracy Conviction into a “Crime of Violence” Under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)**

Roussos’ conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846—conspiracy to distribute narcotics—is not a “crime of violence” as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The BOP’s contrary conclusion rests on an erroneous interpretation of § 924(c)(3) and Program Statement 5162.02, both of which improperly conflate a sentencing enhancement with the underlying offense’s character.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that sentencing enhancements do not alter the nature of the predicate offense. In *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), the Court held that for purposes of the Armed Career Criminal Act, a sentencing enhancement for prior violent felonies did not retroactively transform an otherwise non-violent offense into a violent one. *Id.* at 602. Similarly, in *United States v. McAllister*, 27 F.3d 105 (3d Cir. 1994), this Court rejected the argument that a § 924(c) enhancement could reclassify a drug offense as a crime of violence, emphasizing that the enhancement addresses conduct *related* to the offense, not the offense itself. *Id.* at 107.

Here, the sentencing court’s two-level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1) for firearm possession was based on conduct *collateral* to the conspiracy—namely, the presence of firearms in Roussos’ residence. As the Third Circuit has recognized, such enhancements do not alter the legal or factual nature of the underlying offense. *See United States v. Palma-Rivera*, 947 F.2d 62 (3d Cir. 1991) (holding that a § 2D1.1(b)(1) enhancement does not convert a drug offense into a crime of violence). The BOP’s reliance on Program Statement 5162.02, which categorically labels § 846 convictions as crimes of violence if a firearm enhancement was applied, is therefore inconsistent with controlling precedent.

**II. The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 Exceeds Its Statutory Authority and Violates the Plain Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B)**

The BOP’s interpretation of “nonviolent offense” in § 3621(e)(2)(B) is entitled to deference only to the extent it is reasonable and consistent with the statute’s text. *See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984). Here, the Program Statement impermissibly expands the definition of “crime of violence” beyond the statutory bounds set by § 924(c)(3).

The Third Circuit has made clear that statutory terms must be given their ordinary meaning unless Congress has defined them otherwise. *See United States v. Sczubelek*, 402 F.3d 175 (3d Cir. 2005). Section 924(c)(3) requires an offense to either (1) have force as an *element*, or (2) by its *nature* pose a substantial risk of force. *Id.* at 180. The mere fact that a sentencing enhancement was applied for firearm possession does not satisfy either prong. As the Supreme Court held in *Leocal v. Ashcroft*, 543 U.S. 1 (2004), a sentencing enhancement for a collateral act does not transform the underlying offense into one involving force. *Id.* at 11.

The BOP’s Program Statement, by contrast, treats *any* § 846 conviction with a firearm enhancement as a crime of violence, regardless of whether the offense inherently involved violence. This categorical approach conflicts with the statute’s text and prior judicial interpretations. *See United States v. Orellana-Blanco*, 294 F.3d 1143 (9th Cir. 2002) (rejecting BOP’s attempt to classify drug offenses as crimes of violence based solely on sentencing enhancements). The Third Circuit should similarly reject this overbroad interpretation.

**III. The BOP’s Action Violates the Administrative Procedure Act’s Requirement of Reasoned Decisionmaking**

Even if the Program Statement were entitled to deference, it would still fail under the APA’s arbitrary-and-capricious standard. *See 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A)*. The BOP’s refusal to distinguish between offenses that inherently involve violence and those where a firearm was merely present during sentencing is not a reasoned exercise of discretion. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29 (1983) (requiring agencies to consider relevant factors and articulate a rational connection between facts and policy).

Here, the BOP offers no justification for its blanket rule, which sweeps in offenses like Roussos’ where the firearm was not used or threatened. This lack of reasoned decisionmaking renders the Program Statement invalid. *See City of Arlington v. FCC*, 569 U.S. 290 (2013) (agency action must be supported by reasoned analysis).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the BOP’s denial of Roussos’ early release eligibility is contrary to law. The sentencing enhancement does not transform his § 846 conviction into a crime of violence, and the BOP’s Program Statement exceeds its statutory authority. The district court’s judgment should be reversed, and Roussos should be granted relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2241.
