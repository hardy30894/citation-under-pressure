**ARGUMENT**

**I. The search warrant lacked probable cause because the affidavit failed to establish a nexus between Joubert’s parents’ home and evidence of sexual exploitation.**

A warrant may issue only upon a showing of probable cause to believe that evidence of a crime will be found at the place to be searched. *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 114 (1964). The affidavit must provide a “substantial basis” for concluding that the sought evidence is likely to be located at the specified premises. *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410, 415 (1969). While the affiant stated that Joubert disassembled a computer at his parents’ home, the affidavit contained no facts indicating that any incriminating material remained there at the time of the search. *See Sgro v. United States*, 287 U.S. 206, 210–11 (1932) (staleness undermines probable cause). The officer’s bare assertion that “persons engaged in molestation often maintain” evidence is insufficient to bridge the gap between Joubert’s past conduct and the specific residence. *United States v. Ventresca*, 380 U.S. 102, 108 (1965) (warrant must be supported by particular facts, not conclusory statements). Because the warrant lacked any nexus to the home, the search violated the Fourth Amendment. *Bringgar v. United States*, 338 U.S. 160, 175–76 (1949).

**II. The district court abused its discretion by admitting testimony of uncharged child molestation, which was impermissible propensity evidence.**

Evidence of other crimes is inadmissible to prove a defendant’s character or criminal propensity. *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475–76 (1948). The testimony from other alleged victims—of incidents never charged—served only to portray Joubert as a habitual pedophile, thereby prejudicing the jury. *Greer v. United States*, 245 U.S. 559, 561 (1918) (prejudicial effect of other-crimes evidence must be closely guarded). No recognized exception justified this evidence; it was not relevant to motive, intent, or plan, as the government’s sole purpose was to show that Joubert acted in conformity with a pattern. *Boyd v. United States*, 142 U.S. 450, 456–57 (1892) (propensity evidence violates fundamental fairness). The district court’s admission of this highly inflammatory testimony was an abuse of discretion requiring reversal.

**III. Congress’s application of 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert’s intrastate conduct exceeds its Commerce Clause power.**

The federal commerce power is limited to “commerce with foreign nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. The production and possession of a VHS tape entirely within New Hampshire—without any proof of interstate movement—falls outside this authority. *Kidd v. Pearson*, 128 U.S. 1, 20–22 (1888) (intrastate manufacturing not subject to federal regulation). Congress cannot regulate purely local activities that do not substantially affect interstate commerce. *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 546–48 (1935) (local poultry sales beyond commerce power); *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 303–06 (1936) (mining local activity). The government offered no evidence that the tape moved in interstate commerce or that Joubert’s conduct had any economic impact on interstate markets. To uphold such an application would eviscerate the distinction between intrastate and interstate activity that the Founding Fathers carefully preserved. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1, 194–95 (1824) (commerce power defined). The statutes therefore cannot constitutionally be applied to Joubert.

**IV. The 480-month sentence is substantively unreasonable and violates the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that punishment be proportionate to the crime. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 368–73 (1910) (sentence of fifteen years for falsifying document held disproportionate). A forty-year sentence for non-violent offenses involving a single victim is grossly disproportionate to both the gravity of the conduct and Joubert’s personal circumstances. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100–01 (1958) (Eighth Amendment embodies “evolving standards of decency”). The district court failed to weigh Joubert’s advanced age, lack of prior convictions, and the non-violent nature of the acts, imposing a sentence that far exceeds any legitimate penological interest. The sentence must be vacated as unreasonable.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the conviction should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial, or, in the alternative, the sentence should be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing.
