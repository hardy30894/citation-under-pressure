**ARGUMENT**

The case is moot. Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement demands that a live dispute persist throughout litigation. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). When the challenged policy is repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to it, federal courts lack jurisdiction. *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct” while the suit was pending, and Uzuegbunam graduated. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages. That is insufficient to avoid mootness.

**I. The Policy Change Extinguished All Ongoing Controversy**

A plaintiff must demonstrate that a live controversy is definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests. *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). Once the law that allegedly caused injury has been repealed, no controversy remains. *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893) (moot when “the act complained of” has been “abandoned”). The college’s new policy permits expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged code-of-conduct provision has been removed. There is no reasonable expectation that the college will reimpose the prior restrictions. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953) (voluntary cessation moots case if “there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated”). Because the policy is gone, both the facial and as-applied challenges are moot.

**II. Nominal Damages Do Not Revive a Moot Case**

A plaintiff cannot maintain a suit after the right to relief has been extinguished simply by requesting nominal damages. *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653. Nominal damages are a technical remedy that presupposes a live controversy; they cannot create one where none exists. *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943) (per curiam) (moot after sentence served and no collateral consequences). The Court has consistently held that once the underlying dispute ends, the case must be dismissed even if nominal damages are sought. *Brownlow v. Schwartz*, 261 U.S. 216, 218 (1923) (moot when “the subject-matter of the controversy has disappeared” and no “actual controversy” remains, despite claim for damages). The only exception is when the plaintiff has suffered a concrete, continuing injury. *Carafas v. LaVallee*, 391 U.S. 234, 237–38 (1968) (collateral consequences from conviction prevent mootness). Here, the complaint alleged no actual injury—only an abstract violation. Abstract injury, without more, cannot sustain a case after the policy is gone.

**III. The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The voluntary cessation doctrine can avoid mootness only if the defendant is “free to return to its old ways.” *W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 632. But the college’s policy change was a formal, permanent revision, not a temporary retreat. *Brownlow*, 261 U.S. at 218 (moot when challenged condition “has been removed” and no probability of recurrence). There is no “reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated.” *W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 633. Because the college’s action was unambiguous and permanent, the case is moot.

**Conclusion**

Because the challenged policies were repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to them, the case is moot. A prayer for nominal damages cannot supply the ongoing controversy required by Article III. The district court properly dismissed the complaint.
