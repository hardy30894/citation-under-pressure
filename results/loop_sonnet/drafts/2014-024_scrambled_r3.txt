**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that the requirement of intent as a predicate for criminal punishment is deeply rooted in the common law and reflects a considered judgment that culpability ordinarily depends on a blameworthy state of mind. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement, not to dispense with it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter, such as regulatory violations involving inherently dangerous or heavily regulated conduct. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from the regulatory context of *Balint*; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court read a "willfully" requirement into a federal civil-rights statute to require that the defendant act "in reckless disregard of constitutional prohibitions or guarantees fairly implied from the fact that they are so often expressed and understood," 325 U.S. at 105, precisely to avoid punishing defendants for conduct they did not appreciate as unlawful. Likewise, this Court has instructed that criminal statutes touching on protected liberty must be construed narrowly, resolving doubt against extending criminal liability by inference. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952) ("the vagueness may not have the ambiguity of prophecy" and courts should not "make it a criminal offense" absent clear congressional intent). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." Only "no essential part of any exposition of ideas" — narrow, historically unprotected categories of speech — fall outside the First Amendment's shelter, and such categories must be defined with precision. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). This Court's decision in *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242 (1937), confirms that the inquiry cannot rest on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous or provocative language. There, the Court reversed a conviction for inciting insurrection because the State's theory allowed the jury to find guilt based on the predictable reaction of some listeners to the defendant's words, without requiring proof that the defendant himself intended to bring about the unlawful result. *Id.* at 259–263. The Court's focus was on the speaker's own purpose in communicating, not merely on how the message might be received by an audience.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression — dark humor, hyperbole, rap lyrics, venting — because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the First Amendment demands "breathing space" for such expression and that laws touching speech must not be construed to sweep protected activity within their reach merely because some hearers might take offense or alarm. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Where scienter requirements are absent, "the ordinance tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter," because a speaker of ordinary prudence will steer far wider of the forbidden zone than the Constitution requires. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959); *see also Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958) (recognizing that uncertainty as to legal consequences "will inevitably cause" speakers "to steer far wider of the unlawful zone"). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before punishment may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951) (requiring proof that speech was intended to produce unlawful ends before liability may be imposed).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense — punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952); *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–102 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
