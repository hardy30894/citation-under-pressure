The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. As the Supreme Court held in Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), interpreting the statute to permit indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns. To avoid these concerns, the Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, beyond which detention is no longer authorized.

The Court's holding in Zadvydas is grounded in the principle that indefinite detention of aliens who have been admitted to the United States but subsequently ordered removed raises significant due process concerns. The Court noted that the statute "does not apply narrowly to 'a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,' ... but broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations." Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 691. This broad application, combined with the lack of a clear time limit on detention, led the Court to conclude that the statute must be read to contain a reasonable time limitation.

The Court established a presumptively reasonable period of detention of six months, after which the government must provide evidence sufficient to rebut an alien's showing that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 701. As the period of post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the "reasonably foreseeable future" conversely shrinks. Id.

In this case, the petitioner has been detained for an extended period beyond the 90-day removal period, and efforts to remove him have been unsuccessful. Under the Supreme Court's holding in Zadvydas, the government must demonstrate that there is a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future to justify continued detention. The government has failed to make such a showing, and therefore, the petitioner's continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.

The Fifth Circuit's prior decision in this case, which held that the government may detain a resident alien based on danger to the community or risk of flight while good faith efforts to effectuate the alien's deportation continue, is no longer controlling in light of the Supreme Court's decision in Zadvydas. The Supreme Court rejected a standard that would require an alien to show the absence of any prospect of removal, holding that this demands more than the statute can bear. Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 702.

In conclusion, the post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court's holding in Zadvydas establishes that detention is only authorized for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien's removal, and the government has failed to demonstrate that there is a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Therefore, the petitioner's continued detention is unlawful, and he should be released subject to supervised release conditions.
