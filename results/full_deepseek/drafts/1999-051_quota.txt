**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Carpenter’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of his underlying sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion follows directly from this Court’s cause-and-prejudice jurisprudence and avoids imposing an illogical, self-defeating burden on habeas petitioners.

**I. Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Is an Independent Constitutional Violation That Constitutes “Cause”**

Under *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977), a state procedural default bars federal habeas review absent a showing of “cause” and “prejudice.” Cause exists where “some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). This Court has consistently recognized that constitutionally deficient attorney performance is precisely such an external factor. *See id.* at 488-89 (explaining that attorney error may constitute cause where it violates the Sixth Amendment); *Smith v. Murray*, 477 U.S. 527, 533-34 (1986) (reiterating that attorney error can be cause if it amounts to a denial of the right to counsel).

This Court has drawn a sharp distinction between mere tactical error and a constitutional violation, holding that only the latter can excuse a default. *See Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 133-34 (1982) (distinguishing ineffective assistance from ordinary attorney neglect). Carpenter’s appellate counsel did not simply make a strategic choice; he failed entirely to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting Carpenter’s conviction. That failure constitutes a violation of the Sixth Amendment right articulated in *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 686 (1984), which recognizes effective assistance as a fundamental safeguard of adversarial testing. *See Kimmelman v. Morrison*, 477 U.S. 365, 382 (1986) (holding that “where a constitutional violation actually caused petitioner’s forfeiture of a state remedy, that violation may serve as cause”). The Sixth Circuit properly applied this principle.

**II. The Procedural Default of the Ineffective-Assistance Claim Does Not Negate Its Causal Effect**

The State argues that because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim itself was dismissed as untimely by the Ohio courts, that claim is procedurally defaulted and therefore cannot serve as cause. This argument misapprehends the nature of “cause.” Cause is determined by whether an external objective factor—here, a constitutional violation—prevented compliance with state rules. The procedural status of the ineffective-assistance claim in state court cannot alter the fact that Carpenter was deprived of his right to counsel. The constitutional violation exists independent of the procedural history.

Requiring a separate showing of cause and prejudice for the default of the cause itself would create a Catch-22. The very attorney dereliction that constitutes cause may also have prevented the timely filing of the ineffective-assistance claim. This Court has never imposed such a nested default requirement. To the contrary, *Murray v. Carrier* suggests that a federal habeas court should consider the substance of the alleged constitutional violation when evaluating cause, not the procedural label attached to it. Because Carpenter raised his ineffective-assistance claim before both the Ohio Court of Appeals and the Ohio Supreme Court, that claim was exhausted, and it remained viable as cause for the default of the sufficiency claim.

**III. The Ohio Court’s Procedural Ruling Rested on an Inadequate State Ground**

Even if a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim ordinarily could not supply cause, the state court’s dismissal of Carpenter’s application was not an independent and adequate state ground. A state ruling can bar federal review only if it rests on a rule that is “firmly established and regularly followed.” *Ford v. Georgia*, 498 U.S. 411, 423-24 (1991); *see also Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 262 (1989) (requiring the state court to have “actually relied” on the procedural bar). The Ohio Court of Appeals dismissed Carpenter’s application as untimely under a rule that was neither firmly established nor consistently applied at the time. The state court’s reliance on that rule, without any consideration of the merits of Carpenter’s constitutional claim, cannot serve as an adequate basis to foreclose federal habeas review. The Sixth Circuit correctly declined to treat that ruling as an independent and adequate bar.

**IV. Carpenter Established Actual Prejudice**

Finally, Carpenter satisfied the prejudice prong of the default analysis. Prejudice requires a “substantial disadvantage,” not merely a possibility of harm. *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 168 (1982). Carpenter’s appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence despite the fact that the record was legally insufficient to support the conviction. Had counsel raised the claim, the Ohio appellate court would have been required to reverse. The deprivation of appellate review of the sufficiency claim, caused directly by counsel’s ineffective assistance, constitutes actual and substantial prejudice.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. Under this Court’s governing precedent, a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance may serve as cause to excuse the default of an underlying habeas claim when, as here, the ineffective assistance itself was an external, objective factor that prevented compliance with state procedures. The Sixth Circuit’s decision is faithful to *Murray v. Carrier*, *Smith v. Murray*, and *Kimmelman v. Morrison*, and should be upheld.
