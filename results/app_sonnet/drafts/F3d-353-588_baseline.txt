**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. Summary judgment is proper only if "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). The Court views the evidence "in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party" and draws all justifiable inferences in that party's favor. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986); *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986).

**II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Epps's ADA and MHRA Claims Because Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled**

The ADA extends protection to a person who is "regarded as having" a substantially limiting impairment. 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C). This category covers an employer's mistaken belief that an employee has a substantially limiting impairment, or its mistaken belief that an actual, non-limiting impairment substantially limits a major life activity. *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). The MHRA is analyzed under the same framework.

The record here supports an inference that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. Chief Hardy's March 17, 2000 memorandum relied on Dr. Mirkin's degenerative-disk-disease diagnosis to conclude that Epps "would never be able to function as a regular officer." Hardy did not limit his assessment to a single task; he determined Epps could not perform the general duties—bending, squatting, and running—required of the department's officers as a class. Where an employer regards an employee as unable to perform a class of jobs rather than one position, the employee may proceed under the major life activity of working. *Murphy v. United Parcel Serv., Inc.*, 527 U.S. 516, 523 (1999) (an individual is regarded as substantially limited in working only if regarded as significantly restricted in the ability to perform a class or broad range of jobs).

This Court has likewise held that an employer regards an employee as disabled when it terminates him based on a belief that his impairment prevents him from working in his chosen field. *Wooten v. Farmland Foods, Inc.*, 58 F.3d 382, 385 (8th Cir. 1995). Hardy's memoranda repeatedly invoked Dr. Mirkin's note that Epps would "likely need to seek a different occupation," and Hardy relied on that note—together with Epps's sick leave and workers' compensation filings—in recommending termination twice. A reasonable jury could find Pine Lawn terminated Epps because it believed his condition rendered him generally unemployable as a police officer, a class of jobs sufficient under *Sutton*, 527 U.S. at 491–92.

The district court's contrary conclusion resolved disputed facts against, rather than in favor of, Epps. Because a genuine dispute exists as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled, summary judgment was improper. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255.

**III. Sovereign Immunity Does Not Bar Epps's Statutory Workers' Compensation Retaliation Claim**

Missouri's sovereign immunity statute, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 537.600, governs common law tort claims against public entities; it does not extend to a statutory cause of action the legislature created without exception for public employers. Section 287.780 provides that "no employer shall discharge or in any way discriminate against any employee" for exercising workers' compensation rights—language drawing no distinction between public and private employers. The Missouri Supreme Court has recognized this provision as an independent statutory cause of action distinct from the common law torts to which sovereign immunity traditionally applies. *Hansome v. Northwestern Cooperage Co.*, 679 S.W.2d 273, 275 (Mo. 1984). Because § 287.780 reflects a legislative choice to protect all employees, including municipal employees, the district court erred in applying § 537.600 to bar Epps's claim.

**IV. The Dismissal of Epps's Intentional Infliction Claim Should Be Reconsidered**

Sovereign immunity does not shield intentional conduct undertaken outside an official's discretionary public duty. *Kanagawa v. State ex rel. Freeman*, 685 S.W.2d 831, 835 (Mo. banc 1985). Because Epps's ADA, MHRA, and retaliation claims arise from the same course of conduct by Hardy and the Board, and must be reinstated for the reasons above, this Court should remand the intentional infliction claim for reconsideration in light of its rulings on the other counts.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Epps respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
