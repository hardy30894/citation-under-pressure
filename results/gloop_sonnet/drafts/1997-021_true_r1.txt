ARGUMENT

I. THE BOARD MUST APPLY ITS "REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD IN A MANNER CONSISTENT WITH THE ACT'S CENTRAL COMMITMENT TO EMPLOYEE FREE CHOICE.

The National Labor Relations Act protects an employee's right to reject, as well as to select, union representation. This Court has insisted that mechanisms for testing employee sentiment must fairly permit expression of both preferences. In Brotherhood of Railway & Steamship Clerks v. Association for the Benefit of Non-Contract Employees, 380 U.S. 650 (1965), the district court "enjoined the Board from conducting an election with a ballot that did not permit an employee to cast a vote against collective bargaining representation," id. at 650, and this Court's review proceeded on the understanding that such a right is integral to the statutory scheme. A poll, no less than a Board-conducted election, exists to vindicate that same right. When the Board's polling standard is administered so restrictively that an employer can virtually never satisfy it, the practical effect is to deny employees any mechanism—short of an unfair-labor-practice gauntlet—for registering rejection of a union that has, in fact, lost majority support.

This concern is not academic. Recognition of a union as exclusive bargaining representative has always depended on the union's actual majority status among the employees it purports to represent, not on the mere assertion of that status. In United Mine Workers of America v. Arkansas Oak Flooring Co., 351 U.S. 62 (1956), the union claimed a right to recognition "because it was authorized by more than a majority of such employees to represent them," id. at 62–63, and the employer's obligation to bargain was understood to rise or fall with that majority fact, not with the union's say-so. Similarly, in La Crosse Telephone Corp. v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Board, 336 U.S. 18 (1949), this Court examined a dispute over which union, if any, employees had "chosen" as their "collective bargaining representative," id. at 18–19, underscoring that the Act's protections attach to the employees' actual choice. A polling standard so demanding that it forecloses employers from ever confirming that choice cannot be squared with these principles.

The Act's structure, moreover, reflects a considered balance between employer and employee interests that the Board may not disturb by administrative fiat. In Marine Engineers Beneficial Association v. Interlake Steamship Co., 370 U.S. 173 (1962), this Court traced how Congress calibrated the reach of collective-bargaining obligations to avoid compulsion inconsistent with the statute's purposes, noting Congress's purpose "to free employers from compulsion to treat supervisory personnel as employees for the purpose of collective bargaining." Id. at 173. The Board's application of the reasonable-doubt standard here—crediting only a fraction of the objective indicia an employer identified and discounting others through categorical rules—upset a comparable balance by making the employer's statutory right to test the union's status illusory.

II. THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED A GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

Even accepting the Board's own standard, its factual determination cannot stand. Judicial review of Board orders is not a rubber stamp; the courts of appeals sit to review Board action under the statutory grant of jurisdiction to "petition any court of appeals of the United States . . . for the enforcement of such order," and to entertain petitions by "[a]ny person aggrieved by a final order of the Board," 29 U.S.C. § 160(e)–(f), quoted in Boire v. Greyhound Corp., 376 U.S. 473, 476–77 (1964) n.2. That review authority is not confined to legal error; it extends to whether the Board's factual findings are supported when the record is examined as a whole. As this Court has explained, an agency's factual rulings are subject to review by the courts of appeals within the scope defined by Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), and Labor Board v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co., 340 U.S. 498 (1951); "[t]he correction of errors of law by a Court of Appeals on review of administrative agencies is committed to this Court through its certiorari jurisdiction." FCC v. Allentown Broadcasting Corp., 349 U.S. 358, 364 (1955). Universal Camera's "record as a whole" standard requires more than a search for isolated evidence supporting the Board; it requires a fair reckoning of all the evidence, including that which cuts against the Board's conclusion.

Measured against that standard, the Board's decision to discount the statements of Ridgick, Marsh, Bloch, and Mohr, and to exclude entirely the sentiments of employees no longer on the payroll at the critical date, cannot be reconciled with a fair reading of the whole record. An employer's duty to bargain under Section 8(a)(5), as this Court recognized in McLeod v. General Electric Co., 385 U.S. 533 (1967), is not owed in a vacuum; it is owed to a union that in fact commands majority support, and the Board's fact-finding process for testing that predicate must itself rest on substantial evidence, not on categorical rules that exclude probative indicia from consideration.

Nor can the Board's approach be justified by reference to the broader labor policy of the Act. See Retail Clerks International Ass'n v. Lion Dry Goods, Inc., 369 U.S. 17, 17 (1962) (referring to "the enforcement of the national labor policy"). That policy is served, not disserved, by ensuring that bargaining obligations track actual, not fictional, majority support—a principle reflected throughout this Court's duty-of-representation jurisprudence. See Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171 (1967).

Because the Board's finding rests on an artificially narrowed evidentiary base rather than the record as a whole, it fails the substantial-evidence test, and the judgment below should be reversed.
