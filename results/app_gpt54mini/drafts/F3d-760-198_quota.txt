## ARGUMENT

### I. The district court erred in limiting Moll’s hostile-work-environment claim to overtly sexual incidents and in failing to consider the totality of the evidence.

A hostile-work-environment claim under Title VII and the NYSHRL must be assessed by looking to “all the circumstances,” not by isolating only those acts that are overtly sexual. Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 23 (1993). This Court has squarely held that “facially neutral incidents may be included” in the hostile-environment analysis when they are part of the same course of conduct, and that harassment need not consist only of explicit sexual acts. Alfano v. Costello, 294 F.3d 365, 375-76 (2d Cir. 2002). A plaintiff may rely on conduct that is facially neutral so long as it is “sufficiently related” to sex-based hostility. Id. at 377.

Here, the district court incorrectly treated Moll’s claim as limited to the handful of explicitly sexual incidents and then dismissed the claim because no such “sexually offensive acts” fell within the limitations period. That approach conflicts with National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan, 536 U.S. 101, 115-18 (2002), which held that a hostile-work-environment claim is composed of a series of separate acts that may be considered together so long as one contributing act occurs within the filing period. Under Morgan, the court had to consider the entire pattern of conduct, including Irving’s exclusionary management practices, denial of work-from-home opportunities, disparate treatment in vacation approvals, refusal to permit email or telephone communications, and the exclusion of women from work-related events. See id. at 117; Alfano, 294 F.3d at 375-77.

The district court’s truncated analysis is especially flawed because hostile environment may be shown by conduct that is not overtly sexual but that reinforces sex-based animus. See Petrosino v. Bell Atl., 385 F.3d 210, 221-24 (2d Cir. 2004). Moll alleged a pattern beginning with sexually suggestive notes and calls, then continuing with differential treatment by male supervisors, unequal access to flexible work arrangements, and exclusion from opportunities afforded to male colleagues. Viewed collectively, these allegations are more than sufficient to permit a jury to find that her workplace was “permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult.” Harris, 510 U.S. at 21.

### II. Summary judgment on the retaliation claim was improper because Moll presented evidence of materially adverse conduct and causation.

Retaliation under Title VII requires proof that the employer took action that “well might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination.” Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). This Court has repeatedly held that retaliatory actions are not limited to ultimate employment decisions. See Hicks v. Baines, 593 F.3d 159, 164-65 (2d Cir. 2010). Moll alleged that after she complained, Verizon transferred her sales group, imposed restrictive reporting requirements, denied her work-from-home flexibility, and placed her under severe stress that culminated in disability leave. Those allegations easily satisfy Burlington Northern’s material-adversity standard. See 548 U.S. at 68; Hicks, 593 F.3d at 165.

The district court also failed to draw the reasonable inference of causation that the record permits. Temporal proximity can support an inference of retaliatory intent where the adverse action follows protected activity closely enough. See Gorzynski v. JetBlue Airways Corp., 596 F.3d 93, 110-11 (2d Cir. 2010). Here, Moll complained to management and the EEOC and thereafter experienced continuing adverse treatment, including the Syracuse transfer she reasonably understood as retaliatory. The sequence of events was sufficient to create a triable issue of fact. See Kaytor v. Electric Boat Corp., 609 F.3d 537, 550-51 (2d Cir. 2010).

### III. The Equal Pay Act and Title VII pay-discrimination claims should not have been dismissed or resolved against Moll on summary judgment.

The Equal Pay Act prohibits paying women less than men for work requiring equal skill, effort, and responsibility under similar working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 206(d)(1). A plaintiff may establish a prima facie EPA claim by showing that she performed substantially equal work for less pay than male comparators. Lavin-McEleney v. Marist Coll., 239 F.3d 476, 480-81 (2d Cir. 2001). Moll alleged that she was the lowest-paid sales engineer in the office while male counterparts with comparable duties earned more. That showing was sufficient to require Verizon to prove an affirmative defense. Id.

Title VII likewise reaches sex-based compensation discrimination, and the standards are not narrower than the EPA. Lenzi v. Systemax, Inc., 944 F.3d 97, 110-11 (2d Cir. 2019). By crediting Verizon’s explanations and rejecting Moll’s comparator evidence at summary judgment, the district court usurped the jury’s role. See Weinstock v. Columbia Univ., 224 F.3d 33, 42 (2d Cir. 2000).

### IV. The district court abused its discretion in applying the “sham issue of fact” doctrine to exclude the challenged testimony.

The “sham issue of fact” doctrine is “to be applied with great caution.” Palazzo ex rel. Delmage v. Corio, 232 F.3d 38, 43 (2d Cir. 2000). It bars a party from manufacturing a factual dispute by flatly contradicting earlier sworn testimony. Id. But the doctrine does not authorize a court to disregard a non-party witness’s later testimony merely because it conflicts with an earlier statement. See Rojas v. Roman Catholic Diocese of Rochester, 660 F.3d 98, 106-07 (2d Cir. 2011). Nor may a court make credibility determinations on summary judgment. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). To the extent the district court discounted the witness testimony on which Moll relied, it improperly resolved a credibility dispute that should have been left to the jury. See Jeffreys v. City of New York, 426 F.3d 549, 553-54 (2d Cir. 2005).

### CONCLUSION

The judgment should be vacated insofar as it entered summary judgment against Moll, and the case remanded for trial on her hostile-work-environment, retaliation, and pay-discrimination claims.
