Petitioner’s case turns on settled retroactivity principles. Congress does not lightly attach new legal consequences to completed conduct, and an administrative statute will not be read to upset settled expectations absent clear expression. Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 265-73 (1994); Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp., 488 U.S. 204, 208-09 (1988). The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), should therefore not be applied to a noncitizen who reentered the United States before the provision took effect. And even if the statute reaches that class, its application here is impermissibly retroactive because it attaches a new and severe disability to petitioner’s pre-enactment reentry and long-settled residence.

The starting point is the presumption against retroactivity. In United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413-14 (1806), the Court held that statutes are not to be construed to operate retrospectively unless Congress has clearly so directed. That principle has been repeated consistently. See Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 757 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.); United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) at 413-14. The rule reflects “the injustice of depriving a person of a right which he has acquired under existing laws,” and the “distinguishing and characteristic feature” of retroactive laws is that they “give a past event a different legal effect from that which it had when it transpired.” Soc’y for the Propagation of the Gospel, 22 F. Cas. at 757; Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87, 135 (1810).

Here, petitioner’s reentry occurred in 1982, long before § 1231(a)(5) became effective in 1997. Under the law then in force, a person who had reentered unlawfully could remain physically present in the United States and, if otherwise eligible, seek relief from deportation through available immigration procedures. See INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 321-24 (2001); Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70. Section 1231(a)(5), by contrast, commands reinstatement of the prior removal order and bars “any relief” under the Act. That is not a mere procedural change. It alters the legal consequences of petitioner’s earlier reentry and strips away a class of relief that was available when he made the conduct-based choice to remain and later regularize his status. See Londoner v. Denver, 210 U.S. 373, 385-86 (1908); Graham v. Goodcell, 282 U.S. 409, 429-30 (1931).

The government may argue that the statute regulates present presence rather than past conduct. But that contention fails where the present disability is triggered by a completed act and operates by changing its legal effect. In McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102, 123-24 (1898), the Court recognized that a statute is retroactive when it “takes away or impairs vested rights” or “imposes new duties, or attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past.” Likewise, in Railway Co. v. Grant, 98 U.S. 398, 401 (1879), the Court refused to construe legislation to disturb antecedent rights absent unmistakable language. Petitioner’s decades-long residence and his later marriage-based adjustment application were concrete reliance interests developed under the prior regime; the reinstatement bar obliterates them.

Nor does Congress’s use of broad language eliminate the retroactivity problem. General words do not ordinarily reach past events if doing so would work injustice or unsettle established rights. See United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) at 413-14; Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, 22 F. Cas. at 757. If Congress had intended § 1231(a)(5) to extinguish already-accrued opportunities for relief in cases of pre-1997 reentry, it needed to speak unmistakably. Bowen, 488 U.S. at 208-09.

Finally, the constitutional avoidance canon reinforces the narrower reading. See Murray v. The Charming Betsy, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 64, 118 (1804). A construction that would impose new legal consequences on completed conduct and bar all relief for persons who reentered years earlier should not be adopted where the statute fairly admits of a prospective-only interpretation. At minimum, petitioner’s case is the very sort of “new and burdensome consequences” situation against which retroactivity doctrine protects. Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, 22 F. Cas. at 757; Fletcher, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) at 135.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that § 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. If the Court concludes otherwise, it should further hold that applying the statute to petitioner is impermissibly retroactive because it attaches a new disability to a completed act and destroys relief that was available when petitioner acted. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-73; St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321-24.
