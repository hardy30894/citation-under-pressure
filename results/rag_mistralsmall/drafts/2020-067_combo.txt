**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Limited Its Review to the Trial Record When Assessing the Impact of Plain Error.

A reviewing court may correct plain error only if it “affected the defendant’s substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). To determine whether an error affected substantial rights, the court must examine whether the error “prejudiced” the defendant. *Id.* at 734. This prejudice inquiry is inherently record-bound; it requires assessing whether the error “deprived the defendant of a fair trial.” *Id.* at 736. The Eleventh Circuit therefore properly confined its analysis to the trial record, as it is the only source of evidence relevant to the fairness of Greer’s trial. *See United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (plain-error review requires assessing whether the error “resulted in a miscarriage of justice”); *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 240 (1940) (appellate courts must “guard against the use of the discretionary power to reverse for error not prejudicial”).

II. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err by Considering Greer’s Prior Convictions to Assess the Prejudicial Impact of the Error.

The Eleventh Circuit did not improperly expand its review beyond the trial record. It considered Greer’s prior convictions only to assess whether the error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial—a permissible inquiry under *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985). The court did not rely on these convictions as substantive evidence of guilt but rather to evaluate whether the error undermined the trial’s fundamental fairness. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982) (plain-error review requires assessing whether the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings”); *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62-63 (2002) (courts may consider the totality of the record, including sentencing materials, to assess prejudice).

III. The Eleventh Circuit’s Conclusion That Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice Is Supported by the Record.

Greer’s claim of prejudice rests on the absence of jury instructions regarding his knowledge of his felon status. However, the record shows that the jury was instructed on the elements of § 922(g), including possession of a firearm. *See Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) (a guilty plea must be knowing and voluntary, but a jury trial’s fairness is assessed by the instructions given). The error, if any, did not deprive Greer of a fair trial because the jury was still required to find the essential elements of the offense. *See In re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95, 102 (1895) (a conviction is valid if the record demonstrates the defendant’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt).

IV. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Abuse Its Discretion by Declining to Vacate Greer’s Conviction.

The court’s refusal to vacate the conviction was consistent with the narrow scope of plain-error review. *See United States v. Gryger*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948) (appellate courts must not “lightly” disturb convictions absent clear prejudice). The court’s analysis was confined to the trial record, as required by *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160, and its conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice was not an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly limited its review to the trial record and did not err in assessing the prejudicial impact of the error. Its judgment should be affirmed.
