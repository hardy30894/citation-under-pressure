**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and its reversal must be affirmed. The record in this case presents substantial evidence of mental impairment that should have triggered a good-faith doubt about petitioner’s capacity to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of fundamental constitutional rights. The trial court’s failure to conduct a competency hearing under the proper standard violated due process and rendered petitioner’s waiver and guilty pleas constitutionally infirm.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of constitutional rights—particularly the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial—must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). This standard is not satisfied by mere surface compliance with procedural formalities. When a defendant seeks to represent himself or change a plea to guilty, the court must ensure that the decision is the product of a rational and informed choice among available alternatives. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988). This requires a higher level of mental functioning than the standard for competency to stand trial.

The constitutional standard for competency to waive counsel or plead guilty is not merely a “rational understanding” of the proceedings, but a capacity for “reasoned choice.” *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (hereinafter *Evans II*). This standard demands that the defendant comprehend not only the nature of the charges and the potential penalties, but also the consequences of forgoing counsel, the risks of waiving the right to present mitigating evidence, and the long-term implications of a guilty plea. *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981). The court must assess whether the defendant can “weigh the advantages and disadvantages of the alternatives” and make a decision that reflects a “rational understanding of the consequences.” *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856.

In this case, the trial court failed to apply this heightened standard. Petitioner had recently attempted suicide, was hospitalized for a self-inflicted gunshot wound, and was still recovering when he appeared for his plea hearing. He was taking four psychotropic medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—each of which affects cognitive function, mood, and decision-making capacity. The record shows that petitioner responded to the court’s questions with monosyllabic answers, offered no independent explanation of his decisions, and stated only that he wanted to prevent any mitigating evidence from being introduced. His conduct was inconsistent with the kind of deliberate, reflective decision-making required by the “reasoned choice” standard.

The state court’s post-conviction finding of competency was based solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier to determine competency to stand trial. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975). Those evaluations were not designed to assess the higher level of mental functioning required for a valid waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. The psychiatrists did not evaluate the effects of medication on petitioner’s decision-making capacity, nor did they consider whether petitioner could rationally weigh the consequences of his choices. The state court’s reliance on these reports to support a finding of competency to waive constitutional rights was legally erroneous. *Evans I*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir.1973); *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518.

Moreover, the state court applied the wrong legal standard. It concluded that petitioner was competent because two psychiatrists had found him competent to stand trial. But the standard for competency to stand trial—“the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense”—is constitutionally distinct from the standard for waiver of counsel. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 171, 95 S.Ct. at 903. A defendant may meet the former without satisfying the latter. *Evans I*, 705 F.2d at 1480. The state court’s failure to apply the correct standard renders its finding of competency legally insufficient and subject to independent federal review. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 857.

The Due Process Clause mandates that a court initiate a competency hearing whenever there is a good-faith doubt about a defendant’s ability to make a reasoned choice. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973). Here, the record provided ample basis for such a doubt: recent suicide attempt, ongoing psychiatric treatment, medication use, monosyllabic responses, and a desire to foreclose all mitigating evidence. The court’s failure to inquire further violated due process. *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir.1981), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946, 102 S.Ct. 2013, 72 L.Ed.2d 469 (1982).
