**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

Petitioners respectfully submit that the government must bear the burden of persuasion to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant limitations period. This principle is firmly rooted in the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment and this Court’s precedent governing conspiracy prosecutions.

In *United States v. Gaudin*, the Supreme Court held that "[a]ny fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt." 515 U.S. 506, 522-23 (1995). Conspiracy liability under 18 U.S.C. § 371 is such a fact because it exposes a defendant to penalties beyond those prescribed for the underlying substantive offense. *See United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 15 (1994) ("[A] conviction for conspiracy under § 371 requires proof of an agreement to commit a crime, not the commission of the crime itself."). Thus, the government must prove the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt throughout the limitations period.

This Court has long recognized that "[t]he statute of limitations is a ‘fundamental safeguard’ that ‘rests upon the policy that the State should not indefinitely retain the power to punish." *United States v. Habig*, 390 U.S. 222, 226 (1968) (quoting *United States v. Lindsay*, 506 F.2d 166, 169 (D.C. Cir. 1974)). A defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the limitations period severs his liability for subsequent acts of co-conspirators. *See United States v. Hickey*, 391 F.3d 1069, 1075 (D.C. Cir. 2004) ("[A] conspirator who effectively withdraws from a conspiracy is not liable for substantive offenses committed by his co-conspirators after his withdrawal."). The government cannot shift the burden to the defendant to disprove his continued participation; rather, it must affirmatively prove that the conspiracy persisted into the limitations period. *See United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. at 15 ("The Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant conspired to commit an offense against the United States.").

**II. The Jury Instruction Misallocated the Burden of Persuasion**

The jury instruction in this case erroneously placed the burden on Petitioners to prove withdrawal from the conspiracy prior to the limitations period. This contravened settled precedent holding that the government bears the burden of proving all elements of the offense, including the duration of the conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt.

In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." The jury instruction here failed to require the government to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, instead implying that Petitioners bore the burden to disprove their participation. This misallocation of the burden of persuasion was reversible error. *See Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307, 313 (1985) ("[A] jury instruction that places the burden of persuasion on the defendant violates due process.").

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Continuing Conspiracy**

Even if the burden had been properly allocated, the evidence was insufficient to prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. The government’s case relied on evidence of pre-withdrawal acts, which cannot support a conviction for post-withdrawal conduct. *See United States v. Hickey*, 391 F.3d at 1075 ("[A] conspirator who effectively withdraws from a conspiracy is not liable for substantive offenses committed by his co-conspirators after his withdrawal.").

The government failed to present evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders, Moore and Gray, continued their operations after the limitations period. Without such proof, the jury could not reasonably conclude that Petitioners remained members of the conspiracy. *See United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. at 15 ("The Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant conspired to commit an offense against the United States.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully request that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand for further proceedings consistent with due process and this Court’s precedent.
