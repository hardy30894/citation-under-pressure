The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case undermines the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA by holding that a plaintiff's "actual knowledge" of a breach or violation requires more than just awareness of the underlying transactions. This interpretation is contrary to the plain meaning of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) and the case law of this Court and other circuits.

The statute provides that an action under Section 1104 may not be commenced more than "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly noted that ERISA does not define "knowledge" or "actual knowledge," but it failed to apply the correct interpretation of this phrase.

This Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "actual knowledge" in Section 1113(2), but the Ninth Circuit's own precedent provides guidance. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that "actual knowledge" is triggered by a plaintiff's knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law. Id. at 991-92. The court reaffirmed this holding in Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), stating that the "statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law." Id. at 1423 (alterations in original).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case deviates from this precedent by requiring that a plaintiff have actual knowledge not only of the transactions but also of their imprudence. This interpretation effectively reads the "actual knowledge" standard out of the statute, as it allows plaintiffs to claim a lack of knowledge of the breach or violation even when they have been provided with all relevant information.

The correct interpretation of "actual knowledge" is that it is satisfied when a plaintiff has knowledge of the underlying transactions that constitute the alleged breach or violation. This is consistent with the holdings of other circuits, which have held that "actual knowledge" requires only knowledge of the facts underlying the claim, not knowledge of the law or the alleged breach. See, e.g., Gluck v. Unisys Corp., 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992); Brock v. Nellis, 809 F.2d 753, 755 (11th Cir. 1987).

In this case, Intel disclosed all relevant information about the investments to Sulyma through various documents, including Fund Fact Sheets and other disclosures. Sulyma's claim that he did not read or recall reading these documents does not negate his "actual knowledge" of the breach or violation. The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary undermines the statute of limitations in ERISA and allows plaintiffs to delay filing claims indefinitely.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to enter summary judgment in favor of Intel.
