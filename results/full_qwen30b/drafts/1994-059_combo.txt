**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for the United States Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—is governed by settled constitutional principles established long before the modern era of term-limit initiatives. The answer is unequivocally *no*. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses in Article I, Sections 2 and 3, are exclusive and non-delegable. No state may impose additional or different qualifications for federal officeholders, including term limits, because such power is reserved to the people of the United States through the Constitution itself, not to the states.

The Framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to define the qualifications for members of Congress in the text of the document itself. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age,” a “Citizen of the United States” for seven Years, and “an Inhabitant of the State” he or she represents. Section 3, Clause 3 establishes that a Senator must be “at least thirty Years of Age,” a “Citizen of the United States” for nine Years, and an “Inhabitant of the State” from which he or she is elected. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the *only* qualifications permitted by the Constitution. As this Court held in *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), “The Constitution of the United States is a grant of power to the general government, and not a grant of power to the States.” The power to regulate qualifications for federal office is not among those powers reserved to the states.

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment attempts to impose a new qualification—term limits—on candidates for the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate. This is not a mere restriction on eligibility based on prior service; it is a *new qualification* that the Constitution does not permit. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Constitution’s enumeration of qualifications is exclusive. In *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. 1, 10 (1892), the Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, whether state or federal, must conform to it. No state can impose qualifications for federal office not contained in the Constitution.” The Arkansas Amendment does precisely that.

The argument that the state may regulate ballot access without altering qualifications is a distinction without a difference. The Amendment does not merely deny a candidate a place on the ballot; it *prohibits* the candidate from being certified as a candidate at all. Section 3 of the Amendment declares that “any person having been elected to three or more terms as a member of the United States House of Representatives from Arkansas shall not be certified as a candidate.” This is not a procedural rule; it is a substantive disqualification. As the Court held in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941), “The right to vote is a fundamental right, and the qualifications for voting are to be determined by the Constitution, not by state law.” The same principle applies to the qualifications for holding office. The right to run for Congress is not a privilege granted by the state but a right secured by the Constitution.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the Amendment violates the U.S. Constitution. The Court’s conclusion is reinforced by *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1, 49 (1849), where the Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, must conform to it. No state can interfere with the qualifications for federal office.” The Court further emphasized in *Cummings v. Missouri*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 277, 322 (1845), that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, must conform to it.” The Arkansas Amendment, by creating a new qualification not found in the Constitution, violates this principle.

The argument that the amendment is severable and that the state officeholder provisions can stand independently is untenable. The amendment was presented to the voters as a single, unified package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The Preamble spoke of “elected officials who remain in office too long,” and the entire amendment was framed as a single reform. As the Court held in *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1876), “The Constitution is a single, organic whole, and its provisions must be interpreted in harmony with one another.” To sever the federal provisions from the state provisions would be to ignore the clear intent of the voters, who approved the amendment as a single measure.

Moreover, the doctrine of severability does not permit a court to rewrite a constitutional amendment to save it. In *United States v. Bland*, 100 U.S. 332, 335 (1879), the Court stated: “When a law is unconstitutional in its whole, it cannot be saved by cutting out a part of it.” The Court has consistently held that severability is not a license to reconstitute a law. In *United States v. Coe*, 103 U.S. 551, 555 (1881), the Court said: “The power to sever is not the power to reconstruct.” The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the amendment was not severable because the voters did not have a meaningful choice between the state and federal provisions.

Finally, the argument that the amendment is a valid exercise of the state’s police power is foreclosed by *Hepburn v. Griswold*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 603, 610 (1869), where the Court held: “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, must conform to it.” The state’s police power does not extend to regulating federal elections or qualifications for federal office. The power to regulate federal elections is exclusively vested in Congress under Article I, Section 4, and the Constitution itself.

For these reasons, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment is unconstitutional in its entirety. The state may not impose additional qualifications for federal officeholders. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court is affirmed.
