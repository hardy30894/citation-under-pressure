The judgment below should be affirmed because Bond lacks standing to press a Tenth Amendment challenge to her conviction. The Supreme Court has held that a private party, absent the States or their officers, cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to invalidate federal action. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), the Court rejected a private utility’s federalism objection and explained that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment.” That holding controls here.

The Third Circuit correctly recognized that Tennessee Electric Power is not a throwaway remark. The Court decided the standing question before reaching the merits, and standing is a threshold jurisdictional issue. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95, 101-02 (1998). Because a federal court may not decide the constitutionality of a statute until it has ensured its own power to hear the claim, Bond cannot sidestep the standing bar by recasting her federalism objection as a merits argument. Id. at 101-02.

Bond’s contrary position is unsupported by Supreme Court precedent. The Court has consistently treated the Tenth Amendment as a structural limitation vindicable by the States, not as a roving cause of action for criminal defendants. In Tennessee Electric Power, the Court explained that “there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states,” and therefore the private plaintiffs lacked standing. 306 U.S. at 144. That principle fits this case: Bond is a private criminal defendant, not a sovereign State, and she seeks to assert a state’s reserved powers to defeat a federal prosecution brought under a duly enacted statute. See also Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945) (“the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States”).

The fact that some courts of appeals have permitted private Tenth Amendment challenges does not alter the answer. Decisions such as Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999), and Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. United States Department of Energy, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), cannot override the Supreme Court’s contrary instruction in Tennessee Electric Power. Other circuits have correctly adhered to that decision and denied standing. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004).

Nor does Bond gain standing simply because she was prosecuted under a federal criminal statute implementing a treaty. The source of Congress’s authority is irrelevant to the antecedent standing defect. Whether Congress acted under the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18, and the Treaty Power, U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2, does not create a private right to invoke the Tenth Amendment. Indeed, the Supreme Court has upheld treaty-implementing legislation as a valid exercise of federal power. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920); United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004). Bond may argue that § 229 reaches conduct that ought to be left to the States, but that is the very kind of federalism objection the Supreme Court has said a private party may not litigate.

Bond also cannot convert her personal grievance into a structural injury. Her complaint is not that the State of Pennsylvania’s sovereignty has been invaded; it is that she, individually, was prosecuted and convicted. But “undifferentiated, generalized grievance[s]” do not confer standing. Dillard v. Chilton County Commission, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007). The Tenth Amendment protects the federal balance between sovereigns, and the proper sovereign to assert that balance is the State. Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35.

Because Bond lacks standing to press her Tenth Amendment claim, the Court need not reach the merits of her federalism challenge. Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 94-95, 101-02. The conviction should be affirmed.
