The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. The statute's language and legislative history do not clearly indicate that it was intended to apply retroactively. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Supreme Court established a presumption against retroactive application of statutes, which can only be overcome by clear congressional intent. 

The Court has consistently applied this presumption in cases involving immigration statutes. For example, in United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), the Court emphasized the importance of clear congressional intent when interpreting immigration statutes. Similarly, in Harisiades v. Shaughnessy, 342 U.S. 580 (1952), the Court held that deportation statutes should be construed narrowly to avoid retroactive application.

Applying these principles to the present case, it is clear that Section 241(a)(5) should not be applied to Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the United States in 1982, well before the statute's effective date. The statute's retroactive application would attach new legal consequences to Fernandez-Vargas' pre-enactment conduct, namely his re-entry into the United States. This is impermissible under the principles established in Landgraf and subsequent cases.

Furthermore, even if the statute were intended to apply retroactively, its application to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive. The Court has held that a statute is impermissibly retroactive if it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." See, e.g., Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co., 231 U.S. 190 (1913). In this case, Fernandez-Vargas' re-entry into the United States was a completed event before the statute's enactment, and the statute's application would attach new legal consequences to that event.

The Tenth Circuit's decision to the contrary was incorrect. The court failed to properly apply the presumption against retroactivity and did not consider the potential new legal consequences attached to Fernandez-Vargas' pre-enactment conduct. As established in cases such as Chew Heong v. United States, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), and United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), immigration statutes should be construed to avoid retroactive application whenever possible.

Therefore, we respectfully submit that the judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
