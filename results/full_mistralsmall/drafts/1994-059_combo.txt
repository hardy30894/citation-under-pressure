**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution Are Exclusive and May Not Be Altered by the States.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly adds to the qualifications for membership in Congress set forth in the Constitution. Article I, Section 2, clause 2 provides that no person shall be a Representative who “shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. Article I, Section 3, clause 3 similarly provides that no person shall be a Senator who “shall not have attained to the Age of thirty Years, and been nine Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State from which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These clauses are exclusive; they “fix[] the qualifications required for membership in each house.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969). A state may not “add to or alter” these qualifications. *Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 U.S. 572, 575 (1928) (citing *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 480 (1917)). The Court has long held that “the qualifications for membership in Congress are prescribed in the Constitution, and cannot be added to by state action.” *Leser v. Garnett*, 258 U.S. 130, 137 (1922). Arkansas’s attempt to impose term limits on its federal representatives and senators thus violates the Constitution.

**II. The Term Limitation Amendment Is an Unconstitutional Attempt to Impose Additional Qualifications.**

The Arkansas Amendment does not merely regulate the electoral process; it alters the qualifications for federal office. By barring from the ballot those who have served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate, the Amendment creates an additional qualification for those offices. This is precisely what the Constitution forbids. As the Court explained in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 318 (1941), the power to regulate the “manner of holding elections” does not include the power to “establish qualifications for electors or candidates.” The Court has repeatedly struck down state-imposed qualifications for federal office. See *Cook v. Fortson*, 321 F.2d 517, 520 (5th Cir. 1963) (state cannot impose durational residency requirement for congressional candidates); *Davis v. Schnell*, 81 F. Supp. 872, 874 (S.D. Ala. 1949) (state cannot impose literacy test for federal candidates), aff’d, 339 U.S. 812 (1950). The Arkansas Amendment is no different.

**III. The Ballot-Access Mechanism Does Not Save the Amendment.**

Arkansas argues that its Amendment merely regulates ballot access rather than qualifications. This distinction is illusory. The Amendment does not merely make it more difficult to win an election; it makes it impossible for certain candidates to appear on the ballot at all. The Court has rejected this argument before. In *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23, 31 (1968), the Court held that a state cannot “exclude candidates from the ballot” under the guise of regulating elections. Similarly, in *Bullock v. Carter*, 405 U.S. 134, 143 (1970), the Court recognized that “the existence of filing fees and other requirements that result in a denial of access to the ballot” violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The Arkansas Amendment, by barring certain candidates from the ballot, is an unconstitutional qualification.

**IV. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Held That the Amendment Violates the Qualifications Clauses.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents. The state court recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that states may not add to them. See *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 312 Ark. 145, 160 (1993) (citing *Leser*, 258 U.S. at 137). The court also correctly held that the Amendment’s ballot-access mechanism does not evade the constitutional prohibition. *Id.* at 161 (citing *Williams*, 393 U.S. at 31). The Arkansas Supreme Court’s decision should be affirmed.

**V. The Severability Clause Does Not Save the Amendment.**

Even if the severability clause could operate, the unconstitutional portion of the Amendment is not severable. The Arkansas Amendment was presented to the voters as a single package, and the state cannot now claim that the unconstitutional federal provisions are severable from the constitutional state provisions. See *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. at 33 (rejecting severability where the unconstitutional provisions were “inextricably bound up with the legitimate provisions”). The Court has long held that where an unconstitutional provision is “so interwoven with the others that [it] cannot be struck out without destroying the symmetry of the plan,” the entire law must fall. *Chastleton Corp. v. Sinclair*, 264 U.S. 543, 547 (1924). The Arkansas Amendment is such a case.

**Conclusion**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment violates the exclusive qualifications set forth in the Constitution. The Amendment cannot be saved by the ballot-access mechanism or the severability clause. This Court should affirm the Arkansas Supreme Court’s decision invalidating the Amendment.
