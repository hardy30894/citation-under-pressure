The district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy’s removal and indefinite retention is clearly erroneous. Clear-error review requires reversal when the record leaves the reviewing court with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). Consent under the Hague Convention must be shown by a preponderance of the evidence, 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2), and must be “unequivocal.” Mozes v. Mozes, 239 F.3d 1067, 1076 (9th Cir. 2001). The evidence here establishes at most a conditional, temporary arrangement that Mena failed to honor.

Gonzalez-Caballero surrendered Danelsy’s documents and accompanied father and child to the airport only after Mena promised to assist her immigration and to return the child if that assistance did not materialize. Such conditional permission does not constitute the knowing, voluntary consent required by the Convention. See Friedrich v. Friedrich, 78 F.3d 1060, 1068 (6th Cir. 1996) (consent defense unavailable where custodial parent’s acquiescence was premised on unfulfilled conditions). Within days of Mena’s failure to call, Gonzalez-Caballero contacted Panamanian authorities, the police, and the Institute for Children, demonstrating that she had not acquiesced in permanent removal. See Ohlander v. Larson, 114 F.3d 1531, 1541 (10th Cir. 1997) (prompt pursuit of remedies negates inference of acquiescence).

The district court also misapplied the burden of proof. Once the petitioner establishes wrongful removal, the respondent must prove consent by a preponderance; mere ambiguity in the record does not satisfy that burden. Rydder v. Rydder, 49 F.3d 369, 372 (8th Cir. 1995). Mena’s testimony that the parties “concluded” Danelsy would have a “better life” in the United States falls short of proving an agreement to permanent retention, particularly where Gonzalez-Caballero’s contemporaneous statements and subsequent actions contradict any such understanding. See Feder v. Evans-Feder, 63 F.3d 217, 226 (3d Cir. 1995) (courts must examine the totality of circumstances, including post-removal conduct).

Finally, the court erred in treating Gonzalez-Caballero’s immigration request as ratification of indefinite retention. A parent’s willingness to relocate to the child’s new country does not waive rights under the Convention when the relocation is conditioned on the abducting parent’s cooperation. Walsh v. Walsh, 221 F.3d 204, 220 (1st Cir. 2000); Blondin v. Dubois, 238 F.3d 153, 160 (2d Cir. 2001). Because the record demonstrates neither unequivocal consent nor acquiescence, the petition for return should have been granted.
