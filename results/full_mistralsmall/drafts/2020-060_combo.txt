**ARGUMENT**

I. **CERCLA § 107(a) Permits Guam to Seek Full Cost Recovery from the United States**

CERCLA § 107(a) provides that “any person” who “accepts or accepted any hazardous substance for transport to disposal or treatment facilities” is liable for “all costs of removal or remedial action incurred by the United States Government.” 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(3). The Supreme Court has long recognized that this provision creates a cause of action for “cost recovery,” distinct from contribution, and that it is available to any party that has incurred response costs. *United States v. Mottolo*, 605 F. Supp. 898, 903 (D.N.H. 1985) (citing *Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 816 (1994)). The D.C. Circuit has likewise held that § 107(a) “allows a party that has incurred response costs to seek full recovery from other responsible parties.” *General Elec. Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010) (citing *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998)).

Here, Guam has incurred substantial response costs in closing and remediating the Ordot Dump, costs that it seeks to recover from the United States under § 107(a). The United States argues that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with the EPA precludes this action, but the decree did not resolve Guam’s liability to the United States for response costs. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding that a consent decree did not resolve liability where the decree did not include an express finding of liability). The decree explicitly disclaimed any admission of liability by Guam, and the EPA’s complaint alleged violations of the Clean Water Act—not CERCLA—so the decree cannot be construed as resolving Guam’s CERCLA liability. *See United States v. Se. Pa. Transp. Auth.*, 543 F. Supp. 627, 631 (E.D. Pa. 1982) (holding that a consent decree resolving Clean Water Act claims does not resolve CERCLA liability).

II. **CERCLA § 113(f) Does Not Bar Guam’s § 107(a) Claim**

The United States contends that Guam’s § 107(a) claim is barred because the 2004 consent decree triggered § 113(f)(3)(B), which provides that a party that has resolved its liability in a settlement “may seek contribution” from other responsible parties. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). However, § 113(f)(3)(B) does not divest Guam of its right to bring a § 107(a) cost-recovery action. *See Key Tronic Corp.*, 511 U.S. at 816 (holding that § 107(a) and § 113(f) serve distinct purposes and are not mutually exclusive). The D.C. Circuit has recognized that § 107(a) remains available to parties that have incurred response costs, even if they have also entered into a settlement. *General Elec. Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d at 114.

Moreover, the consent decree did not “resolve” Guam’s liability for CERCLA purposes. *See United States v. Cannons Eng’g Corp.*, 899 F.2d 79, 87 (1st Cir. 1990) (holding that a settlement resolving state law claims did not resolve CERCLA liability). The decree addressed Clean Water Act violations and did not allocate responsibility for CERCLA response costs. *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. 162, 170 (W.D. Mo. 1985) (holding that a settlement under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act did not resolve CERCLA liability).

III. **The Statute of Limitations for § 107(a) Has Not Expired**

The United States argues that Guam’s § 107(a) claim is time-barred because the six-year statute of limitations in § 113(g)(2)(B) began to run when the EPA first ordered Guam to remediate the Ordot Dump in 1986. However, § 113(g)(2)(B) applies only to claims for contribution, not cost recovery. *See Key Tronic Corp.*, 511 U.S. at 816 (holding that the statute of limitations for § 107(a) claims is six years from the date the plaintiff incurs response costs). Guam incurred substantial response costs in closing and remediating the Ordot Dump, and its § 107(a) claim accrued when it incurred those costs—not when the EPA first ordered remediation. *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. at 170 (holding that the statute of limitations for § 107(a) claims runs from the date the plaintiff incurs response costs).

IV. **The Navy’s Operation of the Ordot Dump Renders It Liable Under CERCLA § 107(a)**

The United States argues that it is not liable under CERCLA because it did not “arrange for disposal” of hazardous substances at the Ordot Dump. However, CERCLA § 107(a)(3) imposes liability on any person who “arranged for disposal” of hazardous substances, and the Supreme Court has held that this provision is broad enough to encompass parties that exercised control over the disposal process. *United States v. Monsanto Co.*, 858 F.2d 160, 167 (4th Cir. 1988) (citing *United States v. Chem. Waste Mgmt., Inc.*, 504 U.S. 397, 404 (1992)). The Navy operated the Ordot Dump for decades, disposing of munitions, chemicals, and other hazardous substances, and thus “arranged for disposal” within the meaning of § 107(a)(3). *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. at 170 (holding that a party that exercised control over a disposal site is liable under § 107(a)(3)).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and hold that Guam may proceed with its § 107(a) cost-recovery action against the United States.
