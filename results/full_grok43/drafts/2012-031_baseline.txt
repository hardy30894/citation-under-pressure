ARGUMENT

The district court abused its discretion by certifying a Rule 23(b)(3) class without requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate, by a preponderance of admissible evidence, that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement. See Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011) (certification requires “rigorous analysis” that “frequently … entail[s] some overlap with the merits”); In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig., 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008) (same).

Rule 23(b)(3) demands that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). Predominance fails when “individualized damages determinations” would overwhelm common issues. See Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013) (quoting 7A C. Wright, A. Miller & M. Kane, Federal Practice and Procedure § 1778, at 123–124 (3d ed. 2005)). A plaintiff must therefore “show that damages are capable of measurement on a classwide basis,” using a model that “measure[s] only those damages attributable to [the] theory” of liability that survives. Id. at 35.

Here the district court accepted only one theory of antitrust impact: that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilders. Yet plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. McClave, constructed a model that calculated overcharges from four distinct theories, three of which the court had rejected. The model therefore failed to isolate the sole remaining theory and could not establish that damages were measurable on a class-wide basis through common evidence. See Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 316 (plaintiffs must “demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class”).

The court’s acceptance of this mismatched model rested on clearly erroneous findings. Anderson v. Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573–74 (1985). No admissible evidence showed that the overcharge attributable solely to overbuilder deterrence could be quantified without individualized inquiries. Because the predominance requirement was not satisfied, certification was improper. See Dukes, 564 U.S. at 367 (class certification improper where proof of injury is not common).
