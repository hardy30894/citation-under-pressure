**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA—though no longer serving as a vehicle for the beneficiary’s retirement—remains exempt from creditors’ claims under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code. That holding misreads the statutory text, disregards controlling precedent, and undermines the fundamental purpose of the retirement exemption: to protect funds set aside for the debtor’s own future retirement. The correct interpretation, grounded in statutory construction, legislative intent, and binding authority, is that an inherited IRA does not qualify as “retirement funds” under the Bankruptcy Code because it is not held for the current owner’s retirement. The Court should reverse.

**I. The Statutory Text Requires That the Funds Be Held for the Debtor’s Retirement**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key phrase is “retirement funds,” a term not defined in the Bankruptcy Code. But the context makes clear that the exemption applies only when the funds are *intended for and used in connection with the debtor’s own retirement*. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary reading—treating funds as “retirement funds” simply because they once were—ignores the plain meaning of the statute and transforms a temporal exemption into a perpetual one.

This Court has long held that “the starting point for interpreting a statute is the language of the statute itself.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The phrase “retirement funds” is not a passive descriptor; it denotes a functional purpose. As the Court explained in *Barnes v. Ryan*, 568 U.S. 102, 108 (2013), “a statute’s meaning is determined by its text, read in context and in light of its purpose.” Here, the purpose of the exemption is to shield funds that are *currently* dedicated to the debtor’s future retirement, not those that were once so dedicated by a predecessor.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a trustee holding retirement assets for another is inapt. In *In re Bowers*, 727 F.3d 688, 692 (7th Cir. 2013), this Court held that a trustee’s interest in a retirement account is exempt *only if the funds are held for the benefit of the beneficiary’s retirement*. The trustee is not the beneficiary, but the funds are still used for retirement. By contrast, an inherited IRA is not used for the beneficiary’s retirement at all. It is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, designed to liquidate within five years or by age 70½, *whichever comes first*. 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A). The funds are not preserved for future use but must be distributed *before* the beneficiary reaches retirement age. Thus, the account lacks the essential economic attribute of a retirement fund: the ability to be held until retirement.

**II. The Seventh Circuit’s Interpretation Contradicts Binding Precedent**

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), is misplaced. Those decisions rest on a flawed premise: that because the funds were once “retirement funds,” they remain so. But this Court has repeatedly rejected such atemporal reasoning. In *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 100 (2000), the Court held that “a statute’s meaning cannot be determined by looking at the past status of an object, but rather by its present function.” The same principle applies here: the exemption turns on the *current* status of the funds, not their past.

Moreover, *In re Chilton* and *Nessa* conflict with this Court’s own precedent. In *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985), this Court held that “the exemption depends on the debtor’s interest at the time of filing.” A debtor cannot claim an exemption based on a prior interest. Similarly, in *In re Bowers*, 727 F.3d at 692, the Court emphasized that “the exemption is not based on the source of the funds, but on their use.” An inherited IRA is not used for the beneficiary’s retirement; it is used for current consumption. The Seventh Circuit’s rule would allow a debtor to claim an exemption for any asset that once served a protected purpose—such as a house once used as a homestead, or a farm tool once used in agriculture—contrary to *Barker* and *Bowers*.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Rule Undermines the Bankruptcy Code’s Purpose**

The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement exemption exists to prevent debtors from being forced to liquidate their life savings to pay pre-bankruptcy debts. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954) (noting that “the purpose of the law is to protect the individual from the harshness of the law”). But this protection is not unlimited. It applies only to funds that are *currently* reserved for the debtor’s retirement. If the exemption were to apply to inherited IRAs, it would allow debtors to shield funds that are *not* reserved for retirement—funds that must be distributed within a few years and can be spent on anything.
