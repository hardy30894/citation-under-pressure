ARGUMENT

I. Pakistan's Negotiated Exchange of Land Through Its Representative Constituted "Commercial Activity" Under the Restrictive Theory That Section 1605(a)(2) Was Designed to Codify.

The district court erred in holding that Pakistan's conduct was not "commercial activity" simply because the underlying dispute arose from an act of expropriation. While a bare expropriation is traditionally classified as a sovereign act, jure imperii, this Court should look to the long-established distinction between the public and private acts of foreign states — a distinction articulated well before the enactment of the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and which Congress intended the statute to preserve.

That distinction traces to Chief Justice Marshall's opinion in The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116 (1812), which recognized that a sovereign's jurisdiction, though "necessarily exclusive and absolute" within its own territory, may be qualified when the sovereign chooses to act outside the sphere of its governmental capacity. Id. at 136. That principle matured into the "restrictive theory" of sovereign immunity, under which a foreign state's purely governmental acts remain immune, but its acts of a commercial or private character do not. See Ex parte Republic of Peru, 318 U.S. 578, 588 (1943) (recognizing that the extent of a sovereign's immunity is a matter for determination according to established distinctions between public and private conduct); Republic of Mexico v. Hoffman, 324 U.S. 30, 35 (1945) (immunity depends on "the character of the act," not merely the identity of the actor).

This Court's predecessors applied that same distinction to hold foreign sovereigns amenable to suit where they engaged private parties in transactions of a commercial nature. In National City Bank of New York v. Republic of China, 348 U.S. 356 (1955), the Supreme Court permitted a suit against a foreign sovereign arising from its commercial dealings, explaining that a sovereign which avails itself of the ordinary machinery of commerce cannot thereafter claim the shelter of immunity to avoid the consequences of that participation. Id. at 361-62. Similarly, in Rich v. Naviera Vacuba S.A., 295 F.2d 24 (4th Cir. 1961), the Fourth Circuit recognized that once a foreign government enters into ordinary commercial dealings with private parties, it assumes the incidents of an ordinary commercial actor, including exposure to suit regarding those dealings.

The Second Circuit's opinion in Victory Transport Inc. v. Comisaria General de Abastecimientos y Transportes, 336 F.2d 354 (2d Cir. 1964), is particularly instructive. There, Judge Kaufman explained that courts should resist extending immunity beyond the narrow categories of unquestionably sovereign acts — internal administrative acts, legislative acts, acts concerning armed forces, diplomatic activity, and public loans — and should otherwise treat a foreign state's dealings with private parties as commercial in character. Id. at 360. Beg's allegations fit outside those narrow categories: after the initial expropriation, the Government of Punjab did not merely legislate or administer internally — it dispatched a representative to the United States to negotiate a substitute parcel of land with Beg, a private landowner, as though it were an ordinary party to a real estate transaction. That negotiated exchange, accepted by the Lahore High Court, was a transaction "commercial" in nature under the restrictive theory recognized in Victory Transport, Hoffman, and National City Bank.

II. Pakistan's Commercial Negotiations Conducted in the United States Establish the Requisite Direct Effect.

The district court further erred in concluding that Pakistan's conduct produced no direct effect within the United States. The negotiation of the substitute-land exchange was not conducted at arm's length from this country — Punjab's representative traveled to the United States to negotiate directly with Beg. That the negotiations culminating in the offer occurred on American soil, with an American resident as the counterparty, distinguishes this case from one in which all relevant conduct occurred abroad with only attenuated consequences felt here.

The Supreme Court's decision in Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), while primarily addressing the act-of-state doctrine, confirms that American courts retain authority to adjudicate the consequences of a foreign sovereign's expropriation where the matter is brought before them by a private litigant seeking redress, and that the foreign sovereign's own subsequent commercial conduct respecting the property may be considered independently of the initial taking. Id. at 428. Here, Pakistan's subsequent commercial conduct — negotiating a substitute conveyance through its representative in the United States — is precisely the kind of independent commercial activity carried on in this country that satisfies the jurisdictional requirement.

Finally, the historical retreat from the absolute immunity once recognized for state-owned commercial instrumentalities in Berizzi Bros. Co. v. S.S. Pesaro, 271 U.S. 562 (1926), confirms the trajectory of the law away from blanket immunity and toward the restrictive approach adopted in Hoffman, Ex parte Peru, National City Bank, Rich, and Victory Transport. That trajectory supports treating Pakistan's American-based negotiations as commercial activity carried on in the United States, sufficient to sustain jurisdiction.

For these reasons, the district court's dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction should be reversed.
