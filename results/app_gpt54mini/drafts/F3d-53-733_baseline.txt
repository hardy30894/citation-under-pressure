**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court’s admission of American Steamship’s “expert” cause testimony was reversible error.**  

A witness may offer opinion testimony only if “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” will assist the trier of fact. Fed. R. Evid. 702; *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589-95 (1993). Where a purported expert merely draws inferences from common experience and observed facts, the testimony is not expert in nature. *Fruhwirth v. Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Michigan*, 74 F.3d 154, 157-58 (6th Cir. 1996); *United States v. Kilgus*, 571 F.2d 508, 511-12 (9th Cir. 1978).  

Here, American Steamship’s witness was allowed to opine on the cause of the rope’s parting even though the causal issue required no specialized methodology beyond ordinary reasoning from the condition of the line, the rigging, and the events on deck. That sort of testimony usurps the jury’s function, because causation was a straightforward factual question the jurors could resolve themselves. See *Salem v. United States Lines Co.*, 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962) (expert evidence is improper where the jury can draw the inference without aid); *United States v. Thomas*, 74 F.3d 701, 706 (6th Cir. 1996).  

The error was prejudicial. The central disputed question was why the line failed, and the defense’s “expert” gave the jury an authoritative explanation for the accident. Where improper opinion goes to the core liability issue, a new trial is required. *United States v. Rea*, 958 F.2d 1206, 1216 (2d Cir. 1992); *McGowan v. Cooper Industries, Inc.*, 863 F.2d 1266, 1271 (6th Cir. 1988).  

**II. The court erred in denying Cook judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness.**  

A shipowner owes an absolute duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel and appurtenances reasonably fit for their intended use. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 549-50 (1960); *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100 (1944). Unseaworthiness is a condition, not a negligence inquiry. *Usner v. Luckenbach Overseas Corp.*, 400 U.S. 494, 498-99 (1971). When the evidence permits only one reasonable conclusion—that the vessel or its equipment was not reasonably fit—judgment as a matter of law is proper. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 250-52 (1986); *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946).  

The evidence here established that Cook fell because the line supporting his bos’n’s chair parted. A line used to suspend a seaman during loading operations must be fit for that purpose. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 549; *Mahnich*, 321 U.S. at 100. American Steamship offered no evidence that the line was reasonably fit when it failed under ordinary use. The ship’s later suggestions that Cook’s actions may have contributed to the accident do not answer the seaworthiness question; the vessel remained unseaworthy if its gear was defective or unsafe when put to the use for which it was intended. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550-51; *Ribble v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.*, 809 F.2d 1002, 1005 (3d Cir. 1987).  

The district court therefore should have granted Cook judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness. No reasonable jury could conclude that a line that parted during a routine lowering operation was fit for its intended purpose. *Mahnich*, 321 U.S. at 100; *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550.  

**III. The court properly refused to take comparative negligence from the jury.**  

Under the Jones Act, contributory negligence does not bar recovery but diminishes damages in proportion to fault. 45 U.S.C. § 53; *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). A directed verdict on comparative negligence is improper where the record contains evidence from which a rational jury could find some fault on the part of the seaman. *Rogers v. Missouri Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506-07 (1957); *In re Seaboard Shipping Corp.*, 449 F.2d 132, 137 (2d Cir. 1971).  

Here, the evidence permitted the jury to find that Cook was experienced, knew the conditions were severe, and participated in deicing and rigging operations under time pressure. The jury could reasonably infer that he failed to take some precaution that contributed to the accident. Because the record was not one-sided, the district court did not err in allowing comparative negligence to go to the jury. *Rogers*, 352 U.S. at 506; *Baker v. The Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 502 F.2d 638, 642 (7th Cir. 1974).  

**IV. Evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was admissible.**  

Relevant evidence is admissible if it has any tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable. Fed. R. Evid. 401, 402. A district court’s balancing under Rule 403 is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 174 n.1 (1997); *United States v. Abel*, 469 U.S. 45, 54 (1984). Evidence of a plaintiff’s medical condition is admissible where it bears on damages, credibility, or the cause and extent of claimed injury. *Bohannon v. Pegelow*, 652 F.2d 729, 731-32 (7th Cir. 1981); *Wilson v. Vermont Castings, Inc.*, 170 F.3d 391, 395 (3d Cir. 1999).  

American Steamship introduced evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments not to inflame the jury, but to show a preexisting medical history relevant to damages and alternative causes of claimed impairment. The district court acted within its discretion in admitting that evidence. *Abel*, 469 U.S. at 54; *United States v. Bonds*, 12 F.3d 540, 554 (6th Cir. 1993).  

Because the jury heard improper expert causation testimony, Cook respectfully requests that the judgment be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
