**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims. The record, viewed in Erdman’s favor, supports a reasonable finding that she satisfied the Act’s 1,250-hour requirement, and at minimum creates a genuine dispute of material fact on that point. Summary judgment was therefore improper. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009).

The FMLA protects an “eligible employee,” defined as one who has been employed “for at least 12 months by the employer with respect to whom leave is requested” and who has worked “at least 1,250 hours of service with such employer during the previous 12-month period.” 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The Act’s implementing regulations direct that the hours requirement be determined in accordance with the Fair Labor Standards Act’s principles, under which hours worked includes all time an employee is “suffered or permitted” to work. 29 U.S.C. § 207(g); 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c); *Brennan v. General Motors Acceptance Corp.*, 482 F.2d 825, 827-28 (5th Cir. 1973) (employer may not avoid compensation for work it knew or should have known was performed). Where an employer permits or acquiesces in off-the-clock work, those hours count. *Davis v. Food Lion*, 792 F.2d 1274, 1275-77 (4th Cir. 1986).

Here, the evidence supports a finding that Erdman regularly worked extra hours beyond her scheduled part-time and four-day workweek, with her supervisor’s knowledge and approval. Patty Sarno “consistently authorized payment for these hours or allowed Erdman to use them as ‘comp’ time.” That is classic evidence of hours “suffered or permitted” under the FLSA framework. *Brennan*, 482 F.2d at 827-28. Getgen’s own January 28, 2003 email confirms that she knew Erdman had been doing fieldwork and overtime-related work; Getgen did not deny the existence of the extra hours, but only later, on February 10, 2003, prospectively told Erdman that she could no longer use them for comp time. An employer cannot erase previously worked hours by changing policy after the fact. See *Lindow v. United States*, 738 F.2d 1057, 1062-63 (9th Cir. 1984) (hours actually worked are counted; policy labels do not control).

The District Court’s conclusion that Erdman had not accumulated enough hours improperly resolved a factual dispute against the nonmovant. Whether Erdman worked sufficient overtime, whether those hours were authorized or at least knowingly permitted, and whether they fell within the relevant 12-month lookback period were all contested. Those are questions for a jury. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248-50 (1986). Summary judgment is especially inappropriate where the employer’s own records and supervisors’ testimony support the employee’s account. *Reich v. Gateway Press, Inc.*, 13 F.3d 685, 688-90 (3d Cir. 1994).

Nationwide may argue that Erdman was a non-exempt employee and that some extra work was unauthorized. But the FMLA does not ask whether the employer wished to classify the time as payable overtime; it asks whether the employee actually worked the hours. The relevant inquiry is the hours “worked,” not the employer’s preferred scheduling label. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A); 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). And under FLSA principles, an employer’s knowledge that an employee is performing work is enough to charge the employer with those hours. *Davis*, 792 F.2d at 1275-77. Sarno’s and Getgen’s communications, combined with Erdman’s evidence of regularly worked extra time, easily create a triable issue.

Nor was summary judgment proper on Erdman’s FMLA interference and retaliation theories. An employer interferes with FMLA rights when it “interfere[s] with, restrain[s], or deny[s] the exercise of or the attempt to exercise” those rights. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1); *Callison v. City of Philadelphia*, 430 F.3d 117, 119-20 (3d Cir. 2005). Retaliation claims are likewise actionable where adverse action follows protected activity. *Potenza v. City of New York*, 365 F.3d 165, 167-68 (2d Cir. 2004). Erdman notified Nationwide that she would seek FMLA leave if August vacation were unavailable, then submitted formal paperwork for leave from July 7 to August 29. A human resources employee responded that “as far as the FMLA, I probably don’t see any problems with this.” Soon thereafter, Nationwide fired Erdman. A reasonable jury could infer causation and pretext from that sequence. *Farrell v. Planters Lifesavers Co.*, 206 F.3d 271, 279-81 (3d Cir. 2000).

In short, the record contains ample evidence that Erdman worked sufficient hours to qualify under the FMLA and that Nationwide terminated her after she sought FMLA leave. The District Court’s contrary ruling rested on resolving disputed facts and drawing inferences against Erdman, contrary to Rule 56. *McTernan*, 564 F.3d at 646. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
