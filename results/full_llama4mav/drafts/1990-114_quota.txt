The Missouri Constitution's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges at age seventy does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause.

First, the ADEA does not apply to Missouri's appointed state court judges because they are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). As the First Circuit held in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), judges engage in policymaking by filling interstices in the law and exercising discretion in decisionmaking. Missouri's judges, particularly those on the Supreme Court, exercise significant policymaking authority in supervising the court system and adopting rules governing the judiciary and the bar. See Mo. Const. art. V, Sec. 4; In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). The policymaking level exception is not limited to the executive and legislative branches, as the term "government" encompasses the judiciary. See EEOC v. Massachusetts, 680 F.Supp. 455, 460-61 (D.Mass. 1988).

The legislative history of the ADEA exemptions supports this interpretation, as Congress intended to exempt "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin.News 2179-2180. The state judiciary is a powerful "agency" of state government, and appointed judges are policymakers.

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision would not violate the Equal Protection Clause. The rational basis test applies, as age is not a suspect classification. See Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests, such as ensuring judicial competence and removing aged judges. See Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). Other courts have upheld similar provisions, finding them to be rationally related to legitimate state interests. See, e.g., Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980); Gregory v. Litton Systems, Inc., 472 F.2d 631, 632-33 (9th Cir. 1972).

The Supreme Court has consistently held that mandatory retirement provisions for judges are constitutional. See Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452 (1991); Murgia, 427 U.S. at 313. The Court has also recognized that states have a legitimate interest in regulating their judiciary. See United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971).

In conclusion, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is constitutional and does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the District Court should be affirmed.
