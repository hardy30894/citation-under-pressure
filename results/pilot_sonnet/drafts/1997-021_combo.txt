# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Rigid "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Cannot Be Reconciled with the Act's Purposes.

An administrative agency's chosen standard must rest on reasoned decisionmaking; it cannot simply reflect bureaucratic preference dressed as expertise. As this Court held in *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 332 U.S. 194 (1947), "the orderly functioning of the process of review requires that the grounds upon which the administrative agency acted be clearly disclosed." *Id.* at 196–97. So too in *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156 (1962), this Court refused to accept an agency's action absent "a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." *Id.* at 168. The Board's polling rule fails this test.

The Board requires an employer to possess the identical quantum of evidence to justify a poll, an RM election, or outright withdrawal of recognition—even though these three mechanisms carry vastly different risks to employees and different institutional safeguards. A poll conducted with procedural protections, of the kind approved in *Struksnes Construction Co.*, cannot rationally be held to the same evidentiary threshold as an employer's unilateral withdrawal of recognition, which forecloses bargaining altogether without any check on the employer's judgment. The Board's own remedial authority under the Act is not boundless; it must be exercised, as this Court explained in *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177 (1941), consistently with the statute's purposes, for the Board's power "is remedial, not punitive." *Id.* at 194. A standard that renders polling "marginally useful," in the D.C. Circuit's own words, cannot be squared with the Act's evident tolerance for employer efforts to verify—not merely guess at—the wishes of the bargaining unit.

Nor does the presumption of continuing majority support justify the Board's inflexibility. This Court in *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96 (1954), recognized that the presumption of union majority status, while entitled to substantial weight, is not immutable; it may be tested and overcome by objective considerations once the certification year has passed. *Id.* at 103–04. Where, as here, the bargaining unit itself was reconstituted by a successor employer that hired only a fraction of the predecessor's employees, the presumption's underlying premise—continuity of a previously verified electorate—is weaker still. *Cf. John Wiley & Sons v. Livingston*, 376 U.S. 543 (1964) (recognizing that a successor's bargaining obligations may differ materially from those of its predecessor). This Court has likewise acknowledged that an employer confronted with genuine uncertainty about a union's continued support may act on a good-faith doubt regarding that status. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–95 (1969). A poll conducted with rigorous safeguards is the least disruptive means of resolving that doubt, and the Board's insistence on identical proof for polling and withdrawal frustrates rather than serves the Act's goal of industrial peace grounded in employees' actual, not presumed, wishes.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding cannot stand. Judicial review of Board factfinding demands more than a search for isolated evidentiary crumbs. As this Court held in *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197 (1938), "substantial evidence is more than a scintilla, and must be enough to justify, if the trial were to a jury, a refusal to direct a verdict when the conclusion sought to be drawn from it is one of fact for the jury." *Id.* at 229. And in *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), this Court clarified that reviewing courts must weigh the record as a whole, for "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Id.* at 488.

Measured against that standard, the Board's conclusion collapses. Employees repeatedly and independently volunteered statements disclaiming union support—not solicited by management, not the product of coercion, but spontaneous expressions bearing directly on the very question at issue. The Board discounted these statements piecemeal, employee by employee, without ever confronting their cumulative weight, in precisely the manner *Universal Camera* forbids. Cf. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221 (1963) (Board findings must be grounded in the record, not conjecture). The Board's selective evidentiary approach—crediting only the narrowest sliver of direct, first-person repudiation while ignoring corroborating employee reports because the reporting employee did not personally catalog his methodology—imposes a standard of proof no employer could realistically satisfy short of taking the very poll the Board condemns.

For these reasons, the Board's polling standard should be rejected as arbitrary, and, even if retained, the Board's finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.
