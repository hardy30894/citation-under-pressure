**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying an Evidentiary Hearing Because the Prosecutor’s Unethical Conduct Violated Riddle’s Due Process Rights.**

A prosecutor’s participation as both witness and advocate creates an unacceptable risk of prejudice, depriving a defendant of a fair trial. *See United States v. Prantil*, 764 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1985) (holding that a prosecutor’s dual role “casts a cloud of unfairness” over proceedings). Here, District Attorney Danny Hill testified to rebut Riddle’s claim of prosecutorial misconduct, then remained as chief prosecutor. This dual role violated the Texas Code of Professional Responsibility, which prohibits an attorney from acting as advocate and witness in the same proceeding unless the testimony relates to an uncontested issue. *See Tex. Disciplinary R. Prof’l Conduct 3.08(a)*. Hill’s testimony was not only contested—it directly attacked Riddle’s credibility on a matter central to his defense: his fear of Hill’s threat to seek the death penalty. By testifying, Hill became a “witness against the accused,” undermining the impartiality required of the prosecution. *See United States v. Bird*, 709 F.2d 1224, 1229 (8th Cir. 1983) (finding reversible error where prosecutor’s dual role prejudiced the defendant).

**II. The Prosecutor’s Closing Argument Improperly Bolstered Hill’s Testimony and Denied Riddle a Fair Trial.**

In closing, the assistant prosecutor argued that Riddle was a liar because Hill’s testimony proved he was “incredulous.” This argument grounded the State’s case not in evidence, but in the prosecutor’s own credibility. Such tactics violate due process. *See Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (holding that a prosecutor must refrain from using improper methods to secure a conviction). When Hill’s credibility became the linchpin of the State’s rebuttal, the jury was improperly invited to believe the prosecutor rather than evaluate the evidence. *See United States v. Galloway*, 48 F.3d 899, 905 (5th Cir. 1995) (reversing where prosecutor vouched for a witness’s credibility). The cumulative effect of this misconduct was to deny Riddle his right to a fair trial. *See Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974) (recognizing that prosecutorial misconduct can render a trial fundamentally unfair).

**III. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Denying an Evidentiary Hearing on These Facts.**

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2), a federal court may hold an evidentiary hearing if the petitioner diligently developed the factual basis for his claim in state court and the facts underlying the claim would establish a constitutional violation. *See Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 420, 432 (2000). Riddle diligently sought state habeas relief, but the state court denied review without an evidentiary hearing. The district court then granted summary judgment without hearing testimony from Hill or other witnesses about his threat to seek the death penalty or his role as prosecutor-witness. This was error, as material facts remained in dispute. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (holding that an evidentiary hearing is required when the state court failed to resolve factual disputes). Because the record leaves unresolved whether Hill’s dual role or his closing argument tainted the verdict, a hearing is essential. *See Valdez v. Cockrell*, 274 F.3d 941, 947 (5th Cir. 2001) (remanding for hearing where state court’s factual findings were deficient).

**IV. The District Court’s Summary Judgment Was Premature Given the Unresolved Credibility Determinations.**

Riddle’s claim that Hill threatened him with the death penalty is supported by Riddle’s testimony and Bybee’s corroboration. Summary judgment is inappropriate when material facts are in dispute. *See Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). The district court’s rejection of Riddle’s petition without a hearing effectively credited the State’s version of events without allowing Riddle to develop his evidence. This contravenes the requirement that a habeas court “must consider the totality of the circumstances” to assess a claim of prosecutorial misconduct. *See Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 183 (1986). Here, the totality requires a hearing to evaluate Hill’s credibility and the impact of his testimony.

**V. The Failure to Hold a Hearing Also Violates Riddle’s Right to Effective Assistance of Counsel.**

Riddle’s trial counsel failed to object to Hill’s dual role or the improper closing argument. This ineffective assistance is cognizable under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), which requires showing both deficient performance and prejudice. Counsel’s failure to object to obvious prosecutorial misconduct falls below an objective standard of reasonableness. *See United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984). The prejudice is evident: without objection, the jury heard improper vouching and a prosecutor-witness’s unsworn testimony. The district court’s denial of a hearing foreclosed any factual development of this claim. *See Williams*, 529 U.S. at 432.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of habeas relief and remand with instructions to hold an evidentiary hearing on Riddle’s claims of prosecutorial misconduct and ineffective assistance of counsel. The dual role of the prosecutor, coupled with improper closing argument, deprived Riddle of his constitutional right to a fair trial. An evidentiary hearing is essential to resolve the factual disputes that remain.
