**ARGUMENT**

Section 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages only upon a showing that the respondent “engaged in a discriminatory practice … with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language, read against the common-law backdrop Congress incorporated, requires conduct that is independently egregious or outrageous; proof of intentional discrimination alone does not suffice.

The phrase “malice or … reckless indifference” was drawn from the punitive-damages standard applied to claims under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. See, e.g., Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages under § 1983 require conduct showing “evil motive or intent” or “reckless or callous indifference”). Before 1991, every circuit to address the question held that § 1981 punitive awards demanded more than intentional discrimination; the plaintiff had to prove “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct. See, e.g., Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co., 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987); Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 832 F.2d 194, 205-06 (1st Cir. 1987). Congress is presumed to have adopted that settled meaning. See, e.g., Lorillard v. Pons, 434 U.S. 575, 581 (1978).

The legislative history confirms the point. The House Report stated that § 1981a “demands of plaintiff ‘an even higher standard’ than simple proof of intentional discrimination.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 72 (1991). Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum likewise limited recovery to “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991). These statements are consistent with the common-law requirement that punitive damages be reserved for conduct that is “outrageous” because of the defendant’s “evil motive or reckless indifference.” Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908 cmt. b (1979), quoted in Smith, 461 U.S. at 46-47.

Kolstad’s evidence—that the Association preselected a male colleague and offered shifting explanations—establishes at most intentional discrimination. It does not demonstrate the independent egregiousness the statute demands. The district court therefore correctly refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages, and the judgment should be affirmed.
