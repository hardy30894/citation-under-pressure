**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT LACKED SUBJECT-MATTER JURISDICTION OVER THIS STATE-LAW CLAIM.**

The Pressls’ complaint sought only a declaratory judgment interpreting the scope of their rights under a state-created flowage easement and did not raise any federal question. *See Merrell Dow Pharms. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 813 (1986) (federal jurisdiction exists only where the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint raises a federal issue). Because the Pressls’ claim arises under Virginia property law and does not implicate any federal regulatory authority, the district court erred in exercising jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331.

APCO’s removal argument—that jurisdiction existed under 16 U.S.C. § 825p—fails for the same reason. Section 825p grants federal courts jurisdiction over “any civil action arising under” the Federal Power Act, but only where the action implicates the FERC’s regulatory authority. *See City of Tacoma v. Taxpayers of Tacoma*, 357 U.S. 320, 336 (1958) (limiting § 825p jurisdiction to cases where federal regulatory authority is directly at issue). Here, the Pressls’ claim concerns the interpretation of a private easement and does not challenge APCO’s FERC license or any FERC-ordered condition. *See Town of Norwood v. FERC*, 906 F.2d 1216, 1221 (4th Cir. 1990) (holding that § 825p does not confer jurisdiction over state-law disputes between private parties).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING THE COMPLAINT ON THE MERITS.**

Even assuming jurisdiction existed, the district court misapplied Virginia easement law. Under Virginia law, an easement must be construed in light of its purpose and the intent of the parties. *See Meeks v. Johnson*, 233 Va. 556, 560 (1987). The flowage easement here grants APCO the right to overflow the land below the 800-foot contour but does not expressly authorize APCO to prohibit the Pressls from building a dock. The easement’s grant of APCO’s right to “remove” objects does not encompass a general regulatory power over the Pressls’ property. *See Restatement (Third) of Property (Servitudes) § 1.2 cmt. e* (2000) (an easement does not confer broad regulatory authority absent clear language).

The district court’s reliance on *Smith Mountain Lake Cmty. Ass’n v. APCO*, 2012 WL 1288774 (W.D. Va. 2012), was misplaced. That case involved a dispute over APCO’s compliance with its FERC license, not a private easement dispute. *See id.* at *3 (explicitly distinguishing between FERC-regulated activities and private property rights). The Pressls’ claim does not implicate APCO’s FERC license or any federal regulatory scheme.

**III. THE PRESSLS’ CLAIM DOES NOT REQUIRE EXHAUSTION OF FERC REMEDIES.**

APCO argued, and the district court agreed, that the Pressls must exhaust FERC remedies before filing suit. However, exhaustion is not required where the claim does not challenge a FERC-ordered condition. *See Town of Norwood*, 906 F.2d at 1222 (exhaustion applies only to claims “within the scope of FERC’s regulatory authority”). The Pressls’ claim arises under state property law and does not seek review of any FERC action.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Pressls respectfully request that this Court vacate the district court’s judgment and remand the case to state court.
