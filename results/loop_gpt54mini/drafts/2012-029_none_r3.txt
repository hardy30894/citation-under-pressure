Section 1338(a) does not make every case with a patent question exclusively federal. It confers exclusive jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws, and the Supreme Court has long construed that phrase narrowly. See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 807-09 (1988). Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, the federal issue must appear on the face of the plaintiff’s own claim and be necessary to relief. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 112-13 (1936).

The Court’s older cases say the same. In Shulthis v. McDougal, the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law merely because it “takes its origin in the laws of the United States”; it must “really and substantially involve[] a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). In Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co., jurisdiction existed only because the federal issue was “an essential one” to the plaintiff’s right to relief. 255 U.S. 180, 199-201 (1921). By contrast, when the federal question is only part of the setting for a state-law claim, jurisdiction is absent. Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

That rule controls here. Minton’s suit is a Texas legal-malpractice action. State law supplies the duty, breach, causation, and damages elements, and the patent issue appears only as one step in proving causation under a “case within a case” theory. See Alexander v. Turtur & Associates, Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The complaint does not seek to enforce or invalidate a patent, nor does it ask for relief created by federal patent law. It asks only whether counsel’s alleged negligence caused Minton to lose a state malpractice case. Under Christianson, that is not enough. Federal jurisdiction exists only when patent law “creates the cause of action” or when the plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808-09.

The Court’s pre-1970 patent cases reinforce that distinction. In The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., the Court refused to find federal jurisdiction where the patent issue was embedded in a broader dispute. 228 U.S. 22, 25-26 (1913). In Hartell v. Tilghman, the Court held that federal jurisdiction was absent when the patent question was merely incidental. 99 U.S. 547, 549-50 (1879). And in Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co., the Court made clear that a case does not arise under the patent laws simply because it involves a patent; the cause of action itself must arise under federal patent law. 185 U.S. 282, 287-88 (1902). Those cases foreclose respondent’s position.

Nor does the need to consult patent law make the issue “substantial” in the jurisdictional sense. The Court has repeatedly rejected federal jurisdiction where the federal question is merely evidentiary, defensive, or collateral. See Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 464-65 (1894); Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 672-73 (1950). Here, the patent issue is backward-looking and case-specific: whether Minton’s former lawyers should have raised a particular defense in earlier litigation. That is a malpractice causation question, not a patent case. Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 570.

Exclusive federal jurisdiction would also upset the traditional division of authority between state and federal courts. State courts routinely hear malpractice claims. Nothing in the Patent Act suggests Congress intended to oust them from that ordinary role whenever patent law is implicated. The Court has cautioned against construing jurisdictional statutes so broadly that they displace state authority absent a clear congressional command. Gully, 299 U.S. at 114-15; Louisville & Nashville Railroad, 211 U.S. at 152.

The correct rule is the one the Supreme Court has always applied: a state-law claim does not arise under federal patent law merely because it contains a patent question. Because Minton’s complaint asserts a state malpractice claim and any patent issue is only incidental to that claim, the federal courts lack exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment dismissing for want of jurisdiction should be reversed.
