## ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its plain text and legislative history. The court’s “predominantly internal” standard reads the word “solely” out of the statute and permits the withholding of documents that Congress never intended to exempt.

### I. The Plain Language of Exemption 2 Requires That the Withheld Matter Be “Related Solely to the Internal Personnel Rules and Practices of an Agency.”

Exemption 2 protects only matters that are “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The word “solely” means “exclusively” or “entirely.” The ESQD arc maps and related data are not personnel rules or practices. They are technical safety calculations used to design ammunition storage facilities and to determine safe distances between explosives. These maps do not govern employee conduct, working conditions, or any internal administrative matter. They are engineering data, not personnel rules. See *Consumers Union v. Veterans Administration*, 301 F. Supp. 796, 800 (S.D.N.Y. 1969) (Exemption 2 does not cover “substantive agency policies” or “technical data”).

Even if the maps could be considered “rules” in some sense, they are not “solely” internal. The maps have direct external consequences: they affect the safety of the surrounding community, maritime traffic, and the public’s ability to assess the risks posed by the Navy’s operations. Because the maps are not “solely” internal, they fall outside Exemption 2’s scope. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test improperly dilutes the statutory requirement that the matter be *solely* internal.

### II. The Legislative History Confirms That Exemption 2 Was Intended Only for Trivial Internal Matters.

Congress enacted FOIA to “establish a general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” S. Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 3 (1965). The exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *American Mail Line v. Gulick*, 411 F.2d 696, 700 (D.C. Cir. 1969). The Senate Report explains that Exemption 2 covers only “minor internal matters” such as “rules as to personnel’s use of parking facilities or regulations of lunch hours.” S. Rep. No. 813, at 8. The House Report similarly states that the exemption applies to “matters of internal personnel administration” that are “of no genuine public interest.” H.R. Rep. No. 1497, 89th Cong., 2d Sess. 8 (1966). The Attorney General’s Memorandum on the FOIA likewise describes Exemption 2 as covering “rules and practices concerning the internal administration of an agency” such as “sick leave, lunch hours, and parking.” Attorney General’s Memorandum on the Public Information Section of the Administrative Procedure Act 4 (1967).

The ESQD maps are not trivial internal matters. They concern the storage of explosives and the safety of the public. The Navy itself acknowledges that the maps are “long term planning tool[s]” used to design facilities and organize operations. Such substantive, safety-related data are precisely the kind of information that FOIA was designed to make available to the public. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to cover these maps contradicts Congress’s clear intent.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Test Reads the Word “Solely” Out of the Statute.

The Ninth Circuit adopted the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, which asks whether the matter is “predominantly” for internal use. That standard is inconsistent with the statutory text. Congress used the word “solely” to limit Exemption 2 to matters that are *exclusively* internal. If a document has any external relevance—if it affects the public, informs community safety, or relates to agency operations that impact outsiders—it is not “solely” internal. The ESQD maps have obvious external relevance: they show the potential blast zones that could affect nearby towns, maritime traffic, and the environment. The Navy’s own affidavits confirm that the maps are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities” and to “organize ammunition operations.” These are not internal personnel matters; they are operational decisions with public consequences.

The Ninth Circuit’s test would allow an agency to withhold any document that is primarily used internally, regardless of its external impact. That approach eviscerates the word “solely” and expands Exemption 2 far beyond what Congress intended. This Court should reject the “predominantly internal” standard and hold that Exemption 2 applies only to matters that are *exclusively* about internal personnel rules and practices.

### IV. The Risk of Circumvention Test Does Not Rescue the Ninth Circuit’s Error.

The Ninth Circuit also required that disclosure of the documents “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” Even assuming that test is relevant, the ESQD maps do not present such a risk. The maps are static safety data; they are not guidelines for evading regulations. Unlike a law enforcement manual that could teach criminals how to avoid detection, the ESQD maps simply show the distances at which explosives are stored. Knowing those distances does not enable anyone to circumvent any regulation. The Navy’s concern that the maps could be used to plan an attack is speculative and does not relate to circumvention of agency regulation. The risk of circumvention test, as articulated in the legislative history, applies to materials that “if disclosed, would enable persons to violate the law.” S. Rep. No. 813, at 8. The ESQD maps do not enable anyone to violate any law; they merely inform the public about the location and potential impact of explosives.

### V. Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit’s decision distorts the plain language of Exemption 2, ignores the legislative history, and expands the exemption to cover documents that Congress never intended to withhold. The ESQD maps are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” They are technical safety data with direct external consequences. The judgment should be reversed.
