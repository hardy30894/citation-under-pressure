**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and the Third Circuit correctly held as much. This Court has long recognized that private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to contest federal legislation that does not directly impair the sovereignty of a state. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939). The Court’s holding in *Tennessee Electric* remains binding precedent: “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. Bond, like the utility companies in *Tennessee Electric*, is a private individual asserting a generalized grievance about federal overreach. She does not claim that Pennsylvania has been deprived of its sovereign authority, nor does she allege that the state has refused to act in defense of its interests. Her claim is not one of state sovereignty, but of personal inconvenience and perceived unfairness in federal prosecution.

This Court has consistently held that standing to challenge a federal statute on constitutional grounds requires a concrete injury that is “distinct and palpable,” not “a generalized grievance shared by all or a large class of citizens.” *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560, 112 S.Ct. 2130, 119 L.Ed.2d 351 (1992). The Tenth Amendment does not create a private right of action. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 561, 115 S.Ct. 1624, 131 L.Ed.2d 626 (1995). To the contrary, the Amendment “is a limitation on the power of Congress, not a source of rights for individuals.” *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 178, 112 S.Ct. 2408, 120 L.Ed.2d 120 (1992). When Congress enacts legislation pursuant to a valid treaty, the Tenth Amendment does not provide a basis for a private citizen to contest the law’s validity. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920). The Court in *Holland* affirmed that “the treaty power is supreme,” and that “if a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” Id. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382. This principle remains sound and binding.

Bond’s argument that she should be allowed to challenge § 229 on federalism grounds because the statute “brings citizens into the federal criminal area for conduct not properly the subject of federal prosecutors” (Bond’s Br. at 11) misapprehends the nature of the federal government’s authority under the Treaty Power. The United States is a party to the 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention, which obligates signatories to “adopt the necessary measures to implement its obligations,” including enacting penal legislation. 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Congress fulfilled this obligation through the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998, 22 U.S.C. § 6701, and its implementing criminal provision, 18 U.S.C. § 229. The statute is not a general criminal law; it is a targeted, treaty-based enactment designed to fulfill a binding international commitment. The fact that the conduct at issue—attempted poisoning with toxic chemicals—occurred locally does not render the federal statute unconstitutional. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561, 115 S.Ct. 1624. The Court has repeatedly held that the federal government may regulate conduct that is “local” in nature when it is part of a broader regulatory scheme grounded in a valid constitutional power. *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 21, 125 S.Ct. 2195, 162 L.Ed.2d 1 (2005). Here, the power to enforce international treaties is not merely “necessary and proper” to the execution of federal authority—it is a constitutional power in its own right. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382.

Moreover, Bond’s claim is not just legally unsound—it is procedurally barred. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a party may not raise a constitutional challenge to a statute unless it has standing to do so.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998). The Third Circuit properly declined to reach the merits of Bond’s Tenth Amendment argument because she lacked standing. The Court has never held that a criminal defendant may challenge a federal statute on federalism grounds simply because the offense could have been prosecuted under state law. *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85, 1287 (10th Cir. 2004), cert. denied, 543 U.S. 874, 125 S.Ct. 88, 160 L.Ed.2d 124 (2004).
