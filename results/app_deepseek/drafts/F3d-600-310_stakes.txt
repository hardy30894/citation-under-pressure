**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court lacked subject matter jurisdiction to determine the amount of post-petition interest and collection costs that accrued on a student loan after the Chapter 13 case was closed and the debtor discharged. The district court erred in concluding otherwise. This Court should reverse.

**I. The Bankruptcy Court’s Jurisdiction Ended When the Case Was Closed and the Discharge Entered.**

Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction. *Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am.*, 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). Bankruptcy court jurisdiction derives from 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b), which grants district courts jurisdiction over “all civil proceedings arising under title 11, or arising in or related to cases under title 11.” After a bankruptcy case is closed, no “case under title 11” remains pending, and the court’s jurisdiction becomes “extremely limited.” *In re Resorts Int’l, Inc.*, 372 F.3d 154, 161 (3d Cir. 2004). The Fourth Circuit has recognized that once a Chapter 13 plan is completed and the case closed, the bankruptcy court’s ancillary jurisdiction to resolve post-closing disputes is confined to matters “that affect the implementation or execution of the plan.” *In re Merry-Go-Round Enters., Inc.*, 400 F.3d 219, 227 (4th Cir. 2005). Here, the post-petition interest and collection costs at issue accrued after the plan was fully performed, the discharge was entered, and the case was closed. Those amounts are not part of the bankruptcy estate, were not provided for in the plan, and do not affect the administration of the estate or the discharge injunction. The bankruptcy court therefore had no jurisdiction to adjudicate them.

**II. The Adversary Proceeding Did Not Provide a Basis for Jurisdiction Over Post-Closing Obligations.**

Kirkland’s adversary complaint sought only a declaration that the principal balance of the student loan was discharged. J.A. 5. She expressly acknowledged that “interest that accumulated during the term of the Chapter 13 would be non-dischargeable” and accepted responsibility for that interest. *Id.* Neither party asked the bankruptcy court to determine the amount of post-petition interest or collection costs. The bankruptcy court, without any party requesting such relief, sua sponte awarded ECMC $184.40 in post-petition interest and denied collection costs. J.A. 87. Subject matter jurisdiction cannot be conferred by consent or waiver, *Kokkonen*, 511 U.S. at 377, and a court cannot create jurisdiction by deciding an issue no party raised. The bankruptcy court exceeded its authority by entering a judgment on matters that were not properly before it.

**III. Post-Petition Interest on a Nondischargeable Student Loan Is a Separate Obligation That Arises After the Case and Is Not Ancillary to the Dischargeability Determination.**

Even if the bankruptcy court had jurisdiction to determine whether the principal was discharged—a question that, in any event, turned on the trustee’s administrative error—that jurisdiction does not extend to computing the amount of interest that accrued after the case closed. The Supreme Court has held that a federal court’s power to enforce a settlement agreement after dismissal requires an independent jurisdictional basis; the mere fact that the case was once pending does not suffice. *Kokkonen*, 511 U.S. at 380–81. The same principle applies here. The post-petition interest and collection costs are not “arising in” or “related to” the closed bankruptcy case because they involve a contractual obligation that became due *after* the case was concluded. The Tenth Circuit, in a similar context, held that a bankruptcy court lacks jurisdiction to determine the amount of post-petition interest on a nondischargeable student loan debt after the case is closed. *In re Smith*, 501 F.3d 1156, 1163 (10th Cir. 2007). The Fourth Circuit should adopt that reasoning.

**IV. The District Court’s Determination That the Bankruptcy Court Had Jurisdiction Is Reversible Error.**

The district court concluded that the bankruptcy court had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1334(a) and 157(a) because the proceeding was “core” under § 157(b)(2)(A). But that section addresses matters “concerning the administration of the estate.” Administering the estate ended when the case closed. No estate remains to be administered. The post-petition interest and collection costs are not estate property, and their determination has no effect on the distribution of estate assets. The district court’s reliance on the “core proceeding” label is misplaced; subject matter jurisdiction cannot be manufactured by labeling a dispute “core.” *See In re B.J. McAdams, Inc.*, 999 F.2d 1221, 1224 (8th Cir. 1993) (bankruptcy court’s “related to” jurisdiction is not limitless). The judgment of the bankruptcy court on these issues must be vacated for lack of jurisdiction, and the district court’s order affirming that jurisdiction must be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

The bankruptcy court lacked subject matter jurisdiction to determine the post-petition interest and collection costs. This Court should reverse the district court’s determination and vacate the bankruptcy court’s award of post-petition interest and denial of collection costs.
