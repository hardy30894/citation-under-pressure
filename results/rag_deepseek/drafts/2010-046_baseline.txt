## Argument

The three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity fully complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA), 18 U.S.C. § 3626. The PLRA permits prospective relief that is “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” § 3626(a)(1)(A). The order satisfies each requirement.

### I. The Court Properly Found Ongoing Eighth Amendment Violations Caused by Overcrowding

The PLRA requires a finding of a “current and ongoing violation of a Federal right” before granting prospective relief. § 3626(a)(1)(A). After a lengthy trial, the three-judge court determined that severe overcrowding was the “primary cause” of systemic deficiencies in medical and mental health care, depriving inmates of “the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities.” *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981). The court found that California’s prisons operated at nearly 200% of design capacity, leading to delayed and denied care, rampant disease, and preventable deaths. These conditions constitute the “unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain” forbidden by the Eighth Amendment. *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294, 297 (1991) (quoting *Whitley v. Albers*, 475 U.S. 312, 319 (1986)). The state does not dispute that its prison health care system is constitutionally inadequate; it argues only that the remedy exceeds PLRA limits.

### II. The Population Cap Is Narrowly Drawn and Necessary

The PLRA does not prohibit population caps. To the contrary, this Court has recognized that courts may order population reductions when overcrowding is the root cause of constitutional violations. In *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978), the Court upheld a comprehensive remedial order that included a 30-day limit on punitive isolation, noting that the district court “was justified in entering a comprehensive order to insure against the risk of inadequate compliance” after years of failed efforts. Here, the three-judge court similarly acted after decades of unsuccessful litigation, consent decrees, and a receivership that failed to remedy the violations. The population cap is not a punitive measure; it is a structural remedy tailored to the specific constitutional deficiency.

The order is “narrowly drawn” because it sets a single numerical target—137.5% of design capacity—and leaves the state free to choose how to achieve it. California may build new facilities, expand parole, use alternative sentencing, or adopt any other lawful means. The court did not micromanage prison operations; it identified the constitutional floor and required the state to meet it. This approach is far narrower than the detailed injunctions upheld in *Hutto*, which dictated specific staffing, sanitation, and medical protocols.

### III. The Order Is the Least Intrusive Means

The PLRA requires that relief be “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court exhaustively considered less intrusive alternatives. For years, the state stipulated to remedial orders, appointed a receiver, and approved construction plans—yet the constitutional violations persisted. The state’s own experts testified that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of inadequate care and that no combination of other measures could achieve constitutional compliance without reducing the population. The court found that “nothing else will work.” This finding is entitled to deference. *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 85 (1987) (courts must give “appropriate deference” to prison administrators, but deference does not require acceptance of unconstitutional conditions).

The state argues that the order is not the least intrusive because it mandates release of inmates. But the PLRA does not require the court to accept the state’s preferred remedy; it requires the remedy to be necessary and narrowly tailored. The order is temporary and subject to modification if the state demonstrates that alternative measures succeed. The PLRA’s “termination” provision, § 3626(b), allows the state to seek dissolution of the order once the violation is cured. The order thus respects federalism while ensuring constitutional compliance.

### IV. The PLRA Does Not Bar Population Caps as a Remedy

Nothing in the PLRA’s text or history prohibits population caps. Congress enacted the PLRA to limit frivolous prison litigation, not to strip courts of their equitable power to remedy systemic Eighth Amendment violations. *See Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 332 (2000) (PLRA “does not disturb the court’s authority to order relief” that meets its standards). The PLRA’s requirement that relief be “necessary to correct the violation” is satisfied here because the court found that overcrowding directly caused the constitutional harm. The order is “narrowly drawn” because it targets only that cause. And it is the “least intrusive means” because the state had ample opportunity to solve the problem through less drastic measures and failed.

The judgment of the three-judge court should be affirmed.
