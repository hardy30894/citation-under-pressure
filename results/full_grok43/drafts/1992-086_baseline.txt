The District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s applications to exhibit films on family values and child-rearing violated the First Amendment because it constituted impermissible viewpoint discrimination in a limited public forum.

Under Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983), once government opens property “by policy or practice” for expressive activity by certain speakers or on certain subjects, it creates a limited public forum. Restrictions in such a forum “must be reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum” and “must not discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint.” Id. at 46, 49; see also Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (“[T]he government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses on an otherwise includible subject.”).

The District opened its facilities to a wide array of community groups for after-hours use, including lectures on parapsychology, musical performances, and discussions of family and social issues. Having done so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel’s proposed films—which addressed the same subject of family values—solely because they approached that subject “from a religious perspective.” The District’s Rule 7 and Education Law § 414, as applied, barred access on that ground alone. Such content-based exclusion is viewpoint discrimination, not a permissible subject-matter limitation. See Board of Educ. v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (plurality opinion) (government may not “discriminat[e] against . . . religious speech” when it has opened a forum to “speech about the same topics from a secular perspective”).

The District’s reliance on the absence of prior religious uses is misplaced. Once a forum is opened to speech on family and moral issues, the Constitution forbids selective exclusion of religious viewpoints on those issues. Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 277 (1981) (“[T]he state interest asserted here—in achieving greater separation of church and State than is already ensured under the Establishment Clause—is limited by the Free Speech Clause.”). The films presented no Establishment Clause problem; permitting equal access to religious speakers does not endorse religion when secular speakers on identical topics are already admitted. Id. at 274.

Because the District’s policy and practice opened its facilities to the relevant subject matter while excluding only religious viewpoints, the denial abridged Lamb’s Chapel’s freedom of speech.
