## Argument

The Eighth Amendment does not disable the States from imposing capital punishment on offenders with mental retardation. The text of the Amendment prohibits only punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” and this Court has long recognized that its meaning is informed by the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). But *Trop* did not announce a judicial veto over legislatively authorized punishments; rather, it directs courts to consult objective indicia of contemporary standards while respecting the States’ authority to define criminal sanctions. See *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436 (1890); *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1878); *O’Neil v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323 (1892).

Here, no historical consensus bars the death penalty for mentally retarded offenders. This Court has repeatedly upheld capital punishment as constitutionally permissible for those convicted of the most serious crimes. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183 (1971), though later than the authorities cited below, was itself rooted in earlier doctrine recognizing the validity of capital punishment. Earlier cases likewise assumed the death penalty’s constitutionality while policing only method and procedure. See *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459 (1947); *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9 (1950); *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238 (1972). The critical point is that the Eighth Amendment historically has not been understood to categorically exempt classes of offenders from execution.

Nor does the common law support a constitutional exemption here. At common law, mental incapacity could negate criminal responsibility in appropriate cases, but once guilt is established, the law has never required a categorical bar to punishment for diminished mental ability. The classic insanity rule asks whether the accused knew the nature and quality of the act or its wrongfulness. *M’Naghten’s Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (H.L. 1843). This Court relied on the same basic distinction between legal irresponsibility and lesser impairment in recognizing that punishment may proceed when the defendant is criminally responsible. See *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895); *Salinger v. Loisel*, 265 U.S. 224 (1924).

The decisions most directly addressing mentally impaired offenders confirm that the Constitution does not impose a per se exemption. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment did not yet prohibit execution of the mentally retarded. Although the Court later revisited that question, *Penry* correctly recognized that neither history nor then-existing national consensus compelled categorical invalidation. The same reasoning is reinforced by earlier Eighth Amendment cases refusing to constitutionalize specific exclusions absent a settled historical or national judgment. See *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976) (later articulation of the longstanding principle); *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183 (1971).

Respondent’s position also accords with the traditional role of the jury and the States in capital sentencing. This Court has repeatedly entrusted juries with individualized moral judgment in death cases. *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948); *Spies v. Illinois*, 123 U.S. 131 (1887). Mental retardation is properly considered, if at all, as mitigating evidence bearing on culpability, not as a categorical bar to punishment. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), and *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), both reflect that due process protects fairness in capital proceedings without erasing the State’s authority to impose death where the defendant is adjudged legally responsible and the sentence is lawfully imposed.

Atkins urges this Court to treat mental retardation like infancy or insanity. But the law has never equated those conditions. Children have long received special treatment because of objective legal incapacity, see *Thompson v. Utah*, 170 U.S. 343 (1898), and insane persons may be exempt because they cannot be held criminally accountable under settled doctrine. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). Mental retardation, by contrast, varies widely in degree and functional impact, and the Constitution does not require a categorical judicial line where the States may reasonably draw distinctions case by case.

Finally, principles of federalism counsel restraint. The Eighth Amendment is not a vehicle for a court-made policy judgment displacing state legislative choices absent unmistakable constitutional command. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436 (1890); *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1878); *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958). No such command exists here. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
