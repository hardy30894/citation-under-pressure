# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT APPLIED THE WRONG STANDARD IN GRANTING MANDATORY PRELIMINARY RELIEF

The ordinary rule in this Circuit is that a party seeking a preliminary injunction must show irreparable harm and either a likelihood of success on the merits or serious questions going to the merits together with a balance of hardships tipping decidedly in the movant's favor. *Jackson Dairy, Inc. v. H.P. Hood & Sons, Inc.*, 596 F.2d 70, 72 (2d Cir. 1979) (per curiam). But the injunction here is not merely prohibitory — it commands Cadman Towers to displace whatever resident currently occupies a ground-floor space and hand that space to Shapiro before her turn on the waiting list arrives. Such mandatory relief, which alters rather than preserves the status quo, may issue only upon a clear showing that the moving party is entitled to the relief requested. *Abdul Wali v. Coughlin*, 754 F.2d 1015, 1025 (2d Cir. 1985). The district court never applied this heightened standard, and the record cannot satisfy it.

## II. SHAPIRO'S DEMAND FOR IMMEDIATE, OUT-OF-TURN ASSIGNMENT OF A GROUND-FLOOR SPACE EXCEEDS THE "REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION" REQUIRED BY THE FHAA

The Fair Housing Amendments Act obligates housing providers to make "reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services" when necessary to afford a disabled person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). The accommodation duty, however, is not limitless. As the Supreme Court held in the analogous context of the Rehabilitation Act, an institution need not make "fundamental alteration[s] in the nature of a program" simply because a disabled individual would benefit. *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 410 (1979). Nor must an entity adopt "substantial" changes to its established practices whenever a less burdensome accommodation exists. *Alexander v. Choate*, 469 U.S. 287, 300-01 & n.20 (1985). And an accommodation is not reasonable if it imposes "undue financial and administrative burdens" on the provider or on similarly situated third parties. *School Bd. of Nassau County v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 287 n.17 (1987).

Cadman Towers' first-come/first-served parking policy is not an arbitrary rule adopted to exclude the disabled; it is the only equitable mechanism available to allocate 66 spaces among 302 apartments at 101 Clark Street alone. Every resident on the waiting list — including elderly tenants and other individuals with mobility impairments who have likewise been waiting for a space — has an equally legitimate claim to the very spot Shapiro seeks. An order compelling Cadman Towers to hand her a space out of turn does not merely modify a "rule" within the meaning of § 3604(f)(3)(B); it wholly displaces the waiting-list system for her benefit and, necessarily, injures another resident who has waited longer. That is precisely the kind of "fundamental alteration" and third-party burden that *Davis* and *Arline* place outside the reach of the reasonable-accommodation requirement.

Cadman Towers did not refuse to accommodate Shapiro's disability. It advised her, through counsel, that once she reached the top of the waiting list in the ordinary course, the Board would work to locate a parking space near her building entrance to minimize the distance she would have to travel — an accommodation tailored to her specific medical needs without requiring Cadman Towers to strip another resident of an assigned space. The FHAA does not entitle a disabled tenant to the *most* beneficial accommodation, only to a reasonable one. *See Alexander*, 469 U.S. at 300 (recognizing that the statute guarantees "meaningful access," not optimal access). Cadman Towers' offer satisfied that standard.

## III. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS AND THE PUBLIC INTEREST DO NOT FAVOR THE INJUNCTION

Even under the ordinary *Jackson Dairy* formulation, the balance of hardships does not tip, let alone tip decidedly, toward Shapiro. *Jackson Dairy*, 596 F.2d at 72. Shapiro has functioned for two years using public transportation, private car services, and on-street parking with a handicapped placard. While plainly burdensome, these arrangements are not so intolerable as to justify commandeering a scarce cooperative resource that another family — quite possibly another disabled or elderly resident — has been waiting years to receive. By contrast, forcing Cadman Towers to eject a current space-holder, or to freeze the waiting list indefinitely to satisfy the injunction, works a concrete and immediate hardship on identifiable third parties who are not before the court and who have committed no wrong.

The public interest likewise counsels against the injunction. Housing cooperatives administering scarce resources among hundreds of residents — many of whom are themselves elderly or disabled — need the latitude to apply neutral, transparent allocation rules, subject only to accommodations that do not require sacrificing one disabled or elderly resident's interests for another's. A rule permitting any resident who obtains a doctor's letter to bypass the waiting list threatens to convert the reasonable-accommodation provision into a vehicle for queue-jumping that undermines the very fairness the waiting list was designed to secure.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court should be reversed.
