**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—requires this Court to resolve a critical tension between the constitutional right to a fair trial and the government’s obligation to prove every element of a crime. The D.C. Circuit’s decision, which affirmed the conviction despite the government’s failure to prove continuity of the conspiracy into the limitations period, rests on a fundamental misreading of *United States v. Herring*, 467 U.S. 358 (1984), and *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506 (1995), and fails to recognize that the burden of persuasion on conspiracy membership remains with the government throughout the trial, even where a defendant claims withdrawal.

The government’s burden to prove conspiracy membership beyond a reasonable doubt is not diminished by a defendant’s assertion of withdrawal. In *Herring*, this Court held that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at some time during its duration.” 467 U.S. at 365. That burden does not shift merely because a defendant claims to have withdrawn. The government must still prove that the defendant was a member during the relevant period—i.e., within the statute of limitations. *Id.* at 366. The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that the government need only prove the existence of a conspiracy at some point in time, and that the jury could infer continued membership based on circumstantial evidence alone, effectively relieves the government of its constitutional obligation to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the burden of persuasion on all elements of a crime—including conspiracy membership—remains with the government. In *Gaudin*, the Court reaffirmed that “the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt,” including the defendant’s knowledge and intent. 515 U.S. at 510. The Court further held that “the jury must be instructed that the prosecution has the burden of proving each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Id.* at 511. The D.C. Circuit’s instruction to the jury—that it could infer continued conspiracy membership from the mere existence of a conspiracy and the defendant’s prior involvement—violated this principle. The jury was not told that the government bore the burden of proving, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant remained a member during the limitations period.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d 102 (D.C. Cir. 1978), is misplaced. In *Brawner*, the court upheld a conviction where the government presented evidence of a single, ongoing conspiracy that spanned the limitations period. 577 F.2d at 107. There was no claim of withdrawal, and the evidence of continuity was direct and substantial. Here, the government conceded that the leaders of the conspiracy—Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray—split up before the limitations period began. The government offered no evidence that Smith or Raynor had any contact with the remaining conspirators after that point. The mere fact that a conspiracy existed at some time does not justify inferring continued membership without proof of ongoing participation.

This Court has long recognized that withdrawal from a conspiracy is a complete defense if properly proven. In *United States v. Brawner*, the D.C. Circuit itself acknowledged that “a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy and thereby absolve himself of liability for subsequent acts.” 577 F.2d at 107. But withdrawal is not a mere assertion—it must be proven. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court held that withdrawal must be “voluntary and complete,” and that the defendant must “communicate his withdrawal to the other conspirators.” *Id.* at 108. The government bears the burden of proving that the defendant did not withdraw. *Id.* at 109. The D.C. Circuit’s failure to instruct the jury on this burden—indeed, its failure to even recognize that the government must prove continued membership—violated the defendant’s right to a fair trial.

The government’s burden is not satisfied by mere circumstantial evidence of association. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court rejected the idea that “mere presence at a meeting” or “participation in a single act” could establish continued membership. 577 F.2d at 107. Here, the government offered no evidence that Smith or Raynor had any contact with the remaining conspirators after the split. The only evidence of ongoing conspiracy was the testimony of a single co-conspirator who claimed to have seen Smith at a meeting in the limitations period—but that testimony was contradicted by other evidence and was not corroborated. The jury was not instructed that such evidence must be weighed against the defendant’s claim of withdrawal.

This Court has repeatedly held that the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), the Court declared that “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” Id. at 364. The D.C. Circuit’s decision undermines this principle by allowing the government to rely on speculative inferences to prove conspiracy membership. The jury was not told that the government had to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Smith was a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Instead, the jury was told that it could infer membership from the existence of a conspiracy and the defendant’s prior involvement.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *United States v. Gaudin*, where the Court held that “the jury must be instructed that the prosecution has the burden of proving each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” 515 U.S. at 511. The government’s burden does not shift to the defendant simply because the defendant claims to have withdrawn. The burden remains with the government to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period.

For these reasons, the D.C. Circuit’s decision must be reversed. The government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period, even if the defendant claims to have withdrawn.
