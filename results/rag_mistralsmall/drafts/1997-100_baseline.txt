**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE APPLIES TO NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS WHERE IT INVOLVES ISSUES ESSENTIALLY SIMILAR TO THOSE AT GUILT PHASES.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment—made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (*Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969))—bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that when a sentencing proceeding "in all relevant respects was like the immediately preceding trial on the issue of guilt or innocence," double jeopardy protections apply. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438 (1981). This principle was reaffirmed in *Rumsey v. Maryland*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-210 (1984), where the Court held that double jeopardy protections attach when sentencing proceedings "resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause."

Here, California statutorily guarantees defendants a jury trial on prior conviction allegations (*Cal. Penal Code § 1025*), requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt (*People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993)), and affords confrontation and cross-examination rights (*People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 228 (1996)). These procedural safeguards render the sentencing proceeding indistinguishable from a trial on guilt for double jeopardy purposes. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438 ("[T]he capital sentencing proceeding was sufficiently like the trial on guilt or innocence that it should be treated as a part of the trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.").

The California Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion—that double jeopardy does not apply in noncapital sentencing—ignores the Supreme Court’s repeated emphasis on functional equivalence. *See Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994) (noting that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were "based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding," but declining to limit their reach). The Court has never held that double jeopardy protections vanish merely because a proceeding is labeled "sentencing." Instead, it examines whether the proceeding’s structure and function resemble a trial. *See Sorola v. Texas*, 493 U.S. 1005 (1989) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (applying *Bullington* to noncapital sentencing where jury determined punishment).

**II. THE CALIFORNIA SUPREME COURT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH SUPREME COURT PRECEDENT.**

The California Supreme Court erred in concluding that double jeopardy protections do not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. This holding directly conflicts with *Bullington*, where the Court applied double jeopardy protections to a noncapital sentencing proceeding because it "in all relevant respects was like the immediately preceding trial on the issue of guilt or innocence." 451 U.S. at 438. The Court rejected the argument that double jeopardy protections were limited to capital cases, emphasizing that the functional similarity—not the label—of the proceeding controlled. *Id.* at 439 ("The fact that the proceeding was not a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence does not, of course, preclude application of the Double Jeopardy Clause.").

Subsequent cases reinforce this principle. In *Rumsey*, the Court applied double jeopardy protections to a noncapital sentencing proceeding where the jury found the defendant "not a dangerous special offender," reasoning that the proceeding "resemble[d] a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." 467 U.S. at 209-210. The Court expressly rejected the notion that double jeopardy protections were limited to capital sentencing. *Id.* at 210 ("We see no reason to draw such a distinction.").

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949)—which held that due process does not require formal sentencing procedures—is misplaced. *Williams* addressed due process, not double jeopardy, and involved a sentencing scheme with no procedural safeguards. *Id.* at 245-246. Here, California statutorily guarantees a jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and confrontation rights, rendering the proceeding functionally equivalent to a trial on guilt. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438.

**III. RETRIAL ON PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS VIOLATES THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial where a prior conviction allegation was found insufficient at the first proceeding. *Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33, 40 (1988) ("[W]here the evidence offered by the State and admitted by the trial court . . . would have been sufficient to sustain a guilty verdict, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial."). Here, the California Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to support the prior conviction allegation, and the state sought to retry it. This violates double jeopardy principles because the prior conviction allegation—like a prior conviction itself—is an "offense" for double jeopardy purposes. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 446 ("[T]he finding of the sentencing jury that the State had failed to prove one of the aggravating circumstances . . . is an acquittal of that circumstance.").

The California Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion—that double jeopardy does not apply—ignores the Supreme Court’s repeated recognition that sentencing proceedings with trial-like procedures trigger double jeopardy protections. *See Freddie Davis v. Georgia*, 459 U.S. 891 (1982) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (applying *Bullington* to noncapital sentencing where jury found aggravating circumstances insufficient). The Court has never held that double jeopardy protections vanish merely because a proceeding is labeled "sentencing." Instead, it examines whether the proceeding’s structure and function resemble a trial. *See Sorola*, 493 U.S. at 1005 (Rehnquist, J., dissenting).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings where, as here, the proceeding is functionally equivalent to a trial on guilt.
