**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That the Defendant’s Contacts With the Forum Make It Fair to Require Defense There, Not That Those Contacts Cause the Particular Injury.**

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction has never been a strict causal test. In *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court abandoned Pennoyer’s rigid territorial formalism, see *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714 (1878), in favor of a standard asking whether the defendant has such “minimum contacts” with the forum that maintaining suit there “does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.’” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316 (quoting *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940)). The Court held that “the exercise of that privilege may give rise to obligations,” and that jurisdiction attaches where the corporation’s activities are “not only continuous and systematic, but also give rise to the liabilities sued on.” *Id.* at 317. That formulation was disjunctive from the outset: liabilities that *arise from* forum activities suffice, but the Court never held that a strict but-for causal chain to a single transaction is the exclusive avenue to jurisdiction. Ford's continuous, systematic marketing, sale, and servicing of vehicles—including the very Explorer model at issue—in Montana are activities from which the Estate's claims naturally relate.

**II. Ford’s Purposeful and Continuous Activity Within Montana Satisfies Due Process.**

This Court has repeatedly sustained jurisdiction where a corporation purposefully directs commercial activity into a State, even absent contacts with the precise transaction sued upon. In *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a nonresident motorist based on the State’s interest in regulating those who use its highways, reasoning that use of the state’s roads implied consent to be sued there for resulting injury. *Id.* at 356. Ford’s conduct is far more extensive than the single use of a highway: Ford has, for decades, advertised, sold, and serviced Explorers throughout Montana, cultivating a dealer network that markets these very vehicles to Montana residents. That systematic and continuous course of dealing is the functional equivalent of, and indeed exceeds, the contact this Court found sufficient in *Hess*.

In *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), the Court sustained jurisdiction over a foreign corporation whose wartime activities in Ohio, though unrelated to the cause of action, were so continuous and substantial that requiring it to answer suit there comported with fair play. *Id.* at 445–48. Ford’s Montana contacts are of the same character—systematic and substantial—and here they bear an even closer relationship to the claim, since Ford markets, sells, and services precisely the Explorer model whose tire failed.

**III. Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice, and the State’s Manifest Interest in Protecting Its Residents, Support Jurisdiction.**

In *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), the Court emphasized that “a trend is clearly discernible toward expanding the permissible scope” of state jurisdiction over foreign corporations, reflecting “a fundamental transformation of our national economy.” *Id.* at 222–23. The Court further recognized that the forum State “has a manifest interest in providing effective means of redress for its residents.” *Id.* at 223. That interest is squarely implicated here: Montana has a manifest interest in ensuring that its residents, like Ms. Gullett, may seek redress in their home forum for injuries suffered on their own highways from products that the manufacturer purposefully placed into the stream of Montana commerce.

*Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), likewise sustained jurisdiction based on the persistent course of business a foreign association conducted with forum residents, holding that "it is settled . . . that groups of individuals" may be subject to a state's power "when they enter into arrangements that are to be performed in the future" in that state. *Id.* at 647. Ford's arrangement with its Montana dealer network, through which it continuously supplies, advertises, and services Explorers, is exactly such a persistent course of dealing with the forum's residents.

**IV. Ford Purposefully Availed Itself of Montana’s Market and Cannot Escape Jurisdiction Through the Fortuity of the Chain of Resale.**

*Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235 (1958), confirms that the critical inquiry is whether the defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State.” *Id.* at 253. *Hanson* cautioned only against jurisdiction resting on the "unilateral activity of another party." *Id.* But there is nothing unilateral about Ford's own decision to advertise, distribute, and service Explorers throughout Montana. Ford, not some third party, created the market into which this very vehicle model was placed, and Ford, not the itinerant chain of private resale, chose to cultivate Montana consumers as customers for parts, service, and future purchases. Ford’s corporate presence, dealership network, and warranty and service relationships within Montana are precisely the sort of purposeful contacts that, under *Henry L. Doherty & Co. v. Goodman*, 294 U.S. 623 (1935), subject a corporation "doing business" in a state to suit there on claims connected to that business. *Id.* at 627–28.

**CONCLUSION**

Under *International Shoe* and its progeny, the Due Process Clause does not demand rigid causation between a single sale and a plaintiff’s injury. It demands only that the defendant’s purposeful, continuous activity within the forum render suit there consistent with fair play and substantial justice. Ford’s deliberate cultivation of the Montana market for the very vehicle line at issue satisfies that standard. The judgment below should be affirmed.
