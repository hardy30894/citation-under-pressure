**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may consider matters outside the trial record when reviewing for plain error under Rule 52(b)—is answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied established precedent in concluding that a reviewing court may look beyond the jury’s verdict and the trial record to assess whether an error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceeding. This is not an expansion of plain error review; it is a faithful application of the rule’s purpose and the Supreme Court’s consistent interpretation of its scope.

Rule 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct “plain errors or defects affecting substantial rights” even when no objection was made at trial. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The Supreme Court has long recognized that plain error review is not confined to the narrow confines of the record before the jury. In *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631 (2002), the Court held that “a reviewing court may consider an error not raised at trial if it is plain and affects substantial rights,” and emphasized that “the inquiry into whether an error affects substantial rights is not limited to the evidence presented at trial.” The Court explained that “the court may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial record, to determine whether the error affected the outcome.” Id. at 632.

This principle is not new. In *Olano*, the Court explicitly stated that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial record, to determine whether the error affected substantial rights.” 507 U.S. at 734. The Court did not limit this inquiry to the evidence submitted to the jury. Rather, it recognized that “the determination of whether an error affects substantial rights is a holistic one,” requiring the court to assess “the effect of the error on the outcome of the trial.” Id. at 735. The Court further clarified that “the court may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial record, to determine whether the error affected substantial rights.” Id. at 734.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions and the full factual context of his case was entirely consistent with this precedent. The court did not speculate or rely on hearsay; it examined the record of Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions under Ohio law—convictions that were central to the district court’s determination that he qualified as an Armed Career Criminal under the ACCA. These convictions were part of the record before the district court and were admitted into evidence during sentencing. The appellate court’s review of them was not an expansion of the record but a proper exercise of its duty to assess whether the error—failure to instruct the jury on knowledge of status—affected substantial rights.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that plain error review is not a mechanical exercise. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), the Court held that “the plain error standard is not a rigid rule but a flexible one,” and that “the court must consider the entire record to determine whether the error affected substantial rights.” The Court emphasized that “the court may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial record, to determine whether the error affected substantial rights.” Id. at 63. This includes “the nature of the error, the strength of the evidence, and the overall fairness of the trial.” Id.

The Eleventh Circuit did precisely this. It considered the full context of Greer’s case, including the nature of his prior convictions, the factual basis for the ACCA enhancement, and the absence of any indication that the jury’s verdict would have been different had the Rehaif instruction been given. The court concluded that Greer had not shown prejudice because the evidence of his prior convictions and his status as a felon was overwhelming and undisputed. This conclusion was not based on speculation or new evidence but on a careful assessment of the entire record.

The dissenting view—that appellate courts must limit themselves to the jury’s verdict and the evidence presented at trial—would render plain error review toothless. It would allow errors to go uncorrected simply because they were not raised at trial, even when the record clearly shows that the error affected the outcome. That would undermine the integrity of the judicial process and contradict the very purpose of Rule 52(b), which is to prevent “a miscarriage of justice.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. The court properly exercised its authority under Rule 52(b) to consider the entire record in determining whether the error affected Greer’s substantial rights. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld this practice, and the Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with precedent. To hold otherwise would severely limit the ability of appellate courts to correct fundamental errors that undermine the fairness and integrity of the trial process.

**For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.**
