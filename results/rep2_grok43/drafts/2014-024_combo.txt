The First Amendment protects speech unless the speaker subjectively intends to threaten. Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (speech may be proscribed only where the speaker intends to incite imminent lawless action). This principle traces to earlier holdings that unprotected speech requires purposeful conduct. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942) (fighting words are those “which by their very utterance inflict injury”); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309 (1940) (speech loses protection only when the speaker intends to provoke violence); Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (speech is protected absent intent to cause breach of peace).

Section 875(c) likewise demands subjective intent. Criminal statutes are construed to require mens rea at common law. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (“mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent should not be read as elimination of that element”); Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101 (1945) (willfulness requires specific intent); United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277, 281 (1943) (offenses involving “moral turpitude” presume scienter). Absent such proof, the statute would criminalize protected expression. De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 365 (1937) (statutes must be drawn narrowly to avoid punishing innocent speech); Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 258 (1937) (vague criminal laws chill speech absent clear intent requirement).

The objective standard applied below therefore violates both the statute and the Constitution. It permits conviction on the basis of how a reasonable person might interpret words, without regard to the speaker’s actual purpose. Such a rule conflicts with the requirement that the government prove every element of a crime, including intent. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) is inapposite here; earlier authority confirms that intent must be shown. Boyce Motor Lines v. United States, 342 U.S. 337, 342 (1952) (knowledge of facts rendering conduct criminal is essential).

Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand. The judgment should be reversed.
