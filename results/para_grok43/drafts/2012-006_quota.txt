The Due Process Clause requires the government to prove every element of a conspiracy offense, including the defendant's membership during the limitations period, beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must establish that the accused remained a member within the statutory window. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 399 (1957) (government must prove "that the conspiracy, as contemplated, continued" into the limitations period).

Withdrawal terminates a defendant's liability for subsequent acts of coconspirators. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). The government therefore bears the burden to prove ongoing membership rather than requiring the defendant to negate it. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910) (liability "depends upon whether the conspiracy is continuing"); see also United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205, 210 (1940) (proof of agreement and participation must be shown).

This allocation follows from the principle that the prosecution must prove the existence of a single, ongoing conspiracy encompassing the charged period. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 755 (1946); Blumenthal v. United States, 332 U.S. 539, 556-557 (1947). Evidence of withdrawal shifts the inquiry to whether the government has carried its burden on the element of continued membership. Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684, 704 (1975) (state may not shift burden on element of offense).

The same rule applies under the Fifth Amendment when the limitations period is at issue. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 215 (1977) (burden of persuasion remains with prosecution on elements). Requiring the government to prove membership within the limitations period after withdrawal evidence is introduced preserves the presumption of innocence and prevents conviction on stale conspiracies. See also Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510, 524 (1979) (instructions may not relieve prosecution of proving every element).

The court of appeals erred in placing any burden of persuasion on petitioners once they adduced evidence of withdrawal prior to the limitations period. The government was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners remained members of the charged conspiracy during the relevant time.
