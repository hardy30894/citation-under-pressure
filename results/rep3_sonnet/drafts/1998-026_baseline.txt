**ARGUMENT**

**I. A technical violation of Rule 32(a)(2) does not, by itself, entitle a federal prisoner to relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255.**

This Court has long held that not every procedural misstep in a criminal proceeding warrants collateral relief. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Court considered a comparable claim—that a district court's failure to comply with the predecessor to Rule 32(a)(2) required vacating a sentence—and held that the omission, standing alone, was not a proper basis for relief. The Court explained that Rule 32(a) is designed to secure a right, and that the failure to follow the Rule is best remedied not through automatic vacatur but through an inquiry into whether the defendant was actually deprived of that right. *Id.* at 428.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), holding that a "formal violation" of Rule 11, unaccompanied by a showing of prejudice, does not warrant collateral relief. The Court emphasized that collateral review exists to correct "fundamental defect[s] which inherently result[] in a complete miscarriage of justice," not to police technical noncompliance with procedural rules. *Id.* at 783 (quoting *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428). The same logic controls here. Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant knows he may appeal; it does not exist to create an independent entitlement to a particular sentencing-day script, divorced from whether the defendant in fact possessed the knowledge the Rule is designed to protect.

**II. Because Peguero already knew of his right to appeal, the District Court's omission caused no prejudice and Rule 32(a)(2)'s purpose was fully served.**

The District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that although it failed to give the required advice, Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is decisive. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is not ritual for its own sake; it is to make certain that criminal defendants are not deprived of the appellate process through ignorance of their rights. Where, as here, that knowledge independently exists, the Rule's objective has been achieved notwithstanding the court's oversight.

This Court's harmless-error jurisprudence confirms that outcome. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court held that an error is harmless when it does not affect the substantial rights of the parties, and that reviewing courts must ask "whether the error itself had substantial influence." *Id.* at 764–65. Peguero's substantial rights were not affected: he possessed, independent of any court advisement, the very information the Rule aims to supply. A defendant who already knows he may appeal is in the same position as one who received the advisement—he suffers no loss of the opportunity to appeal because of the court's omission.

*United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), further supports application of a prejudice requirement to collateral claims of this kind. There, the Court required a showing of "cause and actual prejudice" before permitting collateral relief for a procedural default, explaining that collateral review is not "a substitute for direct appeal" and should not be triggered by errors that did not work to the petitioner's actual disadvantage. *Id.* at 165, 167–68. Peguero cannot show actual prejudice, because the sentencing court's silence changed nothing about his access to appellate review; he elected, with full knowledge of his right, not to appeal.

**III. Petitioner's proposed automatic-vacatur rule is inconsistent with this Court's precedent and would produce a windfall untethered to any actual injury.**

Petitioner asks this Court to adopt a rule under which any Rule 32(a)(2) violation, regardless of the defendant's actual knowledge, mandates relief. That approach cannot be reconciled with *Hill* and *Timmreck*, both of which rejected precisely this kind of formalistic, prejudice-free theory of collateral relief. It would also produce an anomalous result: a defendant who is fully informed of his appellate rights by defense counsel, family, or prior experience with the criminal justice system—as Peguero was—would receive a remedy identical to one afforded a defendant who was genuinely left in the dark. Nothing in Rule 32(a)(2)'s text or purpose supports elevating the mechanics of judicial notice above the substance of the right being protected.

Moreover, an automatic-vacatur rule would ignore the "harmless-error inquiry" that governs analogous Rule 11 and Rule 32 claims and would instead treat sentencing-day formalities as jurisdictional prerequisites—a status this Court has never assigned them. *See Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 783–84. Respondent submits that the proper standard, consistent with *Hill*, *Timmreck*, *Kotteakos*, and *Frady*, asks only whether the defendant was deprived of the right the Rule protects. Because the District Court found that Peguero was not so deprived, the Third Circuit correctly affirmed the denial of relief.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
