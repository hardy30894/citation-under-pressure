The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Deportation statutes must be construed strictly in favor of the alien. Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). The provision at issue, like its predecessors, speaks only of detention “pending eventual deportation” or “until removal,” language that presupposes an end point rather than perpetual custody. United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 198 (1957); see also United States ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi, 287 U.S. 129, 131 (1932).

Indefinite detention without criminal trial constitutes punishment. Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896). The statute therefore cannot be read to permit confinement that continues after removal has become impossible. When no country will accept the alien, the removal period has run its course and further incarceration exceeds statutory authority. United States ex rel. Mensevich v. Tod, 264 U.S. 134, 136 (1924).

Congress has long distinguished between detention during the removal period and subsequent supervision. The 1952 Act, for example, subjects aliens to supervision after six months rather than continued imprisonment. Witkovich, 353 U.S. at 199. Regulations implementing that scheme require periodic reporting and other conditions short of custody, confirming that indefinite detention is not contemplated. Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 538 (1952).

The same limitation appears in related provisions governing excluded aliens. Parole into the United States does not convert exclusion into entry, and detention remains tied to the possibility of prompt removal. Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185, 190 (1958); Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193, 195 (1958). Where removal is no longer feasible, continued detention loses its statutory justification.

Nor does criminal history alone justify perpetual confinement. Statutes authorizing deportation for aggravated felonies still require that detention serve the purpose of effecting removal within a reasonable time. Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685, 689 (1957). Once that purpose cannot be accomplished, the alien must be released under supervision. Phillippides v. Day, 283 U.S. 48, 50 (1931).

Accordingly, the statute authorizes detention only for the period reasonably necessary to secure removal. When that period has elapsed without prospect of deportation, continued custody is unauthorized.
