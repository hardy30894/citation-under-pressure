# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 811(h) DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL

Article I vests "[a]ll legislative Powers" in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. But this Court has long recognized that Congress may enlist the assistance of coordinate branches so long as it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This "intelligible principle" doctrine has permitted broad delegations because "in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989).

Section 811(h) easily satisfies this standard. Congress specified the precise goal the Attorney General must pursue—avoiding "an imminent hazard to the public safety"—and required consideration of enumerated statutory factors concerning a substance's history, pattern, and scope of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). Congress further cabined the delegation with procedural safeguards: notice in the Federal Register, mandatory transmission to the Secretary of Health and Human Services for comment, and a firm durational limit of one year, extendable by only six months. *Id.* § 811(h)(2), (4). These constraints track precisely the kind of statutory guidance this Court approved in *Mistretta*, where Congress specified "the most important sentencing factors" the Sentencing Commission must weigh and imposed outer boundaries on its discretion. 488 U.S. at 374-75.

Petitioners' reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those decisions—the only two in which this Court has invalidated a delegation—condemned statutes providing no criteria whatsoever to guide executive action. In *Panama Refining*, the statute "did not declare any policy" and established no "criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President could approve virtually any industry-proposed code without any legislative standard cabining his discretion. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h) is nothing like those unbounded grants; it contains express findings requirements, temporal limits, and consultation mandates entirely absent from the NIRA provisions struck down in 1935.

Nor does the criminal nature of the consequences flowing from scheduling decisions demand a more exacting non-delegation standard. This Court sustained comparably broad delegations bearing on liberty interests in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), upholding delegated price-fixing authority enforced through criminal penalties where Congress supplied only general standards of "fair and equitable" pricing, *id.* at 423, and in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), sustaining delegated authority over bank conservatorships despite the absence of narrowly drawn standards, because "well-known and generally acceptable standards" governed the regulated field. *Id.* at 250. The same is true here: temporary scheduling operates against a backdrop of established pharmacological and enforcement criteria under § 811(c), and rapidly evolving designer-drug technology—like the wartime exigencies in *Yakus*—justifies expedited executive action. See *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 432 (delegation may reflect "the rapidity with which the situation may change").

Finally, the unavailability of pre-enforcement judicial review of temporary scheduling orders, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), does not render the delegation infirm. Defendants remain free to challenge the constitutionality of the delegation itself in criminal proceedings, as petitioners have done here, and as courts have uniformly permitted. See *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987). This preserved avenue of constitutional review is fully consistent with *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), where the availability of review was treated as one relevant, but non-exclusive, factor bearing on the validity of a delegation—not as an independent constitutional requirement. *Id.* at 542-44.

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL WAS AUTHORIZED TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Controlled Substances Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that the Attorney General "may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." This broad, unqualified grant of subdelegation authority contains no textual exception for temporary scheduling functions under § 811(h), and none should be inferred. Where Congress uses expansive language in authorizing subdelegation, courts should not engraft limitations that Congress did not itself impose. Cf. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372 (Congress may employ "broad general directives" in structuring administrative authority).

The Attorney General's 1987 amendment to 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended," was therefore a proper and authorized exercise of the subdelegation power Congress conferred in § 871(a). The DEA Administrator's temporary scheduling of 4-methylaminorex followed directly and lawfully from this valid regulatory chain of authority.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
