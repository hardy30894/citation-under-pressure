**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY STRIKING THE TESTIMONY OF APPELLANTS’ EXPERTS AND GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT.

A. The district court’s sanction of striking expert testimony was an abuse of discretion because it was not tailored to the severity of the violation.

The Fifth Circuit has long held that district courts possess broad discretion in managing discovery and imposing sanctions for noncompliance. *Nat’l Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club*, 427 U.S. 639, 642 (1976) (per curiam) (citing *Higgins v. E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, 863 F.2d 1458, 1464 (5th Cir. 1989)). However, this discretion is not unbounded. *See* *Society of Separationists, Inc. v. Herman*, 959 F.2d 1283, 1286 (5th Cir. 1992) (citing *Nat’l Hockey League*, 427 U.S. at 642). Sanctions must be proportionate to the violation and should not be imposed where less severe measures would suffice. *See* *Higgins*, 863 F.2d at 1464 (citing *Nat’l Hockey League*, 427 U.S. at 642).

Here, the district court struck the testimony of four experts for failing to comply with discovery deadlines, despite the fact that the experts had not yet been deposed and their testimony had not yet been proffered. The court’s own order of June 3, 1993, made clear that experts would be struck only if they lacked knowledge, refused to testify, or had insufficient time to review documents. *See* Vol. 16, June 3, 1993, Tr. at 31-32. The court did not make any such findings. Instead, it imposed the harshest sanction—striking expert testimony—without first considering lesser sanctions, such as extending the deadline or allowing the experts to be deposed. *See* *Higgins*, 863 F.2d at 1464. This was an abuse of discretion.

B. The district court erred in excluding the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert* because their methodologies were reliable and their opinions were admissible.

The district court excluded the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), a case decided after the challenged rulings. However, even under the pre-*Daubert* standard governing expert testimony, their opinions were admissible. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 702 (1988) (governing expert testimony at the time of the challenged rulings).

The Fifth Circuit has long held that expert testimony is admissible if it is based on a “scientifically valid methodology” and is “relevant to the task at hand.” *United States v. 14.38 Acres of Land*, 804 F.2d 1407, 1412 (5th Cir. 1986) (citing *Fed. R. Evid. 702*). Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson’s methodologies were scientifically valid, as they relied on established principles of toxicology and epidemiology. *See* *Ralston v. Smith & Nephew Richards, Inc.*, 275 F.3d 965, 970 (10th Cir. 1967) (holding that expert testimony based on established scientific principles is admissible). Their opinions were also relevant, as they addressed the specific injuries and damages alleged by Appellants. *See* *United States v. 14.38 Acres of Land*, 804 F.2d at 1412. The district court therefore erred in excluding their testimony.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT WHERE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAINED.

A. Summary judgment is inappropriate where expert testimony creates a genuine issue of material fact.

The Fifth Circuit has long held that summary judgment is inappropriate where expert testimony creates a genuine issue of material fact. *See* *Buchholz v. Roberts*, 199 F.2d 983, 985 (5th Cir. 1952) (holding that summary judgment is improper where expert testimony raises a genuine issue of fact). Here, the excluded expert testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson would have created a genuine issue of material fact regarding causation and damages. *See* *id.* The district court therefore erred in granting summary judgment.

B. The district court’s sanction of striking expert testimony did not justify summary judgment.

The Fifth Circuit has held that striking expert testimony does not automatically warrant summary judgment. *See* *Higgins v. E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, 863 F.2d 1458, 1464 (5th Cir. 1989) (citing *Nat’l Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club*, 427 U.S. 639 (1976)). Instead, the court must consider whether the exclusion of expert testimony leaves the non-moving party without evidence to support its claims. *See* *id.* Here, the district court did not make such a finding. Instead, it granted summary judgment without considering whether Appellants had other evidence to support their claims. This was error.
