**Argument**

I. **Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date.**

This Court should begin with the presumption that statutes operate prospectively unless Congress has clearly indicated otherwise. In **United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160 (1928)**, the Court held that a statute should not be given retroactive effect absent clear congressional command. The same principle was applied in **Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529 (1922)**, where the Court explained that retrospective operation is disfavored because it may upset settled expectations. Here, Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States in 1982, long before the 1997 reinstatement provision became effective. Nothing in the statutory text unmistakably commands application to pre-enactment reentries.

That reading is reinforced by **United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399 (1806)**, which recognized that statutes are ordinarily construed as prospective unless the legislature speaks clearly. Likewise, **White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545 (1903)**, instructed that courts should avoid attributing retroactive reach where the statutory language admits a prospective construction. Section 241(a)(5) contains no clear statement that Congress intended to strip aliens of legal consequences based solely on conduct completed years before enactment.

The government’s position would also offend the longstanding rule that statutes should not be construed to attach new legal consequences to prior acts. In **Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386 (1798)**, Justice Chase explained that retroactive legislation is suspect when it “changes the legal character of an act.” Although Calder addressed ex post facto principles, its reasoning has long informed statutory interpretation in civil cases. See **Satterlee v. Matthewson, 27 U.S. (2 Pet.) 380 (1829)**. Reentry before 1997 was a completed event; applying § 241(a)(5) to that event imposes a new and harsher legal regime than existed when petitioner reentered.

Congress knew how to speak clearly when it intended retroactive application. In **The Lockerbie Case, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 168 (1869)**, the Court emphasized that legislative intent must be apparent before past conduct is reached. No such clarity appears here. At most, the statute governs future conduct after its effective date, not a reentry that occurred fifteen years earlier.

II. **Even if the statute reaches pre-1997 reentries, that application is impermissibly retroactive.**

Retroactivity analysis asks whether the new statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” See **Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994)**. That formulation reflects a principle deeply rooted in earlier decisions. In **Societe Francaise des Asphaltes v. New York, 223 U.S. 321 (1912)**, the Court rejected a construction that would have imposed new burdens on past transactions. In **McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102 (1898)**, the Court likewise refused to read a statute as impairing vested legal positions absent clear legislative intent.

Fernandez-Vargas’s position in 1997 was materially different from his position after the statute’s enactment. He had already lived in the country for years and was eligible to seek relief under then-existing law. The reinstatement provision eliminated that possibility after the fact. That is the very kind of legal change the Court has long disfavored. See **Graham v. Goodcell, 282 U.S. 409 (1931)**; **Dodge v. Board of Education, 302 U.S. 74 (1937)**. Although Congress may alter immigration rules prospectively, it may not do so by retrospectively destroying previously available avenues of relief based on a past reentry.

The government may argue that petitioner had no vested right to discretionary relief. But this Court has never held that absence of a vested right ends the retroactivity inquiry. To the contrary, in **United States v. St. Louis, San Francisco & Texas Railway Co., 270 U.S. 1 (1926)**, the Court made clear that retroactivity concerns arise whenever a statute changes the legal effect of antecedent conduct. Here, petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry is the antecedent conduct; the statute’s elimination of relief is the new disability.

The better reading, consistent with longstanding interpretive principles, is that § 241(a)(5) applies only to illegal reentries occurring after its effective date. That construction avoids retroactive impairment, respects congressional silence, and preserves the settled rule that statutes are construed prospectively absent unmistakable contrary intent. See **United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399 (1806)**; **Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529 (1922)**; **United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160 (1928)**.

III. **At minimum, the statute should be construed to avoid serious retroactivity concerns.**

Where one construction creates serious retroactive consequences and another avoids them, courts choose the latter. See **The Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103 (1801)**; **United States v. Burr, 25 F. Cas. 30 (C.C.D. Va. 1807)**. That settled canon compels a prospective reading here. Because Fernandez-Vargas reentered before 1997, § 241(a)(5) should not have been applied to bar his adjustment application or to reinstate his old removal order.

For these reasons, the petition should be granted.
