ARGUMENT

The decision of the Railroad Retirement Board denying Petitioner’s request to reopen his 2006 benefits claim is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Board’s determination conclusively resolved the matter, leaving no further administrative remedies available to Petitioner. This Court has long recognized that administrative decisions—including denials of reopening—are presumptively reviewable when they mark the culmination of the agency’s decisional process and produce direct and immediate consequences for the claimant.

I. The Board’s Denial of Reopening Is a Final Decision

A decision is “final” when it “ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.” *Catlin v. United States*, 324 U.S. 229, 233 (1945). The Board’s refusal to reopen Petitioner’s 2006 claim meets that standard: the hearing officer concluded that the four-year window for reopening under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2 had expired, and the Board had no further proceedings to conduct. That determination “marks the ‘consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process’” and is “one by which ‘rights or obligations have been determined,’ or from which ‘legal consequences will flow.’” *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967) (quoting *FPC v. Idaho Power Co.*, 344 U.S. 17, 20 (1952)). Here, the denial foreclosed any possibility of revisiting the benefits decision, a direct legal consequence for Petitioner.

In *Rochester Telephone Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125, 130 (1939), this Court held that a denial by the Federal Communications Commission of a petition for modification of a prior order was a final, reviewable order because it “determined finally the right of petitioner to have its application considered.” The same reasoning applies here: the Board’s denial finally determined that Petitioner could not obtain reconsideration of his 2006 claim.

II. The Railroad Retirement Act Does Not Exclude Reopening Decisions from Review

Section 355(f) provides for review of “final decisions” of the Board. Nothing in the Act excludes decisions denying reopening. When Congress intends to bar review of a particular class of agency actions, it says so explicitly. Cf. *Cheng Fan Kwok v. INS*, 392 U.S. 206, 216 (1968) (holding that denials of discretionary relief are reviewable because Congress did not expressly preclude review). The Board’s regulation governing reopening does not purport to insulate its decision from judicial scrutiny. To the contrary, this Court has long recognized that the Board’s adjudications are subject to judicial review. *Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad*, 295 U.S. 330, 347 (1935) (reviewing constitutionality of Board’s action); *Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167, 172 (1936) (holding that claims for annuities under analogous retirement statute are justiciable). The Court in *Dismuke* emphasized that “the adjudication of claims under [the Retirement Act]” is not committed solely to administrative officers. 297 U.S. at 172. That principle applies fully here.

III. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on *Roberts* Is Inconsistent with Supreme Court Precedent

The court below followed its prior decision in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which held that § 355(f) limits review to decisions on the merits after exhaustion. But *Roberts* relied heavily on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), a Social Security case that postdates the pre-1970 authorities governing this action. The proper analysis must be guided by this Court’s earlier decisions, which establish that agency decisions denying reopening are reviewable when they finally dispose of a party’s rights.

In *SEC v. Louisiana Pub. Serv. Comm’n*, 353 U.S. 368, 375 (1957), the Court recognized that a denial of a petition to reopen “finally disposed of the matter” and could be challenged, even though the underlying original order could not be revisited. Similarly, *International Union v. Eagle-Picher Mining & Smelting Co.*, 325 U.S. 335, 338–39 (1945), demonstrates that courts of appeals have jurisdiction to consider an agency’s own motion to reopen a prior decree—implying that a denial of such a motion is equally reviewable. Moreover, in *Pennsylvania Railroad v. Day*, 360 U.S. 548, 554 (1959), this Court observed that administrative determinations under the Railway Labor Act are subject to court review when they foreclose further agency action. Here, the Board’s denial foreclosed any further administrative avenue.

IV. The Board’s Decision Is Not Committed to Agency Discretion

The Board’s regulations authorize reopening for “new and material evidence” or “administrative error.” 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. The hearing officer applied those criteria and found them unsatisfied. This was a judgment on legal and factual standards, not an unreviewable exercise of discretion. Cf. *Dodge v. Board of Education*, 302 U.S. 74, 78 (1937) (recognizing that courts may review whether an agency’s decision complied with statutory requirements). The Board’s decision is therefore subject to meaningful judicial review to ensure compliance with its own rules and the Act.

In *Robertson v. Chambers*, 341 U.S. 37, 41 (1951), the Court held that even decisions by military review boards are reviewable when they involve statutory interpretation or procedural regularity. The same principle governs here. The Board’s refusal to reopen based on a time bar and lack of error is precisely the type of determination that courts can and do review.

V. Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for review of the Board’s decision on the merits.
