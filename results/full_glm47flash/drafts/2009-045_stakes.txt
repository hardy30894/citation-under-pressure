**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY ENHANCING THE FEE BASED ON THE QUALITY OF COUNSEL’S PERFORMANCE RATHER THAN SPECIFIC EVIDENCE OF EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS.**

The Supreme Court has firmly established that an attorney’s fee award may not be enhanced based on the quality of representation or the results obtained unless the applicant offers specific evidence demonstrating that the lodestar figure failed to provide a reasonable award. In *Blum v. Stenson*, the Court held that the "quality of representation" and the "results obtained" cannot serve as an independent basis for increasing the basic fee award only because the attorneys offered no evidence of an exceptional result or superior performance. 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984). This requirement for "specific evidence" was reiterated in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, where the Supreme Court stated that upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible in certain "rare" and "exceptional" cases, but only when supported by both "specific evidence" on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts. 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986).

Here, the district court failed to meet this stringent standard. The court enhanced the fee based on its own subjective observations of counsel’s performance, rather than on specific evidence in the record. The district judge admitted that he was motivated to enhance the award by his "own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel." *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D.Ga. 2006). This reliance on the judge’s personal experience and observations constitutes an abuse of discretion. The Supreme Court has made clear that a district judge may not act as a juror, weighing the quality of a lawyer’s work without the objective, evidentiary foundation required by *Blum* and *Delaware Valley*.

**II. THE "RARE CASE" EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY BECAUSE THE DISTRICT COURT LACKED THE NECESSARY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE.**

While the Supreme Court has acknowledged that an upward adjustment may be justified in the "rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged" and resulted in "exceptional" success, *Delaware Valley*, 478 U.S. at 565, such an exception is not a license for district judges to enhance fees based on their own subjective impressions. The district court’s findings in this case were conclusory and unreviewable. The court did not identify specific instances of superior performance or exceptional results that the lodestar calculation failed to capture. Instead, the court simply concluded that the "superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." *Kenny A.*, 454 F.Supp.2d at 1286.

Because the district court did not base its enhancement on specific evidence of exceptional success, but rather on the judge’s own testimony regarding the quality of counsel’s work, the award must be vacated. The Eleventh Circuit should not affirm a decision that allows district judges to circumvent the Supreme Court’s mandate for specific evidence by relying on their own personal observations of the attorneys' performance.
