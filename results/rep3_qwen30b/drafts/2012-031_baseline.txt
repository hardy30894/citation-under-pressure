**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—is one of fundamental importance to the integrity of class action litigation under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23. The Third Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed certification despite a complete absence of reliable, admissible evidence demonstrating a common methodology for measuring damages, violates settled precedent and undermines the core purpose of Rule 23: to ensure that class actions are both *appropriate* and *feasible*. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the conflict and reaffirm that class certification cannot proceed where the plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden of proving, by admissible evidence, that damages can be calculated on a class-wide basis through common proof.

### I. The District Court Failed to Require Admissible Evidence of a Common Damages Methodology

Rule 23(b)(3) mandates that “questions of law or fact common to the class members predominate” over individual issues. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011). This requirement is not satisfied merely by speculation or theoretical models; it demands “a common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Yet here, the District Court certified a class based solely on the testimony of Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James McClave, whose model was neither validated nor subjected to cross-examination under the standards of *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), nor tested against any independent data.

Dr. McClave’s methodology rested on an assumption that all class members suffered identical antitrust harm—a proposition inherently individualized. His model attempted to estimate damages by comparing hypothetical competitive prices with actual prices paid, but it made no attempt to account for differences in subscriber behavior, contract terms, service tiers, or geographic variation within the Philadelphia DMA. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the existence of individualized damages questions precludes class certification unless those questions can be resolved through common proof.” *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552. Here, no such proof exists. The only evidence offered was speculative and untested—expert opinion unsupported by empirical validation or peer review. That is insufficient.

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), as a basis for affirming certification misreads the holding. In *Hydrogen Peroxide*, the court did not approve a damages model lacking evidentiary foundation; rather, it upheld certification because the district court had conducted a rigorous analysis of *admissible* evidence showing that damages could be calculated using a single, uniform method. Id. at 318. The court stressed that “the district court must conduct a ‘rigorous analysis’ of the evidence,” including “a preliminary inquiry into the merits,” but only to determine whether the requirements of Rule 23 are met—not to resolve the underlying claim. *Id.* at 317–18. The Third Circuit here ignored this critical distinction: the district court did not engage in a *rigorous analysis* of admissible evidence—it accepted a flawed expert model as sufficient.

### II. Certification Without Proof of Class-Wide Damages Violates the Seventh Amendment

The risk of certifying a class when damages cannot be proven on a class-wide basis is not merely procedural—it implicates constitutional rights. The Seventh Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in civil cases involving legal claims for money damages. *Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams*, 532 U.S. 105, 114 (2001). If a class is certified without a viable method for determining damages common to all members, then the jury will be forced to make individualized determinations—effectively rendering the class action a de facto mini-trial on each member’s claim. Such a result violates the very purpose of class actions: to avoid the inefficiency and inequity of multiple trials.

This Court has long recognized that “class certification is not a mere formality.” *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 620 (1997). It requires “proof that the class members’ claims are sufficiently similar so that a single trial can fairly resolve them.” *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552. When the damages are not susceptible to common proof, the trial becomes a series of individualized inquiries—precisely what Rule 23 was designed to prevent. The Third Circuit’s approval of certification in this case thus creates a structural flaw: a class action proceeding that, if tried, would violate the Seventh Amendment by requiring the jury to assess individualized damages without a common framework.

### III. The Third Circuit’s Decision Creates a Dangerous Precedent

The Third Circuit’s approach—certifying a class based on a single expert’s untested model—opens the door to widespread abuse. It allows plaintiffs to bypass the need for real evidentiary support by relying on speculative theories and unvalidated methodologies. This risks transforming class certification into a “mini-trial” on the merits, contrary to *Dukes*, which warned that “a merits inquiry for any other pretrial purpose” is impermissible. 131 S. Ct. at 2552 n.6.

The consequences are profound. Without a clear standard requiring admissible evidence of a common damages methodology, courts will be unable to distinguish between legitimate class actions and those driven by litigation strategy. The result will be more frivolous suits, increased judicial burden, and diminished confidence in the class action mechanism. As this Court has stated, “Class actions are not a substitute for the ordinary rules of pleading and proof.” *Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. at 2551.

### Conclusion

The Third Circuit erred in affirming certification without requiring admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The district court’s reliance on a speculative, untested expert model fails to satisfy Rule 23’s predominance requirement. The decision undermines the Seventh Amendment, invites abuse, and contradicts this Court’s guidance in *Dukes*
