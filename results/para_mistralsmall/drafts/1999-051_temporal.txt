**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse the default of another habeas claim.**

This Court has long held that a federal habeas court may not review a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim unless the prisoner demonstrates both “cause” for the default and “actual prejudice” resulting therefrom. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 417 (1963). The Court has further recognized that ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause to excuse a procedural default, but only where the ineffective assistance prevented the prisoner from raising the underlying claim in the state courts. *Id.* at 432-33. Thus, the cause exception is limited to situations where the very counsel whose ineffectiveness is alleged was responsible for the default. *Id.* at 433.

Here, Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim is itself procedurally defaulted, as the Ohio courts dismissed his application to reopen his direct appeal as untimely. The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that an ineffective-assistance claim—even if defaulted—may excuse the default of another claim. This Court has never extended the cause exception to permit a defaulted claim to excuse another default. To the contrary, the Court has emphasized that the cause requirement must be strictly construed to prevent prisoners from circumventing state procedural rules. *See generally id.* at 438-39 (noting that federal habeas review is not a “second appeal” and that state procedural rules must be respected).

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), where the Court refused to excuse a procedural default based on a claim of ignorance of the law. The Court reasoned that such a rule would “fritter away” the finality of state court judgments and undermine the orderly administration of justice. *Id.* at 179. The same principle applies here: permitting a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would similarly undermine the finality of state court judgments and encourage prisoners to bypass state procedural rules.

**II. The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s precedents governing the scope of federal habeas review.**

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that federal habeas corpus is not a substitute for a state prisoner’s direct appeal. *See, e.g., Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953) (noting that federal habeas review is “not in derogation of state authority” but “in aid of it”). The Court has emphasized that federal courts must respect state procedural rules and may not “federalize” state criminal proceedings. *See id.* at 487-88.

The Sixth Circuit’s holding does precisely that by allowing a defaulted claim to excuse another default, thereby permitting federal habeas review of claims that the state courts have already declined to consider on procedural grounds. This approach undermines the principles of comity and federalism that govern federal habeas review. *See id.* at 486-87 (explaining that federal habeas review is “limited” and must be exercised with “scrupulous regard for the rightful independence of state governments”).

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s holding would encourage prisoners to bypass state procedural rules.**

This Court has long recognized that federal habeas review must not be used to circumvent state procedural rules. *See, e.g., Ex parte Spencer*, 228 U.S. 652, 660 (1913) (noting that federal habeas corpus is not a “writ of error” to review state court judgments). The Sixth Circuit’s holding would encourage prisoners to default claims in state court and then rely on a separate, defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the first default. This would frustrate the orderly administration of justice and undermine the finality of state court judgments.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse the default of another habeas claim. The Court’s precedents require strict adherence to state procedural rules and prohibit the federalization of state criminal proceedings. The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with these principles and should be rejected.
