**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedural Default That Is Not the Product of the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Bar Reliance on the Underlying Constitutional Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

This Court has long recognized that the writ of habeas corpus is not to be shackled by mechanical procedural rules where a petitioner's own conduct did not produce the default. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that "the federal habeas judge may in his discretion deny relief" only to a petitioner "who has deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts." *Id.* at 438. The touchstone is the petitioner's personal, intelligent choice to forgo a state remedy—not the tactical or negligent omissions of counsel. Carpenter did not himself choose to abandon his sufficiency claim; his appellate lawyer did. Under *Fay*, that distinction is decisive. A default should not be treated as though it were the petitioner's own considered relinquishment of a claim merely because appointed counsel failed to raise it; that would attribute to the petitioner a choice he never made and a forfeiture he never intended.

**The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a mere procedural nicety; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court explained that "government hiring lawyers to prosecute and defendants who have the money hiring lawyers to defend are the strongest indications of the wide-spread belief that lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries." *Id.* at 344. That same Term, in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), the Court extended the guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that a State may not condition the vindication of federal rights on the fortuity of retained counsel while indigent appellants go unrepresented. *Id.* at 357–58. Carpenter's claim—that his appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the kind of constitutional deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A default of that claim, itself born of the same disabling failure of counsel, should not extinguish its capacity to explain and excuse the companion default it caused.

**Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require an Independently Exhausted Cause Claim Where the Default Flows from Counsel's Own Failure**

The exhaustion requirement exists, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), so that a state prisoner's federal claim is ordinarily entertained by a federal court only after the remedies available in the state courts, including appellate remedies, have first been exhausted. *Id.* at 116–18. It is a rule of comity, not a rule of forfeiture. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise described exhaustion as resting on considerations of "comity between the state and federal sovereignties," *id.* at 204, not on a desire to bar meritorious federal claims. Ohio had every opportunity to correct counsel's failure when Carpenter sought reopening of his appeal; the state court declined only on a timeliness ground unrelated to the merits. Nothing in the comity rationale of *Hawk* or *Darr* requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself first survive a second, independent gauntlet of exhaustion before it may serve its traditional function of explaining a related default.

**This Court's Broader Habeas Corpus Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Rules**

The great writ has always been treated with a flexibility that guards against the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), Justice Holmes made clear that this Court retains authority to examine whether a state proceeding satisfied the demands of due process notwithstanding the state's own procedural rulings, and that constitutional rights cannot be defeated by the failure of state corrective machinery to operate. *Id.* at 91–92. And in *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), the Court cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake," *id.* at 8, and that abuse-of-writ principles must be applied with equitable discretion rather than rigid formalism, *id.* at 17–18. Likewise, in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), the Court confirmed that federal habeas courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims, rather than treating state-court determinations as automatically dispositive of a petitioner's entitlement to relief. *Id.* at 312–13.

Taken together, *Fay*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Townsend* establish a consistent principle: procedural defaults attributable to counsel's own failings, rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice, should not be permitted to compound themselves into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient constitutional substance to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
