**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test disregards the plain text of OCSLA and this Court's settled rules of statutory interpretation. Section 1333(b) extends LHWCA benefits only to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That phrase imposes a geographic situs requirement: the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Statutory Text Requires an Injury on the Shelf**

The controlling language is unambiguous. Congress did not compensate all injuries "related to" or "arising out of" shelf operations; it compensated injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf," 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). When statutory language is clear, a court must apply it as written. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). This Court has likewise instructed that the meaning of a statute must be sought in the language in which it is expressed. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The statutory phrase "occurring as the result of" connects the injury to the locus of the operations—the shelf. An injury onshore is not an injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the shelf; it occurs at a different place.

**II. The Structure of OCSLA Confirms the Geographic Limitation**

OCSLA contains two separate extension provisions. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) extends the law of the nearest state to "artificial islands and fixed structures" on the shelf. Section 1333(b), by contrast, extends LHWCA benefits only to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The deliberate difference in phrasing shows that Congress knew how to extend coverage to structures but chose to limit compensation to injuries on the shelf. *See FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941) (courts should give effect to differences in statutory language).

**III. Federal Offshore Jurisdiction Is Defined by Geographic Boundaries**

This Court has long recognized that federal authority over submerged lands is limited to the defined offshore area. *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947). OCSLA was enacted to govern activities on the outer Continental Shelf, 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a), and does not extend federal authority inland. *Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (federal laws are construed to apply within territorial limits absent contrary intent). Reading OCSLA to reach onshore injuries would transgress those boundaries.

**IV. Statutory Coverage Is Not Expanded Beyond Its Terms**

Although workers' compensation statutes are remedial, they remain subject to the plain meaning of their text. *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946). This Court has never countenanced reading a compensation statute to cover injuries outside the territory specified by Congress. From the earliest days, this Court has warned that courts must not extend statutes beyond their language. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test does precisely what *Wiltberger* condemned: it substitutes judicial policy for congressional text.

**V. Valladolid's Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility in California, miles from the outer Continental Shelf. Under the plain text of § 1333(b), his injury did not occur on the shelf. The Ninth Circuit's remand for application of a "substantial nexus" test must be reversed.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
