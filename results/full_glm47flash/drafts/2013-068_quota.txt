**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE IS A VALID TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION**

The First Amendment permits the Commonwealth to enact a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation designed to ensure safe passage to reproductive health care facilities. Under *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989), such regulations are subject to strict scrutiny and must be justified by a significant government interest, narrowly tailored to serve that interest, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication.

First, the statute is content-neutral. As the Supreme Court held in *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 488 (1988), a regulation is content-neutral if it is “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.” The Massachusetts law regulates conduct—prohibiting entry into a specific area—rather than speech itself. This distinction is critical, as the Supreme Court has noted that “the distinction between speech and conduct is fundamental.” *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000).

Second, the regulation is narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest. The Commonwealth’s interest in ensuring the safety and security of patients and clinic staff is substantial. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719. The 35-foot buffer zone is a reasonable means of achieving this goal without unduly restricting speech. The Supreme Court has upheld similar buffer zones as narrowly tailored, finding that they are “not substantially broader than necessary” to protect the flow of traffic. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719.

Third, the statute leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The First Circuit correctly observed that the law does not ban speech, but merely confines it to a specific area. *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 298 (1984). Petitioners retain the right to speak from a distance, distribute literature, and pray outside the buffer zone. As the Supreme Court stated in *Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414, 424 (1988), the government may restrict speech at a specific location so long as it leaves the speaker with “ample opportunity to be heard.”

**II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT PROPERLY APPLIED THE *HILL* FRAMEWORK AND REFUSED TO REVISIT FACIAL VALIDITY**

The First Circuit correctly applied the framework established in *Hill v. Colorado* and properly refused to revisit the facial validity of the statute based on subsequent Supreme Court decisions. *Hill* explicitly recognized sidewalks and areas outside healthcare clinics as “‘quintessential’ public forums.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. The First Circuit’s decision to uphold the Massachusetts statute as a valid time, place, and manner regulation is fully consistent with *Hill*.

The First Circuit also properly applied the law of the case doctrine, refusing to revisit the facial validity of the statute in light of *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 336 (2010); *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 453 (2011); and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552, 571 (2011). The First Circuit correctly noted that these cases “are no more than conventional First Amendment principles recited by the Supreme Court in the context of factual scenarios far different than the scenario at issue here.” The petitioners’ reliance on *Citizens United* does not justify a departure from the established *Hill* precedent. The *Hill* framework remains the controlling authority for evaluating buffer zones around healthcare facilities.
