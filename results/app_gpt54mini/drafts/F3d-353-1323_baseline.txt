**Argument**

The district court erred in dismissing for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. Under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, a foreign state is presumptively immune unless an enumerated exception applies, but the commercial-activity exception reaches not only private-style conduct by a sovereign, it also reaches conduct with a direct nexus to commercial activity carried on in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2); *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 610–14 (1992). At the pleading stage, Mr. Beg was required only to allege facts bringing Pakistan’s conduct within one of the statute’s three clauses, and the complaint does so. See *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 355–57 (1993); *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 461 U.S. 480, 489–91 (1983).

I. Pakistan’s conduct falls within § 1605(a)(2) because the case concerns commercial activity with a direct effect in the United States.

The Supreme Court has explained that “commercial” activity is determined by the nature of the act, not its purpose. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. A sovereign acts commercially when it engages in conduct “by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce.” Id. at 614–15. Here, Pakistan and the Government of Punjab allegedly did more than merely exercise sovereign authority by taking land. According to the complaint, they later sent a representative to the United States to offer a substitute parcel in exchange for the expropriated land. That alleged offer and attempted settlement is the kind of real-estate and property transaction in which private parties commonly engage, and it is commercially cognizable under *Weltover*. See id.; *Republic of Argentina v. NML Capital, Ltd.*, 573 U.S. 134, 141–42 (2014).

The district court erred by treating the entire controversy as an unreviewable sovereign taking. The FSIA asks whether the “action is based upon” commercial activity or an act in connection with such activity. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Court must focus on the gravamen of the suit, not merely the label attached by the foreign state. *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 356–58. Mr. Beg’s suit is based not only on the initial expropriation, but also on Pakistan’s later conduct concerning substitute land and compensation. That later conduct is quintessentially commercial because it involved negotiation over land transfer and compensation. See *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614–16.

II. Alternatively, Pakistan’s conduct caused a direct effect in the United States.

Section 1605(a)(2) also withdraws immunity where an act outside the United States causes a “direct effect in the United States.” The Supreme Court has held that a direct effect is one that follows “as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s . . . activity.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618 (internal quotation marks omitted). The direct-effect requirement does not demand a physical injury within the United States; a financial or legal consequence can suffice. Id. at 618–19; *Guevara v. Republic of Peru*, 468 F.3d 1289, 1296–97 (11th Cir. 2006) (en banc).

Mr. Beg alleges that Pakistan’s refusal to honor the property exchange and to compensate him left him remediless and forced him to seek compensation here after the Government itself directed dealings into the United States by sending a representative here. Where a foreign sovereign reaches into the United States to negotiate, offer, or facilitate a property resolution, the refusal to perform has a direct domestic consequence. See *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618–20; *Kensington Int’l Ltd. v. Itoua*, 505 F.3d 147, 155–56 (2d Cir. 2007). The district court’s contrary conclusion improperly discounted the U.S.-based negotiation alleged in the complaint.

III. The district court also erred by resolving disputed jurisdictional facts against the pro se plaintiff.

When FSIA jurisdiction turns on contested facts, a court must give the plaintiff the benefit of well-pleaded allegations unless the sovereign carries its burden to establish immunity. See *Butler v. Sukhoi Co.*, 579 F.3d 1307, 1312–13 (11th Cir. 2009); *Devengoechea v. Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela*, 889 F.3d 1213, 1223–24 (11th Cir. 2018). Mr. Beg alleged that Pakistan used the land for military housing, later negotiated a replacement parcel through a representative in the United States, and then refused to honor the exchange. Those allegations are enough to invoke jurisdictional discovery, at minimum, because they plausibly describe commercial dealings and a U.S.-based direct effect. See *Venetian Casino Resort, L.L.C. v. Pardo*, 580 F.3d 1193, 1202–04 (11th Cir. 2009).

Because the complaint alleges conduct falling within § 1605(a)(2), the FSIA does not bar suit. The dismissal should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
