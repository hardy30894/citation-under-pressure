# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Confirm That State Law Operates as Surrogate Federal Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Coverage.

OCSLA extends "the Constitution and laws … of the United States" to fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This Court has recognized that "the purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf." *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress modeled this scheme on federal enclave law, under which state law is not concurrently applicable of its own force but is "adopted" only where federal law does not otherwise supply a governing rule. *See United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 294 (1958) (sustaining prospective assimilation of state criminal law into federal enclaves on the understanding that state law fills interstitial gaps in an otherwise federally governed territory). Congress's choice of the word "applicable" in § 1333(a)(2)(A)—rather than "not inconsistent" alone—signals that state law must be *needed* to supply content that federal law lacks. A standard asking only whether state law "pertains to the subject matter" and is not affirmatively preempted collapses this two-part inquiry into one, reading "applicable" out of the statute entirely.

## II. *Rodrigue* Confirms That State Law Becomes Federal Law on the Shelf Only When No Federal Remedy Otherwise Governs.

In *Rodrigue*, Louisiana wrongful-death law was adopted as surrogate federal law only because this Court first determined that the federal Death on the High Seas Act did not reach accidents on fixed platforms; the Court explained that once the federal remedy was found inapplicable "of its own force," this "remove[d]" the "obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]." 395 U.S. at 360, 366. The inquiry was sequential and gap-driven: state law entered only after federal law was shown not to occupy the field. This Court applied the same logic in *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97, 101–02 (1971), borrowing Louisiana's limitations period as surrogate federal law under OCSLA precisely because no federal limitations period existed for the claim at issue—again, filling a void rather than merely avoiding inconsistency.

## III. The Fifth Circuit's Gap-Filling Test in *Continental Oil* Correctly Implements OCSLA.

The Fifth Circuit, applying this framework, held that state law is incorporated only upon "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law. *Cont'l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). Even the later three-part test of *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990), first asks whether federal maritime law applies "of its own force" before state law may be borrowed—a threshold inquiry into whether federal law already covers the ground, i.e., a gap analysis. Nothing in that line of authority supports the Ninth Circuit's view that state law applies whenever it merely "pertains to the subject matter" and escapes express preemption.

## IV. The FLSA's Comprehensive Scheme Leaves No Gap for California Wage Law to Fill.

OCSLA "declares the Constitution and laws … of the United States" applicable on the Shelf, establishing federal law's primacy there. *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473, 480 (1981). The FLSA, together with its implementing regulations, comprehensively governs minimum wage, overtime, and meal-period-adjacent pay practices nationwide, including on offshore platforms. Its savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state law only where state law already applies of its own force—a condition that, under *Rodrigue*, does not exist on the Shelf absent an independent gap. Department of Labor regulations implementing the FLSA carry "the force and effect of law," *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 295–96 (1979), and further demonstrate that federal law already speaks comprehensively to the wage-and-hour subject matter at issue here, leaving no void for California law to occupy.

## V. The Ninth Circuit's "Not Inconsistent" Standard Improperly Converts OCSLA Into Ordinary Preemption Analysis.

Ordinary preemption doctrine presumes state law applies unless it conflicts with federal law. OCSLA reverses that presumption: state law does not apply on the Shelf unless affirmatively adopted as federal law, and only when necessary to fill a gap. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355; *Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. at 294. Treating "applicable and not inconsistent" as synonymous with non-preemption erases the gap-filling requirement Congress built into an enclave-based statute and effectively imports the ordinary preemption default Congress specifically declined to adopt. The Ninth Circuit's judgment should be reversed.
