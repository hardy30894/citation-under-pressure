# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot's RLA Conspiracy Claim.

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in Smoot's favor. Summary judgment is proper only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). A factual dispute is "genuine" if the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party, *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986), and the court's task is not to weigh the evidence but to determine whether a trial is required, *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). The record here presented precisely the kind of contested factual terrain that precludes summary disposition.

A union's duty of fair representation, first recognized in *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 202-03 (1944), requires the union to represent all members "without hostile discrimination, fairly, impartially, and in good faith." That duty is breached when a union's conduct toward a member is "arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith." *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 190 (1967). A claim that a union and an employer conspired to defeat a member's grievance implicates this same standard, because the union's obligation runs independently of, and often in tension with, the interests of the carrier with which it bargains. *See Air Line Pilots Ass'n v. O'Neill*, 499 U.S. 65, 74 (1991) (union conduct must be judged according to a standard that gives "substantial deference" to legitimate union discretion, but that deference does not extend to conduct tainted by bad faith).

The undisputed sequence of events created a genuine issue whether Earley's representation of Smoot at the PLB hearing—and the position UTU took thereafter—reflected arbitrary or bad-faith conduct rather than legitimate advocacy. Elswick initially denied Smoot's claim outright; the Board of Appeals reversed him and directed that the claim proceed; Elswick then recused himself and installed Earley, who had not been a party to the internal dispute over Smoot's entitlement. Only after the executive session—conducted with CSX's own senior director present and with UTU's chosen advocate alone in the room—did the PLB rule against Smoot. That a UTU member, Ruck, later lodged formal charges against Earley for "arguing against the official position of the UTU" during that very session, see *Earley v. Executive Bd. of the UTU*, 957 F.Supp. 997, 999 (N.D.Ohio 1996), is itself evidence from which a jury could infer that Earley's advocacy inside the closed session diverged from what a faithful representative owed Smoot. A reasonable factfinder could conclude that the coordination between Earley and CSX's Emerick in a closed-door session, followed by an adverse ruling and internal union discipline mirroring Smoot's own charges, was more than coincidental. That inference, not a directed verdict for the defendants, is what *Anderson* and *Matsushita* require be tested by trial.

## II. Even If Liability Under the Wiretap Act Were Properly Established, the Damages Award Cannot Stand.

Assuming arguendo that Smoot's recording of the executive session fell outside any protection for a participant's justifiable expectation of privacy, *see Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 361 (1967) (Harlan, J., concurring) (privacy protected only where there is an expectation "that society is prepared to recognize as 'reasonable'"), and outside the participant-recording contours this Court has recognized under 18 U.S.C. § 2511, *see Boddie v. American Broadcasting Cos.*, 731 F.2d 333, 339 (6th Cir. 1984) (interception liability turns on whether the communication was made under circumstances justifying an expectation that it would not be intercepted), the damages awarded below were nonetheless excessive and improperly calculated.

Statutory damages under 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2) are not an automatic per-violation windfall; courts must calculate damages according to the statutory formula—the greater of actual damages, or $100 per day of violation or $10,000, and may not simply multiply an arbitrary daily figure without evidentiary support. *See Reynolds v. Spears*, 93 F.3d 428, 435 (8th Cir. 1996) (vacating damages calculated without regard to the statute's structure). The $250,000 statutory award here bears no demonstrated relationship to any permissible calculation and must be vacated and recalculated.

Punitive damages, moreover, require proof that the defendant acted with malicious or reckless disregard, not merely that a technical violation occurred. *See Rodgers v. Wood*, 910 F.2d 444, 448 (7th Cir. 1990) (punitive damages under the Act require a showing beyond simple intent to intercept). Smoot's recording arose from a good-faith belief that he was entitled to memorialize proceedings affecting his own grievance; nothing in the record establishes the egregious conduct required to sustain a $100,000 punitive award. The associated fee award, resting on the same inflated liability theory, should likewise be vacated and remanded for reconsideration.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed as to the RLA conspiracy claim, and the damages, punitive damages, and fee awards under the Wiretap Act should be vacated and remanded.
