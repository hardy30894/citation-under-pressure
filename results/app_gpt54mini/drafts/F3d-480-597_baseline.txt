### A. The Government Failed To Prove That Nieves-Castaño Knew the Weapon Was a Machine Gun

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) cannot stand unless the Government proved beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm possessed the characteristics that made it a machine gun. In *Staples v. United States*, the Supreme Court held that where Congress regulates a commonly possessed item, the government must prove the defendant knew of the facts that made the possession unlawful. 511 U.S. 600, 602, 619 (1994). The Court rejected any rule that would allow felony liability based on mere possession of a firearm without proof of knowledge of the weapon’s prohibited features. *Id.* at 619.

That principle controls here. Section 922(o) criminalizes possession of a machine gun, but the Government was still required to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the gun had the defining characteristics of a machine gun. See *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 619; *United States v. Ollie*, 450 F.3d 129, 135-36 (2d Cir. 2006) (knowledge of machine-gun characteristics required); *United States v. Stewart*, 451 F.3d 1071, 1075-76 (9th Cir. 2006) (same). The record does not meet that standard.

The proof showed only that Nieves-Castaño knew a rifle was inside the golf bag and that she had once looked inside and identified it as an AK-47. That evidence may support knowledge that she possessed a rifle, but it does not establish knowledge that the weapon fired automatically or otherwise qualified as a machine gun. The Supreme Court has distinguished between knowledge that one possesses a firearm and knowledge of the specific characteristics that trigger criminal liability. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 610-11, 619. Knowing that a weapon is an AK-47 is not the same as knowing it is capable of fully automatic fire. Semi-automatic and automatic weapons are not interchangeable as a matter of law or common understanding. See *United States v. Harris*, 959 F.2d 246, 258-59 (D.C. Cir. 1992) (reversing where evidence did not show knowledge of automatic-fire capability); *United States v. Haile*, 685 F.3d 1211, 1219-20 (11th Cir. 2012) (same principle under *Staples*).

The Government may argue that possession of an AK-47 permits an inference of knowledge because such weapons are associated with military-style firepower. But *Staples* forecloses that shortcut. The Court emphasized that a firearm’s dangerousness does not eliminate the mens rea requirement where Congress has not clearly done so. 511 U.S. at 610-15. A defendant’s awareness that she is dealing with a gun does not permit a jury to infer knowledge of an internal mechanical feature absent evidence that the defendant observed, handled, or was otherwise told about that feature. See *United States v. Barr*, 32 F.3d 1320, 1324-25 (8th Cir. 1994) (reversing where proof showed possession of weapon but not knowledge of automatic capability); *United States v. Thomas*, 531 F.3d 1129, 1133-34 (10th Cir. 2008) (knowledge of weapon’s unlawful characteristics must be shown).

Nor did the surrounding circumstances supply the missing proof. The weapon was hidden in a golf bag and stored in Nieves-Castaño’s apartment at the request of another person. Those facts are equally consistent with innocent storage of a firearm for someone else and with knowledge only that the bag contained a rifle. The Government’s evidence that she “knew it was an AK-47” does not show she knew it was a machine gun, because an AK-style rifle may be configured as either semi-automatic or automatic. See *United States v. Fennell*, 53 F.3d 1296, 1301-02 (D.C. Cir. 1995) (proof must connect defendant’s knowledge to automatic fire, not merely to possession of a firearm). At most, the record proved awareness of the gun’s identity, not its prohibited status.

Because the Government failed to prove the mens rea required by *Staples*, the § 922(o) conviction cannot stand. See *United States v. Rivera-Santiago*, 872 F.2d 1073, 1084-85 (1st Cir. 1989) (insufficiency requires reversal where evidence cannot support essential element); *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (conviction valid only if any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt). The judgment on the machine-gun count should be reversed.
