**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the equal footing doctrine, as interpreted by the United States Supreme Court, mandates that a state’s title to riverbeds beneath navigable waters is determined by the navigability of those waters at the time of statehood. This principle is not only well-established in federal constitutional law but is also fully consistent with the historical and legal framework governing the transfer of public lands to new states.

The equal footing doctrine, rooted in Article IV, Section 3, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution, ensures that when a new state enters the Union, it does so with the same sovereign rights and property interests as the original states. *See United States v. California*, 386 U.S. 1, 10 (1967). A central component of this doctrine is the recognition that states automatically acquire title to the beds of navigable rivers within their borders upon statehood. *See *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (“[T]he States, upon admission into the Union, acquired title to the beds of navigable rivers within their boundaries.”). This title is not contingent on subsequent conditions or modern usage but is instead fixed by the navigability of the river at the moment of statehood.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the test for navigability for title purposes is historical and objective: whether the river was navigable *at the time of statehood*, not whether it is navigable today. *See *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (“The test of navigability is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.”). In *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, the Court emphasized that the determination must be made based on the river’s condition and use at the time of statehood, not on speculative or post-statehood developments. The Court explained that “the question is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.” *Id.* at 470. This principle was reaffirmed in *Montana v. United States*, where the Court held that the State’s title to riverbeds “depends on whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood.” *Montana*, 450 U.S. at 551.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled doctrine. It found, based on substantial historical evidence—including period newspapers, government reports, and archival records—that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889. The trial court’s findings were supported by the affidavits of the State Archivist and other historical experts, which were properly admitted under Montana Rules of Evidence 803(8) and 902. These documents, including official reports from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and contemporaneous accounts of river traffic, demonstrated that the rivers were used for commercial and transportation purposes during the late 19th century. *See* *Mont. v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 552 (noting that “the use of a river for commercial purposes is a strong indicium of navigability”).

PPL’s argument that the navigability inquiry should be based on modern conditions is contrary to binding precedent. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that navigability for title purposes is a dynamic or evolving standard. *See *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. at 470; *United States v. California*, 386 U.S. at 10. To hold otherwise would undermine the predictability and stability of property rights, which are essential to the equal footing doctrine. As the Court stated in *United States v. California*, “the doctrine of equal footing is not a mere formality; it is a constitutional guarantee of equal status and rights.” *Id.* at 10.

Moreover, PPL’s claim that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts state claims for compensation is without merit. The FPA does not displace state ownership of riverbeds, nor does it extinguish state property rights. *See *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946) (recognizing that the FPA “preserves the role of state law” in matters of property rights). The FPA explicitly requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the “beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water,” 16 U.S.C. § 802, and permits state law claims for damages. *See *Nez Perce Tribe v. Idaho Power Co.*, 847 F. Supp. 791, 812 (D. Idaho 1993) (holding that FPA does not preempt state tort claims based on property use). Thus, the State’s right to seek compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is fully consistent with federal law.

In sum, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes. The State’s title to the riverbeds at issue was determined by the navigability of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. The historical evidence, properly admitted under Montana’s rules of evidence, supports this conclusion. The judgment of the District Court is therefore affirmed.
