ARGUMENT

I. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the Government Because the Installation of Excess Fiber Optic Capacity and Leasing to Third Parties Constituted a Physical Taking.

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the government from taking private property without just compensation. U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical taking occurs when the government physically occupies or invades private property, thereby depriving the owner of its rights to exclude others and to use the property as it sees fit. Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419, 435-36 (1982). The government's actions in this case, installing excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties, constitute a physical taking.

The Court of Federal Claims held that the scope of the easement was irrelevant to the takings analysis because it "cannot give rise to a physical taking." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. However, this reasoning is flawed. The government's actions must be evaluated in light of the easement's terms, which permit the installation of "signal lines... necessary in connection therewith." The government's installation of excess capacity far beyond its current needs, and its leasing of that excess capacity to third parties, exceeds the scope of the easement and constitutes a physical invasion of Tuthill's property.

The Supreme Court has held that a physical occupation, no matter how small, is a per se taking. Loretto, 458 U.S. at 435. In this case, the government's actions have resulted in a physical occupation of Tuthill's property, as the excess fiber optic cables remain on the property, and the government is deriving revenue from leasing them to third parties. This occupation is not merely a "physical presence" as the Court of Federal Claims characterized it; rather, it is a tangible invasion of Tuthill's property rights.

The government's argument that the installation of excess capacity was necessary to accommodate future growth is unavailing. The fact that the government may need the excess capacity in the future does not justify its current use for unrelated purposes. The government's actions are not merely a "natural upgrade" to its infrastructure, as it claims; rather, they represent a significant expansion of its use of Tuthill's property beyond the scope of the easement.

The Court of Federal Claims' suggestion that Tuthill may have a remedy for trespass is not a substitute for a takings claim. The government's physical occupation of Tuthill's property is a taking, regardless of whether it may also be a trespass. See, e.g., Lucas v. S.C. Coastal Council, 505 U.S. 1003, 1015 (1992) (noting that a physical taking is a "paradigmatic" taking).

Because the government's actions constitute a physical taking, the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government. This Court should reverse the judgment and remand for further proceedings to determine the just compensation owed to Tuthill.

II. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Denying Tuthill's Motion for Summary Judgment.

For the same reasons that the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government, it also erred in denying Tuthill's motion for summary judgment. The undisputed facts demonstrate that the government's actions constituted a physical taking, and Tuthill is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. This Court should reverse the denial of Tuthill's motion and remand with instructions to grant summary judgment in favor of Tuthill on the issue of liability.
