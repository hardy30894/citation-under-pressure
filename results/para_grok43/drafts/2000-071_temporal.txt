The school district's exclusion of the Good News Club rests on settled principles that a public school may not lend its facilities or authority to religious instruction and worship. In McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), this Court held that "the use of tax-supported property for religious instruction" violates the Establishment Clause, because the state may not "aid all religions" by furnishing "the tax-established and tax-supported public school system" to sectarian teaching. Id. at 210-211. The same prohibition applies whether the instruction occurs during or after regular school hours; the constitutional defect lies in the school's provision of its premises for the inculcation of religious doctrine.

The Club's proposed meetings consist of prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Christ as personal Savior. These activities are not merely discussions of morality from a religious perspective; they are religious instruction and worship. Permitting them on school property would therefore constitute the very "utilization of the tax-established and tax-supported public school system to aid religious groups to spread their faith" forbidden in McCollum. Id. at 210.

The district's community-use policy, which bars religious purposes while allowing secular civic and recreational uses, is a lawful means of complying with that command. In Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Court reaffirmed that the First Amendment "requires the state to be a neutral" in matters of religion and "cannot pass laws which aid one religion [or] all religions." Id. at 18. A policy that withholds school facilities from religious instruction while making them available for nonreligious youth activities simply maintains the required neutrality; it does not discriminate among viewpoints on an otherwise open subject.

Nor does the policy abridge free speech. The school has not opened its buildings as a general forum for all speech; it has limited access to specified secular purposes. When government property is not dedicated to indiscriminate expressive use, reasonable restrictions that prevent an Establishment Clause violation are permissible. See, e.g., Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314-315 (1952) (distinguishing permissible off-site religious programs from impermissible use of public-school machinery to advance religion). The district's refusal to convert its facilities into a venue for sectarian worship and evangelism therefore violates neither the Free Speech Clause nor the Establishment Clause.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
