**ARGUMENT**

The judgment should be reversed. The release does not bar Bancorp’s claims, because a release is construed according to its plain terms and does not reach claims outside the parties’ expressed bargain. And the claims are timely because, under New York law, a cause of action does not accrue until the wrong produces a legally cognizable injury and is capable of suit. Here, the FDIC’s challenged conduct continued through the receivership and, at minimum, gave rise to claims that accrued no earlier than termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995.

I. **The release does not bar claims arising from the FDIC’s later conduct as receiver.**

A release covers only the claims the parties intended to extinguish. The Supreme Court has long held that contracts, including releases, are enforced according to their terms, and that courts may not extend a general phrase beyond the scope of the transaction actually released. See *United States v. Armour & Co.*, 402 U.S. 673, 681-82 (1971) (recognizing that a consent decree must be read within its four corners); *Meyer v. United States*, 213 U.S. 117, 120-21 (1909) (release construed according to the parties’ expressed language). New York follows the same rule: a release “is a jural act of high significance” and bars only those matters fairly within its scope. *Mangini v. McClurg*, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562-63 (1969). Where language is general, it is limited by the subject matter and context. *Kaminsky v. Gamache*, 298 N.Y. 155, 156-57 (1948). And if the text is ambiguous, it must be construed against the drafter and in favor of the releasor. *Brady v. American Nat’l Ins. Co.*, 277 N.Y. 82, 88 (1938).

Measured against those principles, the release here does not extinguish Bancorp’s claims. Bancorp released the FDIC “from any and all claims” it had “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language addresses claims tied to the OCC’s closure decision. It does not plainly cover the FDIC’s subsequent acts as receiver: paying itself post-insolvency interest, charging legal and office expenses to the estate, allegedly selling assets for less than fair value, or failing to account properly for the estate. Those are not claims “with respect to” the OCC’s decision in the sense New York law requires; they are claims challenging how the FDIC administered the receivership after the closure.

New York cases refuse to stretch a release beyond the controversy being settled. See *Mangini*, 24 N.Y.2d at 562-63; *Kaminsky*, 298 N.Y. at 156-57. The record here confirms that the release was executed as part of the settlement of separate actions involving different claims and different parties. Bancorp was not even a party to the actions giving rise to the settlement. A release in such circumstances should not be read to give the FDIC immunity for future breaches of duty never identified in the agreement. See *Meyer*, 213 U.S. at 120-21.

II. **The claims are timely because they accrued when the FDIC’s wrongful receivership conduct injured Bancorp, not when the bank was closed.**

Under New York law, a statute of limitations begins to run when a claim accrues. See *Hume v. Beale’s Ex’rs*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 336, 339 (1873) (limitations run from accrual of the cause, not merely from antecedent events); *United States v. Anderson*, 269 U.S. 422, 441-42 (1926) (a liability accrues when the obligation is fixed). For equitable claims, New York looks to when the wrongful acts causing injury occur and when the plaintiff can sue. *Higgins v. Crouse*, 147 N.Y. 411, 416-17 (1895); *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 348 (1874) (equity does not permit a wrongdoer to profit from delayed discovery where the injury is concealed). And where the wrong is continuing, limitations do not bar suit before the wrongful conduct ends. *Birkett v. Knickerbocker Life Ins. Co.*, 110 N.Y. 304, 312-13 (1888).

Those principles compel timeliness here. Bancorp’s claims are not about the OCC’s June 1985 closure alone. They challenge the FDIC’s administration of the receivership over the next decade: self-payment of interest beginning in 1991, the charging of expenses to the estate, the disposition of assets, and the failure to account. Each alleged wrong caused injury when committed, and the receivership itself did not end until November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995, within six years of the most recent challenged conduct and before the receivership’s termination.

The district court’s contrary approach improperly treated the closure decision as the sole accrual date. But where a fiduciary or receiver engages in repeated acts of self-dealing or mismanagement, the cause of action accrues with each actionable wrong. See *Birkett*, 110 N.Y. at 312-13; *Higgins*, 147 N.Y. at 416-17. The FDIC cannot convert ongoing fiduciary misconduct into a stale claim simply by pointing back to the initial closure of the bank.

Because the release does not reach these claims and the action was timely brought, summary judgment should be reversed.
