**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Membership in a Conspiracy Within the Limitations Period**

A conspiracy is a continuing offense. The statute of limitations for a federal conspiracy charge begins to run when the conspiracy ends, not when it began. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). Because a conspiracy is continuing, the government must prove not only that a conspiracy existed, but also that it continued into the limitations period and that the accused was a member of it during that period. *United States v. Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376, 383 (2d Cir. 1964).

Withdrawal ends a defendant’s membership in the conspiracy. A defendant who has effectively withdrawn before the limitations period cannot be convicted for the conspiracy’s continued existence after his withdrawal. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464 (1978). Withdrawal, of course, must be shown by some affirmative evidence. But once the defendant produces evidence sufficient to place withdrawal before the limitations period in issue, the burden of persuasion must rest with the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period.

This allocation follows from *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), which requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. For a continuing offense such as conspiracy, continuing membership within the limitations period is such a fact. The government cannot satisfy its burden by proving only that a conspiracy once existed. It must prove that the conspiracy continued into the five-year limitations period and that the defendant participated in it during that time. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3282(a); 18 U.S.C. § 1962(d).

The decision below is inconsistent with this rule. Petitioners did not rest on a bare assertion of withdrawal. They presented evidence that Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, the alleged leaders of the conspiracy, split before the limitations period. That split did not merely put petitioners’ individual participation in issue; it demonstrated that the alleged single conspiracy itself had ended. Once petitioners made that showing, the government was required to persuade the jury beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners remained members of a conspiracy within the limitations period. The government did not do so.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying the New Trial Motion**

The district court denied petitioners’ new trial motion on the theory that the evidence could support a finding of a continuing single conspiracy. But the question is not simply whether the evidence could support such a verdict. The question is who bore the burden of persuasion and whether the jury was instructed on that burden. The jury should have been instructed that the government was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners were members of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Without such an instruction, the verdict cannot stand.

Criminal limitations statutes are to be liberally interpreted in favor of repose. *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970). Permitting conviction on the prosecution’s assertion that a conspiracy continued, after petitioners produced evidence that the leaders had split and the conspiracy had ended, defeats that purpose. The burden of persuasion must remain with the government.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
