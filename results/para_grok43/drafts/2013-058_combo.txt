The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds turns on whether the assets function as such in the debtor’s hands at the commencement of the case. Exemptions exist to effectuate the fresh-start policy for the debtor personally. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). They are not vehicles for preserving assets merely because those assets once enjoyed exempt status in another person’s estate. White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924) (exemption status “is determined as of the date of the filing of the petition”).

The Code therefore withholds the protection when the debtor cannot claim the requisite personal nexus. Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903) (creditors may reach property the debtor holds only as trustee or conduit). An inherited IRA, by statutory command, must be distributed within a fixed period measured from the original owner’s death and cannot be rolled over or augmented by the beneficiary. Those features sever any link to the present debtor’s retirement. The funds are therefore reachable by creditors in precisely the same manner as any other non-exempt asset acquired by inheritance. Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. v. Hall, 229 U.S. 511, 515 (1913).

This conclusion follows from the settled rule that bankruptcy exemptions are strictly construed in favor of the estate. Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554 (1915). Courts have long refused to extend an exemption beyond the precise category Congress identified. Stellwagen v. Clum, 245 U.S. 605, 616 (1918). Allowing the exemption here would convert a time-limited tax deferral into a perpetual shield, contrary to the principle that “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” See Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 187 (1902).

The same temporal and personal limitations appear in analogous contexts. A homestead exemption requires actual occupancy by the debtor at the relevant time, not merely prior family use. Straton v. New, 283 U.S. 318, 322 (1931). Likewise, property that once qualified as exempt loses that character once it passes into the hands of a successor who cannot satisfy the statutory conditions. Segal v. Rochelle, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966). Because the Clarks cannot demonstrate that the inherited IRA constitutes their own retirement funds, the exemption is unavailable.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
