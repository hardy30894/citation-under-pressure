# ARGUMENT

## I. RESPONDENT HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN OHIO'S CLEMENCY PROCEDURES PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

Clemency is an act of grace, not a component of the adjudicatory process to which due process protections attach. This Court's decision in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), controls this case and forecloses Respondent's due process claim. In *Dumschat*, this Court held that a prisoner had no constitutionally protected liberty interest in a commutation decision, explaining that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.* at 464. The Court further explained that "[a] state cannot be said to have created a constitutionally protected liberty interest in the discretionary act of a commutation" merely by establishing a procedure through which such discretion may be exercised. *Id.* at 465.

The reasoning of *Dumschat* flows directly from this Court's earlier decision in *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215 (1976), which recognized that not every government decision affecting a prisoner's prospects for release triggers due process scrutiny. Rather, due process protections attach only where the State has created a legitimate expectation—not a mere hope—of a particular outcome. *Id.* at 225. Ohio's clemency scheme, which vests "almost unfettered discretion" in the Governor to grant or deny clemency, creates no such legitimate expectation. Indeed, as the Ohio Supreme Court has recognized, the General Assembly may not even bind the Governor's substantive discretion by statute. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994).

The Sixth Circuit erred by concluding that some due process protection attaches to clemency proceedings because Ohio has made clemency review "an integral part" of its system for finally adjudicating guilt, relying on *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), and *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). This reasoning improperly extends principles developed for the appellate process to the wholly distinct executive clemency function. *Evitts* held that where a State creates an appeal as of right, due process requires that the appeal be more than "a meaningless ritual." 469 U.S. at 394 (quoting *Griffin*, 351 U.S. at 24). But *Evitts* itself recognized limits to this reasoning, noting that the right to counsel does not extend to discretionary proceedings such as habeas corpus. *Id.* And this Court in *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974), likewise declined to extend due process protections to discretionary review beyond the first appeal as of right. *Id.* at 610-11. Clemency, unlike a first appeal, is not part of the adjudicatory process at all—it is, by design, "an act of grace." *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. To hold otherwise would, as this Court cautioned in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), "create disincentives for States" to maintain procedural regularity in discretionary proceedings for fear of judicial entanglement. *Id.* at 482.

## II. VOLUNTARY PARTICIPATION IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

The Ohio clemency procedure at issue does not compel Respondent to be a witness against himself. The regulation gives Respondent the *option* of a personal interview; it does not require him to answer any question he chooses not to answer, nor does it impose any penalty for declining the interview altogether. Because participation is voluntary, no compulsion within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment exists.

This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), is directly on point. There, the Court held that any claim regarding the use of statements made during a voluntary prison disciplinary proceeding in a hypothetical future criminal prosecution was "premature." *Id.* at 324. The same reasoning applies here with even greater force. Respondent has represented that he "does not contemplate that this action will have any effect on his state convictions and/or sentences," yet he now asks the courts to fashion Fifth Amendment protections against a purely speculative risk—that some future statement, in some future proceeding, might be used against him. Such speculative harm does not present a live Fifth Amendment controversy. *See also Chavez v. Martinez*, 538 U.S. 760, 767 (2003) (plurality opinion) (the core of the Fifth Amendment privilege is implicated only when compelled statements are used in a criminal case).

Nor does the "unconstitutional conditions" doctrine transform this voluntary option into compulsion. That doctrine applies where the State conditions a benefit on the surrender of a constitutional right. *See Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 434 U.S. 357, 363 (1978) (permitting the State to offer a benefit—leniency—in exchange for a defendant's choice, so long as the defendant is "free to accept or reject the prosecution's offer"). Here, Respondent's participation in the interview is entirely voluntary, and the clemency board will consider "all other relevant information" whether or not he chooses to speak. There is no threat that clemency will be denied *because* Respondent asserts his privilege—Respondent has identified no such conditioning in the regulation itself.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed.
