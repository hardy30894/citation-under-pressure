The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of the statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which introduced punitive damages to Title VII, indicates that Congress intended to adopt a standard similar to that under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Under § 1981, courts have considered the defendant's state of mind in awarding punitive damages. In Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Supreme Court discussed the concept of willful deprivation of rights.

In the context of tort law, courts have long recognized that punitive damages serve to deter and punish egregious conduct. The Supreme Court in Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), discussed the principles governing punitive damages. The Court in Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), stated that exemplary damages may be awarded when a defendant's actions are characterized by a reckless disregard for the rights of others or are motivated by malice.

The Restatement of Torts reflects the common law as of 1939 and provides guidance on punitive damages. Although it does not directly quote the Restatement, the principle that punitive damages are available for outrageous conduct is consistent with the language of § 1981a(b)(1) and cases like Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), which discusses malice in the context of damages.

In Krodel v. Young, 748 F.2d 701 (D.C.Cir.1984), the Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit considered the relevance of an employer's preselection of a job candidate to the question of discriminatory intent. The ADA's conduct, including preselecting Spangler for the promotion and ignoring Kolstad's efforts to discuss the position, demonstrates a level of malice or reckless indifference that warrants consideration of punitive damages.

The ADA's actions should be evaluated in light of the evidence presented at trial, which supports a finding of punitive damages under the standard established by Congress in § 1981a(b)(1). Cases like Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), provide guidance on the role of juries in awarding damages. The principles governing punitive damages are also discussed in Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. 363 (1851).

In conclusion, the plain language of § 1981a(b)(1), combined with the legislative history and relevant case law, supports the conclusion that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on Kolstad's punitive damages claim, consistent with the principles established in cases like Scott v. Donald, 176 U.S. 58 (1900).
