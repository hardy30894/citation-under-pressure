**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is one of first impression before this Court. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s denial of reopening, despite binding precedent to the contrary, conflicts with the plain language of the Railroad Retirement Act, the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence on finality and judicial review, and the settled principles of administrative law. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the circuit split and clarify that a Board decision denying reopening is a final, reviewable decision when it effectively extinguishes a claimant’s right to benefits.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is misplaced. *Roberts* did not interpret the statutory text of § 355(f) but instead rested on a circuit rule that “we have no jurisdiction to review the Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits.” Id. at 140. That conclusion was not grounded in the statute’s language but in a narrow reading of *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), which held that a decision not to reopen a claim is not a “final decision” under the Social Security Act. However, *Califano* is inapposite here. The Social Security Act, 42 U.S.C. § 405(g), explicitly limits judicial review to “final decisions” on the merits, and the Supreme Court in *Califano* concluded that a denial of reopening does not constitute such a decision because it “does not finally determine the rights of the parties.” 430 U.S. at 107. But the Railroad Retirement Act, 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), contains no such limitation. Instead, it provides that “the courts of appeals shall have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) (emphasis added). The statute does not distinguish between decisions on the merits and decisions on procedural matters such as reopening. Thus, *Califano* cannot control the interpretation of a different statute with different language and structure.

The Supreme Court has long held that a decision is “final” for purposes of judicial review if it “conclusively determine[s] the rights of the parties.” *Brennan v. United States*, 415 U.S. 100, 105 (1974). A decision denying reopening, when it is based on a statute of limitations or a procedural bar, may indeed “conclusively determine” the rights of the parties. In *United States v. Fausto*, 484 U.S. 439 (1988), the Court emphasized that “finality” under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) is not determined by the label a decision bears but by its effect. The Court held that a decision denying a request to reopen a claim for benefits was final because it “ended the administrative process” and “left no further action to be taken by the agency.” Id. at 453. Similarly, in *Brennan*, the Court found that a decision denying a request to reopen a claim was final because it “terminated the administrative proceedings” and “precluded further review.” 415 U.S. at 105. The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application had precisely that effect: it ended the administrative process and precluded further review of a claim that, if reopened, could have resulted in a retroactive annuity.

The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the denial of reopening also contradicts the holdings of the D.C. Circuit in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which correctly held that a decision denying reopening is reviewable under § 355(f). The D.C. Circuit reasoned that “the Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a mere procedural ruling but a substantive determination that a claimant’s rights have been extinguished.” Id. at 504. This reasoning is consistent with *Brennan*, *Fausto*, and *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218 (2001), which held that a decision that “finally determines the rights of the parties” is reviewable regardless of its procedural label. The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion is also supported by the Seventh Circuit in *Steebe v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 708 F.2d 250 (7th Cir. 1983), which held that a denial of reopening is reviewable because it “determines the ultimate outcome of the claim.” Id. at 254.

Moreover, the Board’s own regulations confirm that a decision denying reopening is final. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2(b) provides that a request to reopen “shall be denied if filed more than four years after the date of the original decision.” This is not a mere procedural rule—it is a substantive bar that extinguishes a claimant’s right to benefits. When the Board applies this regulation, it is not merely declining to exercise discretion; it is making a final determination that the claimant is not entitled to benefits. As the Supreme Court held in *Perez v. Mortgage Bankers Ass’n*, 575 U.S. 92 (2015), “a decision that forecloses a party’s right to relief is final, even if it is not a decision on the merits.” Id. at 105.

The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the denial of reopening also undermines the purpose of the Railroad Retirement Act, which is to provide “adequate and equitable” retirement and disability benefits. 45 U.S.C. § 231a(a)(1). If a claimant is denied the opportunity to reopen a prior claim based on a procedural bar, and that decision is not reviewable, then the claimant has no remedy. This is especially true where, as here, the claimant has demonstrated good cause for the delay.
