**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Service cannot collect a partnership’s tax deficiency from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners or obtaining judgments against them. Because the IRS did neither within the applicable limitations period, its claims against respondents are time-barred.

**I. The IRS Must Assess Each Individual Partner to Extend the Statute of Limitations for Collection**

The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess any tax within three years after the return is filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). An assessment must “record[] the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A “taxpayer” includes both a partnership and an individual. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The implementing regulation requires that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

Here, the IRS assessed only the Partnership, not respondents individually. The assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations for collection to ten years only as to the Partnership. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). It had no effect on the limitations period applicable to respondents as separate taxpayers. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien cannot exist against an individual unless the tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “the assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership, but it left unaltered the limitations period applicable to Debtors.” *United States v. Galletti*, 299 F.3d 913, 919 (9th Cir. 2002).

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the partner had voluntarily paid the tax and sought a refund. The court held only that a partner could not recover taxes he had paid simply because the assessment did not name him. *Id.* at 910. The district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that to collect from an unwilling partner, the IRS would need to “resort to court action” or individually assess the partner. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). *Young* thus supports respondents’ position that the IRS cannot collect from partners without individual assessments.

*United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is also distinguishable. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the statute of limitations was tolled during the partnership’s bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. The Seventh Circuit had “no opportunity to address the question before us.” *Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 916.

**II. State Law Does Not Permit Collection Without a Judgment Against the Partners**

The IRS argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allowing collection without individual assessments. But California law requires a creditor to obtain a judgment against the partner before satisfying a debt from the partner’s assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) (“A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”). *See also* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (partner must be individually named and served, and judgment entered, to reach personal assets).

The IRS obtained no judgment against respondents. The time to do so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (assessment against partnership insufficient to support lien against individual partner). *Accord United States v. Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 922-23 (citing *Coson* and rejecting IRS’s state-law theory).

**III. The Bankruptcy Court Properly Disallowed the Claims**

Under the Bankruptcy Code, a claim may be disallowed if it “is unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor, under ... applicable law.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Because the IRS’s claims against respondents are barred by the statute of limitations, they are unenforceable. *See In re Goldston*, 104 F.3d 1198, 1200-01 (10th Cir. 1997) (IRS must bring action within three years if no assessment). The bankruptcy court correctly sustained respondents’ objections.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
