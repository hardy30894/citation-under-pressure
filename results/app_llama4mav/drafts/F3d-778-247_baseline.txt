ARGUMENT

I. The Search Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus to the Place Being Searched

The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant be supported by probable cause and particularly describe the place to be searched and the items to be seized. United States v. Vigeant, 176 F.3d 565, 569 (1st Cir. 1999). To establish probable cause, there must be a "nexus ... between the items to be seized and the place to be searched." United States v. Feliz, 182 F.3d 82, 86 (1st Cir. 1999) (quoting United States v. Procopio, 88 F.3d 21, 29 (1st Cir. 1996)). The affidavit supporting the warrant must provide a "substantial basis" for concluding that evidence of a crime will be found at the place to be searched. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

Here, the affidavit failed to establish a sufficient nexus between the items to be seized and Joubert's parents' home. The affidavit relied heavily on SJ's statements that Joubert had become "anxious" and "very concerned" about destroying his computer's hard drive and had torn apart the computer tower. However, SJ did not see Joubert discard the computer components, and there is no evidence that the components were stored at his parents' home. The affidavit's conclusory statement that "persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of ... minors often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims" is insufficient to establish a nexus to the place being searched. See United States v. Weber, 923 F.2d 1338, 1345 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that an affidavit's "boilerplate" language about the habits of child molesters was insufficient to establish probable cause).

The lack of a nexus between the items to be seized and the place to be searched renders the search warrant invalid, and the evidence seized during the search should be suppressed.

II. The District Court Abused its Discretion in Admitting Testimony of Uncharged Child Molestation

Federal Rule of Evidence 414 allows the admission of evidence of uncharged child molestation in a criminal case where the defendant is accused of child molestation. However, the rule requires that the evidence be relevant and that its probative value not be substantially outweighed by the risk of unfair prejudice. United States v. LeMay, 260 F.3d 1018, 1026 (9th Cir. 2001). The district court's decision to admit such evidence is reviewed for abuse of discretion. United States v. Enjady, 134 F.3d 1427, 1433 (10th Cir. 1998).

Here, the district court erred in admitting testimony of uncharged child molestation without properly considering the risk of unfair prejudice. The testimony was not necessary to prove the charged conduct, and its admission may have unfairly prejudiced the jury against Joubert.

III. The Commerce Clause Supports Application of the Federal Criminal Statutes to Joubert

Joubert's argument that the Commerce Clause cannot support application of the federal criminal statutes under which he was indicted is without merit. The Supreme Court has held that Congress has the authority to regulate the possession of child pornography under the Commerce Clause, as it has a substantial effect on interstate commerce. United States v. Morales-de Jesus, 372 F.3d 6, 12 (1st Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 558-59 (1995)). The First Circuit has also upheld the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 2251(a) under the Commerce Clause. United States v. Morales-de Jesus, 372 F.3d at 12-13.

IV. The Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

Given the errors in the district court's evidentiary rulings and the lack of a valid search warrant, Joubert's conviction should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing. If the conviction is reversed, the sentence imposed by the district court would necessarily be vacated. Therefore, this Court need not reach the issue of substantive reasonableness at this time.

For the foregoing reasons, Joubert's conviction should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
