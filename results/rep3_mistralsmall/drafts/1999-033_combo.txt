**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A "CLASS OF ONE" THEORY OF LIABILITY WHERE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ILLEGITIMATE, ARBITRARY, OR DISCRIMINATORY MOTIVES.**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits state action that is "invidious" or "arbitrary." *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886). This principle extends to cases where a plaintiff is intentionally singled out for adverse treatment without any rational basis. *See Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445–46 (1923) (holding that intentional discrimination against one property owner, while permitting similar conduct by others, violates equal protection). The Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary and irrational state action, even absent membership in a suspect class. *See Connolly v. Union Sewer Pipe Co.*, 184 U.S. 540, 560 (1902) (invalidating a law that arbitrarily imposed burdens on one class of persons while exempting others).

This Court has further clarified that equal protection violations may occur where state action is "deliberately conceived" to harm a particular individual. *See Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (holding that a plaintiff states a claim where state officials arbitrarily withheld a benefit to which he was entitled under state law). The principle is rooted in the fundamental requirement that the government act with evenhanded justice. *See Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 112–13 (1949) (Jackson, J., concurring) ("The equal protection clause commands that no person or class of persons shall be denied the same protection of the laws which is enjoyed by other persons or other classes in like circumstances.").

II. **A PLAINTIFF NEED NOT ALLEGE MEMBERSHIP IN A PROTECTED CLASS TO STATE AN EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM WHERE THE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ARBITRARY OR ILLEGITIMATE MOTIVES.**

The Court has repeatedly held that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational state action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect or protected class. *See Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 109–10 (upholding a law that treated some commercial speech differently from others, but emphasizing that equal protection scrutiny applies to all state action, not just classifications based on suspect traits). In *Snowden*, the Court rejected the argument that a plaintiff must demonstrate membership in a suspect class to state an equal protection claim, holding that the clause protects individuals from arbitrary state action irrespective of class-based distinctions. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 7–8.

Similarly, in *Yick Wo*, the Court invalidated a facially neutral law that was applied discriminatorily against a particular individual, emphasizing that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary enforcement of the law. *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74. The Court has also recognized that equal protection violations may occur where state action is motivated by "personal or political animosity." *See Wieman v. Updegraff*, 344 U.S. 183, 192 (1952) (invalidating a loyalty oath requirement that was applied arbitrarily to exclude certain individuals). These cases establish that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary and discriminatory state action, even where the plaintiff does not allege membership in a suspect or protected class.

III. **THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT A PLAINTIFF MAY STATE AN EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM BY ALLEGING THAT STATE ACTION WAS MOTIVATED BY A "SPITEFUL EFFORT TO 'GET' HIM FOR REASONS WHOLLY UNRELATED TO ANY LEGITIMATE STATE OBJECTIVE."**

The Court of Appeals properly recognized that a plaintiff states an equal protection claim where state action is motivated by "spite" or arbitrary animus. *See Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 160 F.3d 386, 391 (7th Cir. 1998) (quoting *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 8). This principle is consistent with the Court’s longstanding recognition that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits state action that is "invidious" or "arbitrary." *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74. The Court has repeatedly invalidated state action that was motivated by personal or political animosity, even where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a suspect class. *See Wieman*, 344 U.S. at 192; *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 8.

The Court of Appeals’ holding is also consistent with this Court’s recognition that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary and irrational state action. *See Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 109–10. The principle that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary state action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class, is well-established. *See Connolly*, 184 U.S. at 560; *Sioux City Bridge Co.*, 260 U.S. at 445–46.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The Equal Protection Clause recognizes a "class of one" theory of liability where state action is motivated by arbitrary or illegitimate motives, and a plaintiff need not allege membership in a protected class to state such a claim. The Court of Appeals correctly applied these principles in reversing the District Court’s dismissal of Olech’s equal protection claim.
