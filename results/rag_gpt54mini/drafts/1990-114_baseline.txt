Respondent Gregory, Nugent, and Greene cannot prevail because Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the ADEA as applied to appointed state judges, and it survives rational-basis review under the Equal Protection Clause.

I. The ADEA does not reach appointed Missouri judges.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office,” “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff,” and “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The text, structure, and purpose of the statute support the Eighth Circuit’s holding that appointed state judges fall within the policymaking-level exception. Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d 598, 603-10 (8th Cir. 1990).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the ADEA is a broad remedial statute, but also one that contains carefully drawn exemptions. See Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston, 469 U.S. 111, 120-21 (1985); Western Air Lines, Inc. v. Criswell, 472 U.S. 400, 412-13 (1985). Where federal law would significantly intrude on state sovereignty, courts require clear congressional expression before reading the statute to displace state authority. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 604-05 (citing United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971)). That principle is especially important here, because “the tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance to a state.” Gregory, 898 F.2d at 604.

The judges’ argument that judges do not “make policy” is too cramped. As the Eighth Circuit explained, judging is not merely mechanical application of rules; judges necessarily resolve unsettled questions and thereby “create law.” Gregory, 898 F.2d at 608-09. This understanding accords with the First Circuit’s reasoning that “policymaking” includes lawmaking to fill “the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Appointed state judges therefore qualify as “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” under § 630(f). Gregory, 898 F.2d at 607-10; see also EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d at 55; EEOC v. Illinois, 721 F. Supp. 156, 160-62 (N.D. Ill. 1989); EEOC v. Vermont, 717 F. Supp. 261, 264-66 (D. Vt. 1989).

Nor is the conclusion altered by the legislative history. The conference report stated that the exemption covered persons appointed to policymaking positions “at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments,” and that it should be construed narrowly. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 610 (quoting Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers, H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180). The Eighth Circuit correctly recognized that the state judiciary is part of state government and that appointed judges exercise significant policymaking responsibility. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 610-12.

II. Missouri’s retirement age survives Equal Protection review.

Age is not a suspect classification, and a mandatory retirement rule is valid if rationally related to a legitimate state purpose. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1976) (per curiam); Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97-112 (1979). This Court has upheld age-based retirement classifications where the State could rationally conclude that age serves as a proxy for declining capacity or where the rule promotes administrative efficiency. See Murgia, 427 U.S. at 316-17; Vance, 440 U.S. at 109-12.

Gregory identified several rational bases for Missouri’s rule: preserving judicial competence, avoiding costly and embarrassing individualized removal proceedings, reducing delay caused by death or disability, and ensuring a judiciary that reflects the experiences of the electorate. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 619-23. Similar rationales have been accepted by other courts. See Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

Under deferential review, the Constitution does not require Missouri to prove its policy is the best or most empirically precise. Vance, 440 U.S. at 111-12. It is enough that the State could rationally conclude that age seventy is a reasonable cutoff for judicial service. Gregory, 898 F.2d at 623-24. The Equal Protection Clause therefore is not violated.

The judgment should be affirmed.
