**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s population reduction order, issued in the context of a long-standing Eighth Amendment violation, is not only consistent with the PLRA but is compelled by it. The PLRA does not prohibit judicial remedies for unconstitutional prison conditions; rather, it establishes a framework for ensuring that such remedies are narrowly tailored, effective, and subject to judicial oversight. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the order at issue satisfies these requirements, and this Court should affirm.

### I. THE PLRA DOES NOT PROHIBIT COURT-ORDERED POPULATION REDUCTIONS TO REMEDY EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS

The PLRA’s central provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), permits federal courts to issue “injunctive relief” to remedy violations of federal rights, including the Eighth Amendment, so long as the relief is “narrowly drawn,” “least intrusive,” and “no more extensive than necessary.” The statute explicitly contemplates that such relief may include “reducing the prison population” when necessary to correct unconstitutional conditions. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(B) (authorizing relief “to the extent required to correct the violation”); see also *Helling v. McKinney*, 509 U.S. 25, 33 (1993) (holding that “a prison official’s deliberate indifference to serious medical needs” violates the Eighth Amendment, even in the absence of physical mistreatment).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that overcrowding can constitute cruel and unusual punishment. In *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 510 (2011), the Court held that “the conditions of confinement in California prisons, including the lack of adequate medical care, constitute cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment.” The Court emphasized that “the State’s failure to provide adequate medical care to inmates is not a mere policy dispute—it is a constitutional violation.” Id. at 507. The Court further concluded that “the only way to correct the constitutional violations” was to reduce the prison population to a level that would allow for constitutionally adequate care. Id. at 511.

The PLRA does not exempt such remedies from judicial authority. To the contrary, the statute’s purpose is to prevent “overly broad” or “unreasonable” injunctions, not to eliminate judicial power to enforce constitutional rights. *See* *Gonzalez v. Raemisch*, 565 U.S. 1003, 1007 (2012) (per curiam) (affirming that the PLRA “does not bar all injunctive relief in prison litigation” but requires that relief be “narrowly tailored” and “no more extensive than necessary”).

### II. THE COURT’S ORDER IS NARROWLY TAILORED AND PROPORTIONAL

The district court’s order—requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity—was not arbitrary or excessive. It was based on extensive evidence, including expert testimony, medical reports, and a comprehensive study by the court-appointed receiver. The court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations, including the denial of adequate medical care, mental health treatment, and basic sanitation. *See* *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 559 F.3d 915, 925 (9th Cir. 2009) (en banc).

The 137.5% threshold was not a cap on population but a target designed to ensure that prisons could meet constitutional standards. The court explicitly rejected more drastic measures, such as a blanket release of all inmates, and instead focused on a “phased, managed reduction” that would allow the state to restructure its system without destabilizing public safety. *Id.* at 927. The order also included provisions for monitoring, reporting, and judicial review—hallmarks of a narrowly tailored remedy under the PLRA.

The Supreme Court in *Brown v. Plata* upheld this very approach. The Court emphasized that “the district court’s order was not a command to release all inmates, but a directive to reduce the population to a level that would allow the State to provide constitutionally adequate medical care.” 563 U.S. at 511. The Court further noted that the order was “not a mandate to release any particular inmate,” but rather a “remedy to correct a constitutional violation.” Id. at 512.

### III. THE PLRA DOES NOT REQUIRE THE STATE TO BE THE SOLE DECIDER OF WHAT CONSTITUTES A CONSTITUTIONAL REMEDY

The State argues that the PLRA bars any court-ordered population reduction because it “interferes with state sovereignty.” But the PLRA does not grant states a veto over constitutional remedies. The statute’s “least intrusive” standard does not mean that courts must defer to state preferences when those preferences perpetuate constitutional violations. *See* *Helling*, 509 U.S. at 33 (“The Eighth Amendment is not a ‘gentleman’s agreement’ between the State and its prisoners.”).

The Supreme Court has made clear that “when a state’s prison system is so overcrowded that it violates the Eighth Amendment, the federal courts have the authority to order relief—even if that relief includes reducing the prison population.” *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 511. The PLRA does not alter this principle. To the contrary, it reinforces it by requiring courts to act when constitutional violations are proven. *See* *Gonzalez*, 565 U.S. at 1007 (“The PLRA does not immunize a state from constitutional liability.”).

### IV. THE ORDER IS CONSISTENT WITH THE PLRA’S REQUIREMENTS FOR JUDICIAL SUPERVISION

The PLRA mandates that any injunctive relief be subject to “periodic review” and “modification” as circumstances change. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3). The district court’s order included all of these safeguards: a receiver with broad authority to implement reforms, regular reporting requirements, and a mechanism for judicial review. The court retained jurisdiction to modify or terminate the order if the State achieved compliance or if conditions changed.

This structure is precisely what the PLRA contemplates.
