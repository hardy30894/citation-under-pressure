**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to reentries completed before its April 1, 1997 effective date.**

The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), “applies to all aliens” who reenter illegally after having been removed. Because Mr. Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982, the statute reaches conduct completed fifteen years before enactment. “A statute may not be applied retroactively . . . absent a clear indication from Congress that it intended such a result.” *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001). The text contains no such indication. It neither references pre-enactment reentries nor supplies an express effective-date command overriding the presumption against retroactivity. *See Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994) (“[C]ongressional enactments . . . will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.”).

The government’s contrary reading would render the statute applicable whenever an alien remains present after the effective date. That construction converts a statute silent on temporal reach into one that operates retroactively whenever the alien has not yet departed—an interpretation *St. Cyr* rejected. 533 U.S. at 317.

**II. Application of § 241(a)(5) to pre-enactment reentries is impermissibly retroactive.**

Even if Congress intended retroactive reach, the statute may not be so applied because it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70. Before 1997, an alien who reentered illegally could seek adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255(i) once he became the beneficiary of an immediate-relative petition. Section 241(a)(5) eliminates that avenue entirely, converting a completed reentry into a permanent bar to relief. This “new disability” is precisely the type of retroactive effect *St. Cyr* held impermissible. 533 U.S. at 321.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Mr. Fernandez-Vargas lacked a “protectable expectation” of adjustment misapplies the test. Retroactivity turns not on subjective expectations but on whether the statute “increase[s] a party’s liability for past conduct” or “impose[s] new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. By stripping eligibility for adjustment that existed at the time of reentry and during the ensuing fifteen years of residence, the statute imposes exactly such a new disability.

Because the statute lacks the requisite clear statement and because its application to pre-1997 reentries produces the forbidden retroactive effect, the reinstatement order must be vacated.
