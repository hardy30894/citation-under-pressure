**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a federal appellate court reviewing for plain error may consider the entire record—including matters outside the trial evidence—to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This conclusion follows from settled principles governing plain-error review and harmless-error analysis, all established well before 1970.

**I. Plain-Error Review Permits Consideration of the Full Record**

The plain-error doctrine, rooted in *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936), allows an appellate court to notice “plain errors or defects affecting substantial rights” even if not raised below. But the defendant must show prejudice—that the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946). Critically, the appellate court assesses prejudice “in light of the entire record.” *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 67 (1942). The court is not confined to the evidence presented to the jury; it may consider all materials properly part of the record on appeal. *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189, 200 (1943) (reviewing “the whole record” to determine whether plain error was harmless).

Here, the Eleventh Circuit examined Greer’s prior convictions—which were part of the presentence report, plea agreement, and district court record—to conclude that he could not show prejudice from the *Rehaif* error. That approach is consistent with *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), where this Court considered facts outside the trial evidence to assess whether an error was harmless. And in *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), the Court reviewed the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, to determine whether a plain error required reversal. The plain-error doctrine exists to prevent “miscarriages of justice,” *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 107 (1945), and that inquiry necessarily looks beyond the trial record to the full context of the case.

**II. The Appellate Court May Take Judicial Notice of Undisputed Facts**

The Eleventh Circuit did not err by considering Greer’s prior convictions because those convictions were matters of public record and could be judicially noticed. *See Varcoe v. Lee*, 181 U.S. 412, 418 (1901) (courts may take judicial notice of public records). Greer’s status as a felon was undisputed; he had five prior aggravated-burglary convictions. The court could properly rely on that fact to conclude that he knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. *Cf. Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (recognizing that courts may consider “common knowledge” in assessing evidentiary presumptions).

Moreover, the harmless-error standard articulated in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), and *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86 (1963), requires the reviewing court to determine whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. That determination is not limited to the trial evidence; it encompasses “the whole record.” *Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 24. Here, the record overwhelmingly demonstrated that Greer knew he was a felon—he had served multiple prison terms for felony offenses. The error therefore could not have affected his substantial rights.

**III. Greer’s Contrary Argument Would Undermine the Plain-Error Standard**

Greer contends that an appellate court may consider only the evidence presented to the jury when assessing prejudice. But that view conflicts with the discretionary nature of plain-error review. As *Atkinson* explained, the doctrine exists to ensure that “substantial justice” is done. 297 U.S. at 160. An appellate court cannot determine whether an error affected the “fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings” without examining the full record, including facts that the defendant himself admitted. *See Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 763–64 (prejudice assessed “in the setting of the particular case”). To hold otherwise would require reversal even when the defendant’s own prior convictions conclusively establish the missing element—a result that would serve no legitimate purpose and would undermine public confidence in the judicial system.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Eleventh Circuit properly considered Greer’s prior convictions, which were part of the record, to conclude that the *Rehaif* error did not prejudice him. This Court should affirm.
