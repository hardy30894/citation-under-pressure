ARGUMENT

I. This Court Has Long Refused to Let Rigid Procedural Rules Foreclose Habeas Review of Substantial Constitutional Claims

The Great Writ has never been treated by this Court as a creature of technical forfeiture. In Walker v. Wainwright, 390 U.S. 335 (1968), this Court reversed a lower court that had refused even to consider a prisoner's constitutional claims because a favorable ruling "would not result in the petitioner's immediate release from prison." The Court rejected that mechanical approach, making clear that a habeas court may not use a procedural rule to insulate a conviction from any review of its constitutional validity. The same solicitude animated Peyton v. Rowe, 391 U.S. 54 (1968), where the Court abandoned the rule that a prisoner serving consecutive sentences could not challenge a sentence he had not yet begun to serve, precisely because that rule "not[ed]... would not arrive until" decades later and thus effectively barred review altogether. These decisions reflect a consistent principle: where a procedural rule threatens to place a constitutional claim permanently beyond judicial reach, this Court has favored the rule that preserves access to review.

That principle controls here. Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was never abandoned by choice; it was lost because his appellate counsel—the very agent charged with protecting his right to be heard—failed to raise it. To hold that this default can never be excused unless Carpenter first independently overcomes a separate default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself is to build exactly the kind of impenetrable procedural wall that Walker and Peyton refused to erect.

II. A Default Caused by Counsel's Own Constitutional Deficiency Cannot Be Treated as the Petitioner's Own Considered Choice

The right to counsel "guaranteed by the Constitution," Nowakowski v. Maroney, 386 U.S. 542, 542 (1967) (citing Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335), is not satisfied by the mere appointment of a lawyer; it requires effective assistance. When appellate counsel's ineffectiveness causes a claim to be defaulted, the resulting forfeiture is not attributable to the petitioner in any meaningful sense—it is attributable to the State's failure to provide the assistance the Constitution demands.

This Court's decisions concerning waiver of counsel confirm that a forfeiture of constitutional magnitude cannot be assumed absent a knowing and intelligent choice by the petitioner himself. In Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941), the Court examined whether the record sustained a finding that the petitioner "had competently and intelligently waived his right to such assistance" before treating any forfeiture as valid. And in United States ex rel. McCann v. Adams, 320 U.S. 220, 220 (1943), the Court refused to treat a claim resting on matters "dehors the trial proceedings" as forfeited without a hearing, explaining that it could not say the petition was "palpably unmeritorious, and should have been dismissed without more." The same reasoning applies with full force to a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim offered as cause: the default was not Carpenter's considered choice, and it should not be treated as if it were.

III. Requiring Independent Exhaustion of the Cause Claim Itself Would Frustrate, Not Serve, the Purposes of the Exhaustion Doctrine

The exhaustion requirement exists to give state courts a fair opportunity to pass on federal claims before a federal court intervenes—not to erect successive, self-defeating procedural barriers. As this Court explained in Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 200 (1950), the doctrine asks only that "all state remedies available... have been exhausted" so that comity is respected. Carpenter satisfied that purpose: he presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio Court of Appeals through an application to reopen his direct appeal, giving the State a full opportunity to correct the error. That the state court declined to reach the merits on timeliness grounds does not diminish the fact that the State's courts had their chance. Insisting that Carpenter now show independent cause and prejudice for that default before it may excuse the default of his underlying claim would not serve comity; it would simply multiply procedural obstacles without ever reaching the constitutional violation at issue.

This Court's traditional concern for indigent prisoners' meaningful access to collateral review reinforces this conclusion. In Smith v. Bennett, 365 U.S. 708 (1961), the Court considered the burdens indigent prisoners face in navigating post-conviction procedures, and in Long v. District Court of Iowa, Lee Cty., 385 U.S. 192 (1966), the Court recognized the necessity of a transcript for an indigent prisoner to meaningfully pursue a habeas appeal. Both decisions recognize that formal procedural requirements must yield when they would otherwise deny a prisoner any real opportunity to vindicate a federal right. See also Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266 (1948) (recognizing habeas corpus's broad remedial function in securing a prisoner's liberty interests).

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
