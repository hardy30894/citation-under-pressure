**ARGUMENT**

I. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That a Fee Enhancement May Be Awarded for Exceptional Performance and Results.

The Eleventh Circuit properly affirmed the district court’s award of an enhancement because it was supported by specific evidence of superior performance and exceptional results. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984) (holding that an enhancement may be justified in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and resulted in “exceptional” success). The district court’s detailed findings—based on its observation of counsel’s performance over the course of the litigation—demonstrate that this is precisely such a case. *See id.* at 897-901 (explaining that the lodestar figure is presumed reasonable but may be adjusted in exceptional circumstances).

This Court has long recognized that fee enhancements are permissible when justified by extraordinary circumstances. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 537 (1882) (holding that a court of equity may allow counsel fees when the litigation has conferred a substantial benefit on the plaintiff class). The same principle applies in statutory fee-shifting cases, where Congress has authorized reasonable attorney’s fees to ensure access to justice. *See Alyeska Pipeline Serv. Co. v. Wilderness Soc’y*, 421 U.S. 240, 262 (1975) (recognizing that fee-shifting statutes are designed to encourage private enforcement of important public rights). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents, which permit enhancements in rare and exceptional cases. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899; *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 718 (1967) (affirming an award of attorney’s fees under a statutory fee-shifting provision).

II. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Fee Award.

The district court’s decision to enhance the lodestar was well within its broad discretion. *See Sprague v. Ticonic Nat’l Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 166 (1939) (holding that a court has discretion to award attorney’s fees when necessary to achieve justice). The court’s findings—based on its firsthand observation of counsel’s performance—were specific and supported by the record. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899 (requiring “specific evidence” to justify an enhancement). The Eleventh Circuit correctly deferred to the district court’s assessment, as it is uniquely positioned to evaluate the quality of advocacy. *See id.* at 897-901 (explaining that the lodestar is presumed reasonable but may be adjusted in exceptional cases).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that fee awards must be reasonable and tailored to the circumstances of the case. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537 (holding that fees must be reasonable and proportionate to the benefit conferred). The district court’s enhancement was justified by the extraordinary results achieved and the exceptional skill demonstrated by plaintiffs’ counsel. *See Fleischmann Distilling Corp.*, 386 U.S. at 718 (affirming a fee award where the litigation conferred a substantial benefit on the class). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents, which permit enhancements in rare and exceptional cases. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899; *Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 166.

III. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That the District Court Did Not Violate Due Process.

The dissent’s due process concerns are unfounded. The district court’s findings were based on its own observations of counsel’s performance—not ex parte testimony—and were fully supported by the record. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899 (requiring “specific evidence” to justify an enhancement).
