I.

The release executed on June 6, 1988, does not bar the claims. Its operative language releases only claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” The present claims concern the FDIC’s post-closure conduct as receiver—payment of post-insolvency interest beginning March 15, 1991, sale of assets below franchise value, and charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These events occurred after the OCC’s closure decision and are therefore outside the release’s terms.

Releases “are to be construed in accordance with the intent of the parties.” Mangini v. McClurg, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969). When the language is unambiguous, courts give effect only to the matters expressly covered. See Lucio v. Curran, 2 N.Y.2d 157, 161 (1956). The release here was exchanged in settlement of the September 1985 and May 1986 actions, which addressed the OCC’s closure decision and the seizure of Chuang’s offices. Nothing in the text extends to the FDIC’s subsequent administration of the receivership. The district court’s broader reading therefore contravenes the plain language.

II.

The claims are also timely. N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) supplies a six-year limitations period for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste. A cause of action against a receiver for misconduct in administering the estate accrues upon termination of the receivership. See Armstrong v. McAlpin, 699 F.2d 79, 89 (2d Cir. 1983) (fiduciary claims accrue when the fiduciary relationship ends and the plaintiff can ascertain the injury). The FDIC terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995. Because the limitations period began to run only upon termination, the action is timely.

The district court’s alternative holding that the period commenced earlier is inconsistent with the rule that a receiver’s final accounting and discharge mark the point at which claims for mismanagement become actionable. See Dabney v. Levy, 191 F.2d 201, 205 (2d Cir. 1951). Accordingly, neither the release nor the statute of limitations bars the claims.
