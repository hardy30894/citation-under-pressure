The district court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal affords no basis for habeas relief. Collateral attack lies only where the asserted error constitutes "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A mere omission under Rule 32 does not meet this standard when the defendant already knew of the right and elected to forgo appeal. See United States v. Morgan, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954); United States v. Hayman, 342 U.S. 205, 219 (1952).

Habeas corpus is unavailable to correct non-prejudicial procedural defaults. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938); Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275, 286 (1941). The writ tests only whether the prisoner is detained in violation of the Constitution or laws in a manner that actually denied a substantive right. Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 104 (1942); Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953). Knowledge of appellate rights eliminates any claim of prejudice. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963).

Even constitutional errors are subject to harmless-error review on collateral attack. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967). Where the record establishes that the defendant was aware of and deliberately waived the right to appeal, the Rule 32 violation caused no injury and supplies no ground for vacating the sentence. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). The judgment below should be affirmed.
