**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing enhancements—contradicts settled precedent and undermines a fundamental protection against governmental overreach. The Court must reverse, for the Double Jeopardy Clause applies not only to trials on guilt or innocence but also to sentencing determinations that carry the same risk of cumulative punishment and stigma. The Clause’s core purpose is to protect individuals from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense, and when a prior conviction is alleged to trigger a harsher sentence, the sentencing proceeding is not a mere administrative formality—it is a critical stage of the criminal process that implicates the same constitutional concerns as a trial on guilt.

The lead opinion in *Monge* attempts to distinguish *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), on the ground that those cases involved capital sentencing and thus are inapplicable to noncapital cases. But this distinction is legally unsound. In *Bullington*, the Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a sentencing enhancement after a prior jury had acquitted him of a capital sentencing factor—specifically, the presence of aggravating circumstances that would justify a death sentence. The Court emphasized that “the sentencing proceeding in a capital case is not a mere formality” and that “the defendant is entitled to the full panoply of procedural protections that attend a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439, 441 (citing *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 582 (1968)). The Court did not limit its reasoning to capital cases; rather, it grounded its holding in the nature of the proceeding itself—the fact that the defendant was being subjected to a second prosecution for a fact that could result in a life-or-death sentence. The same logic applies when a defendant faces a sentence that is doubled or otherwise substantially increased based on a prior conviction. The *risk* of enhanced punishment is not diminished by the absence of a death penalty.

The Court has repeatedly affirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing determinations when those determinations involve factual findings that are “indistinguishable in kind” from those at trial. In *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389 (1995), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial on a sentencing enhancement after a prior conviction was overturned due to insufficient evidence. The Court reasoned that “the sentencing proceeding is not a mere formality” and that “the defendant is entitled to the protection of the Double Jeopardy Clause when the government seeks to impose a greater punishment based on a fact that was previously litigated.” *Witte*, 515 U.S. at 398. The Court further emphasized that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense,” and that “the same offense” includes not only the underlying crime but also the factual determinations that trigger enhanced punishment. *Id.* at 397.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection in noncapital cases is misplaced. *Williams* upheld a sentencing judge’s reliance on untested, out-of-court information—such as confessions and identifications—without a jury trial or confrontation rights. But *Williams* did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause; it addressed due process and the right to a fair sentencing hearing. The Court in *Williams* explicitly stated that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251. That statement does not imply that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable. Indeed, the Court has since reaffirmed that *Williams* does not preclude double jeopardy protection in sentencing proceedings. In *U.S. v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial on a sentencing enhancement after a prior finding was reversed. The Court stated that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense,” and that “the same offense” includes “the factual determinations that trigger enhanced punishment.” *Watts*, 519 U.S. at 155.

The California Supreme Court also misreads *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), which held that due process does not require a hearing on sentencing factors. But *Specht* did not address double jeopardy. The Court in *Specht* emphasized that “the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a judge to have hearings and to give a convicted person an opportunity to participate in those hearings when he comes to determine the sentence.” *Specht*, 386 U.S. at 606. This is a due process holding, not a double jeopardy one. The two clauses serve different purposes: due process ensures fairness in the process, while double jeopardy protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense. The fact that a state may not be constitutionally required to provide a hearing does not mean that a defendant may be retried after a prior conviction is overturned.
