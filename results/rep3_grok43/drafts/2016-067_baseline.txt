**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s],” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a term the statute defines to reach “any person who . . . regularly collects or attempts to collect . . . debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” The Act therefore “generally does not regulate creditors when they collect debt on their own account.” The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the loans outright and thereafter sought to collect them for its own account. That conduct places Santander outside the statutory definition.

The statutory text confirms the distinction. The phrase “owed or due another” refers to debts owed to a third party at the time of collection, not debts that originated with another. See Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (Act covers person “who regularly collects or attempts to collect . . . debts owed [to] . . . another”); Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (“our inquiry . . . is whether [the entity] regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection”); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the . . . interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another’”). Because Santander was collecting debts it owned, it was not collecting debts “owed or due another.”

The default status of the debt at the time of assignment is irrelevant to this threshold inquiry. The Act’s definitional structure requires a court first to determine whether an entity satisfies one of the affirmative definitions in § 1692a(6) before considering any exclusion. Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1314. Nothing in the text conditions the “owed or due another” requirement on whether the debt was in default when acquired. The legislative history likewise focuses on third-party collection activity, not on the timing of default. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would subject every entity that both purchases defaulted debt and services loans for others to FDCPA liability whenever it collects its own accounts—an outcome the statute’s text and structure do not support. The complaint therefore fails to allege that Santander was acting as a “debt collector” when it engaged in the challenged communications, and the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
