# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 241(a)(5) DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS, LIKE PETITIONER, WHO RE-ENTERED THE UNITED STATES BEFORE THE STATUTE'S 1997 EFFECTIVE DATE

Congress did not clearly state that the reinstatement provision, enacted as part of IIRIRA in 1996 and effective April 1, 1997, was meant to reach re-entries that occurred years before that date. This Court has long insisted that statutes altering the legal consequences of completed conduct require a clear congressional statement before they will be read to operate on events predating enactment. The text of Section 241(a)(5) speaks in terms that most naturally describe conduct occurring after the statute exists—an alien who "has entered" illegally after a prior removal order is subject to reinstatement without further hearing. Nothing in that text expressly reaches back to re-entries, like Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's 1982 re-entry, that occurred fifteen years before the provision existed.

This Court's decisions construing analogous immigration provisions confirm that Congress must speak with precision when it intends statutes to operate on pre-enactment conduct. In *Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Phinpathya*, 464 U.S. 183 (1984), the Court undertook a careful textual analysis of the "continuous physical presence" requirement of former Section 244(a)(1), recognizing that eligibility determinations turn on the specific statutory language Congress chose, not on the agency's preferred policy outcome. The same interpretive care is due here. Where Congress *has* intended immigration statutes to reach pre-enactment conduct, it has said so explicitly. In *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685 (1957), the relevant provision stated in terms that it applied "notwithstanding (1) that any such alien entered the United States prior to the date of enactment of this Act, or (2) that the facts, by reason of which any such alien belongs to any of the classes enumerated in subsection (a), occurred prior to the date of enactment of this Act." *Id.* at 686. The companion case, *Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692 (1957), applied that same explicit retroactivity clause to conduct—a 1925 narcotics conviction—that long predated the statute rendering it a deportation ground. Congress plainly knows how to write a statute that reaches backward in time. It did not do so in Section 241(a)(5).

The absence of such language is telling. Where Congress omits the kind of explicit temporal reach found in the statute construed in *Lehmann*, this Court should not supply it. The Tenth Circuit's contrary holding effectively imports into Section 241(a)(5) language Congress never enacted.

## II. IF SECTION 241(a)(5) IS READ TO APPLY TO PRE-1997 RE-ENTRIES, THAT APPLICATION IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE

Even if the statutory text could be stretched to cover re-entries predating its enactment, applying Section 241(a)(5) to Mr. Fernandez-Vargas attaches new and severe legal consequences to conduct and a status that were complete before the statute existed. Prior to 1997, an alien in Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's position retained the possibility of seeking discretionary relief, including adjustment of status based on marriage to a United States citizen. Section 241(a)(5) eliminates that possibility entirely and does so by operation of a re-entry that occurred under a wholly different legal regime.

This Court's decisions confirm that discretionary relief from deportation is a substantial and legally cognizable interest, not a mere procedural nicety that can be stripped away without retroactivity concerns. In *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72 (1957), the Court treated an alien's eligibility for suspension of deportation as a matter of real consequence, tied to specific statutory findings the Attorney General was required to make, including whether deportation "would result in serious economic detriment to a citizen or legally resident alien who is the spouse, parent, or minor child of such deportable alien." *Id.* at 73. Similarly, in *Foti v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 375 U.S. 217 (1963), the Court recognized that the denial of suspension of deportation was significant enough to trigger judicial review as a "final order of deportation," underscoring that eligibility for such discretionary relief is a substantial interest the law protects through structured procedures.

Mr. Fernandez-Vargas built a life in the United States for nearly two decades, married a United States citizen, and sought to adjust his status through the very channels immigration law made available to him at the time he acted. Applying Section 241(a)(5) retroactively does not merely change forum or procedure; it eliminates a substantive avenue of relief based on a re-entry that occurred under the prior statutory scheme, when other consequences attached to that same conduct. This is precisely the kind of "unfair surprise" and disruption of settled expectations that retroactivity doctrine exists to prevent.

The Tenth Circuit's assertion that Mr. Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" mischaracterizes the relevant inquiry. The question is not whether he had a guaranteed right to relief, but whether the new statute attaches new legal consequences to his prior re-entry that did not exist when he acted. Because it does—transforming a decades-old re-entry into an automatic, relief-foreclosing basis for summary removal—Section 241(a)(5)'s application here is impermissibly retroactive.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
