The Bankruptcy Act exempts only those assets that serve the statutory purpose of preserving a debtor's post-petition livelihood. See Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (exemptions "must be construed in light of the purpose" of affording "a fresh start"). An inherited IRA fails this test. Once the original owner dies, the account ceases to be a vehicle for anyone's retirement and instead becomes a time-limited tax-deferral device that the beneficiary must draw down for immediate use. See Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 190 (1902) (exemptions turn on the character of the property "as it stands at the date of the petition").

This Court has long held that property loses its exempt status when it no longer fulfills the function for which the exemption was created. See White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924) (homestead exemption requires actual occupancy "at the time of the filing of the petition"); see also Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 626 (1943) (same). Just as a house inherited and rented out is no longer the debtor's homestead, funds inherited from another's IRA are no longer retirement funds of the present owner. The statutory text therefore supplies no basis for exemption.

The same result follows from the rule that exemptions are strictly limited to the categories Congress has specified. See Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 190 (1904) (courts may not enlarge exemptions beyond the "plain language" of the statute). Because an inherited IRA is not held for the debtor's retirement, it falls outside the exemption and remains property of the estate.
