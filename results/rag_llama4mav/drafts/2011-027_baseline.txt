The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the state acquired title to the riverbeds at issue because those rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. The equal footing doctrine, as established by the Supreme Court, provides that upon admission to the Union, a state gains title to the lands underlying navigable waters within its boundaries. Utah Division of State Lands v. United States, 482 U.S. 193, 195-96 (1987). The test for determining navigability for title purposes is whether the watercourse was navigable at the time of statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931).

PPL argues that the trial court erred in determining navigability at the time of statehood, but this argument is unfounded. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the navigability of a watercourse is determined at the time of statehood. Idaho v. United States, 533 U.S. 262, 272 (2001) (citing United States v. Alaska, 521 U.S. 1, 5 (1997)). The trial court's determination of navigability was based on a thorough examination of historical evidence, including reports and documents from the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that this evidence was admissible under various hearsay exceptions, including M. R. Evid. 902 and 803(8).

The equal footing doctrine is deeply rooted in history and is an essential attribute of sovereignty. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 3 How. 212 (1845). The doctrine is not limited to the original 13 states, but applies to all states that have been admitted to the Union. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 15 (1894). The Supreme Court has consistently applied this doctrine to determine title to submerged lands. See, e.g., Oregon Ex Rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co., 429 U.S. 363 (1977); Grand River Dam Authority v. Grand-Hydro, 335 U.S. 359 (1949).

PPL's argument that the trial court should have considered more recent evidence of navigability is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has made clear that the relevant timeframe for determining navigability is the time of statehood. Idaho, 533 U.S. at 272. The trial court's focus on the historical evidence from 1889, the year Montana was admitted to the Union, was therefore correct.

In conclusion, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine and the test for determining navigability for title purposes. The trial court's determination of navigability was based on a thorough examination of historical evidence, and the state's title to the riverbeds at issue is established. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
