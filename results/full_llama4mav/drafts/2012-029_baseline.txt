The Supreme Court of Texas correctly determined that federal courts possess exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it requires the application of federal patent law. As the Court held, the federal patent issue presented here is necessary, disputed, and substantial within the context of the overlying state legal malpractice lawsuit, and its determination will not create a jurisdictional imbalance between state and federal courts. See Minton v. Gunn, 355 S.W.3d 634, 641 (Tex. 2011).

Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has construed this language to extend federal jurisdiction to cases in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). In Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308 (2005), the Court refined this test, holding that federal jurisdiction exists when a state-law claim necessarily raises a stated federal issue that is actually disputed and substantial, and which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Id. at 314.

Minton's legal malpractice claim against Gunn satisfies all four elements of the Grable test. First, resolving the federal patent issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim because Minton's malpractice claim depends on the application of the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar, a federal patent law concept. See id. at 312-13. Second, the federal issue is actually disputed, as Minton and Gunn disagree on whether the experimental use exception would have saved Minton's patent infringement claim. See id. at 313. Third, the federal issue is substantial, as it involves a critical aspect of patent law that is central to Minton's malpractice claim. See id. at 314.

Finally, federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities because patent law is a complex and specialized area that is best left to federal courts. See id. at 315. The Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), support this conclusion, as they held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-law malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law.

The dissenting opinion's criticism that the majority followed Federal Circuit case law without properly applying the Grable test is unfounded. The majority correctly applied the Grable factors and considered the federalism concerns at play. See Minton, 355 S.W.3d at 641-42. The fact that some other courts have reached different conclusions on similar issues does not undermine the Texas Supreme Court's decision, which was based on a thorough analysis of the relevant law and facts.

In conclusion, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it requires the application of federal patent law and satisfies the Grable test. The Supreme Court of Texas correctly determined that it lacked subject-matter jurisdiction to consider Minton's appeal, and its decision should be affirmed.
