**Argument**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 applies unless another federal agency has actually exercised statutory authority over the specific working conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court has repeatedly recognized that § 4(b)(1) is a narrow preemption provision, not a free-standing exemption for whole industries. See *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 243–46 (2002); *Southern Ry. Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 539 F.2d 335, 339–40 (4th Cir. 1976); *Columbia Gas of Ohio, Inc. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 636 F.2d 913, 915–16 (3d Cir. 1980). Because the Coast Guard had not exercised authority over the hazardous working conditions that caused the explosion aboard Rig 52, OSHA retained jurisdiction. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 247–53.

1. **Section 4(b)(1) preempts OSHA only to the extent another agency has in fact regulated the particular working conditions at issue.**

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA coverage only when “other Federal agencies” exercise statutory authority “to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Supreme Court has held that this requires “actual exercise” of authority over the specific working conditions in question, not merely the existence of some broader regulatory power. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 246–47; *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119, 121–22 (2d Cir. 1978); *Secretary of Labor v. Trinity Indus., Inc.*, 504 F.2d 642, 644–45 (5th Cir. 1974). That construction accords with the statute’s text, which speaks of agencies that “exercise” authority, not agencies that could exercise it someday. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 244–45.

Here, the Coast Guard’s post-accident investigation did not identify any vessel-safety regulation governing the conditions that led to the blast. The Court of Appeals erred by treating the Coast Guard’s general maritime authority as if it were a complete occupational-safety regime. But broad regulatory responsibility is not enough; § 4(b)(1) requires regulation of the working conditions that OSHA cited. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 247–50; *Texas Eastern Transmission Corp. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 695 F.2d 717, 721–22 (3d Cir. 1982).

2. **Minimal Coast Guard regulation of uninspected vessels does not displace OSHA.**

This Court in *Chao* held that the Coast Guard’s limited rules for uninspected vessels did not preempt OSHA because those rules did not cover the cited conditions. 534 U.S. at 248–52. The Court explained that the Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels addressed only discrete subjects such as firefighting equipment, life preservers, ventilation, and related marine safety matters. *Id.* at 249–51; see also 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. pts. 24–28. That limited regulatory scheme was not the “comprehensive occupational safety and health standards” that would exclude OSHA. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 250–52.

That is this case. Rig 52 was an uninspected drilling barge subject only to minimal Coast Guard regulation. The Fifth Circuit’s rule—that any Coast Guard jurisdiction over seamen or vessels categorically ousts OSHA—cannot be reconciled with *Chao*’s insistence on condition-specific, actual regulation. 534 U.S. at 247–53. Indeed, the Supreme Court expressly rejected the argument that Coast Guard oversight of uninspected vessels creates an industry-wide OSHA exemption. *Id.* at 251–53.

3. **The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on prior circuit precedent cannot overcome the Supreme Court’s construction of § 4(b)(1).**

The Fifth Circuit relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). But *Chao* explained that those decisions overread § 4(b)(1) by treating the Coast Guard’s maritime authority as exclusive even where the cited hazards were not regulated. 534 U.S. at 244–45, 252–53. A circuit rule cannot survive when the Supreme Court has clarified that preemption depends on actual regulation of the working condition cited. *Id.* at 247–53; *Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 499 U.S. 144, 150–51 (1991).

Nor does the fact that Rig 52 was a vessel change the analysis. OSHA and Coast Guard authority can coexist unless and until the Coast Guard has exercised authority over the specific hazard. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 247–50; *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 827–29 (5th Cir. 1983) (recognizing concern with overlapping regulation, but not deciding a case involving the limited regulatory record present here).

4. **Because the Coast Guard did not regulate the cited hazards, OSHA had jurisdiction.**

The statute asks whether another federal agency has exercised authority “with respect to” the working conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Supreme Court in *Chao* answered the question here: minimal Coast Guard regulation of an uninspected drilling barge does not preempt OSHA where the cited safety conditions were not subject to Coast Guard standards. 534 U.S. at 247–53. That holding controls. The Court of Appeals’ contrary rule would create exactly the sort of broad, automatic maritime exemption that *Chao* rejected. *Id.* at 251–53.

For these reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment recognizing OSHA’s jurisdiction and sustain the citations.
