The power of a court of equity to award counsel fees has long been recognized as including discretion to consider the nature and value of services rendered. As this Court explained, "the allowance of counsel fees ... is ... a matter of discretion, to be exercised in view of the circumstances of the case." *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 536 (1882). That discretion extends to the results achieved, for "where one ... has created or preserved a fund," the fee may reflect "the benefit conferred." *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 126-27 (1885).

This authority is not confined to a mechanical calculation of hours and rates. In *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court affirmed that equitable power permits an award "in accordance with the established doctrines of equity," *id.* at 166, without rigid limitation to ordinary compensation. The same principle governs when services prove "extraordinary" in producing an outcome beyond the ordinary run of litigation. *See id.* at 167 (recognizing fees "as part of the costs" when the litigant has "at his own expense" secured a benefit shared by others).

Accordingly, a fee measured initially by time and rate may be increased when the record demonstrates that counsel's performance yielded results of exceptional character. The governing rule remains that stated in *Greenough*: the amount rests in the sound discretion of the court, exercised upon the facts of the particular case. 105 U.S. at 536. No pre-existing formula precludes an adjustment that reflects both the quality of advocacy and the concrete success obtained.
