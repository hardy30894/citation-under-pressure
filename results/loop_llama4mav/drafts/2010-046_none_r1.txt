ARGUMENT

The district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in the state's correctional facilities. This Court should affirm the order, as it is narrowly tailored to address the constitutional violations and does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) or California's Eleventh Amendment immunity.

I. The District Court's Order Complies with the PLRA

The PLRA requires that prospective relief in prison conditions cases be "narrowly drawn, extend[s] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The district court's order meets this standard, as it is designed to alleviate unconstitutional overcrowding in California's prisons. See Talley v. Stephens, 247 F. Supp. 683 (E.D. Ark. 1965); Holt v. Sarver, 300 F. Supp. 825 (E.D. Ark. 1969).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have the authority to order remedial relief, including population reduction, to address unconstitutional prison conditions. See Coffin v. Reichard, 143 F.2d 443 (6th Cir. 1944); cf. Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936). The district court's order is consistent with this authority and is a necessary measure to correct the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

II. The District Court's Order Does Not Violate California's Eleventh Amendment Immunity

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar the district court's order, as it is a prospective injunction aimed at remedying ongoing constitutional violations. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts may enjoin state officials from violating federal law, even if the relief has a significant impact on the state's treasury. See Smyth v. Ames, 169 U.S. 466 (1898); Missouri v. Fiske, 290 U.S. 18 (1933).

The district court's order is not a retroactive award of damages, but rather a prospective injunction designed to alleviate unconstitutional conditions. As such, it is not barred by the Eleventh Amendment. See Gunter v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co., 200 U.S. 273 (1906); Louisiana v. Jumel, 107 U.S. 711 (1883). The State's reliance on Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), is misplaced, as that case involved a retroactive award of damages, not a prospective injunction.

III. The District Court's Order Is a Permissible Exercise of Its Authority

The district court has broad discretion to fashion relief that is necessary to correct constitutional violations. See United States v. Montgomery County Bd. of Educ., 395 U.S. 225 (1969). The court's order is designed to alleviate unconstitutional overcrowding and is a necessary measure to correct the violations. The State has had ample opportunity to address the constitutional violations, but has failed to do so. The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy these violations.

In conclusion, the district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. It complies with the PLRA and does not violate California's Eleventh Amendment immunity. This Court should affirm the order.
