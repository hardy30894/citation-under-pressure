# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Refusal to Reopen Salinas's 2006 Claim Is a "Final Decision" Because Judicial Review of Agency Action Is Presumed Absent Clear Congressional Intent to Preclude It

The starting point for construing the scope of judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) is the settled principle that Congress rarely intends to insulate agency action from judicial scrutiny. This Court has long held that "judicial review of a final agency action by an aggrieved person will not be cut off unless there is persuasive reason to believe that such was the purpose of Congress." *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 140 (1967). That presumption traces back decades. In *Stark v. Wickard*, 321 U.S. 288 (1944), the Court explained that administrative determinations affecting legal rights are reviewable unless Congress has plainly withdrawn that remedy, and in *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48 (1955), the Court refused to infer preclusion of review from statutory silence, insisting instead on clear evidence of congressional intent to foreclose it. *See also Rusk v. Cort*, 369 U.S. 367 (1962) (reaffirming that restrictions on judicial review require unmistakable legislative direction).

Nothing in § 355(f) evinces an intent to exempt reopening denials from review. The statute authorizes review of "any final decision" of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A decision refusing to reopen a benefits claim is the Board's last word on the claimant's entitlement to relief; it terminates the administrative proceeding and leaves the claimant with no further recourse within the agency. Under the ordinary understanding of finality applied throughout this Court's jurisprudence, that is precisely what makes a decision "final." *See Estep v. United States*, 327 U.S. 114, 122–23 (1946) (treating a Selective Service classification as "final" once the Board's process was complete and no further administrative avenue remained, subject only to review for whether "there was any basis in fact" for the determination).

## II. This Court's Decision in *Dismuke v. United States* Confirms That Finality Clauses in Analogous Retirement Statutes Do Not Bar Review of Legal Determinations Underlying Reopening Decisions

The Railroad Retirement Act's structure mirrors that of other federal retirement statutes this Court has already construed. In *Dismuke v. United States*, 297 U.S. 167 (1936), the Court confronted a provision of the Civil Service Retirement Act stating that decisions of the retirement board "shall be final and conclusive and not subject to review." The Court held that this language did not strip the courts of authority to examine whether the agency had correctly applied the law to a claimant's application for an annuity; the finality clause reached only the Board's factual and discretionary judgments, not questions of law bearing on eligibility. *Id.* at 171–72. Salinas's challenge to the Board's refusal to reopen his 2006 denial rests on precisely such a question — whether the Board correctly applied its own regulatory reopening standards, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, to the record before it. *Dismuke* teaches that a "final and conclusive" label does not immunize that kind of legal determination from review, and the same logic applies with even greater force here, where § 355(f) contains no comparable restrictive language at all.

## III. The Second Circuit Correctly Held in *Szostak* That Reopening Denials Are Reviewable "Final Decisions" Under § 355(f)

The Second Circuit confronted this exact question under this exact statute and reached the correct result. In *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), the court held that the Board's denial of a petitioner's request to reopen a prior benefits determination constituted a "final decision" subject to review under § 355(f). *Id.* at 254–55. The court recognized that nothing in the statutory text distinguishes between a decision on the initial merits of a claim and a decision refusing to reopen a prior claim; both dispose finally of the claimant's rights and both flow from the same statutory grant of Board authority. That reasoning should control here. Salinas's 2006 claim, like Szostak's, was conclusively terminated by the Board's refusal to reopen it, leaving him with no further administrative remedy — the hallmark of finality this Court has consistently recognized.

## IV. The Remedial Purpose of the Railroad Retirement Act Requires Construing "Final Decision" to Encompass Reopening Denials

The Railroad Retirement Act, like the Social Security Act to which it is closely related, is remedial legislation designed to protect the economic security of railroad workers and their families. This Court has instructed that such statutes "must be liberally construed in favor of those seeking its benefits" rather than narrowed by technical readings that defeat their protective purpose. *Social Security Board v. Nierotko*, 327 U.S. 358, 364 (1946). Reading § 355(f) to exclude reopening denials from judicial review would produce the anomalous result that a claimant wrongly denied benefits on the merits could obtain review, while a claimant wrongly denied the opportunity to correct that same error through reopening could not — even though the practical effect on the claimant's rights is identical. Nothing in the Act's text or purpose supports that asymmetry.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Board's decision refusing to reopen Salinas's 2006 disability annuity application is a "final decision" reviewable under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
