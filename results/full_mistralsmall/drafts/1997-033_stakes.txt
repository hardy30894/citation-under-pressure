**ARGUMENT**

I. **Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Transferor District for Trial, and Section 1404(a) Cannot Be Invoked to Defeat That Command**

Section 1407(a) provides that when the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation transfers an action for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings, the Panel “shall remand” the action “to the original district” at or before the conclusion of such proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (emphasis added). This statutory command is unambiguous and mandatory. *See Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 35 (1998) (“The statute’s text is clear: the Panel ‘shall remand’ transferred actions ‘to the original district’ at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings.”). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary suggestion that § 1404(a) may be invoked to assign a transferred case to the transferee court for trial is legally erroneous.

The text of § 1407(a) does not authorize the transferee court to retain the case for trial. *See id.* at 36 (“Section 1407(a) says nothing about trial, and it does not empower the transferee court to retain the action for trial.”). Section 1404(a), by contrast, permits transfer of venue “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but it does not empower a transferee court to override § 1407(a)’s remand mandate. *See Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636 (1964) (explaining that § 1404(a) is a venue-shifting mechanism, not a mechanism to defeat § 1407(a)’s remand requirement). The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Those cases improperly conflated § 1404(a) with § 1407(a), ignoring the clear statutory distinction between pretrial coordination and trial venue. *See Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 37 (rejecting the argument that § 1404(a) may be used to defeat § 1407(a)’s remand mandate).

II. **The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation, Not the Transferee Court, Retains Authority Over Post-Pretrial Proceedings**

The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation is the sole entity empowered to determine the scope and duration of pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a). *See Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 35 (“The Panel’s authority is limited to pretrial proceedings; it has no power to order trials.”). Once pretrial proceedings conclude, the Panel must remand the action to the transferor district. *Id.* at 36. The transferee court’s attempt to invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial is an impermissible usurpation of the Panel’s statutory authority. *See id.* at 37 (“The transferee court’s power to conduct pretrial proceedings does not include the power to order a trial in the transferee district.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) is unavailing. Rule 14(b) cannot override the clear command of § 1407(a). *See United States v. Texas*, 507 U.S. 529, 534 (1993) (“An administrative rule cannot supersede a statute’s plain meaning.”). The Panel’s rulemaking authority is limited to procedural matters consistent with the statute. *See Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 37 n.10 (noting that Rule 14(b) “does not authorize the transferee court to retain the case for trial”).

III. **The Transferee Court’s Exercise of § 1404(a) Power Here Violates the Statutory Scheme and Undermines Congressional Intent**

Congress enacted § 1407(a) to streamline pretrial proceedings in complex cases while preserving plaintiffs’ choice of forum for trial. *See Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 35 (citing H.R. Rep. No. 88-722, at 2 (1963)). The transferee court’s attempt to retain the case for trial subverts this intent by forcing plaintiffs to litigate in a remote forum. *See id.* at 38 (“The transferee court’s retention of the case for trial frustrates the statute’s purpose by depriving plaintiffs of their chosen forum.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s suggestion that remand would cause inconvenience is irrelevant. *See id.* at 36 (“The inconvenience of remand is not a justification for ignoring the statute’s clear command.”). The statute’s text controls, and the transferee court’s attempt to circumvent it is unlawful. *See id.* at 37 (“The statute’s plain language must be enforced, regardless of policy concerns.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The transferee court lacked authority to invoke § 1404(a) to assign this case to itself for trial. Section 1407(a) mandates remand to the transferor district, and the transferee court’s contrary action is contrary to law. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to vacate the transferee court’s order assigning the case for trial.
