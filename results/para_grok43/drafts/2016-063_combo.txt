The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of Merit Systems Protection Board decisions according to the character of the claims presented. When an employee alleges that an adverse action rested on discrimination prohibited by federal statute, the case falls within the concurrent jurisdiction of the district courts. Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 681 (1946) (jurisdiction exists when the complaint is drawn to seek recovery under the Constitution or laws of the United States). The allegations themselves determine the forum; a later determination that the Board lacked jurisdiction over the underlying personnel action does not divest the district court of authority to adjudicate the discrimination component. Id. at 682.

Federal statutes condemning racial, age, and disability discrimination in employment have long been enforced through original actions in district court. Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co., 392 U.S. 409, 412 (1968); Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167, 172 (1961). Those enactments supply both the substantive right and the procedural avenue. The same principle governs here: once the employee has asserted that the agency action was infected by prohibited discrimination, the case is no longer an ordinary personnel dispute but one presenting a federal question properly heard in district court. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954); Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954).

This allocation of jurisdiction is not altered by the Board’s conclusion that the retirement or suspension was voluntary. The complaint’s well-pleaded allegations control. Bell, 327 U.S. at 681. A jurisdictional dismissal by the Board merely confirms that the personnel claim cannot proceed administratively; it leaves untouched the independent federal claim of discrimination, which remains cognizable under the statutes that authorize district-court suits. Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States, 379 U.S. 241, 249 (1964); Katzenbach v. McClung, 379 U.S. 294, 296 (1964).

Uniformity of interpretation is achieved by directing all such mixed claims to the district courts, whose judgments are reviewable in the regional courts of appeals. Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938). Fragmenting review between the Federal Circuit and the district courts would produce the very disuniformity the governing statutes were designed to avoid. United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 316 (1941). Accordingly, the petition for review should be heard in the district court.
