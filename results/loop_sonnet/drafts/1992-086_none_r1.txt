# ARGUMENT

## I. Once the District Opened Its Facilities to Community Groups for Expressive Uses, It Created a Forum in Which Content-Based Exclusions Are Presumptively Invalid.

The First Amendment's protection of expression is not confined to the streets and parks that have "immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). When government makes property available to the public for expressive activity, it assumes the same obligation of even-handedness that governs the traditional forum. The District here did precisely that: it repeatedly permitted civic organizations, musical performers, and lecturers to use its auditorium after school hours. Having thrown open its doors to community expression on secular subjects, the District could not then slam them shut on the sole ground that Lamb's Chapel's message on family and child-rearing happened to be presented from a religious perspective.

That the Chapel's message took the form of a film does not diminish its claim to protection. This Court has long recognized that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas," *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952), entitled to the same constitutional solicitude as the spoken or printed word. The District cannot escape its obligations of neutrality by mislabeling a film about family values as something other than the protected speech it plainly is.

## II. The District's Exclusion of Religious Speech, While Tolerating Comparable Secular Speech, Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly struck down government action that denies public facilities to speakers precisely because their message is religious, while permitting similar secular uses. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court invalidated the denial of a park permit to a religious group where secular and other religious groups had been allowed to use the same park, holding that the denial rested not on neutral criteria but on official disapproval of the group's beliefs. The Court reached the identical result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), and again in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), where unbridled licensing discretion was used to silence a speaker because officials disagreed with his religious message. See also *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (condemning a licensing scheme that left officials free to pick and choose among speakers without objective standards).

The District's own correspondence confirms the same defect here. Its clerk denied Lamb's Chapel's application because the film "does appear to be church related," even as the District permitted a "Psychology and the Unknown" lecture series touching on parapsychology and metaphysics and a gospel-influenced musical performance by the Southern Harmonizers. As in *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Kunz*, the District drew its line not along neutral, content-independent criteria, but along the forbidden axis of religious viewpoint. No official "can prescribe what shall be orthodox in politics, nationalism, religion, or other matters of opinion." *West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943).

## III. That the Forum Is School Property Does Not License Suppression of Disfavored Viewpoints.

Public school property is not an exception to these settled principles. This Court has made clear that "state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism," and that persons who use school facilities do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." *Tinker v. Des Moines Indep. Community Sch. Dist.*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). Nor does the collective character of Lamb's Chapel's request diminish its protection; the right of like-minded citizens to advance shared beliefs through joint activity is itself a "form of ordered group activity" protected by the First Amendment. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 430 (1963). The District cannot invoke the school setting to justify a form of discrimination that would be plainly unconstitutional in a park or on a street corner.

## IV. Equal Access for Lamb's Chapel Would Vindicate, Not Violate, the Establishment Clause's Command of Neutrality.

Anticipating an Establishment Clause defense, the District errs in assuming that equal treatment of religious speech amounts to state endorsement of religion. This Court's Establishment Clause jurisprudence demands neutrality, not hostility, toward religious expression. As *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947), makes clear, government "cannot exclude" individuals "because of their faith, or lack of it," from benefits otherwise available to the public. And in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), the Court explained that to bar accommodation of religious activity "would be to find in the Constitution a requirement that the government show a callous indifference to religious groups." Extending to Lamb's Chapel the same access afforded secular civic and cultural groups would reflect the neutrality the Establishment Clause requires—not offend it.

## CONCLUSION

The District's categorical exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Hague*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Kunz*, *Saia*, *Burstyn*, *Barnette*, *Tinker*, *Button*, *Everson*, or *Zorach*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
