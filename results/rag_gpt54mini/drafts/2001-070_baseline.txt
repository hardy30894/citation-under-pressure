**Argument**

The Eighth Amendment does not categorically forbid the execution of mentally retarded offenders. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely rejected the claim that “imposition of the death penalty was not prohibited by virtue of Penry’s mental retardation.” Id. at 306. The Court held that mental retardation is relevant mitigating evidence, but it declined to announce a per se constitutional bar. Id. at 328-30. That controlling precedent remains binding unless and until the Court concludes that a national consensus and the Court’s own judgment now require a different rule. *Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 237 (1997).

Atkins asks the Court to overrule *Penry*, but the record does not demonstrate the sort of settled national consensus that warrants constitutional invalidation of a penalty long accepted by legislatures. The Court has repeatedly explained that the Eighth Amendment draws meaning from the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Yet the Court has also insisted that objective indicia—especially legislative judgments—provide the principal evidence of those standards. *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989). As *Penry* observed, although some States had already limited execution of mentally retarded offenders, “there is no evidence of a national consensus against this practice.” 492 U.S. at 335.

Indeed, the very structure of capital punishment law confirms that mental retardation has historically been treated as a mitigating circumstance, not an absolute bar. In *Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 115-16 (1982), the Court held that sentencers must be allowed to consider youth and background as mitigation. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978). *Penry* extended that principle to mental retardation, requiring that the jury be able to give mitigating effect to such evidence. 492 U.S. at 328-29. The Constitution thus demands individualized sentencing; it does not require a categorical exemption. *Jurek v. Texas*, 428 U.S. 262, 276 (1976) (plurality opinion).

Nor does Atkins’ reliance on diminished culpability justify a constitutional rule of exemption. The Court has recognized that diminished culpability may matter in capital sentencing, but it has not treated it as constitutionally dispositive. *Thompson v. Oklahoma*, 487 U.S. 815, 835 (1988) (plurality opinion), forbade execution of offenders under 16 because of their youth and lesser culpability; *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 380, declined to extend that rule to older juveniles. Likewise, the Court has never held that reduced intellectual functioning alone removes a murderer from the class of death-eligible offenders. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 340.

The decision below correctly applied *Penry*. The Virginia Supreme Court recognized that Atkins had presented evidence of mental disability, but it also found that the sentencer could consider that evidence and still conclude that the aggravating circumstances justified death. That is precisely what the Eighth Amendment permits under *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 197 (1976), and *Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862, 879 (1983). So long as the sentencing process allows the jury to consider mitigating evidence, the Constitution leaves the ultimate moral judgment to the State. *California v. Brown*, 479 U.S. 538, 545 (1987).

Finally, stare decisis counsels restraint. *Penry* is not an outlier to be casually discarded; it is the governing decision on the precise question presented. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that it is “especially important” to adhere to precedent in constitutional cases, where legislative correction is unavailable. *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991). Atkins offers policy arguments, but policy belongs first to the States. The Constitution, as interpreted in *Penry*, allows the death penalty for mentally retarded offenders when a jury, after considering mitigation, finds death appropriate.

The judgment should be affirmed.
