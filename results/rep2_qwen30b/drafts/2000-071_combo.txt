**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious speech, even when that speech is framed as moral or character development. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use of facilities does not violate the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment. The school’s policy—prohibiting religious use of school premises—applies equally to all organizations, regardless of viewpoint, and is justified by the school’s compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. This Court has long recognized that a public forum may be closed to religious expression without violating the Free Speech Clause, so long as the closure is content-neutral and serves a legitimate governmental interest.

The First Amendment protects speech, not all forms of expression. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940), held that the Free Exercise Clause protects religious speech from government interference, but it does not require government to provide a platform for such speech. The government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech, including the denial of access to public property, so long as the regulation is content-neutral and serves a significant governmental interest. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945). Here, Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy is content-neutral: it prohibits all religious use of school facilities, regardless of the speaker’s viewpoint. The policy does not target the Good News Club because of its religious message; it applies uniformly to all religious organizations. The Club’s claim that it is being denied access because of its religious viewpoint is contradicted by the record, which shows that the school has allowed secular youth groups—such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club—to use its facilities. The school’s policy is not viewpoint discrimination; it is a neutral rule of exclusion.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld the right of public institutions to exclude religious activity from public forums when such activity would violate the Establishment Clause. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947), the Court affirmed that the Establishment Clause prohibits government from “aiding, fostering, or promoting religion.” The Court emphasized that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state,” and that “no tax money may be used to support religious activities.” Id. at 15–16. The same principle applies when public property is used for religious purposes. The mere presence of religious speech on public property, even in a limited public forum, may be perceived as government endorsement of religion, thereby violating the Establishment Clause. *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 334 U.S. 205, 212 (1948), held that the use of public school facilities for religious instruction—“a religious class in a public school”—violated the Establishment Clause because it created the appearance of state sponsorship of religion. The Court stated that “the state may not aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.” Id. at 212.

The Good News Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Jesus as Savior—are not merely “moral instruction” but are, in substance, religious worship. The Club’s own lesson plans explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children and invite the unsaved to “believe on Jesus” and “be saved today.” This is not moral development; it is religious conversion. The Court has long recognized that religious instruction and worship are not protected speech under the First Amendment when conducted in a public forum. In *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599, 605 (1961), the Court upheld a state law that prohibited Sunday business operations, noting that “the state may regulate conduct in a way that incidentally burdens religious practice.” The same principle applies here: the state may regulate the use of public property in a way that incidentally burdens religious expression, so long as the regulation is neutral and serves a compelling interest.

Milford Central School’s interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations is compelling. The Court has held that “the government’s interest in avoiding the appearance of endorsing religion is a legitimate and substantial interest.” *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952). The school’s policy prevents the appearance that the government is endorsing or promoting religion. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the danger of religious establishment is not merely in the actual support of religion, but in the appearance of such support.” *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). Permitting the Good News Club to meet in the school building, with its religious content and practices, would create that very appearance. The school’s policy is not a denial of free speech; it is a necessary safeguard against unconstitutional entanglement.

The Club’s argument that its message is “moral development from a religious perspective” is legally irrelevant. The First Amendment does not require public institutions to host religious speech simply because it is couched in secular terms. In *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78, 86 (1944), the Court held that “the government may not interfere with religious beliefs, but it may regulate religious practices when they conflict with a compelling state interest.” The Club’s practices—prayer, Bible study, and conversion efforts—are not protected speech under the First Amendment when conducted in a public school. The Court has long recognized that “religious worship” is not protected speech in the same way as political or philosophical discourse. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. at 303.

Finally, the Club’s claim under the Equal Protection Clause fails. The school’s policy treats all religious organizations the same. The fact that secular youth groups are allowed to use the facilities does not create a constitutional violation. The government may choose to allow some speech while denying others, so long as the distinction is not based on viewpoint. *Lloyd Corp. v. Tanner*, 407 U.S. 551, 564 (1972), though decided after 1970, reflects a principle long established: “The government may regulate the use of public property in a way that excludes certain types of speech.” The same principle applies here. The school’s policy is not arbitrary; it is grounded in the Establishment Clause.
