**ARGUMENT**

**THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE THE ACTUAL DAMAGES NECESSARY TO SUSTAIN AN AWARD UNDER SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT.**

A violation of section 2(a) of the Robinson-Patman Act, 15 U.S.C. § 13(a), does not, by itself, entitle a private plaintiff to treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a). The two inquiries are analytically distinct. The first — whether a defendant’s pricing practice created a “reasonable possibility” of competitive injury — is the standard the Federal Trade Commission applies in an administrative proceeding. *See FTC v. Morton Salt Co.*, 334 U.S. 37, 49 (1948) (holding that a showing of a “reasonable possibility” that a price differential “may” harm competition suffices to establish a Commission violation). But a private plaintiff seeking money damages under section 4 must do far more than establish that possibility; it must prove that the discrimination in fact caused it *actual* injury and must offer evidence from which the amount of that injury can be determined. *Perkins v. Standard Oil Co. of Ky.*, 395 U.S. 642, 648 (1969) (a Robinson-Patman plaintiff must “make some showing of actual injury attributable to” the price discrimination before treble damages may be awarded). This Court applied the identical principle in *Byram Concretanks, Inc. v. Warren Concrete Products Co. of N.J.*, 374 F.2d 649, 651-52 (3d Cir. 1967), holding that proof of a price differential, without evidence tying that differential to the plaintiff’s own lost sales or profits, cannot support a private damages verdict.

Stelwagon’s proof fell squarely on the wrong side of that line. Stelwagon showed, at most, that Tarmac sold MAPs to Standard and Celotex at lower prices than it charged Stelwagon. It offered no competent evidence establishing that this differential — as opposed to Stelwagon’s own pricing decisions, its narrower customer base, general market conditions, or the loss of other product lines it voluntarily abandoned to promote Tarmac’s products — caused any particular quantum of lost sales or profits. Under *Perkins* and *Byram Concretanks*, that failure of proof is fatal to the antitrust damages award, whatever the merits of the underlying liability finding.

The deficiency here is not merely one of degree; it goes to the very existence of a causal link between the challenged price differential and Stelwagon’s asserted loss. It is well settled that “the rule which precludes the recovery of uncertain damages applies to such as are not the certain result of the wrong, not to those damages which are definitely attributable to the wrong and only uncertain in respect of their amount.” *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931). Uncertainty as to the *amount* of damage may be tolerated once causation is established; uncertainty as to whether the wrong caused *any* damage at all may not be. *Id.* Stelwagon’s expert testimony addressed only the second, permissible form of uncertainty — the calculation of a dollar figure — while leaving unaddressed the antecedent question whether the discriminatory pricing, rather than other market forces, was the cause of Stelwagon’s claimed losses. That is precisely the gap that *Perkins* and *Byram Concretanks* forbid a jury to fill by inference alone.

Nor can Stelwagon rely on the settled principle that antitrust damages need not be proven with mathematical exactness. It is true that “the wrongdoer is not entitled to complain that [damages] cannot be measured with the exactness and precision that would be possible if the case, which he alone is responsible for, were otherwise,” and that an injured party “may resort to probable and inferential, as well as direct and positive, proof.” *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927). And this Court’s sister courts have long recognized that “[t]he most elementary conceptions of justice and public policy require that the wrongdoer shall bear the risk of the uncertainty which his own wrong has created.” *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 265 (1946). But those principles presuppose that the plaintiff has first shown that the defendant’s wrong was in fact a cause of some loss; they relax only the requirement of precision in quantifying an already-established injury. *See Enterprise Indus., Inc. v. Texas Co.*, 240 F.2d 457, 458 (2d Cir. 1957) (private Robinson-Patman plaintiff must still show, with reasonable certainty, that the discrimination caused the claimed loss). Stelwagon offered no evidence isolating the effect of Tarmac’s discriminatory pricing from the myriad other factors bearing on its sales — a deficiency of causation, not merely of arithmetic.

Because Stelwagon failed to carry its burden of proving that Tarmac’s price discrimination actually caused it injury, the district court should have granted Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the Robinson-Patman claim. This Court should vacate the judgment insofar as it awards treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act.
