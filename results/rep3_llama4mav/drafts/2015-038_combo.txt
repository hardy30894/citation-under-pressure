The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) grants the National Park Service (NPS) authority to regulate activities within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including those on non-federal lands. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) supports this interpretation, and historical case law reinforces the federal government's authority to regulate navigable waters within CSUs.

The Court has long recognized the federal government's authority to regulate navigable waters. In United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co., 174 U.S. 690 (1899), the Court held that the federal government has the power to regulate navigable waters, even if they are located within a state's boundaries. The Court stated that "the power to regulate commerce among the several States is granted to Congress in terms as absolute as is the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations." Id. at 702-03 (citing Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1 (1824)). This authority is not limited to navigation, but extends to other activities that may impact the waters, such as the operation of hovercraft.

In United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377 (1940), the Court further clarified the scope of the federal government's authority over navigable waters. The Court held that the federal government's power to regulate navigable waters is not limited to the protection of navigation, but extends to the protection of the waters themselves and the lands beneath them. Id. at 426-27. This authority is grounded in the Commerce Clause, which grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the several states. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3.

The Court has also recognized that the federal government has a dominant navigational servitude over navigable waters. In United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222 (1956), the Court held that the federal government's navigational servitude gives it the authority to regulate the use of navigable waters, even if that use is not directly related to navigation. Id. at 224-25 (citing Gibson v. United States, 166 U.S. 451 (1897) and Scranton v. Wheeler, 179 U.S. 141 (1900)). This servitude is not limited to navigation, but extends to other activities that may impact the waters.

In addition to its authority over navigable waters, the federal government has the power to regulate activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897), the Court held that the federal government has the authority to regulate activities on private lands within a national forest, even if those activities are not directly related to the management of the forest. Id. at 525-26. This authority is grounded in the Property Clause, which grants Congress the power to regulate federal property. U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2.

United States, 220 U.S. 523 (1911), the Court held that the federal government has the authority to regulate grazing on public lands, even if those lands are not directly managed by the federal government. Id. at 536-37. This authority is grounded in the federal government's power to regulate its own property and to protect its resources.

Weller, 244 F.2d 147 (9th Cir. 1957), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held that the federal government has the authority to regulate activities within national forests, including those on non-federal lands. Id. at 149-50 (citing Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897)). This authority is not limited to national forests, but extends to other CSUs, including national parks.

In Gratiot, the Court held that the federal government has the authority to lease mineral lands and to regulate the activities of lessees. Id. at 537-38. This authority is grounded in the Property Clause, which grants Congress the power to regulate federal property.

In Gibson, the Court held that the federal government has the authority to regulate the construction of structures in navigable waters, even if those structures are located on private property. Id. at 455-56. This authority is grounded in the Commerce Clause and the federal government's navigational servitude.

In conclusion, the plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) and historical case law support the NPS's authority to regulate activities within CSUs, including those on non-federal lands. The federal government's authority to regulate navigable waters and to protect its resources within CSUs is well-established, and the NPS's regulation of hovercraft within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is a valid exercise of that authority.
