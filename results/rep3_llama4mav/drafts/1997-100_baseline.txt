The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969)), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its decisions in Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), provide guidance.

In Bullington, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they share "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" (Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439). The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding resembled a trial, with a separate hearing, a jury, and a burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt (id.). Similarly, in Rumsey, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a capital sentencing proceeding in Arizona, where the sentencing judge made a factual determination regarding the defendant's eligibility for the death penalty (Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209-210).

The same principles that led the Court to apply the Double Jeopardy Clause in capital sentencing proceedings should apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings, such as the one in this case. California's "three-strikes" law requires a trial-like proceeding to determine the truth of a prior conviction allegation, with the prosecution bearing the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt (People v. Tenner, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993)). The trial court's determination of the prior conviction allegation is a factual finding that is subject to review on appeal, and the prosecution's failure to prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt renders the finding an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes.

The California Supreme Court's attempt to distinguish Bullington and Rumsey is unpersuasive. The Court's suggestion that the Supreme Court has "suggested" that Bullington does not apply to noncapital cases is unfounded. The Supreme Court's decision in Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), merely noted that Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases, but did not hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause is limited to capital cases.

Furthermore, the procedural protections afforded to defendants in noncapital sentencing proceedings, such as the right to a jury trial and the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, are similar to those in capital cases. The California Legislature has elected to grant defendants a statutory right to a jury trial of prior conviction allegations (Cal. Penal Code § 1025), and the courts have expanded this right to include various procedural guarantees.

In conclusion, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The trial-like nature of the sentencing proceeding, the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the factual determination required to establish the prior conviction allegation all support the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The California Supreme Court's decision to the contrary should be reversed.
