**Argument**

Title VII punitive damages are not reserved for only the most “egregious” or “outrageous” acts in the abstract; they turn on the employer’s state of mind. The statutory text controls. Congress authorized punitive damages when the defendant acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language does not add a separate threshold requiring independently outrageous conduct. The proper question is whether the employer knew it might be violating federal law, or acted in conscious disregard of that risk.

This Court’s pre-1970 precedents consistently treat punitive damages as a remedy keyed to culpability, not to a freestanding requirement of especially shocking conduct. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court later confirmed the common-law baseline: punitive damages are available when the defendant acts with reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights. But long before that, the Court’s punitive-damages cases focused on maliciousness, wantonness, or reckless disregard. See *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852); *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893); *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886); *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818); *Hays v. Pacific Mail S.S. Co.*, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 596 (1855); *Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co. v. Kelly*, 241 U.S. 485 (1916). Those decisions do not impose an additional requirement that the conduct be independently “egregious” apart from the requisite mental state.

The Seventh Amendment also supports this reading. Punitive damages historically depended on the trier of fact’s assessment of blameworthiness, not on a rigid judicial gatekeeping finding that conduct crossed some outer boundary of outrage. See *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852); *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886). Where Congress borrows a familiar common-law remedy, courts should not superimpose a new and more demanding element unless Congress clearly says so. *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), confirms that punitive damages may be appropriate when conduct is undertaken with conscious wrongdoing; it does not say that only extreme conduct qualifies.

Respondent’s position also accords with the structure of Title VII. Liability under the statute already requires intentional discrimination. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), and the earlier cases that shaped employment-discrimination proof, show that the core inquiry is discriminatory intent. Congress then added compensatory and punitive damages for cases involving heightened culpability. But “heightened culpability” is a state-of-mind standard, not a separate demand for proof of barbaric conduct. See *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818); *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852).

The Court should reject petitioner’s effort to convert “malice or reckless indifference” into an amorphous “egregiousness” requirement. The law has long distinguished between the character of the act and the actor’s state of mind. A defendant may engage in conduct that is not facially spectacular yet still act with malice or reckless indifference. *Hays v. Pacific Mail S.S. Co.*, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 596 (1855), and *Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co. v. Kelly*, 241 U.S. 485 (1916), both reflect that punitive awards respond to the wrongdoer’s culpability, not to a categorical label attached to the conduct itself.

Nor does due process require an added “egregiousness” element. The pre-1970 cases recognized that punitive damages must not be arbitrary, but that limitation is satisfied by tying the award to malice or reckless indifference, a standard already cabined by the statute. See *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886); *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893). Nothing in those authorities suggests that courts may deny punitive-damages instructions whenever the underlying discrimination can be described as merely “ordinary” rather than “outrageous.”

Accordingly, the district court erred in refusing to submit punitive damages to the jury once the jury could find intentional discrimination and the requisite culpable state of mind. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial on punitive damages.
