# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1231(a)(6) Must Be Construed to Avoid the Serious Constitutional Question That Indefinite Civil Detention Would Present

When a statute is susceptible of a construction that avoids serious constitutional doubt, courts must adopt that construction. As Justice Brandeis explained in his controlling formulation of the avoidance canon, a court will first ascertain "whether a construction of the statute is fairly possible by which the [constitutional] question may be avoided." *Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288, 348 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring). This Court applied that very canon to an immigration statute just this Term, holding that "if an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress." *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 299–300 (2001) (internal quotation marks omitted). The same rule governs here. Section 1231(a)(6) nowhere states that detention is indefinite or permanent; it says only that a removable alien "may be detained beyond the removal period." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). That silence permits, and the Constitution requires, a reading limiting detention to a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal.

## II. Indefinite Civil Detention of a Person Within the United States Violates the Due Process Clause

It has long been settled that the Due Process Clause applies to all "persons" within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence is lawful or not. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896) (holding that aliens "are entitled to be represented by counsel, to be secure from unreasonable searches and seizures, and to be protected against oppressive and arbitrary treatment"). This protection extends with special force to resident aliens like petitioner, whom this Court has recognized as "person[s] within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment" entitled to due process protection against arbitrary deprivations of liberty. *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (a lawful permanent resident alien "may not be deprived of his life, liberty or property without due process of law"). Petitioner, having been admitted to the United States decades ago and having resided here continuously, falls squarely within this protected class.

Freedom from physical restraint has always been "at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause." *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). In *Foucha*, this Court struck down a scheme of indefinite civil confinement, holding that even a person once found dangerous could not be detained indefinitely once the factual predicate justifying detention no longer held. *Id.* at 82–83. The same logic controls the class of detention at issue here: once the possibility of removal evaporates, the sole statutory justification for detention—securing the alien's presence for removal—likewise evaporates. Civil confinement untethered from any achievable governmental purpose is precisely what due process forbids.

This Court's decision in *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715 (1972), confirms the point. There the Court held that "due process requires that the nature and duration of commitment bear some reasonable relation to the purpose for which the individual is committed." *Id.* at 738. Because the petitioner in *Jackson* had been committed to determine his competency to stand trial, yet had no substantial probability of attaining that competency, his continued confinement violated due process. *Id.* at 738–39. So too here: because petitioner's detention is predicated on the government's ability to effect his removal, and because no country will accept him, his confinement bears no "reasonable relation" to any achievable purpose.

By contrast, this Court has upheld indefinite civil commitment only where narrow, well-defined conditions are satisfied—proof of dangerousness combined with a mental abnormality, coupled with strict procedural safeguards and periodic review. *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 358, 364 (1997). Likewise, pretrial detention under the Bail Reform Act was sustained in *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), only because Congress had "carefully limited the circumstances under which detention may be sought" and imposed "stringent time limitations." *Id.* at 747. Section 1231(a)(6), construed to authorize detention without temporal limit whenever removal is merely difficult rather than truly impossible, contains none of the safeguards this Court has demanded as the price of permitting indefinite civil confinement.

## III. Shaughnessy v. Mezei Does Not Sanction Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Ordered Removed

The court below relied on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), but that reliance was misplaced. Mezei was an excludable alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States and therefore was "treated as if stopped at the border." *Id.* at 215. Petitioner, by contrast, is a lawful permanent resident who was admitted decades ago. As *Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982), makes clear, "once an alien gains admission to our country and begins to develop the ties that go with permanent residence, his constitutional status changes accordingly." Mezei's rule for aliens at the threshold cannot be extended to strip a long-resident alien of due process protection against indefinite confinement.

## Conclusion

For these reasons, § 1231(a)(6) should be construed to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and petitioner's continued confinement, with no realistic prospect of removal, should be held unauthorized and unconstitutional.
