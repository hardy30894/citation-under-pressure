# ARGUMENT

## I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Procedure Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment's Jury Trial Guarantee

The Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee protects the accused's right to have a jury resolve the question of guilt or innocence of the offense charged. See *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930). This Court has never held that the guarantee extends to every factual determination that bears on the severity of punishment once guilt has been lawfully established. To the contrary, in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), this Court upheld a death sentence imposed by a trial judge notwithstanding a jury's recommendation of leniency, holding that a sentencing court may rely on information never presented to, or found by, the jury. The Court explained that treating sentencing procedure as though it must mirror trial procedure would improperly "fetter the administration of the criminal law." *Id.* at 246. Williams thus confirms that the identity of the sentencer—judge or jury—and the procedures by which aggravating information is weighed, are matters of legislative judgment, not constitutional command.

That principle was reaffirmed in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where this Court sustained a Texas recidivist procedure permitting the sentencer to consider prior-conviction evidence bearing on punishment without the formal safeguards attending proof of guilt, recognizing the States' broad "constitutional responsibility for the administration of their criminal justice systems." *Id.* at 568-69. If a State may allow a judge, rather than a jury, to find the facts of recidivism that expose a defendant to enhanced punishment, it follows a fortiori that Florida may likewise commit the ultimate weighing of aggravation and mitigation to a process combining jury recommendation and judicial fact-finding.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment demand unanimity in a jury's advisory role. This Court's decision in *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), addressed unanimity as a matter of the federal Kidnapping Act's own text, not as a freestanding constitutional imperative applicable to the States. And in *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968), the Court grounded the jury right in the Nation's "deep commitment... to the right of jury trial in serious criminal cases as a defense against arbitrary law enforcement," *id.* at 156—a concern satisfied here, where a jury of Hurst's peers heard the evidence, weighed mitigation, and recommended a sentence that the court could not impose without that recommendation having been rendered. Florida's scheme, which retains the jury's traditional role while assigning the final sentencing determination to the judge, falls squarely within the latitude *Williams* and *Spencer* afford the States.

## II. Florida's Sentencing Scheme Does Not Offend the Eighth Amendment

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). That standard has historically been applied to the *method* and *proportionality* of punishment, see *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910) (disproportion between offense and punishment), and to the character of the punishment itself, see *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (Eighth Amendment concerned with "cruel and unusual" methods of execution)—not to the internal architecture of a State's sentencing procedure. Florida's practice of using an advisory jury recommendation followed by independent judicial weighing of aggravating and mitigating circumstances does not inflict a punishment forbidden by history or by the "evolving standards" this Court has recognized; it is simply a different allocation of sentencing responsibility than other capital jurisdictions employ.

This Court's own capital jurisprudence confirms the point. In *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510 (1968), the Court closely scrutinized the composition of capital sentencing juries yet never suggested that the Constitution requires unanimous jury fact-finding on aggravating circumstances, or that a jury's role must be anything more than advisory. Had such a requirement inhered in the Eighth Amendment, the Court would have said so.

As for the treatment of mental retardation evidence, this Court has long recognized that States retain wide latitude to allocate burdens of proof on issues bearing on culpability. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that due process does not compel the States to adopt any particular procedural rule for such determinations. Florida's requirement that a defendant establish mental retardation as a bar to execution—while still permitting the same evidence to be weighed by the jury as mitigation—reflects precisely the sort of procedural choice *Leland* leaves to the States. Nothing in this Court's decisions transforms that legislative choice into a violation of "those fundamental principles of liberty and justice which lie at the base of all our civil and political institutions." *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 106 (1908).

For these reasons, Florida's capital sentencing scheme comports with both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
