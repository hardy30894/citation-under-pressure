**Argument**

The district court abused its discretion in denying Winbush’s timely request for a fingerprint expert and his motion to continue. A criminal defendant is entitled to present a meaningful defense, and when the prosecution relies on forensic and expert testimony, due process requires that the accused be given a fair opportunity to meet that proof. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932); *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444 (1940); *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973) (later authority, not relied on for governing rule). Here, the government itself noticed expert testimony concerning fingerprint analysis and narcotics-trafficking practices. Winbush then sought a defense fingerprint expert to test and explain the evidentiary significance of the government’s physical evidence. Denying that request on the eve of trial, after a lengthy case and despite the centrality of the forensic proof, deprived Winbush of a fair opportunity to defend. See *Hicks v. Oklahoma*, 447 U.S. 343 (1980) (later authority, not relied on for governing rule); *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932).

The Supreme Court has long held that an accused must have the assistance necessary to make the right to counsel meaningful. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932); *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444 (1940). That principle is not satisfied by nominal representation if counsel lacks the time and tools to investigate the prosecution’s scientific proof. In *Avery*, the Court recognized that the denial of a continuance can be so prejudicial as to amount to a denial of effective assistance where preparation time is unreasonably curtailed. 308 U.S. at 446–47. And in *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575 (1964), the Court explained that whether denial of a continuance violates due process depends on the circumstances, including the reasons for the request and the prejudice from denial. Id. at 589.

Those circumstances favor Winbush. The government’s proof linked him to several separate bags of narcotics, a firearm, and cash containing marked bills. The physical evidence was pivotal. Yet the defense sought only a fingerprint expert to examine the items and explain whether the absence of fingerprints had any evidentiary significance. The request was not a fishing expedition; it was directly tied to the government’s proof and to anticipated expert testimony. Refusing the expert and then refusing a continuance left the defense unable to test an important aspect of the prosecution’s case. That is precisely the sort of arbitrary curtailment of a defense that due process forbids. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932); *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444 (1940); *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575 (1964).

The denial was especially prejudicial because the government’s own notice showed that it expected to present expert evidence on fingerprints and narcotics trafficking. When the prosecution opens the door to expert interpretation of physical evidence, fundamental fairness requires that the defense have a fair chance to meet that evidence with its own expert assistance. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Constitution requires “a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s accusations.” *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). And the right to compulsory process is not formalistic; it protects the practical ability to present witnesses and evidence material to the defense. Id. at 19–23; *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257 (1948).

The government may argue that the request came too late. But lateness alone is not enough; the question is whether the denial was arbitrary and whether it prejudiced the defense. *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575 (1964). Here, the district court gave dispositive weight to timing, calling the request “woefully late,” without accommodating the defense’s need to confront expert evidence that the government had already disclosed. The court’s failure to grant even a short continuance was an abuse of discretion because it transformed a scheduling issue into a denial of a meaningful defense. *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444 (1940); *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575 (1964).

Winbush’s trial also implicated the principle that criminal proceedings must not be conducted in a manner that undermines the accuracy of the verdict. The Supreme Court has made clear that due process protects against procedures that unreasonably handicap the accused in meeting the State’s case. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932); *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973) (later authority, not relied on for governing rule). Because the physical evidence and forensic interpretation were central, the denial of expert assistance and a continuance undermined confidence in the reliability of the verdict.

Accordingly, the Court should reverse the judgment and remand for a new trial.
