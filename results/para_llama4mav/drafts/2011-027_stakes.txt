The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable for title purposes at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889. The test for determining navigability for title purposes is a factual inquiry that considers whether a river was used or susceptible of being used as a highway for commerce at the time of statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931). The court below properly applied this test, relying on historical evidence and expert testimony to conclude that the rivers in question were navigable in 1889.

PPL's argument that the trial court erred in its application of the navigability test is unfounded. The Supreme Court has long held that the navigability of a river for title purposes is determined at the time of statehood. See, e.g., United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) ("The test of navigability... is the capacity of a river in its natural state to be used for commerce."). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this principle, examining the historical evidence presented to determine whether the rivers were navigable in 1889.

The trial court's consideration of historical evidence, including reports and documents from the late 19th century, was proper. Such evidence is admissible under various exceptions to the hearsay rule, including the public records and reports exception. M. R. Evid. 803(8); see also Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc., 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79-81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948. The State Archivist's affidavit, which authenticated the historical documents, provided a sufficient foundation for their admission.

PPL's claim that the trial court erred in relying on hearsay evidence is unpersuasive. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the historical evidence presented was admissible under various rules of evidence, including M. R. Evid. 902, which provides for the self-authentication of certain documents. The court's decision to consider this evidence on summary judgment was proper, as it was based on a thorough examination of the record and applicable law.

In conclusion, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the test for determining navigability for title purposes, and its decision should be affirmed. The trial court's consideration of historical evidence was proper, and PPL's claims of error are without merit. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be upheld.
