**Argument**

**I. The district court erred by supplying a judicial definition of “shielding” that expanded the statute beyond its text and exceeded the jury’s proper role.**

A criminal conviction must rest on the statute Congress enacted, not on a judicial gloss that broadens liability after the fact. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). When Congress uses a term in a penal statute, courts are to construe it according to its ordinary meaning and “resolve ambiguities in favor of lenity.” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820); *United States v. Palmer*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 610, 631 (1818). The district court’s instruction on “shielding” departed from that principle by giving the jury an expansive, nonstatutory definition untethered to the words Congress chose.

The problem is especially acute where the challenged instruction supplied an element-like definition that may have carried the weight of law. Juries decide facts, but the court must not, under the guise of explanation, add meaning that the statute itself does not contain. *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 105-06 (1895). A supplemental instruction may clarify, but it may not distort the statutory standard or invite conviction on a broader theory than the indictment and statute authorize. *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 613-14 (1946).

Here, Ye was charged under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), which penalizes one who “conceals, harbors, or shields from detection” an alien. The terms are distinct. Congress’s use of separate verbs requires each to carry independent meaning; courts may not collapse them into an amorphous “assistance” concept. *Gustafson v. Alloyd Co.*, 513 U.S. 561, 570 (1995) (statutory terms should not be treated as surplusage), but see *A. Leopold & Co. v. United States*, 280 U.S. 123, 127-28 (1929) (courts must give effect to the words actually used). The district court’s instruction effectively converted “shielding” into any conduct that made illegal employees harder to find, even if the conduct was equally consistent with ordinary employment, housing, or payroll practices. That breadth is incompatible with criminal notice. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221-22 (1876).

The record illustrates why the instruction mattered. The jury heard evidence that Ye hired workers, provided housing, paid cash wages, and failed to maintain some forms. But the statute punishes only “shielding from detection,” not mere employment of an undocumented worker. See *United States v. Hudson*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 34 (1812) (courts cannot create crimes by construction). By defining “shielding” in a way that risked equating it with concealment of employment records or routine accommodation of workers, the court invited the jury to convict on conduct Congress separately addressed in the misdemeanor hiring provision. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820).

The instruction also impermissibly diluted the government’s burden on intent. A criminal statute requiring purposeful conduct cannot be satisfied by proof of negligence, carelessness, or general awareness that workers are undocumented. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952); *Carter v. United States*, 530 U.S. 255, 269 (2000) (discussing mens rea as a central feature of criminal liability). Ye was entitled to have the jury decide whether he specifically intended to prevent detection, not whether he engaged in conduct the court deemed generally “shielding.” *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614-15 (1946).

Even if the district court sought to clarify a term that may be unfamiliar, it still had to avoid a definition so broad or vague that the jury could convict on a standard lacking fair warning. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). The rule of lenity applies with special force where a criminal statute is susceptible to more than one reasonable reading and the government urges the broader one. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820); *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 485 (1917). The court’s instruction violated that rule.

**II. The evidence was insufficient to prove Ye specifically intended to prevent government detection of his employees.**

A conviction cannot stand unless the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the government, permits a rational jury to find each element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Cohen v. United States*, 378 F.2d 751, 761 (9th Cir. 1967); *Curley v. United States*, 160 F.2d 229, 232-33 (D.C. Cir. 1947). The evidence here showed, at most, that Ye knowingly employed undocumented workers and failed to maintain proper records. But those facts do not necessarily establish the distinct intent to “shield” them from detection. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952).

To infer the specific intent element, the government needed proof that Ye acted to thwart detection by authorities, not merely to staff his restaurant cheaply or avoid paperwork. The evidence of cash wages, housing, and transportation may suggest accommodation; it does not compel the conclusion that he sought to conceal the workers from immigration officials. See *Hopt v. Utah*, 120 U.S. 430, 439 (1887) (criminal proof must establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt). And Ye’s testimony that he knew the workers were undocumented and helped with hiring does not itself establish the additional objective of detection avoidance. *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703, 711 (1943) (knowledge and participation are not automatically equivalent to the requisite criminal intent).

At bottom, the government proved an improper employment scheme; it did not prove beyond a reasonable doubt the separate and more demanding offense of shielding aliens from detection. Because the instruction blurred that line, and the evidence did not independently supply it, Ye’s conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) should be reversed.
