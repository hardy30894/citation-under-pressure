**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not prescribe a specific standard for employer polling of union support. The Board’s current rule—requiring the same quantum of objective evidence to poll as to withdraw recognition—defeats the very purpose of polling and conflicts with the Act’s design.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987). An employer who doubts that support may either withdraw recognition, seek a Board-conducted RM election, or poll employees. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). Polling serves a distinct function: it allows an employer to test its doubt without prematurely risking an unfair labor practice. *See Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). Yet the Board requires an employer to possess sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal *before* it may poll—effectively demanding certainty before permitting inquiry. That makes polling a “useless” tool. *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984).

Three circuits have correctly rejected this circular standard. They hold that an employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299. This approach respects the Act’s balance: it permits employers to resolve genuine doubts while preserving the union’s presumption until the poll confirms its loss. The Board’s contrary rule forces employers into an all-or-nothing choice—withdraw recognition or do nothing—discouraging the peaceful, non-coercive testing of union sentiment that the Act should encourage.

The Board’s rationale—that polling is “potentially disruptive” and that RM elections are preferred—does not justify its standard. If RM elections are preferred, the standard for polling should be *more* stringent, not identical. *See* Joan Flynn, *A Triple Standard at the NLRB*, 1991 Wis. L. Rev. 653, 660. The Board’s rule creates the anomaly that an employer can withdraw recognition (with no safeguards) on the same evidence required to conduct a supervised poll. That is irrational. The Board’s own logic should have led it to a higher standard for withdrawal, not a higher standard for polling. Its failure to do so renders the polling standard arbitrary and inconsistent with the Act’s goal of stable collective bargaining.

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Lacked Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

Even under the Board’s flawed standard, its conclusion that Allentown lacked objective evidence of lost union support is unsupported by substantial evidence. The record shows overwhelming, unrebutted indications that the union no longer commanded majority support.

Of the 32 employees in the bargaining unit, at least 7 made direct statements repudiating the union. The Board improperly discounted additional evidence. Employee statements during job interviews—such as Mike Ridgick’s remark that “there was no need for a union” and Dennis Marsh’s complaint about dues—were dismissed as insincere or equivocal. But the Board’s own precedent holds that such statements may be considered when the employer has not created a coercive atmosphere. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978). Here, there was no finding of coercion. The Board also rejected Kermit Bloch’s report that the entire night shift (5–6 employees) opposed the union, and union steward Ron Mohr’s statement that the union would lose a vote. The Board’s skepticism of hearsay and “off-the-cuff” remarks is not a valid basis to ignore this evidence when no contrary testimony was offered. *See Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board where “overwhelming unrefuted evidence” showed loss of support).

The Board’s own count of only 7 anti-union employees ignores that the unit was drawn from a larger predecessor unit. The presumption of majority support is weaker when the unit composition has changed. *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 41 n.8. Here, 13 of the original 45 employees were not hired, and the union’s prior majority could have evaporated simply through attrition. *See* dissenting opinion below, slip op. at 10 n.3. The Board offered no evidence that the union actually retained majority support. Its finding that Allentown lacked reasonable doubt is thus arbitrary and capricious.

The poll itself—conducted by secret ballot under a priest’s supervision—yielded a 19-to-13 vote against the union. That result, while not retroactively justifying the poll, confirms that the employer’s doubt was objectively reasonable. The Board’s contrary conclusion cannot stand.
