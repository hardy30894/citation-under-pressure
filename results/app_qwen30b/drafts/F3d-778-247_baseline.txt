**ARGUMENT**

I. THE SEARCH WARRANT FAILED TO ESTABLISH A SUFFICIENT NEXUS BETWEEN THE ALLEGED CRIMES AND THE PLACE TO BE SEARCHED, VIOLATING THE FOURTH AMENDMENT.

The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. This requirement ensures that warrants are not issued based on mere suspicion or speculation, but rather on probable cause supported by a showing of a nexus between the criminal activity and the location to be searched. *United States v. Ventresca*, 380 U.S. 102, 108 (1965). Here, the warrant authorizing the search of Joubert’s parents’ home was constitutionally defective because it failed to establish a sufficient factual basis for believing that evidence of the charged crimes would be found at that specific location.

The affidavit supporting the warrant relied almost entirely on hearsay allegations from multiple complainants, including SJ, Joubert’s son, who reported abuse in the mid-1980s—over two decades prior to the search. While these allegations may have provided some basis for investigating Joubert’s past conduct, they did not establish that he had recently possessed or stored child pornography at his parents’ home. The only direct link between the residence and the alleged crimes came from SJ’s statement that Joubert moved into the home in late May or early June 2012—just weeks before the search—and that Joubert expressed concern about destroying his computer components. Yet even this information is insufficient to satisfy the nexus requirement.

The affidavit states that Joubert “tore apart the computer tower,” removed components, and stacked them inside his parents’ home. But the affiant never observed Joubert discard any components, nor did he confirm that any digital media remained in the house. The inference that evidence of child exploitation would be located there rests solely on speculation: that Joubert might have hidden materials after discarding the hard drive. That speculative leap does not meet the standard set forth in *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 114 (1964), which requires that an affidavit contain “some indication of the reliability of the informant” and “a factual basis for the conclusion that the evidence sought will be found in the place to be searched.”

Moreover, the affidavit fails to identify any contemporaneous connection between Joubert’s presence at the home and the possession of child pornography. There is no mention of Joubert using computers or storage devices at the residence, no evidence of internet activity tied to the home, and no testimony linking the seized VHS tape to the premises. The sole justification for the search—the belief that Joubert “often maintain[ed] possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims”—is a generalization unsupported by facts tying such evidence to the specific location. *See United States v. Grubbs*, 547 U.S. 90, 96 (2006) (“The Fourth Amendment does not permit a warrant to issue simply because a suspect has committed a crime in the past.”).

Without a particularized showing that evidence of the current offenses was likely to be found at the parents’ home, the warrant violated the Fourth Amendment’s particularity clause. *See Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983) (“The ‘totality of the circumstances’ test must still yield a warrant supported by probable cause and with a reasonable nexus to the place to be searched.”). The district court erred in denying Joubert’s motion to suppress the fruits of the search, and the resulting evidence should have been excluded.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY OF UNCHARGED CHILD MOLESTATION, VIOLATING THE RULES OF EVIDENCE AND DUE PROCESS.

The admission of evidence of uncharged misconduct under Federal Rule of Evidence 413(a) is permissible only when the government demonstrates that the acts were “similar in nature” to the charged offense and “relevant to prove motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident.” Fed. R. Evid. 404(b). Even then, the probative value must outweigh the risk of unfair prejudice. *See United States v. Maldonado*, 742 F.3d 1, 10 (1st Cir. 2014).

Here, the government introduced testimony from multiple alleged victims—including SJ, who claimed abuse in the 1980s—about conduct that occurred decades before the charged offenses. The most significant of these was SJ’s account of being molested as a child, which bore no temporal proximity to the crimes charged. The events involving KC, the primary victim, occurred in 2002–2004, while SJ’s allegations predated those by nearly two decades. The similarity in modus operandi—gaining trust within a family, exploiting a position of authority, and photographing minors—is not enough to justify admission under Rule 404(b), especially when the time gap undermines the relevance of the evidence.

More critically, the admission of such remote conduct created a substantial risk of prejudice. It invited the jury to convict Joubert based on a generalized reputation for pedophilia rather than on proof of the specific crimes charged. *See Old Chief v. United States*, 500 U.S. 400, 410 (1991) (“The danger of unfair prejudice increases when the evidence tends to show a propensity to commit crimes of a particular nature.”). The district court failed to conduct a proper balancing under Rule 403, and its decision to admit the testimony was an abuse of discretion.

Furthermore, the use of such evidence violates due process when it risks creating a “trial by reputation.” *See United States v. Baez*, 522 F.3d 100, 107 (1st Cir. 2008). The jury was not instructed to consider the uncharged conduct only for limited purposes, increasing the risk that it would be used to infer guilt by association. The error was not harmless, particularly given the gravity of the charges and the strength of the prosecution’s case against Joubert.
