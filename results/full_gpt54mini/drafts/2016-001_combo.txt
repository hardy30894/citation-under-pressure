**Argument**

The Patent Act’s design-patent remedy does not authorize a windfall untethered to the component actually embodied in the patented design. Where the infringed design patent covers only a component of a multi-component product, damages must be confined to the profits attributable to that component, not the infringer’s entire product profits. That conclusion follows from the statute’s text, this Court’s controlling precedents, and the long-established rule that recovery must correspond to the proven contribution of the patented feature. See **Smith v. Whitman Saddle Co., 148 U.S. 674 (1893)**; **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885)**; **Gorham Co. v. White, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511 (1871)**.

First, the design-patent statute itself limits recovery to the infringer’s “total profit,” not total product revenue, and that phrase must be read in context to refer only to the profit earned from the infringing design. See Act of Feb. 4, 1887, ch. 105, § 1, 24 Stat. 387; **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444–48 (1885)**. In Dobson, this Court rejected an award that treated the defendant’s entire carpet profits as recoverable where the patented design covered only a part of the product. The Court explained that the patentee could not recover more than the profits “actually made by the use of the patented design.” **Id. at 444**. The same principle controls here.

Second, this Court has repeatedly required apportionment when the asserted right covers only part of the defendant’s product or business. In **Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912)**, the Court held that once the patentee shows the defendant’s profits from the infringing article, the defendant may show what portion is attributable to nonpatented features. **Id. at 614–18**. The Court stated that profits must be “apportioned between the patented and unpatented features,” and only if apportionment is impossible may the patentee recover the whole. **Id. at 614–15**. That rule rests on equity and on the basic principle that a plaintiff should recover only what the infringement caused. **See also Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390, 399–401 (1940)**.

Third, this Court’s design-patent cases do not support awarding all profits from an entire smartphone when the design patent covers only a component. In **Gorham Co. v. White, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511 (1871)**, the Court explained that design-patent protection exists because the design gives “peculiar and distinctive appearance” to the article. **Id. at 525–26**. The relevant inquiry is the effect of the patented design on the article embodying it, not the market value of unrelated features. Likewise, **Smith v. Whitman Saddle Co., 148 U.S. 674 (1893)** recognized that the design patent covered the saddle’s ornamental shape and that profits must correspond to the use of that design. **Id. at 681–82**.

Fourth, the Federal Circuit’s contrary “entire profits” rule cannot be squared with this Court’s pre-1970 precedent. A remedy measured by the profits of the whole device, regardless of whether the design patent covers only one component, would resurrect the very overcompensation rejected in **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885)** and cabined in **Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912)**. The law has never allowed a patentee to capture profits attributable to features it did not invent, own, or patent. **Mowry v. Whitney, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 620, 651–52 (1871)**; **Birdsall v. Coolidge, 93 U.S. 64, 68–69 (1876)**.

Respondent may argue that a patented component can influence demand for the whole product, but that point proves the need for apportionment, not its irrelevance. This Court’s profits cases require separating the value of the patented contribution from the value of other, unpatented features. **Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 617–18 (1912)**; **Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390, 399–401 (1940)**. A smartphone’s profits reflect countless noninfringing elements—processor, software, network compatibility, brand, battery, camera, and utility. A design patent covering a faceplate, icon arrangement, or bezel cannot justify taking profits attributable to all of them.

Finally, limiting damages to the component’s attributable profits is consistent with the remedial principle that the remedy must fit the wrong. **Railroad Co. v. Turrill, 107 U.S. 49, 57–58 (1883)**, and **Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646–47 (1915)** both recognize that recovery must be confined to the proven value of the infringement. That is especially important in design-patent cases, where the patented contribution is aesthetic and often only one part of a larger commercial product. **See Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444–48 (1885)**.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to limit damages to the portion of Samsung’s profits attributable to the infringed design component.
