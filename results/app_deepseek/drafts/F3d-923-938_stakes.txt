**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tax Court Erred in Holding That Mr. Meruelo Failed to Establish a Bona Fide Indebtedness Running Directly to Him Because the Economic Substance of the Transactions Demonstrated Actual Economic Outlay.**

A shareholder may deduct S corporation losses only to the extent of the shareholder’s adjusted basis in the corporation’s indebtedness to the shareholder. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). The critical inquiry is whether the shareholder made an “actual economic outlay” that created “bona fide indebtedness” running directly from the corporation to the shareholder. *Underwood v. Comm’r*, 535 F.3d 1185, 1191 (10th Cir. 2008); Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The Tax Court erroneously concluded that Mr. Meruelo failed to satisfy this standard, ignoring that the net transfers from Merco’s affiliates—entities in which Mr. Meruelo held ownership interests—were, in economic reality, advances of his own funds.

**A. The Net Transfers from Merco’s Affiliates Constituted an Actual Economic Outlay by Mr. Meruelo.**

The Tax Court’s holding that the indebtedness ran from Merco to the affiliates, not to Mr. Meruelo, elevates form over substance. This Court has long recognized that tax consequences are determined by the economic substance of a transaction, not its mere form. *See Frank Lyon Co. v. United States*, 435 U.S. 561, 573 (1978). Here, the affiliates that advanced funds to Merco were pass-through entities in which Mr. Meruelo held substantial ownership interests. When an affiliate advanced funds to Merco, it effectively advanced Mr. Meruelo’s own capital, because the economic burden of the advance fell on him as the ultimate owner. *See, e.g., Estate of Leavitt v. Comm’r*, 875 F.2d 420, 422 (4th Cir. 1989) (holding that a shareholder’s guarantee of corporate debt, followed by payment, created basis because the shareholder bore the economic risk).

The record demonstrates that Mr. Meruelo personally obtained a loan to fund the initial $5 million deposit, and that the subsequent net transfers from affiliates were part of an integrated plan to provide working capital to Merco. The affiliates’ payments were not arm’s-length loans to Merco; they were advances made to facilitate Merco’s operations, with the expectation that Mr. Meruelo would ultimately bear the cost. The Tax Court’s rigid insistence that the debt must run “directly” from Merco to Mr. Meruelo ignores that the affiliates were mere conduits for his economic outlay. *See, e.g., Selfe v. United States*, 778 F.2d 769, 773 (11th Cir. 1985) (holding that a shareholder’s guarantee of corporate debt could create basis where the shareholder was “the primary obligor in substance”).

**B. The Promissory Note and Annual Reclassification of Accounts Payable as Shareholder Loans Confirmed the Existence of Bona Fide Indebtedness.**

The Tax Court also erred in discounting the promissory note and the annual reclassification of Merco’s net accounts payable as “shareholder loans.” The note, drafted in March 2004, established a $10 million line of credit from Mr. Meruelo to Merco. Each year, Carreras, the CPA, calculated the net amount of Merco’s accounts payable to its affiliates and reclassified that amount as a loan from Mr. Meruelo under the line of credit. This reclassification reflected the economic reality that the affiliates’ advances were, in substance, loans from Mr. Meruelo to Merco.

The Tax Court’s conclusion that the note was a “sham” because it was not contemporaneously executed or consistently enforced is unsupported by the record. The note was drafted in the same month Merco was formed, and the annual reclassification was a consistent, documented practice. The fact that Merco did not make interest payments does not negate the existence of a bona fide debt, particularly where, as here, the debt was subordinated and the parties treated it as such on their tax returns. *See, e.g., Dixie Dairies Corp. v. Comm’r*, 74 T.C. 476, 493 (1980) (holding that a debt may be bona fide even if interest is not paid, where the parties intended to create a debtor-creditor relationship).

**C. The Tax Court’s “Direct Indebtedness” Requirement Is Inconsistent with the Purpose of Section 1366.**

The purpose of § 1366(d)(1) is to ensure that a shareholder deducts losses only to the extent of his economic investment in the corporation. *See, e.g., United States v. Generes*, 405 U.S. 93, 103 (1972). Mr. Meruelo’s economic investment in Merco included not only his $5 million capital contribution but also the more than $9 million in net advances that he effectively funded through his ownership of the affiliates. To deny him basis for these advances would frustrate the statute’s purpose by allowing the government to tax phantom income while ignoring the shareholder’s actual economic outlay.

The Tax Court’s decision creates a trap for unwary taxpayers who use commonly accepted business structures—such as multiple pass-through entities—to manage cash flow. This Court should reject that formalistic approach and hold that Mr. Meruelo established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him, because the economic substance of the transactions demonstrates that he made an actual economic outlay.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the decision of the Tax Court should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to allow Mr. Meruelo’s claimed deduction.
