**Argument**

Temporary Protected Status does not satisfy § 1255’s admission requirement. Congress used distinct terms, and this Court has long treated lawful entry, admission, and status as separate concepts. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A); *United States ex rel. Polymeris v. Trudell*, 284 U.S. 279, 281–82 (1932) (admission concerns lawful entry); *U.S. ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi*, 287 U.S. 129, 136 (1932) (Congress may attach different consequences to different immigration statuses). Petitioners therefore cannot convert TPS into an “admission” by relabeling lawful presence as lawful entry.

1. **Section 1255 requires a lawful entry; TPS does not create one.**  
The statute authorizes adjustment only for an alien “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). “Admission” is a term of art referring to lawful entry after inspection. *Cf.* *McCandless v. United States*, 298 U.S. 342, 347–48 (1936) (courts must give statutory words their settled meaning). TPS, by contrast, is a temporary humanitarian protection granted to persons already here. A status bestowed after arrival is not the same as the entry Congress required. *See* *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, 169 U.S. 649, 660–61 (1898) (entry is a distinct factual event); *Lewis v. Frick*, 233 U.S. 291, 301–02 (1914) (immigration consequences may turn on the manner of entry).

2. **Congress knows how to deem an alien “admitted” when it wants to do so, and it did not do so here.**  
When Congress intends legal fiction, it says so. *See* *Townsend v. Little*, 109 U.S. 504, 512 (1883) (courts should not add words Congress omitted); *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 60–61 (1930) (statutory exceptions are not to be enlarged by implication). Section 1254a(f)(4) says only that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” It does not say “admitted,” “inspected and admitted,” or “paroled.” The omission is decisive. *See* *United States v. Dickson*, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 141, 165 (1841) (where Congress uses different language, courts presume different meaning); *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (criminal statute not expanded by implication; the same interpretive restraint applies here).

3. **Reading TPS as an admission would collapse distinct statutory terms and upset the structure of the INA.**  
This Court has repeatedly held that related statutory provisions must be read to give each term independent effect. *See* *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 174 (1803) (courts must give effect to enacted law); *United States v. Freeman*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 556, 565 (1845) (each word should be given meaning if possible); *Frost v. Wenie*, 157 U.S. 46, 58 (1895) (avoid constructions that render language meaningless). If TPS were an admission, Congress’s separate reference to lawful status in § 1254a(f)(4) would do all the work of § 1255(a)’s entry requirement, contrary to the settled rule that “every word employed in a statute is to be given effect, if possible.” *Inhabitants of Montclair Tp. v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883). Petitioners’ reading would also nullify the distinction between aliens who entered unlawfully and those inspected and admitted. *See* *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459–60 (1892) (text controls; courts may not rewrite statutes to reach preferred results).

4. **The humanitarian purpose of TPS cannot override the statute’s text.**  
Even broad remedial aims do not permit courts to disregard unambiguous conditions. *See* *Railroad Co. v. Stout*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 657, 663 (1873) (purpose cannot defeat enacted terms); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917) (where statutory text is plain, inquiry ends). TPS shields from removal; it does not confer permanent immigration benefits absent the threshold admission Congress required. *See* *The Aurora*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813) (courts may not supply what Congress left out).

Because petitioners were never “inspected and admitted or paroled,” they are statutorily ineligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. The judgment below should be reversed.
