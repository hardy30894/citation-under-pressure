**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Minnesota ex rel. Pearson v. Probate Court*, 309 U.S. 270 (1940), the Court sustained a state statute addressing psychopathic personality against due process challenge, recognizing that the States retain broad authority to structure the legal treatment of mental abnormality and to determine the procedures by which such conditions bear on criminal and civil proceedings. If a State may constitutionally structure an entire adjudicative scheme around mental abnormality in this fashion, it follows that a State may likewise determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters a criminal trial.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility and emphasized that formulating and reformulating the concepts of criminal responsibility is a task appropriately left to the States rather than imposed by this Court as a matter of federal constitutional command. *Id.* at 535–37. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Pearson* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation.

Moreover, this Court has already sustained the exclusion of similar categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct the jury that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, reasoning that the District of Columbia was entitled to adhere to "the practical difficulties of ingrafting the doctrine of partial responsibility onto our jurisprudence" without violating due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits the evidence to be considered on insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Pearson* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules. In *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court explained that a state procedural rule violates due process only where its enforcement would offend a scheme of ordered liberty so fundamental that its disregard would be intolerable, and the Court cautioned against converting the Fourteenth Amendment into a vehicle for imposing this Court's own preferences regarding state trial procedure. *Id.* at 105. Similarly, in *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not impose upon the States a single, unvarying model of criminal procedure and that the States remain free to develop and modify their own rules of evidence and procedure so long as the result does not deprive a defendant of a fair trial. *Id.* at 110–11.

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, explaining that "the process of adjudication... normally requires the drawing of lines" and that state rules of evidence are entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), is whether "the absence of that fair play" so infects the proceeding as to violate due process. *Id.* at 236. Here, the trial court permitted Clark to introduce the full range of expert psychiatric testimony and to have it evaluated by the factfinder on the issue of insanity—the very evidence he now claims should also negate intent. Nothing in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), which addressed the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be simultaneously admissible for an entirely separate purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend the fundamental fairness safeguarded by *Snyder* and *Twining*, and this Court should affirm.
