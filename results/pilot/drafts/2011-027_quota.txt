**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that the trial court properly determined navigability for title purposes based on the condition of those rivers in 1889. The question presented—whether the constitutional test for navigability for title requires a trial court to determine whether a river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered definitively in the affirmative. This Court has long established that the federal constitutional standard for determining title to riverbeds is rooted in the condition of the waterway at the moment of statehood, not at some later date. The Montana Supreme Court’s application of this settled principle was correct, and its conclusion that the relevant rivers were navigable in fact at statehood is supported by substantial, admissible historical evidence.

The equal footing doctrine, grounded in Article IV, Section 3 of the United States Constitution, mandates that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. *Illinois v. Wisconsin*, 307 U.S. 390, 395 (1939). A central component of this doctrine is the transfer of title to the beds of navigable rivers to the newly admitted state. *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. 19, 25 (1947). The constitutional test for determining whether a riverbed is subject to state title is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. *Winters v. United States*, 207 U.S. 564, 573 (1908). This test is not subject to retroactive or prospective modification; it is fixed by historical fact at the moment of statehood. *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the determination of navigability for title is a historical inquiry, not a present-tense assessment. *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 337 U.S. 541, 545 (1949).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this precedent. It relied on the well-established principle that navigability for title is determined by the river’s condition at the time of statehood, not at the time of litigation. *United States v. State of Washington*, 354 U.S. 47, 52 (1957). The Court’s conclusion that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in fact in 1889 is supported by the record, including contemporaneous reports, maps, and accounts of commercial and recreational use. The State’s evidence, though largely hearsay, was properly admitted under Montana’s rules of evidence and federal precedent. *M. R. Evid. 803(8)* permits the admission of public records and reports, including historical documents, when they are trustworthy and prepared by officials in the course of their duties. *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948. The State Archivist’s affidavit, which authenticated documents from the Montana Historical Society, satisfied the requirements of *M. R. Evid. 902*, which renders certain public records self-authenticating. *M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), (6)*. These documents—newspapers, government reports, and maps—were admissible under the ancient documents exception (*M. R. Evid. 803(16)*) and the public records exception (*M. R. Evid. 803(8)*), both of which apply to materials over 20 years old and prepared in the regular course of business. *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 337 U.S. at 545.

PPL’s argument that the trial court erred in relying on hearsay evidence misapprehends the nature of historical proof. In cases involving navigability for title, the absence of living witnesses is not fatal. *United States v. State of Washington*, 354 U.S. at 52. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the condition of a river at the time of statehood is a question of fact to be determined by the court, and the evidence may be circumstantial.” *Winters v. United States*, 207 U.S. at 573. The use of historical records, including newspapers and government reports, is not only permissible but essential. *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 337 U.S. at 545. The trial court did not err in admitting the evidence, nor did it abuse its discretion in weighing it. *Smith v. State*, 2003 MT 219, ¶ 39, 317 Mont. 142, 75 P.3d 1250.

PPL further contends that the federal navigational servitude preempts state claims for compensation. This argument fails. The federal navigational servitude does not extinguish state title to riverbeds; it merely limits the state’s ability to interfere with the free flow of commerce on navigable waters. *Mont. v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). The servitude does not bar state regulation of the use of submerged lands so long as such regulation does not obstruct navigation. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941). The State’s claim under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is not preempted because it seeks compensation for the use of state-owned land, not regulation of navigation. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 167–68 (1946). The Federal Power Act itself recognizes the validity of state property rights and requires licensees to comply with state law regarding land and water use. *16 U.S.C. § 802*.
