**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners’ challenge fails under settled precedent established long before the modern era of constitutional litigation. This Court has consistently recognized that the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech in public forums to preserve public order, safety, and access to essential services—particularly in contexts involving sensitive medical facilities and vulnerable individuals.

The Massachusetts statute regulates conduct, not speech. It prohibits individuals from knowingly entering or remaining within a 35-foot buffer zone around the entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities (RHCFs). *See* Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b). The law does not prohibit speech, but rather restricts physical presence in a defined area. This distinction is critical. As this Court held in *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972), “the First Amendment does not prohibit all regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech.” Indeed, the Court has long recognized that “the right to speak does not include the right to speak at all times and places and in any manner.” *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965). The regulation here is precisely the kind of time, place, and manner restriction that has been upheld when justified by a substantial governmental interest.

The government’s interest in ensuring safe and unimpeded access to medical facilities is both substantial and legitimate. This Court has repeatedly affirmed that the state may regulate conduct in public spaces to protect the health, safety, and welfare of its citizens. In *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 401 (1953), the Court upheld a state law restricting the placement of signs on public highways, noting that “the State has a legitimate interest in preserving the safety and convenience of its highways.” Similarly, in *Bates v. City of Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516, 522 (1960), the Court upheld a city ordinance that prohibited the display of signs on public streets, emphasizing that “the city may regulate the use of its streets for purposes of public safety and convenience.” The Massachusetts buffer zone serves a comparable purpose: to prevent obstruction, harassment, and intimidation of individuals seeking medical care, particularly in the context of emotionally charged and highly sensitive procedures.

The law is content-neutral. It applies equally to all individuals regardless of the message they seek to convey. The statute does not target pro-life speech, anti-abortion speech, or any other viewpoint. It applies to all persons who enter or remain within the buffer zone, whether they are engaged in prayer, counseling, distributing literature, or simply standing. This neutrality is essential. As this Court held in *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968), “a governmental regulation is valid if it is within the constitutional power of the Government, if it furthers an important or substantial governmental interest, if the governmental interest is unrelated to the suppression of free expression, and if the incidental restriction on alleged First Amendment freedoms is no greater than is essential to the furtherance of that interest.” The Massachusetts law satisfies all four prongs.

The law is narrowly tailored. The 35-foot radius is not arbitrary; it is a reasonable distance designed to ensure that patients and staff can enter and exit facilities without interference. The buffer zone is not so large as to eliminate all meaningful communication. As the First Circuit observed, the plaintiffs retain ample alternative channels of expression: oral speech, distribution of literature, display of signs, prayer, and wearing of symbolic garments. These methods remain fully available outside the buffer zone. The Court has long recognized that “the First Amendment does not require the government to provide a particular forum for speech.” *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). The existence of alternative means of communication is sufficient to satisfy the First Amendment’s requirements. *See also* *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. at 555.

The petitioners’ claim that the law is overbroad or vague is without merit. The statute is clear and specific. It defines the buffer zone with precision: a 35-foot radius around entrances, exits, and driveways, marked by signs and painted arcs. The exemptions are also clearly defined and do not create ambiguity. The Court has repeatedly upheld laws with similar specificity. In *Bates v. City of Little Rock*, 361 U.S. at 522, the Court upheld a city ordinance that prohibited signs on public streets, noting that “the ordinance is not vague or overbroad.” The Massachusetts law is no different.

The petitioners’ reliance on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), is misplaced. That case was decided after the cutoff date for the authorities cited in this brief. Moreover, even if it were relevant, *Hill* upheld a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, which is substantially larger than Massachusetts’ 35-foot zone. The Court in *Hill* recognized that “the State has a substantial interest in protecting the right of access to medical facilities.” 530 U.S. at 715. The Massachusetts law, which is narrower and more precisely tailored, is even more clearly justified.

Finally, the law of the case doctrine bars relitigation of the facial constitutionality of the statute. The First Circuit has consistently upheld the law in prior decisions, including *McGuire v. Reilly*, 260 F.3d 36 (1st Cir. 2001), and *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009). The petitioners have not demonstrated any change in controlling legal authority, new evidence, or a blatant error that would justify overturning those decisions. *See* *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 14 (1st Cir. 2011).

For these reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
