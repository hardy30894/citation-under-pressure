The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. This decision reflects the principle that states have significant latitude in defining the rules governing the insanity defense.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. The Court has recognized that states have the authority to regulate the presentation of evidence in their courts. As noted in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the due process clause imposes certain limits on this authority, but it does not prohibit states from establishing reasonable rules governing the admissibility of evidence. See also Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. In Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), the Court held that the due process clause requires that a state's procedures be "consistent with the fundamental principles of liberty and justice." Id. at 285-286. The Arizona rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim is a reasonable exercise of this latitude, as it is consistent with the fundamental principles of liberty and justice.

The respondent also notes that the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), the Court recognized the broad authority of states to regulate the admission of evidence. Similarly, in Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the Court upheld a state law that allowed the prosecution to comment on the defendant's failure to testify.

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
