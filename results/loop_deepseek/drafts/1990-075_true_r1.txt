**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle that satisfies the nondelegation doctrine. Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This Court has upheld delegations even where Congress has provided only broad directives for the delegatee to follow. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 373 (1989). The Temporary Scheduling Provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), easily meets this standard.

**I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Provides an Intelligible Principle Channeling the Attorney General’s Discretion.**

The Act requires the Attorney General to find that temporary scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is more specific than delegations this Court has repeatedly upheld. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), this Court sustained a delegation to the Price Administrator to fix prices that would be “fair and equitable” and would effectuate the purposes of the Act. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), this Court upheld open-ended delegation to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to prescribe regulations for the orderly and efficient functioning of savings and loan associations. If “fair and equitable” and the general standard of orderly and efficient functioning satisfy the intelligible principle test, the stricter “imminent hazard to the public safety” standard a *fortiori* satisfies it.

The Act further confines the Attorney General by mandating consideration of specified factors: “(4) Its history and current pattern of abuse; (5) The scope, duration, and significance of abuse; (6) What, if any, risk there is to the public health.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6). This Court has held that requiring consideration of enumerated factors is a hallmark of a lawful delegation. See *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948) (sustaining delegation where Congress directed consideration of “such factors as he may deem advisable”); cf. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (delegation to Attorney General to deny bail in “his discretion” upheld).

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is also narrowly limited in duration—one year, with a possible extension of six months. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limit is a “defining boundary,” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374, and far from the open-ended delegations invalidated in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). In *Panama Refining*, Congress gave the President unfettered authority to prohibit interstate transportation of “hot oil” with “no standard,” 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, Congress authorized the President to approve codes of fair competition with no limits on the President’s power to make crime. 295 U.S. at 537-38. Here, by contrast, Congress defined the hazard, prescribed factors, and imposed a sunset. No *Panama Refining* or *Schechter Poultry* problem exists.

**II. This Delegation Involves Criminal Sanctions, but the Intelligible Principle Standard Applies.**

The Toubys argue that a stricter test applies because temporary scheduling creates criminal prohibitions. But this Court has never held that delegating authority to define crimes requires greater specificity than delegating economic or regulatory authority. To the contrary, in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), this Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make regulations governing use of national forests, violations of which were criminal, because Congress had “laid down rules to guide the Secretary” and the statute “provided that his action, like that of other officers, must be reasonable.” So too here.

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is far more concrete than the delegation upheld in *Grimaud*. There, the Secretary had “general authority” to “make rules and regulations.” 220 U.S. at 517. Here, the Attorney General must make specific findings of “imminent hazard to the public safety” and consider three enumerated factors. This is not an uncontrolled grant to define crime. Cf. *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 538 (invalidating delegation where President could prescribe codes of fair competition with no standards whatsoever).

**III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated to the DEA.**

The Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to “promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that officers to whom executive power is delegated may subdelegate that power unless Congress expressly prohibits it. *United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926) (upholding subdelegation by President of authority under Trading with the Enemy Act); *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887) (recognizing implied authority to subdelegate). Here, Congress vested the temporary scheduling power in the “Attorney General,” not in a specific subordinate or bureau. The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator, is a routine administrative arrangement.

The Toubys’ challenge would require this Court to hold that every executive officer must personally exercise every power Congress confers. That proposition has no support in precedent. *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557; *Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. at 11. It would paralyze law enforcement.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Temporary Scheduling Provision provides an intelligible principle—imminent hazard to public safety—coupled with specific factors and a fixed duration. This easily satisfies the nondelegation doctrine as applied in *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420; *Fahey*, 332 U.S. at 250; *Lichter*, 334 U.S. at 778-79; *Carlson*, 342 U.S. at 542-44; and *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 521. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is well within the authority recognized in *Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. at 11, and *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557. The judgment below should be affirmed.
