**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 permits punitive damages under Title VII only where the employer’s discriminatory practice is “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The question presented is whether this standard requires conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous,” beyond the state of mind necessary for liability. It does. Congress incorporated the common-law punitive-damages tradition, under which the character of the defendant’s conduct—not merely its intent—must be sufficiently blameworthy to warrant punishment and deterrence. This Court’s pre-1970 decisions uniformly confirm that punitive damages require conduct that is malicious, wanton, or oppressive; mere intentional discrimination, without more, is insufficient.

**I. The Common Law Requires Conduct That Is “Egregious” or “Outrageous”**

From the earliest days of this Court, punitive damages have been reserved for conduct that is “malicious, fraudulent, or oppressive.” *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851). The Court has repeatedly held that punitive damages are not available for every intentional tort; they require “wanton or reckless disregard of the rights of others.” *Phila., W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858). The standard demands “gross negligence, malice, or wantonness” on the part of the defendant. *Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875). There must be “malice, fraud, or wanton and reckless disregard.” *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886). And “actual malice, willful or wanton misconduct” is required. *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 114 (1893). Consistent with these decisions, the Court has defined punitive damages as a remedy for “fraud, malice, or oppression.” *Am. Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 133, 142 (1898). And for “gross negligence, wantonness, or malice.” *St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. Williams*, 251 U.S. 63, 66 (1919). As Chief Justice Waite explained, punitive damages are imposed “to punish the wrongdoer for his misconduct, and to deter others from like conduct.” *Missouri Pac. Ry. Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885). The unifying theme is that the defendant’s conduct must be “so wanton and reckless as to evince a spirit of maliciousness or oppression.” *Wabash Ry. Co. v. McDaniels*, 107 U.S. 454, 458 (1883).

Thus, the common law does not permit punitive damages simply because the defendant acted intentionally. The conduct itself must be “outrageous” or “egregious” in the sense that it reflects a culpable state of mind beyond the mere intent to do the act—a state of mind that, when combined with the conduct, merits punishment. The statute’s use of “malice” and “reckless indifference” echoes this tradition. Those terms require proof that the employer’s actions were not merely intentional but were taken with a conscious disregard for the plaintiff’s rights that is so severe as to be akin to malice. A routine failure to promote, standing alone, cannot satisfy this standard.

**II. The District Court Correctly Applied This Standard**

The district court denied Kolstad’s punitive damages claim because the evidence did not show that the American Dental Association’s conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous.” The court found that this was a routine discrimination dispute involving a preselection decision and after-the-fact rationalizations. Such evidence, while sufficient to support a finding of intentional discrimination, falls far short of the common-law standard. No evidence showed that the ADA acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to Kolstad’s rights. The preselection of Spangler, the failure to interview Kolstad, and the shifting explanations are examples of classic pretext—not the kind of “wanton or reckless disregard” that the common law demands. *Quigley*, 62 U.S. at 214. The record contains no evidence of hatred, spite, or a deliberate plan to violate the law. The isolated testimony about offensive jokes, while troubling, does not rise to the level of egregious conduct that the common law requires for punitive damages. Indeed, such evidence goes more to workplace climate than to the specific promotion decision at issue.

Moreover, the employer’s conduct must be evaluated in light of the circumstances. The ADA had legitimate reasons for promoting Spangler—his legislative experience, his prior work with O’Donnell, and the fact that the Council on Government Affairs required a manager familiar with its operations. That a jury could find these reasons pretextual does not mean that the ADA acted with malice or reckless indifference. Pretext can arise from subconscious bias, misjudgment, or even simple error. Punitive damages, however, are not a remedy for every mistake; they are a remedy for conduct that is so blameworthy that it warrants punishment. The district court correctly recognized that the evidence here did not clear that high bar.

**III. Petitioner’s Contrary Interpretation Would Evade the Common-Law Tradition**

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any intentional discrimination, without more, satisfies the “malice or reckless indifference” standard. That would effectively read the common-law conduct requirement out of the statute. But Congress expressly invoked the common-law tradition when it enacted § 1981a. The legislative history is consistent with this view: Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum, which Senator Kennedy did not dispute, states that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). This Court’s decisions confirm that punitive damages are not a routine remedy for every intentional tort. *See, e.g., Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371. The standard is a high one: the conduct must be “so gross and wanton as to show a reckless disregard of consequences.” *Humes*, 115 U.S. at 521. The district court properly concluded that the evidence here does not meet that threshold.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed insofar as it remanded for a trial on punitive damages, and the district court’s denial of punitive damages should be affirmed.
