ARGUMENT

A district court's failure to comply with the advice requirement of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32 does not furnish ground for collateral relief when the defendant already knew of his right to appeal and deliberately forwent it. Collateral attack under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 is available only when the asserted error worked actual prejudice. Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962); United States v. Morgan, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954). The district court's finding that Peguero possessed contemporaneous knowledge of his appellate rights therefore disposes of the claim.

This Court has long required a showing of prejudice before disturbing a final judgment on habeas or § 2255 review. Sunal v. Large, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947); Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275, 286 (1941). Where the defendant was already aware of the right to appeal, the omitted advice caused no injury. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938). The purpose of the Rule 32 directive is to ensure that an uninformed defendant learns of the right; that purpose is satisfied when knowledge exists independently. See Hill, 368 U.S. at 429.

Harmless-error principles confirm the same result. A constitutional or procedural lapse that does not affect substantial rights furnishes no basis for reversal or vacatur. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967); Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946). Because Peguero elected not to appeal with full awareness of the opportunity, the omission was harmless.

The same conclusion follows from decisions addressing the right to appeal itself. Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), and Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 357 (1963), protect access to appellate review for those who wish to pursue it. They do not create a remedy for defendants who, knowing the right, choose to waive it. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963), likewise conditions federal habeas relief on the absence of a deliberate bypass; here the bypass was knowing.

Finally, Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963), limits collateral inquiry to cases presenting a substantial federal claim that was not adequately developed in the original proceeding. No such claim exists when the record establishes the defendant's actual knowledge of appellate rights. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
