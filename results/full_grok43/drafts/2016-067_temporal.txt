The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act applies only to those who collect debts owed or due another. Its text must receive its ordinary signification. United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805). A person who purchases defaulted obligations acquires title and thereafter collects on his own account. Mandeville v. Welch, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 277, 286 (1820). The distinction between an owner collecting his own debt and an agent collecting for another is fundamental. See, e.g., Tiernan v. Jackson, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 580, 597 (1831).

Santander purchased the loans outright. Once title passed, the deficiency balances became its own property. It therefore stands in the position of the original creditor, not a third-party collector. The statute does not reach creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. This limitation follows directly from the words “owed or due another,” which presuppose an ongoing separation between the debt and the person seeking payment. See United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (every word is to be given effect).

Petitioners’ contrary reading would convert every assignee into a debt collector merely because the debt was in default at purchase. That construction finds no support in the language and would erase the statutory line between owners and agents. The complaint alleges no facts showing that Santander collected for anyone other than itself. Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed.
