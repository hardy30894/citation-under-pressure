**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner respectfully submits that the post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The text of § 1231(a)(6) is silent as to duration, but this Court has long held that “[s]tatutory silence is not statutory authorization.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 696 (2001) (quoting *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 442 n.29 (1987)). Construing § 1231(a)(6) to permit indefinite detention would raise grave constitutional concerns, and this Court must therefore “construe the statute to contain an implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.” *Id.* at 699.

A. The Statute’s Text and Structure Do Not Authorize Indefinite Detention

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien “may be detained beyond the removal period” if the alien is inadmissible or removable for certain reasons. The statute does not specify any temporal limit on such detention. However, this Court has repeatedly cautioned against construing statutes to permit prolonged deprivation of liberty without clear textual authorization. In *Demore v. Kim*, 538 U.S. 510 (2003), the Court upheld mandatory detention during removal proceedings for certain criminal aliens, but it did so only because the statute explicitly required detention “pending a [removal] decision.” *Id.* at 513 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1226(c)). By contrast, § 1231(a)(6) contains no such limiting language. As the Court recognized in *Zadvydas*, “[t]he absence of a clear statutory language authorizing indefinite detention is itself a reason to doubt that Congress intended such a result.” 533 U.S. at 696.

Moreover, the statute’s structure distinguishes between detention during the removal period and detention beyond it. Section 1231(a)(1) and (2) govern detention *during* the 90-day removal period, while § 1231(a)(6) addresses detention *after* that period. The latter provision authorizes detention only “pending removal,” which implies a temporal limit tied to the Government’s ability to effectuate removal. *See id.* at 698 (“The statute’s text, read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal from the United States.”).

B. Indefinite Detention Raises Serious Constitutional Concerns

This Court has long recognized that “[f]reedom from imprisonment—from government restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690 (quoting *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992)). In *Foucha*, the Court held that a state could not indefinitely detain a person acquitted by reason of insanity once he was no longer mentally ill, even if he posed a danger to society. *Id.* at 86. Similarly, in *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715 (1972), the Court held that a state could not indefinitely commit a criminal defendant found incompetent to stand trial. *Id.* at 738. These cases establish that “[t]he Due Process Clause does not permit indefinite detention without a judicial determination of dangerousness or need for confinement.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690.

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. In *Mezei*, the Court upheld the detention of an excludable alien at Ellis Island while the Government attempted to find a country willing to accept him. *Id.* at 216. However, the Court emphasized that Mezei was an excludable alien who had “never entered the United States” and was therefore “treated as if stopped at the threshold.” *Id.* at 212. By contrast, Petitioner is a lawful permanent resident who has already entered the country and cannot be treated as if he were at the border. *See Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700 (“Mezei is not controlling here because it involved an excludable alien who had not effected entry into the United States.”).

C. The Statute Must Be Construed to Include a Reasonable Time Limitation

Given the constitutional concerns raised by indefinite detention, this Court must construe § 1231(a)(6) to include an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. *See Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699. The Court has repeatedly applied this canon of constitutional avoidance where a statute is susceptible to multiple interpretations, one of which would raise serious constitutional questions. *See, e.g., Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 381 (2005) (“We must construe the statute to avoid serious constitutional doubts if fairly possible.”).

In *Zadvydas*, the Court held that detention under § 1231(a)(6) is reasonable only for a period “reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal.” 533 U.S. at 701. The Court further held that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” *Id.* at 703. The Court did not set a fixed time limit, but it recognized a presumptively reasonable period of six months, after which the Government must rebut a showing that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 704-05.

Petitioner has been detained for far longer than six months beyond the removal period, and there is no realistic prospect of his removal. The Government has conceded that no country will accept Petitioner, and his efforts to obtain citizenship in Lithuania have stalled. *See id.* at 681 n.1. Under *Zadvydas*, continued detention is therefore unreasonable and unauthorized by the statute.

D. The Government’s Reliance on Supervised Release Is Misplaced

The Government argues that Petitioner may be released under supervised conditions, but this does not cure the constitutional infirmity of indefinite detention. As the Court recognized in *Zadvydas*, the choice is not between imprisonment and “living at large,” but between imprisonment and “supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” *Id.* at 702 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(3)). Supervised release is a permissible alternative to detention, but it cannot justify indefinite detention in the first place.

Moreover, the Government’s reliance on *Demore v. Kim*, 538 U.S. 510, is misplaced. In *Demore*, the Court upheld mandatory detention during removal proceedings for certain criminal aliens, but it did so only because the statute explicitly required detention “pending a [removal] decision.” *Id.* at 513. By contrast, § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention, and the Government cannot rely on supervised release to justify what the statute does not permit.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioner respectfully submits that the post-removal-period statute does not authorize his indefinite detention and that the Court should reverse the judgment below.
