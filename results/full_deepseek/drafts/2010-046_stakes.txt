**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Population Reduction Order Satisfies Every Requirement of the Prison Litigation Reform Act.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) permits a prisoner release order only if a three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The court below made precisely those findings after an exhaustive seven-week trial. The PLRA’s additional requirement that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation” is also satisfied. *Id.* § 3626(a)(1)(A).

**A. The Court’s Finding That Overcrowding Is the Primary Cause of the Eighth Amendment Violation Is Supported by Clear and Convincing Evidence.**

The district court documented that California’s prisons operate at nearly 200% of design capacity, creating rampant disease, suicide, and untreated serious medical needs. This overcrowding “is the primary cause of the unconstitutional conditions.” *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088, 1093 (9th Cir. 2010) (affirming the three-judge court’s findings). The PLRA demands clear and convincing evidence, and the court had it—expert testimony, statistical analyses, and decades of failed reform efforts. This Court in *Brown v. Plata* upheld those findings, concluding they were “not clearly erroneous.” 563 U.S. 493, 499 (2011).

**B. The Court Considered and Properly Rejected All Alternative Remedies.**

The PLRA does not require a district court to exhaust every conceivable alternative before ordering a population cap. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 531 (holding that the PLRA does not “require a court to implement alternative remedies that are ‘obviously futile’”). The court below heard extensive evidence that California had already spent billions on facility upgrades, expanded medical staffing, and implemented new policies—yet the constitutional violations persisted. The only remaining option was a reduction in the inmate population. *Id.* at 509-10. The order is “narrowly drawn” because it sets a specific population cap (137.5% of design capacity) and takes effect only after a phased implementation period. *Id.* at 532.

**C. The Order Satisfies the Additional Requirements for Prisoner Release Orders Under § 3626(a)(3).**

The PLRA requires that a prisoner release order be entered only by a three-judge court. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The court below was properly convened and made the requisite findings. The order explicitly states that no other relief would remedy the violation—a finding the Supreme Court affirmed as “not clearly erroneous.” *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 499. The population cap is temporary, terminating automatically when the constitutional deficiencies are corrected. *See id.* at 534 (noting that the order “does not prescribe a definite release date; rather, it requires that overcrowding be reduced to a specified level”).

**II. The PLRA Does Not Prohibit a Population Cap as a Remedy for Systemic Unconstitutional Conditions.**

State petitioners argue that the PLRA bars any relief that orders the release of prisoners. That argument misreads the statute. The PLRA explicitly provides for prisoner release orders under the conditions stated in § 3626(a)(3). Here, those conditions were met. The order is not a punitive removal of prisoners but a carefully calibrated remedy to restore constitutional minimums. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 535 (“The PLRA does not forbid courts from addressing the constitutional violations caused by overcrowding; it only requires that courts do so with rigor.”).

**III. The District Court’s Factual Findings Are Entitled to Deference.**

This Court reviews the district court’s factual findings for clear error and its legal conclusions de novo. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 499. The district court’s findings—that overcrowding is the primary cause of the violations and that no other relief will suffice—are heavily supported by the record. The court heard testimony from over 30 witnesses and reviewed thousands of pages of documents. Its conclusions cannot be disturbed unless they are “clearly erroneous.” *Id.* at 500. They are not.

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court meticulously complied with all PLRA requirements. Its order is narrowly drawn, supported by clear and convincing evidence, and necessary to remedy ongoing constitutional violations. The judgment should be affirmed.
