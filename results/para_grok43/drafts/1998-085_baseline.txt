**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 authorizes punitive damages only upon a showing that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice … with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That standard incorporates the common-law requirement that the defendant’s conduct be egregious or outrageous, independent of the mental state that supports liability for intentional discrimination. See Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 46-48 (1983) (punitive damages available only when conduct is “of the sort that calls for deterrence and punishment over and above that provided by compensatory awards”); Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908 cmt. b (1979) (same).

This Court has long held that punitive damages “are not a favorite of the law” and are confined to cases of “aggravated or outrageous” misconduct. Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1859); Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893). The 1991 Act did not displace that tradition. Its legislative history confirms that Congress intended punitive awards only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). Representative Edwards’s statement that the standards track those under 42 U.S.C. § 1981 is consistent with the circuits that already required egregious conduct for § 1981 punitive damages. See, e.g., Yarborough v. City of Warren, 383 F. Supp. 676, 682 (E.D. Mich. 1974); Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy Health Corp., 635 F. Supp. 125, 127 (E.D. Mich. 1986).

Kolstad’s evidence—preselection of a male colleague, failure to interview her, and stray remarks—establishes at most intentional discrimination. It does not demonstrate the “outrageous” conduct required for punishment. See Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 832 F.2d 194, 205-06 (1st Cir. 1987) (requiring “extraordinary” misconduct beyond intentional violation). The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction.

The court of appeals’ contrary view—that proof of intentional discrimination alone suffices—contradicts both the statutory text and the common-law backdrop against which Congress legislated. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994). Because Kolstad failed to make the requisite showing of egregiousness, the judgment denying punitive damages should be affirmed.
