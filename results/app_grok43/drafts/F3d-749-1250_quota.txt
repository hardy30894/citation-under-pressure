The district court plainly erred by admitting Agent Jones’s testimony. Expert opinion is admissible only if it rests on reliable methods and assists the trier of fact without usurping its role. Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 592-95 (1993); Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137, 147-49 (1999). An expert may not opine on a witness’s credibility or the defendant’s guilt. United States v. Charley, 189 F.3d 1251, 1266-67 (10th Cir. 1999); United States v. Toledo, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993). Agent Jones’s assertions that Stanley’s statements “did not make sense,” displayed “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element,” and were “deceptive” because he invoked faith or said he would “rather die” crossed this line.

Because Stanley did not object, review is for plain error. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 731-37 (1993). The error was clear under existing precedent. United States v. Benally, 541 F.3d 990, 994 (10th Cir. 2008). It affected substantial rights by bolstering the government’s case with an aura of scientific certainty on the central issue of Stanley’s truthfulness. United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1985). The testimony was not harmless, as the jury’s prior deadlock shows the evidence was close. United States v. Mejia-Alarcon, 995 F.2d 982, 988 (10th Cir. 1993).

The government may not introduce evidence that a defendant’s behavior matches that of “the criminal element.” United States v. Rodriguez, 192 F.3d 946, 949-50 (10th Cir. 1999). Nor may an expert vouch for the prosecution’s theory by declaring that innocent persons never make certain statements. United States v. Samara, 643 F.2d 701, 705 (10th Cir. 1981). These violations, taken together, seriously affected the fairness of the trial. United States v. Gabaldon, 389 F.3d 1090, 1097-98 (10th Cir. 2004). Reversal is required.
