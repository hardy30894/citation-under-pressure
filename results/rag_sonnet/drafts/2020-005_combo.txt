# ARGUMENT

Montana's exercise of specific jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company comports with the Due Process Clause because Ford deliberately and systematically cultivated a market for its vehicles—including the exact model at issue—within Montana, and the resulting activities bear a close relationship to the claims asserted.

## I. Due Process Requires Only That the Suit "Arise Out of or Relate to" the Defendant's In-State Activities, Not That the Defendant's In-State Conduct Cause the Injury.

The touchstone of the personal-jurisdiction inquiry is whether the defendant's conduct and connection with the forum State are such that it should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington* is invoked repeatedly in the retrieved authorities as the governing framework, and this Court's subsequent decision in *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), confirms that the constitutional question turns on "appropriate tests" of "general fairness to the corporation," assessed according to "the amount and kind of activities which must be carried on by the foreign corporation in the state of the forum." *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 445. Nothing in that formulation demands that the specific in-state acts be the mechanical cause of the plaintiff's injury. Rather, the inquiry has long been framed disjunctively—jurisdiction attaches when a suit "arises out of or by reason of" an accident connected to the defendant's activity in the forum. This Court's decision in *Wuchter v. Pizzutti*, 276 U.S. 13 (1928), upheld the constitutionality of nonresident-motorist statutes premised on exactly this relational, rather than strictly causal, linkage between the defendant's forum conduct and the resulting suit—there, the statutory agent was authorized to accept service in "any civil suit or proceeding by any resident . . . arising out of or by reason of any accident or collision occurring within the State." *Wuchter*, 276 U.S. at 15 (quoting N.J. statute). The constitutional principle these statutes vindicated—that a party who avails itself of the state's roads, market, and protections may be sued there on claims connected to that activity—applies with equal force to Ford's decades of marketing, selling, and servicing vehicles in Montana.

## II. Ford's Purposeful and Continuous Activities Directed at the Montana Market Establish the Requisite Relationship to Gullett's Claims.

Ford is not a stranger to Montana. It has advertised, distributed, and sold vehicles—including Explorers of the same model as the one that caused Gullett's death—throughout the state for years, and it maintains dealerships and service networks there. This is precisely the kind of purposeful availment of a forum's market that has long justified specific jurisdiction over claims connected to that market. In *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), this Court sustained Massachusetts's exercise of jurisdiction over a nonresident motorist because the state could constitutionally condition "the privilege extended" to nonresidents "of driving" upon their vehicles "within the State" on amenability to suit there. *Hess*, 274 U.S. at 353 (quoting Mass. statute). The rationale is not that Massachusetts caused the accident; it is that the defendant accepted the benefits and protections of operating within the forum and thereby assumed the correlative burden of answering for injuries connected to that activity. Ford's decision to court Montana consumers—flooding the state with advertising, authorizing dealerships to sell and service its vehicles, and profiting from an ongoing stream of commerce into the state—is a far more substantial and voluntary connection to the forum than the single, involuntary act of driving through Massachusetts at issue in *Hess*.

This Court's decision in *Perkins* likewise instructs that the relevant activities need not be the actual instrumentality of the plaintiff's injury; what matters is whether the defendant's forum contacts are extensive enough that subjecting it to suit is fair. 342 U.S. at 445. Ford's activities in Montana—unlike the isolated wartime relocation of Benguet's operations to Ohio in *Perkins*—are exactly the type of continuous, purposeful commercial activity that renders suit foreseeable.

## III. Treating Causation as a Rigid Jurisdictional Requirement Would Contradict This Court's Established Approach to Corporate Amenability to Suit.

This Court's older decisions confirm that a foreign corporation's jurisdictional amenability turns on whether it has purposefully engaged with the forum, not on a but-for causal chain to the plaintiff's injury. In *Provident Savings Life Assurance Society v. Ford*, 114 U.S. 635 (1885), the Court held that a corporation "desirous of doing business in a State other than that in which they have their domicil" may be required to accept service there as "a condition of their doing business in such State." 114 U.S. at 637. Similarly, in *Minnesota Commercial Men's Association v. Benn*, 261 U.S. 140 (1923), the Court examined whether the insurer's Montana contacts—service on the Secretary of State pursuant to statute—sufficed to bind it to a Montana judgment, without demanding that those contacts be the operative cause of the underlying claim. 261 U.S. at 142–44. And in *Western Loan & Savings Co. v. Butte & Boston Consolidated Mining Co.*, 210 U.S. 368 (1908), the Court recognized that a defendant's engagement with the forum's judicial and commercial processes—there, litigating on the merits—can itself establish the necessary submission to jurisdiction, again without any inquiry into strict causation. 210 U.S. at 369–70.

A causation requirement of the kind Ford urges would also produce anomalous results inconsistent with *Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad*, 346 U.S. 338 (1953), which recognized that statutes like the Kentucky nonresident-motorist provision are "not subject to attack for lack of due process," 346 U.S. at 341, precisely because they tie suit to the defendant's forum-directed conduct broadly conceived, not to a narrow causal nexus.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
