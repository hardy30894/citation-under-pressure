**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that "the process of formulating a rule of criminal responsibility... is one in which the legislature of each State has wide latitude," and that due process does not compel adoption of any particular test of criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows a fortiori that a State may also determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This channeling approach is consistent with the historical understanding of insanity as an affirmative defense. Under the rule announced in *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), mental disease was treated as a matter to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a device for negating the prosecution's proof of intent element-by-element. Arizona's rule preserves that traditional separation.

This Court's decision governing federal insanity procedure, *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373 (1897), likewise treated the question of a defendant's sanity as a discrete inquiry once raised by the evidence, rather than as a freestanding avenue for defeating the mens rea element through unchanneled psychiatric testimony. Nothing in *Davis* suggests that the Constitution requires a State to permit mental-disease evidence to be used interchangeably for both purposes.

Lower federal courts confronting the proper scope of mental-disease evidence have likewise recognized that a jurisdiction may adopt a defined standard for evaluating such evidence without offending due process. See *Durham v. United States*, 214 F.2d 862 (D.C. Cir. 1954) (discussing the proper formulation of a test for criminal responsibility based on mental disease or defect). That such a standard exists, and that jurisdictions differ in adopting it, confirms that the structuring of mental-disease evidence is a matter of substantive criminal law entrusted to the several jurisdictions, not a uniform constitutional command.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). The Fourteenth Amendment does not "assure uniformity of judicial procedure," *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 111 (1908), and States remain free "to abolish old and to create new remedies... in the exercise of a wide discretion." *Id.* at 110.

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, explaining that "the process of adjudication... normally requires the drawing of lines" and that state rules of evidence are entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

This Court's fundamental-fairness inquiry, articulated in *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), and *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), asks whether a state procedure works a denial of the kind of basic fairness that due process guarantees, not whether the State has adopted the specific procedural framework a defendant would prefer. Arizona's channeling rule does not deny Clark a fair opportunity to present his mental-disease evidence; it was fully admitted and considered by the trial court on the question of insanity. The rule merely declines to allow that same evidence, freed from the burdens attending the insanity defense, to be redeployed against the mens rea element the State must independently prove.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's rule reflects the same "wide latitude" this Court recognized in *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 800–01, respects the traditional separation between insanity and mens rea reflected in *M'Naghten's Case* and *Davis*, and comports with the fundamental-fairness principles of *Snyder*, *Twining*, *Spencer*, *Betts*, and *Palko*. Because Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people" as to be ranked fundamental, *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, this Court should affirm the judgment below.
