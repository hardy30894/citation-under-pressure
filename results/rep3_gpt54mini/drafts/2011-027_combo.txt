Respondent correctly held that navigability for title turns on the condition of the river at the time of statehood, not on some later-formed conception of present-day use. That rule follows from the equal footing doctrine itself. When a State enters the Union, it takes title to the beds of waters that are then navigable in fact. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75-76 (1931); United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935); United States v. Arizona, 295 U.S. 174, 184 (1935). The question is historical and river-specific. As this Court explained in Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 87-88 (1922), the inquiry asks whether the waterway was used, or was susceptible of being used, as a highway of commerce in its natural and ordinary condition.

This Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to substitute later conditions for the statehood inquiry. In Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 122-23 (1921), the Court held that navigability depends on the river’s condition “at the time of the admission of the State into the Union,” and that later improvements do not control title. Likewise, Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922), explained that the relevant evidence concerns the river as it existed when sovereignty passed, because title cannot be created by post-statehood changes in use or condition. See also United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926) (title depends on whether the water was navigable in fact at statehood).

The same principle governs the scope of the inquiry. In United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931), the Court emphasized that navigability for title is determined “as of the time of statehood,” and that the analysis must be applied to “each separate section” of the river. That is critical here because river navigability is not an all-or-nothing proposition. The Court reiterated in United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 406-07 (1940), that navigability may vary by segment and must be evaluated by reference to the particular stretch at issue. And in United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935), the Court confirmed that the burden is to establish the navigable character of the specific waterway at the relevant historical moment.

Respondent’s approach is also consistent with the foundational cases defining navigability in fact. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871), held that waters are navigable when they form “a continued highway” for commerce. The Montello, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430, 441-42 (1874), explained that navigability does not depend on exact methods of commerce, but it still turns on the river’s natural capacity for use. And The Genesee Chief, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 443, 454-57 (1851), made clear that the governing test is practical, not formalistic, because the inquiry is whether the waterway is capable of serving commerce in fact.

PPL’s contrary position would untether title from the equal footing doctrine and invite after-the-fact reclassification of riverbeds based on changing uses, engineering works, or modern recreational travel. That is precisely what this Court’s cases forbid. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 76-77 (1931); Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 123-24 (1921). The trial court therefore did not err in asking whether the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable when Montana entered the Union.

The judgment should be affirmed.
