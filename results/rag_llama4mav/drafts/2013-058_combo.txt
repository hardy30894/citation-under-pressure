The U.S. Bankruptcy Code's exemption of "retirement funds" from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy does not apply to inherited IRAs because they are not "retirement funds" in the hands of the beneficiary. The plain meaning of "retirement funds" implies that the funds are set aside for the owner's retirement. As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund because it is subject to mandatory distributions and cannot be held until the beneficiary's retirement.

The Bankruptcy Code's exemption provisions should be interpreted in light of the purpose of the exemption, which is to protect assets that are essential for the debtor's fresh start. In Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), the Court held that the Bankruptcy Act does not compel the administration of exempt property as part of the estate in bankruptcy. Similarly, in Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622 (1943), the Court emphasized the importance of allowing debtors to claim exemptions under state law. However, these cases do not support the proposition that inherited IRAs are exempt from creditors' claims.

In fact, the characteristics of inherited IRAs are more akin to assets that are not exempt from creditors' claims. In Kaufman v. Tredway, 195 U.S. 271 (1904), the Court held that a creditor's claim against a bankrupt's estate was not defeated by the fact that the creditor had previously transferred property to the bankrupt. Similarly, in Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co., 297 U.S. 230 (1936), the Court held that a creditor's claim against a bankrupt's estate was not affected by the creditor's withdrawal of its claim. These cases illustrate that the Bankruptcy Code is designed to balance the interests of debtors and creditors.

Furthermore, the tax treatment of inherited IRAs is not dispositive of their status as "retirement funds." In Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States, 391 U.S. 224 (1968), the Court held that employer contributions to a welfare fund were not entitled to priority under the Bankruptcy Act because they did not satisfy the manifest purpose of the priority provision. Similarly, in Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358 (1964), the Court held that post-petition interest on a tax assessment was not discharged in bankruptcy. These cases demonstrate that the tax treatment of an asset is not determinative of its status under the Bankruptcy Code.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is also supported by the principle that exemptions should be narrowly construed. In Schrader v. Manufacturers' Nat. Bank of Chicago, 133 U.S. 67 (1890), the Court held that a statutory exemption should be interpreted in light of its purpose and the context in which it is applied. Similarly, in McKee v. Paradise, 299 U.S. 119 (1936), the Court emphasized the importance of tracing trust funds into the hands of a trustee in bankruptcy. These cases illustrate that exemptions under the Bankruptcy Code should be carefully construed to achieve their intended purpose.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that inherited IRAs are not "retirement funds" within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Code and are therefore not exempt from creditors' claims. The Seventh Circuit's decision should be affirmed.
