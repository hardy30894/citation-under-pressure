**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits Montana to exercise specific jurisdiction over Ford because Ford’s extensive in-state contacts relate to the claims at issue. Specific jurisdiction requires that the suit “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S. A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011). This standard is satisfied when the controversy is “connected with” the defendant’s forum activities. Id. at 919 (quoting von Mehren & Trautman, Jurisdiction To Adjudicate, 79 Harv. L. Rev. 1121, 1136 (1966)).

Ford purposefully availed itself of Montana’s market by advertising, selling, and servicing vehicles there for decades. These contacts are not “random, fortuitous, or attenuated.” Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (quoting World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286, 295 (1980)). They create a “substantial connection” with the State that makes jurisdiction foreseeable. Id. at 475. The claims concern a Ford Explorer whose design, manufacture, and warnings Ford controlled; the vehicle entered Montana through the stream of commerce Ford itself created. That connection satisfies the “relate to” element even without strict causation. See Goodyear, 564 U.S. at 919.

Ford’s contrary rule would immunize manufacturers that systematically exploit a forum yet escape accountability for injuries occurring there. The Constitution does not require such a result. See Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927) (recognizing a State’s power to subject nonresidents using its highways to suit for resulting injuries). Because Ford’s Montana activities are continuous, substantial, and directly tied to the type of product and injury alleged, the exercise of jurisdiction comports with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945) (internal quotation omitted). The Montana Supreme Court’s judgment should be affirmed.
