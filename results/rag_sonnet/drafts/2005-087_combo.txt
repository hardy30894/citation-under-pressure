# ARGUMENT

## Arizona's Restriction on the Use of Mental-Disease Evidence Does Not Violate Due Process

Arizona's channeling of expert mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof, rather than permitting its unrestricted use to negate the mens rea element on which the State bears the burden, is fully consistent with the Due Process Clause. This Court's longstanding precedents confirm that States possess broad latitude to structure the relationship between mental-illness evidence and criminal responsibility, and that no freestanding constitutional right compels the admission of psychiatric evidence for whatever purpose a defendant chooses.

### A. States Have Historically Enjoyed Wide Latitude to Allocate the Burden of Proving Insanity and to Define How Mental-Disease Evidence Bears on Criminal Responsibility

This Court settled the constitutional baseline in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), upholding Oregon's requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court there recognized that even under such a demanding allocation, "the prosecution was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged, including...premeditation, deliberation, malice and intent," while the insanity question remained separately allocated to the defendant. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 790. Critically, *Leland* did not hold that the Constitution mandates unlimited use of mental-disease evidence outside the insanity framework; rather, it approved a system in which the jury considers such evidence "with all other evidence, whether or not you find defendant insane, in regard to the ability of the defendant to premeditate, form a purpose, to deliberate, act wilfully, and act maliciously." *Id.* Arizona's scheme, which channels the use of mental-disease evidence, falls comfortably within the latitude *Leland* confirms belongs to the States.

This Court's decision in *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), reinforces that due process does not demand a particular procedural mechanism for raising mental-condition evidence. The Court explained that if evidence of the defendant's mental condition "raised a substantial doubt as to [his] sanity," the remedy lay in specific procedural avenues—such as moving to withdraw a plea—not in a constitutional entitlement to use the evidence however the defendant might prefer. *Baldi*, 344 U.S. at 561. The State's channeling of such evidence into defined procedural channels, subject to appellate review for abuse of discretion, satisfies due process.

### B. Historical Practice Confirms That the Insanity Defense, Not an Independent Mens Rea Doctrine, Is the Traditional Vehicle for Mental-Disease Evidence

The Court's early decisions in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), and *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373 (1897), establish that mental-disease evidence has traditionally been evaluated through the insanity framework, with courts articulating standards for "distinguishing between right and wrong" as the operative test. *Davis*, 165 U.S. at 373. This Court's own instruction in the second *Davis* case reflects the traditional understanding that insanity evidence serves to negate criminal responsibility through the recognized insanity standard, not through a freestanding channel for negating specific intent untethered from that framework.

*Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413 (1902), likewise confirms that the constitutionally sufficient method for jurors to weigh mental-disease evidence is through the insanity inquiry—asking whether the defendant was "laboring under an insane delusion" that "control[led] his will power, reflection, reason and judgment." *Hotema*, 186 U.S. at 413. Nothing in this Court's precedents suggests that channeling mental-disease evidence through this traditional framework, rather than permitting a parallel, unrestricted mens rea challenge, offends due process.

### C. Lynch v. Overholser Confirms the State's Authority to Structure the Consequences and Scope of Insanity-Related Evidence

In *Lynch v. Overholser*, 369 U.S. 705 (1962), this Court recognized that a defendant "may avoid the ordinary criminal penalty merely by submitting enough evidence of an abnormal mental condition to raise a reasonable doubt of his responsibility at the time of committing the offense," but emphasized that the consequences and procedural treatment of such evidence remain matters of legislative policy. *Lynch*, 369 U.S. at 705. This confirms that the State retains authority to determine how mental-abnormality evidence interacts with criminal responsibility determinations, including the channeling function Arizona has adopted.

### D. Petitioner's Reliance on Competency and Contempt Cases Is Misplaced

Petitioner may invoke *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), and *Panico v. United States*, 375 U.S. 29 (1963), but both are readily distinguishable. *Pate* addressed the distinct due process requirement of a competency hearing where "the evidence raise[s] a 'reasonable doubt' as to his sanity at the time of the offense" for purposes of standing trial—not the scope of evidence permissible at trial on the merits. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 375. *Panico* likewise concerned the procedural adequacy of a summary contempt proceeding lacking any hearing on mental capacity, holding only that "fair administration of federal criminal justice requires a plenary hearing" under the Federal Rules—a procedural guarantee inapposite here, where Clark received a full hearing on his insanity claim. *Panico*, 375 U.S. at 29. Neither case establishes a substantive right to bypass the insanity framework's burden allocation.

### E. Massey and Blackburn Confirm That Due Process Concerns Arise From Denial of Any Opportunity to Litigate Mental Condition, Not From Channeling Rules

*Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), and *Blackburn v. Alabama*, 361 U.S. 199 (1960), both involved defendants denied any meaningful opportunity to have their mental condition adjudicated. *Massey* held the petitioner "entitled to a hearing on the question whether he was insane," where none had occurred. *Massey*, 348 U.S. at 105. *Blackburn* involved a defendant with an extensive institutional history whose confession was challenged as involuntary given his adjudicated insanity. *Blackburn*, 361 U.S. at 199. Clark, by contrast, received a full opportunity to present his mental-disease evidence through Arizona's insanity framework. The channeling of that evidence's use, rather than its exclusion, distinguishes this case entirely from *Massey* and *Blackburn*.

### Conclusion

Arizona's rule respects the traditional insanity framework this Court has repeatedly approved and imposes no due process violation.
