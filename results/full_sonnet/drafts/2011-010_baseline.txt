# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA'S PLAIN TEXT DOES NOT LIMIT LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF

### A. The Statutory Text Requires Only That the Injury Result From OCS Operations, Not That It Occur There

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the OCS. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute's geographic limitation modifies "operations," not "injury." Congress required that the *operations* be "conducted on the outer Continental Shelf"—it imposed no parallel requirement that the *injury* occur there.

This textual structure is decisive. Where Congress wished to impose a situs requirement tied to the location of injury, it knew how to do so explicitly. Compare 33 U.S.C. § 903(a) (Longshore Act coverage limited to injuries "occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States" or specified adjoining areas). Section 1333(b) contains no such language. Had Congress intended to cabin coverage to injuries occurring on the Shelf itself, it could have written "any injury occurring on the outer Continental Shelf as the result of such operations." It did not.

The Third Circuit correctly recognized this distinction in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), holding that a claimant satisfies § 1333(b) so long as the injury occurred "as the result of" OCS operations, applying essentially a but-for causation test. *Id.* at 809–11. Under that approach, an employee injured in an automobile accident en route to a helicopter that would ferry him to an offshore platform was covered, because his injury was causally connected to OCS extractive operations even though it occurred entirely onshore. *Id.* at 806, 811.

### B. This Court's Dictum in *Tallentire* Does Not Compel a Situs-of-Injury Requirement

Petitioner relies heavily on a footnote in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), suggesting that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." *Id.* at 219 n.2. But this Court expressly disclaimed any holding on the point, stating that because the case "does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b), this provision has no bearing on this case." *Id.*

*Tallentire* addressed an entirely different provision—the choice-of-law rule in § 1333(a)(2)(A), which by its own terms applies to "the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That provision's explicit situs language has no counterpart in § 1333(b). The footnote's passing reference to § 1333(b) was unnecessary to the decision, unbriefed by the parties, and unaccompanied by any statutory analysis. Such unconsidered dicta does not warrant the weight petitioner urges. As this Court has long recognized, "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and their "possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821); *see also Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935).

Moreover, even taken at face value, *Tallentire*'s reference to a "situs requirement" is ambiguous as between a situs-of-injury and a situs-of-operations test. Section 1333(b) unquestionably requires that the *operations* occur on the Shelf—a point *Herb's Welding v. Gray*, 766 F.2d 898, 900 (5th Cir. 1985), confirms by holding that injuries on platforms in state waters fall outside OCSLA. But nothing in *Tallentire* addresses whether the *injury itself* must occur there.

### C. The "Substantial Nexus" Test Faithfully Implements Congressional Intent

Given that § 1333(b) requires only that the injury be "the result of" qualifying OCS operations, coverage should extend whenever a substantial nexus exists between the injury and those operations. This causal standard—rather than an atextual geographic limitation—honors both the statutory language and OCSLA's remedial purpose of ensuring compensation for workers whose injuries are attributable to the extraction of the Shelf's natural resources.

The Fifth Circuit's contrary approach in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), engrafts a situs-of-injury requirement nowhere found in the statutory text, relying substantially on the same *Tallentire* dictum and on *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985)—a case addressing the wholly distinct question whether a platform worker in state waters qualified for "maritime employment" status under the Longshore Act itself, not under § 1333(b)'s extension provision. *Id.* at 415–16, 422–24. *Herb's Welding I*'s observation that OCSLA coverage depends on "explicit geographic limitation" addressed the Shelf-based *situs of operations*, not an injury-location requirement.

### D. Respondent's Claim Satisfies the Substantial-Nexus Standard

Juan Valladolid spent approximately 98% of his working time aboard the Hogan platform on the OCS. His death occurred while performing duties—consolidating scrap metal generated by OCS extractive operations—directly connected to those operations. This substantial nexus between his employment on the Shelf and his fatal injury satisfies § 1333(b)'s causation requirement, regardless of the onshore location where the injury occurred.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
