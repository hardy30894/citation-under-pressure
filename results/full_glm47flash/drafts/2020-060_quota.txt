**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Consent Decree is not a “settlement” under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B).**

The D.C. Circuit erred in concluding that the 2004 Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability for purposes of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). The Consent Decree resolved liability for violations of the Clean Water Act (CWA), not for CERCLA liability. *United States v. Rapanos*, 547 U.S. 715, 730 (2006). The Consent Decree required Guam to “complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate.” This remedial action was a CWA remedy, not a CERCLA “response action.” CERCLA § 101(24). Because the Consent Decree did not resolve liability for a CERCLA response action, it cannot trigger the contribution provision of § 113(f)(3)(B).

**II. The Consent Decree did not “resolve” liability because of the express disclaimer.**

Even if the Consent Decree were a CERCLA settlement, it did not “resolve” Guam’s liability. The D.C. Circuit looked to the *substance* of the obligations—paying a penalty, closing the dump, and designing a cover system—rather than the *express language* of the agreement. This approach contravenes the clear statement rule. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The Consent Decree explicitly states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” A disclaimer of liability is controlling. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). Other circuits have reached the same conclusion. *Asarco, L.P. v. Int’l Trade Comm’n*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (D.C. Cir. 2017); *Refined Metals Corp. v. United States*, 937 F.3d 925, 931 (D.C. Cir. 2019). The mere fact that Guam refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt the Consent Decree from the reach of § 113(f)(3)(B). *Refined Metals*, 937 F.3d at 931.

**III. The Statute of Limitations does not run from a non-CERCLA settlement.**

Even assuming arguendo that the Consent Decree qualified as a CERCLA settlement, the three-year statute of limitations for contribution actions did not begin to run until the EPA issued a “Record of Decision.” *United States v. City of New York*, 819 F.3d 5, 12 (2d Cir. 2016). The Consent Decree was entered in 2004, but the EPA did not issue a Record of Decision until 2008. Because the statute of limitations expired in 2011, Guam’s contribution claim is time-barred. CERCLA § 113(g)(3). Guam’s claim under § 107(a) remains the proper avenue for relief, as the district court correctly held.
