# ARGUMENT

## I. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Vests Title in the State Based on Navigability-in-Fact, a Functional Standard That the Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied

Under the equal-footing doctrine, a new State acquires title to the beds of rivers that are navigable at the time of statehood as an incident of sovereignty. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845). The touchstone of navigability for title purposes was fixed by this Court in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870), which held that rivers are "navigable in fact when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water." *Id.* at 563. That test asks a practical, factual question about the river's capacity to serve commerce—not a demand for scientific precision about conditions on the exact date a State was admitted.

Petitioner would convert this functional inquiry into an artificially rigid one, insisting that a court may consider only proof isolated to the instant of statehood and must parse a single, continuously used waterway into micro-segments wherever any natural obstruction required portage. Nothing in this Court's precedent compels that approach. The Montana Supreme Court properly held that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable highways of commerce at statehood, based on the full historical record of their use—precisely the kind of record this Court has always relied upon in equal-footing cases. *See Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931).

## II. Occasional Portages Around Natural Obstructions Do Not Defeat the Navigability of a River That Otherwise Functioned as a Highway of Commerce

Petitioner's principal argument is that the presence of Great Falls on the Missouri River, which required travelers to portage around the falls, means that segment of the River could never have been navigable for title purposes, regardless of the River's overall historical role as a commercial artery. This Court has already rejected that logic. In *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), the Court considered a river whose navigability was interrupted by natural impediments and nonetheless treated the waterway as a highway of commerce for purposes of federal regulatory authority, recognizing that a river does not lose its character as a navigable highway merely because portions of it required portage or improvement to be used continuously for trade.

That principle applies with equal force here. The historical record amply supported the trial court's finding that trappers, traders, and settlers used the Missouri River—including the Great Falls reach—as an essential link in a continuous highway of commerce, portaging goods around the falls precisely because the River on either side remained the indispensable route for transporting furs, supplies, and people. A rule that disaggregates a river into an unlimited number of discrete "stretches" whenever any natural obstacle appears would fracture the equal-footing inquiry beyond what *The Daniel Ball* and its progeny require, and would ignore the practical reality that portage has always been a "customary mode[] of trade and travel on water." 77 U.S. at 563.

## III. Evidence of Use Before and After the Precise Date of Statehood Is Properly Considered as Probative of Navigability-in-Fact at Statehood

Petitioner also contends that the trial court erred by considering evidence of river use that did not correspond to the literal moment Montana was admitted to the Union in 1889. But this Court has long recognized that navigability-for-title determinations necessarily rely on the surrounding historical record, because direct proof of conditions on a single date rarely exists. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court examined evidence of river use spanning decades before and after statehood to resolve navigability for title to the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers, recognizing that the character of a river as a highway of commerce is generally proven through a course of historical conduct rather than a snapshot. Similarly, in *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), the Court again looked to the broader pattern of use to determine navigability of the Great Salt Lake at statehood.

The Montana courts followed this settled methodology, relying on the affidavits, historical publications, and archival records documenting the Rivers' role in the fur trade and territorial commerce during the years surrounding 1889. That evidence—including public records and periodicals properly authenticated under Mont. R. Evid. 902 and 803(8)—was competent and directly probative of the Rivers' navigable character "at the time of statehood." Petitioner's insistence on excluding any evidence not perfectly contemporaneous with statehood would make the equal-footing inquiry nearly impossible to satisfy in any case involving nineteenth-century rivers, contrary to this Court's own practice in *United States v. Utah* and *Utah v. United States*.

## CONCLUSION

The constitutional test for navigability-for-title requires a functional assessment of whether a river served, or was susceptible of serving, as a highway of commerce at statehood—informed by the whole historical record and undefeated by occasional portages. The Montana Supreme Court applied that standard correctly, and its judgment should be affirmed.
