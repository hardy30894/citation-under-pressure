**ARGUMENT**

The District Court did not abuse its discretion in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3), and its determination that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis through common evidence was both legally sound and supported by the record. The Third Circuit correctly affirmed, and this Court should do likewise. The central question—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—must be answered in the affirmative when, as here, the district court conducts a rigorous, fact-based inquiry and finds that common evidence supports both antitrust impact and a common methodology for damages.

### I. The District Court Properly Applied the Predominance Standard Under Rule 23(b)(3)

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This is not a mere pleading standard; it demands a “rigorous analysis” of the evidence, including an examination of the merits where necessary to determine whether common issues predominate. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 317 (3d Cir. 2008). The Supreme Court has made clear that “a class certification decision may require some inquiry into the merits,” so long as the inquiry does not amount to a “full-blown merits trial.” *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, –U.S.–, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011). The District Court here conducted precisely such a limited inquiry, holding a four-day evidentiary hearing, reviewing 32 expert reports, and hearing live testimony from fact and expert witnesses. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). This was not a mini-trial, but a necessary and permissible pretrial assessment of whether common issues predominate.

The Court’s conclusion that common evidence could establish both antitrust impact and a common damages methodology was not only permissible but required. The plaintiffs alleged that Comcast’s clustering strategy—through acquisitions and system swaps—deterred overbuilder competition, thereby maintaining supra-competitive prices for non-basic cable programming. The District Court found that this theory of impact could be proven through common evidence: the market structure, the timing of the transactions, and the absence of overbuilder entry in the Philadelphia DMA. These are not individualized facts but systemic, class-wide conditions. As the Third Circuit correctly held, “the class has met its burden to demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class rather than individual to its members.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154.

### II. The District Court Properly Found a Common Methodology for Measuring Damages

Comcast argues that the absence of a single, uniform price increase across all class members precludes class-wide damages measurement. But this misapprehends the law. Rule 23 does not require that every class member suffered the same dollar amount of harm. It requires only that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis using a common methodology. *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 622 (1997) (“The class action device is not limited to cases in which every member of the class has suffered the same injury or the same amount of injury.”).

Here, Plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. James McClave, proposed a methodology based on a counterfactual market model: he compared actual prices in the Philadelphia DMA to what prices would have been in a competitive market, using data on pricing trends, subscriber behavior, and entry barriers. The District Court found this methodology “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154. The Court did not accept the model uncritically; it subjected it to rigorous scrutiny during the evidentiary hearing. The expert’s assumptions were tested, and the Court concluded that the model was sufficiently reliable and applicable across the class. This is precisely the kind of analysis Rule 23 demands.

Comcast’s reliance on *Dukes* is misplaced. In *Dukes*, the Court found that the plaintiffs could not show that the employer’s policies were applied in a way that could be proven on a class-wide basis. 131 S. Ct. at 2552. Here, by contrast, the plaintiffs presented a coherent, fact-based theory of market impact and a quantifiable damages model. The Court did not “resolve the merits” in the sense prohibited by *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156 (1974). Rather, it assessed whether the plaintiffs could prove their claims through common evidence—a task squarely within the district court’s discretion.

### III. The Third Circuit Correctly Applied the Abuse of Discretion Standard

The Third Circuit reviewed the District Court’s certification decision for abuse of discretion, a standard that “does not entitle a reviewing court to reverse the finding of the trier of fact simply because it is convinced that it would have decided the case differently.” *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 574 (1985). The Court found that the District Court’s factual findings were supported by the record and that its legal conclusions were sound. The Court did not err in concluding that the relevant geographic market was the Philadelphia DMA, a well-established market in antitrust law. *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 330, 337 (1962) (recognizing that geographic markets may be as small as a single metropolitan area). The Court also properly rejected Comcast’s argument that the market should be defined at the household level, which would render the concept of a “market” meaningless. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 160.
