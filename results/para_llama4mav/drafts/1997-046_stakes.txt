The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency process does not implicate a protected life or liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. As the Supreme Court held in Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), a state's clemency procedure does not create a constitutionally protected liberty interest. The Court reasoned that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464. The same principle applies here, as Ohio's clemency process is an executive function that is not subject to the same due process scrutiny as judicial proceedings.

Moreover, the fact that Ohio's clemency process is not an integral part of the adjudicatory process, unlike the appeal process at issue in Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), further supports the conclusion that it is not subject to the same level of due process protection. The Court in Evitts held that a state's appellate process must meet the demands of the Due Process Clause because it is an integral part of the adjudicatory process. Evitts, 469 U.S. at 393. In contrast, clemency proceedings are distinct from the adjudicatory process and are not entitled to the same level of due process protection.

Furthermore, the voluntary nature of the clemency interview does not violate Woodard's Fifth Amendment rights. As the Supreme Court held in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), a state's procedure does not compel a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination if it merely provides an opportunity for a prisoner to make statements that may be used against him in future proceedings. The Ohio Death Penalty Clemency Procedure, which allows a prisoner to request a personal interview with representatives of the parole board, does not require the prisoner to answer any questions or waive his Fifth Amendment rights. Therefore, Woodard's Fifth Amendment claim is without merit, and the district court's judgment should be affirmed.

The Court of Appeals' remand for further consideration of Woodard's Fifth Amendment claim is premature, as the risk of self-incrimination is speculative and may never materialize. As the Supreme Court held in Baxter, it is premature for a federal court to instruct a state on how to deal with situations that may never occur. Baxter, 425 U.S. at 324. In this case, Woodard has not yet participated in the clemency interview, and it is unclear whether he will make any incriminating statements. Therefore, the Court should reverse the Court of Appeals' judgment and affirm the district court's dismissal of Woodard's claims.
