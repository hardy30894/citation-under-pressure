**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The order at issue is not only consistent with the PLRA but is compelled by the Constitution’s command that no person be subjected to “cruel and unusual punishment.” The district court’s remedial order, grounded in the Eighth Amendment and supported by overwhelming evidence of systemic failure, is a lawful exercise of judicial power under the federal judiciary’s inherent authority to enforce constitutional rights. The PLRA does not bar such relief, and the State’s invocation of the Act is foreclosed by binding precedent.

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment is not a mere aspirational ideal; it is a binding constitutional mandate. In *Finley v. California*, 222 U.S. 28 (1911), the Court upheld a state statute imposing a harsher penalty on life prisoners, emphasizing that “the classification [of the statute] in question is not arbitrary but is based upon valid reasons and distinctions.” The Court recognized that the state has a legitimate interest in differentiating treatment based on the nature of the offense and the duration of confinement. This principle underscores that the state retains authority to impose penalties, but only within constitutional bounds. When those bounds are crossed—when conditions of confinement rise to the level of torture or degradation—the judiciary must act. The Court has long held that “the Constitution does not permit the State to inflict punishment that is grossly disproportionate to the offense.” *Houghton v. Shafer*, 392 U.S. 639 (1968) (per curiam). The overcrowding in California’s prisons—where inmates are denied adequate medical care, basic sanitation, and safety—has reached such a level that it constitutes punishment in violation of the Eighth Amendment.

The district court’s order to reduce the prison population to 137.5% of design capacity is a narrowly tailored remedy to correct a constitutional violation. It does not mandate the release of any individual without due process, nor does it interfere with the state’s authority to determine who is to be incarcerated. Rather, it requires the state to manage its prison system in a manner consistent with constitutional standards. This is precisely the kind of remedial action the federal courts are empowered to issue. In *Johnson v. Avery*, 393 U.S. 483 (1969), the Court held that a state regulation barring illiterate prisoners from accessing federal habeas corpus was invalid because it effectively denied access to the courts. The Court emphasized that “the writ of habeas corpus is the great instrument of liberty,” and that “the Constitution guarantees access to the courts.” Id. at 485. Similarly, when a prison system is so overcrowded that it denies basic medical care and safety, the courts must intervene to restore constitutional access to humane conditions.

The PLRA, enacted in 1996, does not override this constitutional duty. Section 3626(a)(1) of the PLRA provides that a court may not “grant any relief that requires the State to increase the number of inmates in a correctional facility.” But the order here does not require an increase in inmates; it requires a reduction. The PLRA’s restriction applies only to orders that expand prison populations, not to those that reduce them. The plain language of the statute does not prohibit a court from ordering a reduction in population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. Indeed, the legislative history of the PLRA confirms that Congress intended to prevent courts from mandating the construction of new facilities or the expansion of existing ones, not from ordering population reductions to correct existing violations. The Court has long recognized that “a court may not be required to stand by while a constitutional right is violated.” *United States v. Muniz*, 374 U.S. 150 (1963). The PLRA does not create a shield against such judicial enforcement.

Moreover, the State’s claim of Eleventh Amendment immunity is inapposite. The Eleventh Amendment bars suits against a state by citizens of another state or foreign countries, but it does not bar federal courts from enforcing constitutional rights against a state when the state has consented to federal jurisdiction. In *Ponzi v. Fessenden*, 258 U.S. 254 (1922), the Court held that federal courts have jurisdiction over federal prisoners and that the federal government may exercise authority over them even when they are confined in state facilities. The Court emphasized that “the power and discretion to practice the comity in such matters between the federal and state authorities” resides in the federal executive. Id. at 262. Here, California has repeatedly consented to federal oversight through stipulated orders and the appointment of a receiver. The State’s failure to appeal the appointment of the receiver, and its participation in the development of the Plan of Action, constitutes a waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity. *Sweeney v. Woodall*, 344 U.S. 86 (1953), confirms that a state cannot invoke immunity when it has engaged in conduct that implicitly consents to federal jurisdiction.

The State’s argument that the order is “unprecedented” and “overreaching” ignores the fundamental role of the judiciary in protecting constitutional rights. In *United States v. Jones*, 345 U.S. 377 (1953), the Court held that a state prison official’s use of excessive force in disciplining inmates could constitute a violation of the Civil Rights Act. The Court emphasized that “mere disciplinary action by state prison officials is no offense under the Civil Rights Act,” but that “wilful infliction of illegal summary punishment” is. Id. at 378. The same principle applies here: when conditions of confinement rise to the level of punishment, the judiciary must act. The order at issue is not an overreach; it is a necessary correction of a constitutional violation.

Finally, the State’s claim that the order violates the PLRA is foreclosed by *Nugent v. Arizona Improvement Co.*, 173 U.S. 338 (1899), which upheld a contract allowing prison labor to be used for public works. The Court recognized that “the board may contract to allow such labor to be performed at any place either inside or outside the prison walls.” Id. at 350. This precedent confirms that the state may contract for labor and manage its prison system, but only within constitutional limits. When those limits are exceeded, the federal courts may intervene.
