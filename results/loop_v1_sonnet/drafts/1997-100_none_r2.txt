# ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has explained that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal" and against multiple punishments for the same offense. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection is not a mere technicality of trial procedure; it is, as this Court held in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), a guaranty "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," binding on California with the same force it binds the federal government. The decision below—holding that this fundamental guaranty simply does not extend to noncapital sentence-enhancement proceedings—cannot be reconciled with the principles underlying this Court's double jeopardy decisions, none of which confines the Clause to the adjudication of guilt on the underlying substantive offense.

## I. A Finding That the Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove the Prior-Conviction Allegation Is the Functional Equivalent of an Acquittal and Forecloses Retrial.

The Court of Appeal held that the evidence failed to establish, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury in his prior offense. That ruling is indistinguishable in substance from a judgment of acquittal, and this Court has consistently treated such judgments as final regardless of whether the reasoning underlying them was correct. In United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), this Court held that a verdict of acquittal is final and "could not be reviewed, on error or otherwise, without putting [the defendant] twice in jeopardy, and thereby violating the Constitution." The Court reaffirmed that principle in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal bars further prosecution even where the trial court erred as a matter of law in reaching it. And in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court held that the government may not obtain appellate correction of an acquittal, because doing so would subject the defendant to a second jeopardy on the same charge.

These decisions rest on a principle of enduring force: once a tribunal charged with weighing the proof has found it insufficient, the government does not get a second opportunity to marshal better evidence. See Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575, 578-80 (1948) (a prior determination resolving an issue against the government may bar relitigation of that same issue in a later proceeding). Nothing in the nature of a sentence-enhancement allegation removes it from this rule. The consequence to the defendant of a successful enhancement—years added to a term of imprisonment—is no less severe than a conviction on a separate count, and the State's incentive to relitigate a losing issue is no less real.

## II. The Multiple-Punishment Component of the Clause Independently Bars Retrial.

The Double Jeopardy Clause also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 717; Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 168 (1874) (once a defendant has suffered lawful punishment, the Constitution forbids imposing a second, additional punishment for the same offense). That protection applies with full force to the enhancement allegation itself—an allegation that California's own procedures require the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt in an adversarial proceeding affording the rights of confrontation and cross-examination. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967) (due process requires that a defendant subject to a factfinding proceeding materially affecting the length of confinement be afforded the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them). Having failed once to carry that burden through a trial-like proceeding, the State should not be permitted to try again.

## III. Williams v. New York Does Not Sanction Repeated Adjudication of a Contested Factual Allegation.

The State may invoke Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), for the proposition that sentencing proceedings need not mirror a trial on guilt or innocence. But Williams addressed only whether due process required a hearing before a judge could rely on information gathered informally, outside a structured adversarial proceeding, in exercising sentencing discretion. Id. at 245-46. It did not hold, and this Court has never held, that a State may relitigate a specific, contested factual allegation—already tried once under the reasonable-doubt standard, with confrontation and cross-examination—simply because the label "sentencing" is attached to the proceeding. Where, as here, the State elects to structure an enhancement proceeding as a trial-like adjudication, it must accept the consequences of that choice. See Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (recognizing that double jeopardy principles are implicated by successive proceedings affecting the severity of punishment, even though finding no violation on the facts presented).

## IV. The Policies Underlying the Clause Confirm Its Application Here.

This Court has explained that "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity." Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957). That concern applies with equal force to a defendant twice hauled before a court to defend against the same prior-conviction allegation, particularly where the State's initial proof already failed a searching, trial-like adjudication. Having voluntarily structured its enhancement proceeding to mirror a trial on guilt or innocence, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend such a proceeding.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Court of Appeal's insufficiency ruling functions as an acquittal, and because the Double Jeopardy Clause's protections against successive prosecution and multiple punishment for the same offense are not confined to adjudication of the substantive charge, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
