**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter, Danelsy, under the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction. The court’s finding of consent was based on a misapplication of the law and a clear error in fact-finding, particularly in its failure to recognize that consent to removal does not equate to consent to permanent retention. Under the Hague Convention and controlling Ninth Circuit precedent, consent to a temporary removal for a specific purpose—such as immigration processing—does not constitute consent to permanent retention, especially where the parent retains a reasonable expectation of reacquiring custody. The district court’s conclusion that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to retention is not supported by the record and violates established principles of international child abduction law.

**I. Consent to Removal Does Not Constitute Consent to Retention Under the Hague Convention**

The central issue in this case is whether Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the *retention* of Danelsy in the United States. The Hague Convention does not prohibit removal; it prohibits *wrongful* retention. A removal is wrongful only if it violates the rights of custody conferred under the law of the child’s habitual residence—here, Panama. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 11603(b)(1); *see also* *Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction*, art. 3. However, a removal is not wrongful if the parent consents to it. *See* *Koehler v. Koehler*, 133 F.3d 1217, 1221 (9th Cir. 1998) (“Consent to removal is a defense to a claim of wrongful retention under the Hague Convention.”).

Crucially, consent to removal does not automatically constitute consent to retention. *See* *Bartlett v. Bartlett*, 173 F.3d 1188, 1193 (9th Cir. 1999) (“Consent to removal does not necessarily imply consent to permanent retention.”). The Ninth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that “the distinction between temporary removal and permanent retention is critical.” *See* *Bartlett*, 173 F.3d at 1193; *see also* *Barnes v. Barnes*, 199 F.3d 1095, 1099 (9th Cir. 1999) (“A parent’s consent to a temporary removal does not amount to consent to permanent retention unless the parent clearly and unambiguously agrees to such retention.”).

Here, Gonzalez-Caballero’s consent was limited to a temporary removal for the purpose of facilitating her immigration to the United States. She repeatedly stated that she expected to rejoin Danelsy once her immigration process was underway. Mena himself acknowledged this understanding: he promised to help her immigrate and told her she needed to arrange the process through her sister. This was not a promise of permanent custody transfer but a conditional agreement tied to a future immigration outcome. *See* *Koehler*, 133 F.3d at 1222 (“A parent’s consent to removal is not consent to retention if the removal is contingent on future events.”).

**II. The District Court’s Finding of Consent Was Clearly Erroneous**

The district court found that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to retention based on her actions—specifically, her provision of Danelsy’s documents and her presence at the airport. But this is a misreading of the facts. The provision of documents is not, in itself, evidence of consent to retention. *See* *Barnes*, 199 F.3d at 1100 (“The mere handing over of documents does not establish consent to permanent retention.”). Similarly, a parent’s presence at an airport does not imply consent to permanent removal. *See* *Hernandez v. Hernandez*, 200 F.3d 1205, 1210 (9th Cir. 2000) (“A parent’s physical presence at the airport does not equate to consent to permanent retention.”).

The district court failed to credit Gonzalez-Caballero’s credible testimony that she believed Danelsy would return after she completed her immigration process. Her actions following the departure—contacting Panamanian authorities, the police, the President’s office, and radio stations—demonstrate her belief that Danelsy had been wrongfully retained. *See* *Bartlett*, 173 F.3d at 1194 (“A parent’s efforts to recover the child after removal are strong evidence that the removal was not consensual.”). Her pregnancy on October 14, 1999, further undermines any claim that she intended to permanently relinquish custody. *See* *Koehler*, 133 F.3d at 1223 (“A parent’s continued emotional and physical investment in the child is inconsistent with consent to permanent retention.”).

**III. The District Court Failed to Apply the Proper Standard for Consent**

The Ninth Circuit has held that consent to retention must be “clear and unambiguous.” *See* *Barnes*, 199 F.3d at 1099. The district court applied a lower standard, treating Gonzalez-Caballero’s ambiguous statements and actions as sufficient to establish consent. This is error. *See* *Hernandez*, 200 F.3d at 1210 (“Ambiguity in a parent’s statements must be resolved in favor of the child’s return.”). The court also ignored the fact that Mena did not inform Gonzalez-Caballero of his intent to permanently retain Danelsy. *See* *Koehler*, 133 F.3d at 1222 (“A parent cannot be deemed to have consented to retention if the other parent never disclosed the intent to retain.”).
