**ARGUMENT**

Congress created a single, coherent path for federal employees who claim that a personnel action covered by the Civil Service Reform Act was infected by discrimination: review in federal district court, with a trial de novo of the discrimination claim. That the Board resolved petitioner's mixed case on jurisdictional rather than merits grounds does not remove it from that path. Ordinary canons of statutory construction confirm as much.

**A. The statutory text asks only what the employee alleges, not how the Board ruled.**

The controlling definition asks whether the employee "has been affected by an action which he may appeal to the [Board]" and "alleges" prohibited discrimination. It does not ask whether the Board ultimately agreed that it possessed jurisdiction to hear the appeal. Where "the language is plain," this Court's "sole function" is "to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The statute is written from the vantage of the employee's allegations, not the Board's eventual disposition, and nothing in it conditions the district-court track on the Board's own ruling.

**B. A bifurcated scheme would produce the kind of anomalous, fragmented review this Court has long refused to impute to Congress.**

Even if the text admitted doubt, the consequences of splitting review would resolve it. This Court refuses to read statutes literally when doing so yields "absurd or futile results," preferring instead to effectuate the evident statutory purpose. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). It is likewise a "familiar rule" that "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit." *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). A rule sending jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit but identical merits dismissals of the same claim to district court would fracture single controversies, invite disputes over characterization, and force employees to relitigate the same discrimination allegations in two forums applying different procedures. That is precisely the sort of piecemeal appellate structure this Court has resisted absent a clear statutory command dividing review of a unitary proceeding. *See Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949) (declining to construe a review statute to multiply appeals and scatter related issues across separate proceedings).

**C. Doubt should be resolved in favor of preserving, not fracturing, meaningful review.**

Statutes providing judicial review of administrative action carry a presumption favoring the availability and coherence of that review; courts do not lightly construe them to divide or diminish it. *Stark v. Wickard*, 321 U.S. 288, 309 (1944). This Court applied that presumption in *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48 (1955), refusing to read a general review provision to displace the ordinary avenue of relief absent a clear expression of contrary congressional intent. The same logic applies here: nothing in the Act clearly assigns jurisdictional mixed-case dismissals to a different court than merits mixed-case dismissals, and the Court should not supply that division by inference.

**D. The Board's own characterization of its jurisdiction should not dictate the forum for reviewing that very characterization.**

This Court held in *Leedom v. Kyne*, 358 U.S. 184 (1958), that an agency's assertion of the scope of its own authority does not insulate that assertion from ordinary judicial scrutiny, particularly where the ruling forecloses the very relief the statute was designed to provide. The same principle counsels against letting the Board's jurisdictional ruling — itself resting on an assessment of whether petitioner's retirement was coerced by discrimination — dictate that review must proceed in a forum without authority to try that discrimination claim at all. This Court has long insisted that the jurisdictional facts underlying an agency's authority to act remain subject to full, independent judicial examination rather than truncated review. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 54–55 (1932). Treating a jurisdictional dismissal as categorically different from a merits dismissal for purposes of selecting the reviewing court inverts that principle: it gives the Board's threshold label controlling weight over the forum in which the correctness of that label — and the discrimination claim entangled with it — will be tested.

**E. The voluntariness and discrimination inquiries here are inseparable.**

The Board's jurisdictional ruling rested on whether petitioner's suspension and retirement were "voluntary," a determination inseparable from his allegation that the settlement was coerced by disability and age discrimination. Requiring petitioner to seek review of that intertwined determination in the Federal Circuit — which cannot adjudicate discrimination claims — would sever from one controversy the very questions Congress meant tried together in district court.

For these reasons, the Board's dismissal, though styled as jurisdictional, disposed of a mixed case within the meaning of the statute, and review lies in the district court.
