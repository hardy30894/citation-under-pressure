# ARGUMENT

## I. SPECIFIC JURISDICTION DOES NOT REQUIRE THAT THE DEFENDANT'S FORUM CONTACTS BE THE CAUSE OF THE PLAINTIFF'S INJURY

Ford asks this Court to graft a strict causation requirement onto the Due Process Clause, transforming the settled "arise out of or relate to" standard into a rule that would bar jurisdiction whenever a defendant's forum contacts did not directly cause the plaintiff's injury. That argument cannot be squared with this Court's precedents, which have long recognized that a defendant's extensive, purposeful activity within a State may support jurisdiction over claims connected to that activity even absent strict but-for causation.

### A. The Due Process Clause Asks Whether the Defendant Purposefully Availed Itself of the Forum, Not Whether the Forum Contacts Caused the Injury

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction is "whether the defendant purposefully established 'minimum contacts' in the forum State." *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 474 (1985) (quoting *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945)). This inquiry focuses on the defendant's own conduct—whether the defendant has engaged in activity by which it "purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." *Id.* (quoting *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)).

Critically, this Court has framed the ultimate question as one of foreseeability regarding the *litigation*, not causation regarding the *injury*: "the foreseeability that is critical to due process analysis... is that the defendant's conduct and connection with the forum State are such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there." *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 474 (quoting *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980)). Ford, which sold, marketed, and serviced thousands of Explorers in Montana for decades, cannot credibly claim it did not anticipate being haled into Montana court to answer for alleged defects in that very model of vehicle.

This Court's decision in *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915 (2011), confirms that specific jurisdiction depends on an "affiliatio[n] between the forum and the underlying controversy," not on strict causation. *Id.* at 919 (quoting von Mehren & Trautman, *Jurisdiction To Adjudicate: A Suggested Analysis*, 79 Harv. L. Rev. 1121, 1136 (1966)). The Court explained that specific jurisdiction is properly "confined to adjudication of 'issues deriving from, or connected with, the very controversy that establishes jurisdiction.'" *Id.* (quoting von Mehren & Trautman, at 1136). The word "relate to" in this formulation is disjunctive from "arise out of," and this Court should give it independent meaning. Ford's proposed causation test would collapse "relate to" entirely into "arise out of," rendering half of this Court's own standard surplusage.

Notably, *Goodyear* denied jurisdiction not because the plaintiffs' claims failed a causation test, but because the foreign subsidiaries there had *no relevant forum contacts whatsoever*—the accident, manufacture, and sale all occurred abroad, and North Carolina courts therefore "lacked specific jurisdiction to adjudicate the controversy" for want of any forum connection at all. *Id.* at 921 (citing *Brown v. Meter*, 199 N.C. App. 50, 57-58 (2009)). That is a fundamentally different case from this one, where Ford has cultivated an enormous, continuous business presence in Montana—advertising, selling, and servicing the very make and model of vehicle at issue.

### B. This Court's Motor-Vehicle Jurisdiction Cases Confirm That States May Regulate and Adjudicate Claims Involving Vehicles Operating Within Their Borders

This Court's early jurisprudence recognized that States possess especially strong authority to assert jurisdiction over out-of-state actors whose vehicles operate on state roads, precisely because of the state interest in regulating the safety of its highways. In *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court upheld Massachusetts's nonresident-motorist statute, recognizing that the State's "power to regulate the use of its highways" justified subjecting nonresidents to suit for accidents occurring on those highways. That principle—that a State has a substantial interest in and connection to litigation involving vehicles operating within its borders—applies with even greater force here, where Ford is not a mere passing motorist but has systematically cultivated a market for the very vehicle model at issue throughout Montana for years.

Ford's reliance on *Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad*, 346 U.S. 338 (1953), is misplaced. That case addressed venue under the federal diversity statute, not the constitutional limits of due process. Nothing in the case suggests that jurisdiction premised on a defendant's connection to in-state vehicle operations offends due process; to the contrary, the case assumes that nonresident-motorist jurisdiction is constitutionally unproblematic.

## II. FORD'S EXTENSIVE, CONTINUOUS BUSINESS ACTIVITY IN MONTANA SATISFIES DUE PROCESS UNDER THE "RELATE TO" PRONG OF SPECIFIC JURISDICTION

Ford's contacts with Montana are not "random," "fortuitous," or "attenuated." *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475 (quoting *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 299). Ford has deliberately cultivated a substantial market in Montana for Ford Explorers—advertising them, selling them through Montana dealerships, and servicing them through authorized Montana repair networks. Gullett's suit alleges a design defect in precisely the type of vehicle Ford purposefully marketed and serviced within the state. This is not the case of an isolated contact bearing no relationship to the claims; it is a case where Ford's systematic in-state business directly relates to the product and injury at issue.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that Ford, "by advertising and selling parts within the state of Montana," had availed itself of the privilege of conducting business there and was properly subject to jurisdiction over claims connected to that business. Nothing in this Court's precedents forecloses that conclusion; indeed, *Burger King*'s purposeful-availment framework compels it.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
