**ARGUMENT**

The 2004 consent decree did not resolve Guam’s liability to the United States for a response action within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Guam therefore retains its right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a), 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a), which carries a six-year limitations period that has not expired.

Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” The statute’s plain text requires an actual resolution of liability. See Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) (describing the provision as creating an “express” contribution right “following” settlement). The 2004 decree contains an express disclaimer stating that it was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. That language precludes any conclusion that liability was resolved. See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (emphasizing that CERCLA liability determinations turn on the precise terms of the parties’ agreement).

The decree also arose exclusively under the Clean Water Act, 33 U.S.C. §§ 1251 et seq., and addressed only “discharges of untreated leachate.” CWA Compl. ¶ 29, J.A. 86. It imposed no obligations under CERCLA and made no determination that any “hazardous substance” within the meaning of CERCLA § 101(14) had been released. Because § 113(f)(3)(B) applies only to settlements resolving CERCLA liability, a Clean Water Act decree cannot trigger the contribution right. See United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA’s liability scheme is limited to “hazardous substances” as defined by the statute).

Even if the decree had addressed CERCLA matters, its substantive provisions required Guam only to design and install a cover system and to pay a civil penalty; they did not adjudicate or concede any proportionate share of responsibility. As this Court has recognized, “the mere fact that [a party] refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt [a consent] [d]ecree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B)” only when the decree’s operative terms otherwise demonstrate resolution. Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Indus., Inc., 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019). Here the operative terms do not.

Because the decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s § 107(a) claim remains available and timely. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
