## ARGUMENT

### I. The First Circuit Correctly Upheld the Massachusetts Buffer Zone Statute as a Content-Neutral, Narrowly Tailored Time-Place-Manner Regulation

The Massachusetts statute, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E ½, is a content-neutral regulation of speech in a quintessential public forum. It is therefore subject to intermediate scrutiny under *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The law is content-neutral because it “applies to all persons who ‘knowingly enter or remain’ within the buffer zone, regardless of the message they seek to convey.” *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000). It does not target “any viewpoint or any message” but instead “merely establishes a minor geographic limitation on where speech may occur.” *Id.* at 719-20.

The statute serves significant governmental interests: ensuring public safety, preserving access to healthcare facilities, and protecting patients and staff from harassment, obstruction, and intimidation. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715 (recognizing “the State’s interest in protecting the health and safety of its citizens” and “ensuring free access to healthcare facilities”). The buffer zone is narrowly tailored to serve these interests. It is only 35 feet—far smaller than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill*—and it is limited to the immediate vicinity of entrances, exits, and driveways. The law leaves open ample alternative channels of communication: petitioners may speak, pray, display signs, distribute literature, and engage in face-to-face counseling from just outside the buffer zone. As the Court has held, “the First Amendment does not guarantee the right to communicate one’s views at all times and places or in any manner that may be desired.” *Heffron v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647 (1981).

Petitioners argue that the buffer zone prevents “close personal contact” with their intended audience, but the First Amendment does not require the government to “compel a listener to attend to the speaker’s message.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726. The statute does not “prevent petitioners from engaging in any expressive activity” but merely “shifts the location of that activity.” *Id.* at 727. Indeed, petitioners have demonstrated that they can and do communicate effectively from outside the zone: they have persuaded “[a]pproximately eighty women” to refrain from seeking abortions. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 174 (1st Cir. 2009).

The law is not a prior restraint; it is a fixed, geographic restriction that “does not authorize the government to prohibit speech in advance.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 735. It is also not vague or overbroad. The statute clearly defines the buffer zone and exempts four categories of persons, including those entering or leaving the facility and those using the sidewalk to reach a destination other than the facility. These exemptions ensure that the law does not “close off all means of communication.” *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 295 (1984).

### II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls This Case and Should Not Be Overruled

*Hill v. Colorado* is directly on point. There, the Court upheld a Colorado statute that created a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, with a narrower 8-foot “no-approach” zone within it. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 707. The Court held that the statute was a “content-neutral, narrowly tailored regulation of the time, place, and manner of protected speech” that served “the State’s interest in protecting the health and safety of its citizens.” *Id.* at 715, 725. The Massachusetts statute is even more narrowly drawn: it is only 35 feet, applies to all speakers equally, and does not include a separate “no-approach” provision.

The principle of *stare decisis* demands that the Court adhere to *Hill* absent “a special justification” for overruling it. *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey*, 505 U.S. 833, 864 (1992). No such justification exists. *Hill* was a 6-3 decision, and the Court has repeatedly reaffirmed its core holding that buffer zones around healthcare facilities are permissible time-place-manner regulations. *See, e.g., Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994) (upholding a 36-foot buffer zone); *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 377 (1997) (upholding a 15-foot buffer zone). The Court has not overruled *Hill* in any subsequent case, and the factual context of abortion-clinic protests has not changed in any constitutionally significant way.

Petitioners ask this Court to limit or overrule *Hill*, but they offer no doctrinal or empirical reason to do so. *Hill* remains good law, and it squarely supports the Massachusetts statute. The First Circuit therefore correctly applied *Hill* and held that the statute is constitutional.
