**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE TITLE VII AND MHRA SEX DISCRIMINATION CLAIMS BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF DISCRIMINATION.

To survive summary judgment on a claim of sex discrimination under Title VII and the Missouri Human Rights Act (MHRA), a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case by showing: (1) she is a member of a protected class; (2) she was qualified for her position; (3) she suffered an adverse employment action; and (4) the adverse action occurred under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973); *Harris v. St. Louis County*, 105 F.3d 1170, 1174 (8th Cir. 1997). Here, Feltmann fails on the fourth prong—she has not shown that the adverse actions occurred under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination.

Feltmann’s claim rests entirely on a series of performance-based decisions made by her supervisor, Bob Rich, in response to declining sales and poor profit margins. The record shows that Rich’s concerns were not based on gender but on objective, measurable performance metrics. Feltmann’s average gross profit per vehicle sold from December 1992 through April 1993 was the lowest among all consultants—a fact that Rich testified was due to her failure to adequately explain vehicle features, a sales tactic directly tied to pricing and profitability. This is not a gender-neutral performance issue; it is a business decision grounded in financial results. See *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 105 F.3d 1163, 1167 (7th Cir. 1997) (holding that “a decision based on performance, even if it disproportionately affects one gender, does not constitute unlawful discrimination if the decision is based on legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons”).

Moreover, Feltmann’s own conduct undermines any inference of discrimination. She was placed on probation in March 1993 alongside Rick Beutel, a male consultant, for low sales in January and February—two months in which Feltmann’s sales were below average. Yet by March, Feltmann sold more vehicles than all but one other consultant, demonstrating her ability to rebound. The fact that she was not selected for the Chicago sales competition is not evidence of discrimination. Rich offered the opportunity to the two top-performing consultants at Plaza Infiniti, who declined. When they declined, Rich did not extend the offer to Feltmann or any other male consultant who expressed interest. This decision was based on performance and availability, not gender. See *Barnes*, 105 F.3d at 1167 (no inference of discrimination where selection was based on performance and not gender).

Feltmann’s claim that she was singled out for restrictions on demo car use is similarly unsupported. Rich issued a personal memo restricting demo car use to the St. Louis area due to rising costs and concerns about misuse. Feltmann was the only employee to receive such a memo, but the record shows that other consultants, including males, occasionally drove their demo cars outside the area. Feltmann’s continued use of the vehicle for a 300-mile round trip to Marion, Illinois, despite the restriction, was a violation of company policy. The fact that no other employee received a written warning does not establish discrimination—only that Feltmann’s conduct was more egregious or persistent. See *Barnes*, 105 F.3d at 1167 (a plaintiff cannot establish discrimination merely by showing that others were treated more leniently unless she proves that the differential treatment was based on gender).

Finally, Feltmann’s claim that she was retaliated against for reporting Mike Barnstead’s comment about Gordon Anzalone fails because the alleged retaliatory acts—memo about work ethic, probation, and exclusion from competition—preceded or were contemporaneous with her complaint, not after. The memo dated January 4, 1993, was issued before Feltmann reported the incident, and the probation was based on sales performance in January and February—months prior to her complaint. See *Barnes*, 105 F.3d at 1168 (retaliation claim fails where adverse action predates protected activity). The district court erred in concluding that these actions were retaliatory when they were based on legitimate, non-discriminatory business reasons.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE RETALIATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A CAUSAL CONNECTION BETWEEN HER COMPLAINT AND THE ALLEGED ADVERSE ACTIONS.

To establish retaliation under Title VII and MHRA, a plaintiff must show: (1) she engaged in protected activity; (2) she suffered an adverse employment action; and (3) a causal connection exists between the two. *Barnes*, 105 F.3d at 1168. Feltmann’s protected activity—reporting Anzalone’s comment—occurred in November 1992. Yet the first adverse action, the January 4 memo, was issued before she reported the incident. The probation in March 1993 was based on performance in January and February—months before the complaint. The exclusion from the Chicago competition occurred after the decision to offer it was made, and no evidence suggests that the decision was influenced by her complaint.

The district court’s conclusion that these actions were retaliatory is unsupported by the record. The timing of the actions, the business justifications, and the lack of any contemporaneous evidence of animus all weigh against a finding of retaliation. See *Barnes*, 105 F.3d at 1168 (no causal connection where adverse action predates protected activity). The court’s reliance on Feltmann’s subjective belief that she was retaliated against is insufficient. See *Harris*, 105 F.3d at 1175 (subjective belief alone does not create a triable issue of fact).
