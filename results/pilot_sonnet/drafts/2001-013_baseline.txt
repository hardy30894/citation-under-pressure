# ARGUMENT

## OSHA HAS JURISDICTION TO ENFORCE THE ACT ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED STATUTORY AUTHORITY TO REGULATE THE WORKING CONDITIONS AT ISSUE HERE

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction only where another federal agency has actually "exercise[d] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court's precedent construing that provision, and the structure of the maritime safety statutes themselves, demonstrate that the Fifth Circuit's categorical exemption for all vessels—inspected and uninspected alike—cannot stand.

### A. Section 4(b)(1) Requires a Vessel-Specific, Regulation-Specific Inquiry

This Court has already construed the operative language of Section 4(b)(1) in an analogous statute. In *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling*, as in the companion context addressed by this Court in construing OSHA's preemption clause, the touchstone is whether the other agency has *actually* exercised authority over the *particular* working condition at issue—not whether the other agency merely *could* regulate the industry in the abstract. The statutory text codifies this functional approach: Section 4(b)(1) refers to "working conditions" being addressed by regulations "affecting occupational safety or health," language that operates regulation-by-regulation rather than industry-by-industry.

The Coast Guard's own regulatory scheme confirms this reading. Congress drew a sharp statutory distinction between "inspected vessels," which are subject to comprehensive Coast Guard oversight under 46 U.S.C. § 3301 et seq., and "uninspected vessels," for which Coast Guard authority is deliberately limited. As the Fifth Circuit itself acknowledged, the Coast Guard's authority over uninspected vessels under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 extends only to discrete equipment items—fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation systems, and signaling devices. The court of appeals catalogued these narrow categories yet nonetheless concluded that this circumscribed authority somehow displaces OSHA's comprehensive jurisdiction over the entire universe of workplace safety hazards, including the well-control and blowout-prevention procedures at issue here. That conclusion inverts the statutory design: Congress could not have intended that a handful of equipment-specific Coast Guard regulations covering life jackets and fire extinguishers would immunize an entire industry from OSHA's comprehensive safety mandate covering drilling operations, electrical systems, machine guarding, and the hundreds of other hazards addressed by the Act's standards.

### B. The Fifth Circuit's Reliance on *Clary* and *Donovan* Is Misplaced

The Fifth Circuit rested its holding on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). Neither decision supports extending exemption to uninspected vessels. *Clary* held only that "OSHA regulations ... do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," 609 F.2d at 1122, in the context of a tort suit where the plaintiff sought to introduce OSHA's deck-marking regulation against a backdrop of comprehensive Coast Guard inspection authority. The *Clary* court never confronted—because the case did not present—the question whether the same result follows where the Coast Guard has issued no regulation addressing the hazard at issue.

*Donovan* reinforces rather than undermines this distinction. This Court there explained that the rationale for exemption was to avoid "the anomaly ... of steaming in and out of OSHA coverage" and to ensure "a single, uniform set of rules" governs "the maritime workplace." 720 F.2d at 829. But no such uniform Coast Guard regime exists for uninspected vessels with respect to blowout prevention, well-control procedures, or the myriad other hazards addressed by OSHA's general industry standards. Where the Coast Guard has not promulgated a "comprehensive" scheme, *id.* at 826, the animating concern of *Donovan*—preventing seamen from "steaming in and out" of overlapping regulatory regimes—simply does not arise, because there is no competing regime to create the anomaly.

### C. The Fifth Circuit's Rule Creates a Regulatory Vacuum Inconsistent with the Act's Remedial Purpose

The court of appeals' holding leaves an entire category of maritime workers—those on uninspected vessels performing inherently dangerous oil and gas exploration work—without meaningful federal safety protection. This Court has recognized that the OSH Act is a broad remedial statute designed "to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions." 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). Construing Section 4(b)(1) to create a categorical exemption based on an employee's technical status as a "seaman," regardless of whether any federal agency has actually regulated the hazard causing injury, contravenes that purpose and cannot be reconciled with the statute's exemption provision, which by its terms operates only where another agency's authority has been affirmatively exercised.

The explosion that killed and injured Mallard's employees arose from well-control failures—hazards the Coast Guard has not regulated for uninspected vessels. Because the Coast Guard exercised no statutory authority over the working conditions that caused this tragedy, Section 4(b)(1)'s exemption does not apply, and OSHA's citation was properly issued.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
