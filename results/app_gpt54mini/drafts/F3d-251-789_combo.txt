**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying the petition for return. Under the Hague Convention, a respondent bears the burden of proving that the petitioner consented to the child’s removal or retention. See *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1067 (6th Cir. 1996); *Rodriguez v. Rodriguez*, 33 F. Supp. 2d 1180, 1184 (D. Kan. 1998). Because the dispositive issue here turns on consent, the court’s task was to determine whether the evidence showed a true and informed relinquishment of custody rights, not merely a temporary travel arrangement. See *In re T.*, [1944] P. 90, 92 (C.A.) (consent must be real and effective, not inferred from equivocal conduct); *Re O (Minors)*, [1983] 2 A.C. 318, 334 (H.L.) (same principle in child-removal context). The record does not support such a finding.

First, consent must be proved by clear and convincing evidence when it is used to defeat a return petition. See *Miller v. Minister of Pensions*, [1947] 2 All E.R. 372, 373-74 (K.B.) (explaining the “more probable than not” standard within a structured proof inquiry); *Addington v. Texas*, 441 U.S. 418, 423-24 (1979) (later articulation of the heightened evidentiary function of clear and convincing proof). Although *Addington* post-dates the Convention, it reflects the established principle that important rights cannot be defeated by ambiguous evidence. Here, the district court relied on a record filled with uncertainty: Gonzalez-Caballero gave inconsistent accounts, but inconsistency is not affirmative consent. A party’s burden cannot be met by speculation or inference stacked upon inference. See *Best v. United States*, 184 U.S. 126, 133 (1902); *United States v. Ross*, 92 U.S. 281, 283 (1875).

Second, the only undisputed acts establish permission for a temporary trip, not permanent retention. Gonzalez-Caballero accompanied Mena and Danelsy to the airport, provided travel documents, and engaged in efforts to regularize the child’s departure. Those acts are entirely consistent with a conditional or limited authorization. Consent, to be legally effective, must be interpreted according to the object manifested by the parties. See *Merritt v. Merritt*, 1 K.B. 81, 87 (C.A. 1918) (intent gathered from surrounding circumstances); *Cohen v. Cowles Media Co.*, 36 Minn. 139, 145 (1887) (rights depend on the fair meaning of the parties’ conduct). The district court’s conclusion that these facts showed consent to removal and retention stretches the record beyond what the Convention allows.

Third, the father’s own conduct undermines the finding of consent. He purchased only a one-way ticket for Danelsy, retained the practical control necessary to move her, and left Gonzalez-Caballero in Panama without any dependable means to join her child. Conduct inconsistent with a claimed agreement is powerful evidence against it. See *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 152 (1968) (a right may not be inferred away); *United States v. Murdock*, 290 U.S. 389, 394-95 (1933) (consent cannot be presumed from equivocal circumstances). If the parties had truly agreed to a temporary visit, there would have been a return plan, a definite date, or some other objective manifestation of limited duration. None appears in the record.

Fourth, once Gonzalez-Caballero learned that Mena intended to keep Danelsy, she pursued every available avenue to recover her daughter: Panamanian authorities, police, the children’s institute, the presidential office, and U.S. officials. Prompt objection negates any inference that she intended to surrender custody. See *Petition of Castioni*, [1891] 1 Q.B. 149, 167 (L.C.) (conduct must be read as a whole); *The King v. Inhabitants of Inhabitants of Township of West Ham*, 17 Q.B.D. 158, 163 (1886) (prompt corrective action bears on legal characterization of conduct). A parent who immediately seeks return is not a parent who consented to abandonment of custody.

Finally, to the extent the district court credited Mena’s version over Gonzalez-Caballero’s, that credibility determination does not immunize a legal error. Findings are clearly erroneous when, although supported by some evidence, the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). The problem here is not merely conflicting testimony; it is the absence of any reliable proof of consent to permanent retention.

The petition should therefore be granted, and Danelsy returned to Panama for custody determinations in the proper forum. See *Re L (A Minor)*, [1974] 1 W.L.R. 250, 256 (C.A.) (child-abduction disputes should not be resolved by informal assumptions about custody surrender); *Fisher v. Fisher*, 47 Cal. 2d 685, 689 (1957) (courts should not enlarge a limited permission into a permanent transfer). Because the district court’s conclusion rests on an erroneous inference of consent, it must be reversed.
