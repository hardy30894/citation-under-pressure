ARGUMENT

The Fourteenth Amendment affords no independent right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate mens rea when a state confines such evidence to its insanity defense. Due process requires only that criminal proceedings conform to fundamental principles of liberty and justice. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934); Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Arizona’s limitation satisfies that standard.

States possess broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the scope of affirmative defenses. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895); Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 417 (1902). In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), this Court upheld a requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, recognizing that the allocation of burdens concerning mental condition lies within legislative discretion. The same principle permits Arizona to channel mental-condition evidence exclusively to that defense.

The right to offer testimony is not absolute. Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 592 (1900); Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 110 (1908). A defendant must still demonstrate that the excluded evidence is essential to a fair opportunity to defend. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932). Expert testimony offered solely to show inability to form intent is not essential when the state has already provided an insanity defense that fully acquits upon proof of mental disease. Matheson v. United States, 227 U.S. 540, 543 (1913).

Nor does the Clause incorporate every evidentiary rule that might benefit the accused. Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942). Arizona’s rule rationally prevents the piecemeal dismantling of mens rea through psychiatric opinion while preserving the traditional insanity acquittal. Because the rule is neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair, it comports with due process. Snyder, 291 U.S. at 105.

The judgment should be affirmed.
