**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below erred in holding that the Merit Systems Protection Board’s dismissal of petitioner’s mixed case for lack of jurisdiction must be reviewed in the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. Under the Civil Service Reform Act, a case in which an employee “has been affected by an action which he may appeal to the Board” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination” is a mixed case, and judicial review lies in federal district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Board’s jurisdictional ruling does not alter the nature of the case as pleaded. This Court has long held that jurisdiction is determined by the complaint’s allegations, not by the agency’s ultimate findings. *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 681–82 (1946); *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Petitioner’s complaint alleged an involuntarily accepted suspension and retirement—actions clearly appealable to the Board under 5 U.S.C. § 7512—and alleged that those actions were motivated by discrimination. That is a mixed case, and the Board’s subsequent jurisdictional determination cannot retroactively strip petitioner of the district court review Congress provided.

The distinction the lower court drew between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals lacks any statutory foundation. The plain language of § 7702(a)(1) focuses on the action affecting the employee, not on whether the Board ultimately deems the appeal procedurally proper or within its subject-matter jurisdiction. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542–43 (1940) (courts must give effect to Congress’s plain meaning). A dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not make the underlying action any less “appealable” as a matter of law; the employee’s right to appeal is determined by the nature of the action, not by the Board’s decision on the facts supporting jurisdiction. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 50 (1932) (jurisdictional facts are subject to independent judicial determination). Here, petitioner’s suspension and retirement were adverse actions that fell squarely within the Board’s statutory jurisdiction. That the Board later found the settlement voluntary does not change the character of the action for purposes of the mixed-case exception. Jurisdiction is “the power to decide a case on the merits,” *Bell*, 327 U.S. at 683, not the outcome of a factual inquiry.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s exclusive jurisdiction over non-mixed Board decisions, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), must be construed in light of Congress’s intent to preserve district court review for discrimination claims. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and related statutes establish a strong presumption that federal employees alleging discrimination may seek redress in district court. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (discrimination suits are “within the scope of federally protected rights”). This Court has long recognized that discrimination claims require “a full and fair hearing” in a court capable of granting broad equitable relief. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155–56 (1908). The Federal Circuit, by contrast, has jurisdiction limited to civil service matters and lacks the remedial authority of district courts in Title VII cases. Congress could not have intended that a Board ruling on its own jurisdiction—often intertwined with the merits of a discrimination claim—would foreclose the very forum it created to vindicate those rights.

The lower court’s reliance on the voluntariness issue as a jurisdictional bar conflates a merits defense with a jurisdictional prerequisite. Voluntariness is a factual determination that often overlaps with the discrimination claim itself—an employee coerced into retirement due to discriminatory animus would have an involuntary action that is both a Board-jurisdictional adverse action and a discrimination claim. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (administrative findings on factual issues are subject to review under substantial evidence standard). When the Board dismisses for lack of jurisdiction based on a factual finding that the action was voluntary, it has effectively resolved the merits of the coercion defense. The proper venue for reviewing that factual determination, especially when discrimination is alleged, is the district court, which can take evidence and make independent findings. *Crowell*, 285 U.S. at 54 (constitutional facts require de novo review).

Allowing the Federal Circuit to review jurisdictional dismissals would create an anomalous scheme: employees whose procedural defects are excused would go to district court, while employees who lose on a jurisdictional fact would be confined to the Federal Circuit—even though both may allege identical discrimination claims. Such a result is arbitrary and without support in the statute’s text. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 143 (1803) (courts must give consistent meaning to statutory jurisdiction). Congress enacted the mixed-case provisions to ensure that discrimination claims are decided by district courts, not the Federal Circuit. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The Board’s decision to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction does not render the case any less a mixed case for judicial review purposes.

Finally, this Court has instructed that statutes providing for judicial review of administrative action should be liberally construed in favor of the party seeking review. *FCC v. Sanders Bros. Radio Station*, 309 U.S. 470, 475 (1940). The lower court’s cramped reading of § 7702(a)(1) eviscerates the very protection Congress intended for federal employees alleging discrimination. Petitioner filed a mixed case; he is entitled to a district court’s review.

The petition for review should be transferred to the appropriate federal district court.
