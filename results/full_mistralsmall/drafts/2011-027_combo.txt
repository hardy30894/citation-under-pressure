**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams under the equal footing doctrine only if the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time Montana achieved statehood in 1889. *See United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“The title thus vested in the State is subject to the paramount power of the United States over navigable waters.”); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 50 (1894) (“The title of the State to the beds of navigable waters within her limits is subject to the public right of navigation and the incidental rights of the riparian owners.”). This Court has long recognized that the equal footing doctrine does not confer title to riverbeds unless the river was navigable at statehood. *See Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876) (“The question whether a river is navigable for the purpose of vesting title in the State at the time of her admission into the Union is determined by the condition of the river at that time.”).

The test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was capable of supporting interstate or foreign commerce at the time of statehood. *See The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870) (“Those rivers must be regarded as public navigable rivers in law which are navigable in fact. And they are navigable in fact when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.”). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the navigability inquiry is historical and fact-specific, focusing on the river’s condition at the time of statehood. *See United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) (“The test of navigability is not whether the stream is navigable at all seasons of the year, or at all stages of the water, but whether it is susceptible of being used as a highway of commerce.”); *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926) (“The true test of navigability is not the occasional or even frequent use of a stream for commercial purposes, but whether it is susceptible of being used as a highway of commerce.”).

PPL’s argument that the trial court should have considered post-statehood evidence of navigability is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. *See Barney*, 94 U.S. at 338 (“The question whether a river is navigable for the purpose of vesting title in the State at the time of her admission into the Union is determined by the condition of the river at that time.”); *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 50 (“The title of the State to the beds of navigable waters within her limits is subject to the public right of navigation and the incidental rights of the riparian owners.”). The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this settled rule, relying on historical evidence to determine the navigability of the rivers at the time of statehood. *See Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (recognizing that historical evidence may be used to establish navigability at statehood).

**II. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Ability to Seek Compensation for the Use of State-Owned Riverbeds.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Federal Power Act (FPA) does not preempt the State’s ability to seek compensation for PPL’s use of state-owned riverbeds. *See First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946) (“The Federal Power Act establishes a dual system of control between the federal and state governments, preserving the role of state law with regard to proprietary rights.”). This Court has long recognized that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for compensation for the use of state-owned lands. *See Public Utility Dist. No. 1 of Pend Oreille County v. Washington Dept. of Ecology*, 976 F.2d 1519, 1525 (9th Cir. 1992) (citing *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76), *aff’d*, 509 U.S. 906 (1993).

The FPA’s licensing scheme explicitly contemplates that state law will govern the use of state-owned lands. *See First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 161 n.6 (noting that the FPA requires applicants to demonstrate compliance with state law regarding “the beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water for power purposes”); *see also* 16 U.S.C. § 802 (1946) (requiring applicants to show compliance with state law). This Court has held that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for compensation for the use of state-owned lands. *See Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for compensation for the use of state-owned lands).

PPL’s reliance on the federal navigational servitude is misplaced. *See United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941) (holding that the federal navigational servitude does not preempt state law claims for compensation for the use of state-owned lands). The navigational servitude does not divest the State of its title to riverbeds or preempt the State’s ability to seek compensation for their use. *See Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (“The title thus vested in the State is subject to the paramount power of the United States over navigable waters.”).

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams under the equal footing doctrine only if the relevant stretches of the rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. The trial court properly applied the historical navigability test, and the FPA does not preempt the State’s ability to seek compensation for PPL’s use of state-owned riverbeds. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
