# ARGUMENT

## I. FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME SATISFIES THE SIXTH AMENDMENT AS INTERPRETED IN *RING v. ARIZONA*

In *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), this Court held that Arizona's scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because a judge alone, with no jury involvement whatsoever, found the aggravating circumstance necessary to expose the defendant to death. *Id.* at 609. The Court explained that "[c]apital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants... are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment." *Id.* Florida's scheme does not suffer from Arizona's defect. Unlike Arizona's judge-only procedure, Florida requires the jury to render an advisory verdict recommending life or death after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances presented at a full adversarial penalty-phase proceeding. § 921.141(2), Fla. Stat. This Court in *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638 (1989), and *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), specifically upheld Florida's hybrid jury-judge procedure, and *Ring* did not overrule those decisions.

*Ring*'s Sixth Amendment holding traces to *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), which requires that "[o]ther than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury." *Id.* at 490. In Florida, the jury—not the judge alone—performs the essential factfinding function contemplated by *Apprendi* and *Ring*: it hears the aggravation and mitigation evidence and returns a recommendation reflecting its judgment on whether death is warranted. The trial judge's subsequent findings, made under *Tedder v. State*, 322 So.2d 908 (Fla. 1975), and its progeny, must give great weight to the jury's recommendation and may impose death only where "the facts suggesting a sentence of death [are] so clear and convincing that virtually no reasonable person could differ." *Tedder*, 322 So.2d at 910. This is fundamentally distinct from the judge-alone system condemned in *Ring*.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment require that the jury's recommendation be unanimous. This Court has long held that the jury-trial guarantee tolerates non-unanimous verdicts in analogous contexts. *See Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 406 (1972) (plurality) (upholding non-unanimous verdicts because "[t]he fact of unanimity... [is not] so fundamental to the essentials of jury trial"); *Johnson v. Louisiana*, 406 U.S. 356, 359 (1972) (same). If unanimity is not constitutionally compelled for the guilt determination itself, it cannot be compelled for the advisory sentencing recommendation, which under Florida law is not equivalent to a factual verdict but a recommendation subject to independent judicial evaluation. Moreover, in *Schad v. Arizona*, 501 U.S. 624, 631–32 (1991), this Court reaffirmed that jury unanimity requirements are matters of degree left substantially to state definition, particularly where, as here, the ultimate sentencing determination remains subject to searching appellate proportionality review. *See Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 253 (1976) (upholding Florida's capital sentencing structure, including its proportionality review, against Eighth Amendment challenge).

## II. FLORIDA'S SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

Petitioner's Eighth Amendment challenge fares no better. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), this Court held only that "death is not a suitable punishment for a mentally retarded criminal," *id.* at 321, and expressly left "to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon [their] execution of sentences," *id.* at 317 (quoting *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 416–17 (1986)). *Atkins* imposed no requirement that a jury—rather than a judge—determine mental retardation, nor did it mandate any particular procedural mechanism. Florida has exercised its reserved authority by adopting a three-pronged clinical test and assigning the factfinding function to the trial judge, who is well-suited to resolve conflicting expert psychological testimony. § 921.137, Fla. Stat.; *Nixon v. State*, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009). That the trial judge, rather than the jury, found that Hurst failed to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence does not offend the Eighth Amendment, which speaks to substantive limits on punishment, not to the particular factfinder Florida employs to enforce those limits.

Petitioner's related argument—that non-unanimous jury recommendations create unacceptable risk under the Eighth Amendment because "death is different," *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 605–06—does not withstand analysis. The "heightened reliability" this Court demands, *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), is satisfied by Florida's multiple layers of protection: a unanimous jury verdict of guilt of first-degree murder; a full adversarial penalty-phase hearing; an advisory jury recommendation; independent judicial weighing of aggravation against mitigation under the *Tedder* standard; and mandatory de novo proportionality review by the Florida Supreme Court. § 921.141(4), Fla. Stat. Nothing in this Court's Eighth Amendment jurisprudence has ever held that unanimity in an advisory jury recommendation is itself a constitutional prerequisite to reliability; rather, this Court has approved capital sentencing structures that vary considerably among the States. *See Proffitt*, 428 U.S. at 252 (recognizing that "each distinct system must be examined on an individual basis"). Florida's system, though structured differently from other States', contains ample safeguards to satisfy the Eighth Amendment's demand for reliability in capital sentencing.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
