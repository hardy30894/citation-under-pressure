**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Was Permissible Subject-Matter Discrimination in a Limited Public Forum.**

Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy created a limited public forum, opening its facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings” and other uses “pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but expressly excluding “religious purposes.” A school that establishes a limited public forum “may reserve the forum for its intended purposes, communicative or otherwise, as long as the regulation on speech is reasonable and not an effort to suppress expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker’s view.” *Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators’ Assn.*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). *See also Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Ed. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (restrictions must be “reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum”).

The prohibition of religious uses is a reasonable subject-matter restriction. The Supreme Court has consistently held that when a public school opens its facilities for secular purposes, it may exclude religious worship or instruction without violating the First Amendment. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (upholding exclusion of religious film series because the forum was limited to “social, civic, and recreational uses”); *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 268 n.5 (1981) (noting that a university could exclude “religious worship” if it created a limited forum for “secular” purposes).

The Good News Club’s proposed activities were not a discussion of morals from a religious viewpoint, but rather “religious instruction and prayer” as the court of appeals found. The meetings included prayers, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and a “Challenge and Invitation” segment that called upon children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” and be saved. 202 F.3d 502, 511-12 (2d Cir. 2000). Such activities constitute religious worship, not a viewpoint on a secular subject. The Club’s materials explicitly distinguished between “saved” and “unsaved” children and aimed to convert the latter. *Id.* at 511. This is the very kind of religious use that the policy was designed to exclude.

**II. The Exclusion Was Viewpoint-Neutral Because It Applied to All Religious Uses, Not to a Particular Religious Viewpoint.**

Viewpoint discrimination occurs when the government “singles out a particular viewpoint for disfavor” while permitting other viewpoints on the same subject. *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Here, the school did not discriminate against the Club’s religious viewpoint on morality; it excluded all religious uses regardless of the specific religion. The policy prohibited “religious purposes” across the board. The Club’s meetings were not a discussion of morals from a religious perspective—they were an exercise in religious devotion and conversion. The other groups allowed to use the facilities, such as the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts, focused on secular moral development without any religious content. *See* 202 F.3d at 509. Thus, the exclusion was based on the subject matter (religion), not on the viewpoint expressed within that subject.

The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious” and “fall outside the bounds of pure ‘moral and character development.’” *Id.* at 521. The Club’s speech was not “the teaching of values from a religious viewpoint” but rather the teaching of religion itself. *Id.* at 522. As the Supreme Court noted in *Lamb’s Chapel*, a school may exclude a religious film because it “would have been used for religious purposes” even though other films on family values were permitted. 508 U.S. at 394. The same principle applies here.

**III. Even If the Exclusion Imposed a Content-Based Restriction, It Was Justified by the Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.**

The Establishment Clause prohibits public schools from endorsing or promoting religion. *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (noting the “coercive pressure” on students to participate in religious exercises). Permitting a religious club to conduct worship services and evangelistic outreach to elementary school children on school premises could reasonably be perceived as official endorsement of religion. The school’s concern was not speculative; it was a legitimate and compelling interest in avoiding entanglement with religion. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271 (recognizing that a state’s interest in complying with the Establishment Clause may justify content-based restrictions). Although the Court in *Widmar* found no Establishment Clause violation for a university forum, the context here is fundamentally different: the audience includes young, impressionable children, and the meetings are held on elementary school grounds. The school’s decision to exclude the Club was a reasonable effort to avoid violating the Establishment Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
