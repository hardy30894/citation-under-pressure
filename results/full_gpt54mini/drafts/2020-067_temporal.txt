The Eleventh Circuit correctly rejected Greer’s attempt to obtain plain-error relief by asking the court of appeals to look beyond the trial record. Plain error review is a limited appellate inquiry grounded in the record made below. This Court has repeatedly held that an error is “plain” only if it is clear under current law, and that reversal is warranted only when the error affects substantial rights and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936); United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982). Because Greer bore the burden on the prejudice and fairness prongs, the court of appeals properly refused to speculate that the omitted Rehaif instruction mattered. See Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 466–67 (1997); Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896).

As a general matter, appellate review is confined to the record. This Court has long recognized that “[i]t is a well-established rule that the record cannot be supplemented by statements in the briefs,” and that an appellate court “cannot, in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction, consider facts not in the record.” United States v. Adams, 383 U.S. 39, 40 (1966) (per curiam); see also United States v. Smith, 331 U.S. 469, 475 (1947) (“the record is the basis of review”). That principle is especially important where the defendant is asking for extraordinary relief from forfeited error. Appellate courts do not conduct a de novo retrial of prejudice; they review the proceedings below for reversible error on the existing record. Chiarella v. United States, 445 U.S. 222, 236 (1980) (plain-error review does not authorize “the raising of issues not presented below”); see also Hormel v. Helvering, 312 U.S. 552, 556 (1941) (appellate courts generally do not decide issues not raised below).

Greer’s contrary theory would erase the distinction between preserved-error review and forfeited-error review. Under Rule 52(b), plain error is a “circumscribed” power reserved for exceptional circumstances. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160; Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717, 718 (1962) (per curiam). The defendant must show not only error that is plain, but also prejudice and a serious impact on the integrity of the proceedings. United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. at 163; Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. at 469–70. That burden cannot be met by inviting the court of appeals to consider materials outside the trial record. To do so would allow a defendant to manufacture prejudice from facts never presented to the jury and never tested in the adversarial process. See Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) (reversal requires that the error have had a substantial and injurious influence on the verdict); Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946) (same).

Nor does Greer gain anything from the argument that the court should look beyond the record to protect the “fairness, integrity, or public reputation” of judicial proceedings. That standard does not dispense with the record; it presupposes an appellate assessment of the proceedings actually tried. Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160. Where the record shows that the defendant had multiple prior felony convictions, a court of appeals may conclude that an omitted Rehaif instruction did not affect substantial rights because the defendant could not plausibly have been unaware of his felon status. See, e.g., cases recognizing that prejudice must be affirmatively shown from the trial record, not presumed from conjecture. Bollenbach, 326 U.S. at 614; Kotteakos, 328 U.S. at 765. A defendant’s own history may be relevant if it is already in the record, but not if the appellate court must leave the record to find it. Adams, 383 U.S. at 40; Smith, 331 U.S. at 475.

The Eleventh Circuit’s approach was faithful to those limits. Greer’s conviction was reviewed for plain error after he failed to preserve a Rehaif objection. The court properly examined whether the omission actually prejudiced him, rather than indulging a presumption of prejudice or searching outside the trial record to find speculative support for reversal. That is exactly what plain-error doctrine requires. Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160; Frady, 456 U.S. at 163; Johnson, 520 U.S. at 466–70.

This Court should therefore affirm. Plain-error review does not authorize appellate courts to step outside the record to decide whether an unpreserved instructional error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The Eleventh Circuit applied the correct limited standard and correctly denied relief.
