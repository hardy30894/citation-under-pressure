# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD FOR POLLING IS ARBITRARY AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE ACT

The Board's requirement that an employer possess the same quantum of evidence to justify a poll as to justify outright withdrawal of recognition cannot be reconciled with rational administrative decisionmaking. An agency's interpretation of its governing statute must be "based on a permissible construction of the statute," Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984), and this Court has never suggested that deference extends to standards that are internally incoherent.

Three courts of appeals have rejected the Board's polling standard precisely because it renders polling "useless" as an independent tool. Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1298 (9th Cir. 1984). Those courts recognized that polling is a "useful and legitimate tool for the employer who is in sincere doubt of the union's majority status," id. at 1299, and accordingly held that an employer may poll when it possesses "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition." Id.; accord A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982).

The Board's own justification for maintaining an identical standard is self-contradictory. In Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), the Board declared that Board-conducted RM elections are the "preferred" method for testing union support because they carry superior procedural safeguards, while describing employer polling as "potentially disruptive and unsettling." Yet having ranked RM elections as preferable, the Board nonetheless imposes upon the disfavored, less-safeguarded method of polling the identical evidentiary threshold required for the favored, safeguarded method—and for outright withdrawal of recognition, which offers no procedural protection whatsoever. An agency's reasoning must satisfy "a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (internal quotation marks omitted). Equating polling with withdrawal, when the Board itself insists the two present markedly different risks to labor stability, fails that standard.

This Court's precedents confirm that an incumbent union's majority status carries only a rebuttable presumption. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer's legitimate interest in testing that presumption through the least disruptive means—rather than being forced to choose between blind recognition and an unsupervised withdrawal—serves the Act's core purpose of fostering stable, voluntary collective bargaining based on employees' actual, uncoerced preferences. See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board's standard perversely encourages employers to bypass polling altogether and proceed directly to withdrawal, since the evidentiary burden is identical but withdrawal avoids the "disruptive" costs the Board attributes to polling. Texas Petrochemicals, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62. That is precisely backward: it discourages employers from using the more employee-protective option to verify their doubts before acting. This Court should hold that the Board's polling standard, divorced from any rational relationship to the actual purpose polling serves, exceeds the bounds of reasoned agency decisionmaking. See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n, 463 U.S. at 43.

## II. SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE DOES NOT SUPPORT THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record as a whole. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (reviewing courts must consider "the record in its entirety," including evidence that "fairly detracts" from the Board's conclusion).

The Board arbitrarily discounted probative evidence of union disaffection through evidentiary rules unmoored from record realities. It refused to credit Kermit Bloch's and Ron Mohr's reports that co-workers—including the entire night shift—opposed the union, discrediting hearsay-type statements as inherently unreliable regardless of context or corroboration. Yet an employer forming a reasonable doubt need not build a case admissible under formal rules of evidence; it need only possess a good-faith, objectively grounded belief. The Board similarly discounted statements by employees Ridgick and Marsh made during job interviews as categorically unreliable, applying a blanket rule rather than assessing the actual circumstances of each statement. See Thomas Industries, Inc., 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board finding of no good-faith doubt where roughly one-fourth of employees made negative statements about representation, holding "we find no substantial evidence to support the Board's conclusion that the Company did not have a good faith doubt as to the Union's majority status").

The bargaining unit here was itself a fraction of the predecessor's certified unit—thirty-two employees drawn from an original forty-five—following Mack Trucks' sale of the dealership to Allentown. See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp., 482 U.S. at 36-41 (successor employer inherits predecessor's bargaining obligation, but underlying presumption of majority support may be tested). Where, as here, the surviving unit is materially smaller than the certified unit, the factual predicate for an unyielding presumption of continued majority support is correspondingly weaker, and the Board's rigid evidentiary approach was particularly unwarranted.

Considering the record "in its entirety," Universal Camera Corp., 340 U.S. at 488, the cumulative statements from numerous employees, considered together with the unit's altered composition, establish that Allentown's doubt was reasonable and objectively grounded—not the product of speculation the Board's crabbed evidentiary approach implies.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals enforcing the Board's order should be reversed.
