ARGUMENT

I. The Government Must Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That the Petitioner Was a Member of the Conspiracy During the Statute of Limitations Period

The Constitution requires the government to prove every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). For a conspiracy charge, the government must prove that (1) an agreement existed, (2) the defendant knowingly joined it, and (3) the conspiracy continued into the applicable limitations period. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 443 (1978). The statute of limitations is not a mere affirmative defense; it is a substantive element that the government must prove when the defendant raises the issue. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1235 (7th Cir. 1981). The petitioner presented evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders, Moore and Gray, ended their association before the limitations period, severing the single conspiracy. Once the defendant proffers such evidence, the burden of persuasion shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued and that the defendant remained a member within the limitations period. *United States v. Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376, 388 (2d Cir. 1964) (Jones, J., dissenting), *overruled on other grounds*, *United States v. Friedman*, 854 F.2d 535 (2d Cir. 1988). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding improperly relieves the government of its constitutional burden.

The government’s reliance on the petitioner’s earlier membership is insufficient. A defendant who withdraws from a conspiracy before the limitations period cannot be convicted for acts committed after withdrawal. *United States v. Sampol*, 636 F.2d 621, 645 (D.C. Cir. 1980). The government must prove that the defendant did not withdraw. *United States v. James*, 528 F.2d 999, 1021 (5th Cir. 1976). Here, the evidence showed that Moore and Gray split up, ending the original conspiracy. The jury could not infer the petitioner’s continued membership from mere prior association. *United States v. Cruz*, 127 F.3d 791, 800 (9th Cir. 1997). The trial court erred by denying the motion for new trial, and the D.C. Circuit erred in affirming.

II. The Lower Court’s Decision Conflicts with Federal Precedent and the Presumption of Innocence

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion places the burden on the defendant to disprove membership during the limitations period. That is impermissible. The presumption of innocence remains with the defendant throughout trial. *Taylor v. Kentucky*, 436 U.S. 478, 486 (1978). The government must bear the full burden of proof on each element. *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008). Every circuit to address the issue holds that once the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal or termination of the conspiracy, the government must prove membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. *See, e.g., United States v. Lakich*, 23 F.3d 1203, 1210 (7th Cir. 1994); *United States v. Hartley*, 678 F.2d 961, 972 (11th Cir. 1982); *United States v. Pungitore*, 910 F.2d 1084, 1142 (3d Cir. 1990). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary rule undermines these settled principles.

The D.C. Circuit also erred by failing to require the government to prove the continuity of the conspiracy itself. A conspiracy that ends before the limitations period cannot support a conviction. *United States v. Mennuti*, 679 F.2d 1032, 1035 (2d Cir. 1982). The petitioner’s evidence that the leaders split was sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt. The government then bore the burden to prove that the conspiracy continued. It did not. The court below should have reversed.

III. The Error Was Not Harmless

The jury’s verdict was not supported by proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the petitioner belonged to a single conspiracy that extended into the limitations period. The erroneous allocation of the burden of persuasion likely affected the verdict. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). The motion for new trial should have been granted.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial.
