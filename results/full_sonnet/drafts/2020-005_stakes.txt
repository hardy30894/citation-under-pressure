**ARGUMENT**

**I. Specific jurisdiction does not require that the defendant’s forum contacts cause the plaintiff’s injury; it requires only that the claims “arise out of or relate to” those contacts.**

Since *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court has held that a State may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant consistent with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice,” id. at 316, so long as the defendant has certain “minimum contacts” with the forum. Critically, this Court has always framed the specific-jurisdiction inquiry disjunctively. In *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984), the Court described specific jurisdiction as jurisdiction over causes of action “arising out of or related to the [defendant’s] activities within the forum State.” The word “relate” would be superfluous if only strict causation sufficed. Ford’s proposed rule—that jurisdiction attaches only when the defendant’s forum contacts are the “but-for” or proximate cause of the plaintiff’s injury—reads the second half of that phrase out of the Constitution.

This Court’s stream-of-commerce cases confirm that causation in the tort-law sense is not the touchstone. In *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297–98 (1980), the Court explained that “the forum State does not exceed its powers under the Due Process Clause if it asserts personal jurisdiction over a corporation that delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State.” The relevant inquiry is whether the defendant’s “conduct and connection with the forum State are such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there,” id. at 297—a foreseeability standard, not a causal one. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472–76 (1985), likewise asks whether the defendant “purposefully availed” itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum, thereby invoking the benefits and protections of its laws—an inquiry addressed to the defendant’s relationship with the State, not to the mechanical origin of the plaintiff’s injury.

**II. Ford’s extensive, purposeful contacts with Montana relate directly to Respondent’s claims.**

Ford does not merely place vehicles into an undifferentiated national market; it purposefully cultivates a market for this exact vehicle line in Montana. Ford advertises, distributes, and sells Ford Explorers through Montana dealerships, supplies replacement parts for Explorers to Montana dealers and repair shops, and trains technicians to service and repair the very component at issue—the tire and its tread. The Montana Supreme Court correctly found that “by advertising and selling parts within the state of Montana, Ford had availed itself of the privilege of doing business in that state.” These are not the isolated or attenuated contacts condemned in *Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014), where the Court required that “the relationship must arise out of contacts that the ‘defendant himself’ creates with the forum State” (quoting *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475). Ford created precisely such contacts: it built a market for Explorers in Montana, serviced Explorers in Montana, and reaped years of profit from Montana consumers who bought, drove, and maintained that model.

Respondent’s claims—that the Explorer’s tire tread was defectively designed and that Ford failed to warn of the danger—relate directly to that Montana market for Explorers. As *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011), explains, specific jurisdiction depends on an “affiliatio[n] between the forum and the underlying controversy.” That affiliation exists here: Ford marketed and sold the Explorer model, and serviced its component parts, to the very population from which the injured plaintiff came, in the very State where the accident occurred. That the particular vehicle first entered commerce in Washington does not sever this affiliation, any more than a magazine publisher’s lack of a subscription office in the forum defeated jurisdiction in *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 775 (1984), where the Court recognized that the plaintiff’s forum residence may “enhance” the defendant’s contacts with the forum.

**III. Bristol-Myers does not require causation and is fully satisfied here.**

Ford leans on *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), for the proposition that “[w]hat is needed … is a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” Id. at 1781. But *Bristol-Myers* involved nonresident plaintiffs who were neither injured in California, nor prescribed or sold the drug there, nor residents of California at all; there was no connection whatsoever between the forum and their claims. Here, by contrast, Respondent’s decedent was a Montana resident, purchased and drove the vehicle in Montana, and was fatally injured on a Montana highway—while Ford simultaneously marketed, sold, and serviced that same vehicle model throughout the State. The “connection between the forum and the specific claims” that *Bristol-Myers* demanded is present in overwhelming measure.

**IV. Exercising jurisdiction comports with fair play and substantial justice.**

Finally, under *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476–78, once minimum contacts are shown, jurisdiction is presumptively reasonable absent a compelling showing of unfairness. Ford, a global manufacturer that litigates nationwide, faces no unfair burden defending a suit in the State where its product was sold, serviced, and caused a fatal injury to a resident. Montana’s interest in adjudicating injuries to its own citizens, and Respondent’s interest in obtaining convenient relief, tip decisively in favor of jurisdiction.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
