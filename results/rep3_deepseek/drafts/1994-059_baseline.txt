**ARGUMENT**

**THE CONSTITUTION’S QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES PROVIDE THE EXCLUSIVE, MAXIMUM QUALIFICATIONS FOR MEMBERS OF CONGRESS, AND STATES MAY NOT ALTER THEM.**

The Constitution establishes the exclusive qualifications for service in the United States Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 sets the qualifications for Senators: at least thirty years old, a citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. These qualifications are a carefully calibrated compromise, reflecting the Framers’ decision to establish uniform national standards for federal office.

This Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969), the Court held that the Constitution “delegates to each House a power to judge the qualifications of its own Members, and that this power is limited to the standing qualifications prescribed in the Constitution.” The Court further explained that “the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 547. This reasoning applies with equal force to the States; if Congress cannot add qualifications, neither can a State.

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does precisely what *Powell* forbids: it adds a qualification—term limits—to those enumerated in the Constitution. The Amendment bars from the ballot any person who has served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate. That is a qualification, not merely a ballot-access regulation. This Court has held that “a state-imposed restriction on access to the ballot” constitutes an additional qualification when it “creates a severity of burden” that effectively excludes candidates. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 789 (1983). Here, the Amendment absolutely disqualifies candidates with prior service, leaving no room for the voters to re-elect them. As the Arkansas Supreme Court recognized, “the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp.” *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill*, 316 Ark. 251, 256, 872 S.W.2d 349, 352 (1994).

The States’ power to regulate elections under Article I, Section 4 does not include the power to add qualifications. This Court has emphasized that the Elections Clause provides only authority over the “Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections,” not over who may be a candidate. *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 523 (2001). The Framers intended the Qualifications Clauses to be “a uniform national standard.” *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 548. Allowing States to add term limits would produce a patchwork of qualifications, undermining the national character of Congress. As the Court stated in *Powell*, “the Constitution ‘leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.’” *Id.* at 547.

Indeed, the historical record confirms that the Framers rejected term limits for Congress. During the Constitutional Convention, delegates debated and voted down proposals to impose term limits on federal legislators. *See The Federalist No. 72* (Alexander Hamilton) (arguing against “the instability of councils” caused by rotation in office). The decision not to include term limits was deliberate, and States cannot impose what the Framers declined to adopt.

Amendments 73’s ballot-access approach is an attempt to circumvent the Constitution. This Court has held that “the Constitution’s text and structure” forbid a State from “evading the Qualifications Clauses by characterizing a qualification as a ballot-access restriction.” *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 829 (1995). Arkansas cannot do indirectly what it cannot do directly.

Finally, the state offices provisions of Amendment 73 cannot stand independently. The Arkansas Supreme Court concluded that the state and federal term limits were “inextricably linked” and presented to voters as an “all or nothing” package. *Hill*, 316 Ark. at 261, 872 S.W.2d at 355. The voters had no opportunity to approve state term limits separately. The severability clause cannot salvage the amendment because, as the Arkansas court held, “there is no way for this court to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders if section 3 had not been in the picture.” *Id.* at 260, 872 S.W.2d at 354. The entire amendment must fall.

For these reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court holding that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution.
