The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may create a limited public forum, opening its property to certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics, without being required to allow all other groups or topics. See Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 46-47 (1983). In such a forum, the government may impose restrictions on speech that are reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum and are viewpoint neutral. See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995).

Here, Milford Central School has created a limited public forum by allowing certain groups to use its facilities after hours. The school's community use policy permits use by groups for "social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community." However, the policy expressly prohibits use "for religious purposes." This restriction is reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum, which is to provide a community space for local residents.

The Good News Club's proposed use of the school's facilities involves activities that are "quintessentially religious" in nature, including singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying. These activities go beyond mere "moral and character development" and are instead focused on religious instruction and worship. As such, they fall within the category of uses prohibited by the school's community use policy.

The Club's argument that its activities represent a "religious viewpoint" on the subject of morals and character is unpersuasive. While the Supreme Court has recognized that a religious perspective can constitute a viewpoint on a variety of subjects, see Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831, the Club's activities are not merely a discussion of morals and character from a religious perspective, but rather are focused on religious instruction and worship.

The Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit correctly held that the school's exclusion of the Club was not viewpoint discrimination, but rather subject matter discrimination, which is permissible in a limited public forum. See Travis v. Owego-Apalachin Sch. Dist., 927 F.2d 688, 692 (2d Cir. 1991). The school's policy is not aimed at suppressing a particular viewpoint, but rather at excluding a particular subject matter - religious worship and instruction.

The Supreme Court's decision in Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist., 508 U.S. 384 (1993), is distinguishable from the present case. In Lamb's Chapel, the Court held that a school district's exclusion of a religious group from using its facilities after hours was viewpoint discrimination, as the group sought to show a film series that discussed family values and child-rearing from a religious perspective. However, the film series was not focused on religious worship or instruction, but rather on a secular subject matter. In contrast, the Good News Club's activities are focused on religious instruction and worship.

The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School does not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the Club's activities fall within the category of uses prohibited by the policy. See Gregoire v. Centennial Sch. Dist., 907 F.2d 1366, 1374-75 (3d Cir. 1990); see also Bronx Household of Faith v. Community Sch. Dist. No. 10, 127 F.3d 207, 214-15 (2d Cir. 1997).

Furthermore, permitting the Club's activities to take place at the school could potentially violate the Establishment Clause, which prohibits government endorsement or promotion of religion. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 587 (1992); see also Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe, 530 U.S. 290, 301-02 (2000). The school's exclusion of the Club is justified by its concern that permitting the Club's activities would violate the Establishment Clause.

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the Club's activities fall within the category of uses prohibited by the policy.
