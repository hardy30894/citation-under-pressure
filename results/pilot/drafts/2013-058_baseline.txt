**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as an “exempt retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and therefore is not protected from creditors’ claims in bankruptcy. The Court of Appeals’ interpretation of the Bankruptcy Code is faithful to the statutory text, consistent with the purpose of the exemption, and supported by precedent. The Clarks’ contrary argument—that the inherited IRA retains its character as “retirement funds” simply because it once served that function for the decedent—misreads the statute and undermines the foundational principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are designed to protect assets *used for the debtor’s own retirement*, not those that have been transformed by law into vehicles for current consumption.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from creditors’ claims “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key inquiry is not whether the funds *originally* served as retirement savings, but whether they *continue to function* as such in the hands of the current owner. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, “an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A) mandates distributions to begin within one year of the original owner’s death, with full payout required within five years (or over the life expectancy of the beneficiary, in some cases). This statutory structure *eliminates* the retirement function: the funds are not preserved for the beneficiary’s post-employment years, but are instead liquidated in advance of retirement.

The Clarks’ argument rests on a flawed analogy to *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), which hold that the “retirement funds” status of an IRA survives inheritance. But those decisions ignore the critical distinction between *origin* and *current function*. The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning—that the phrase “retirement funds” in §522(b)(3)(C) does not require the funds to be the debtor’s retirement savings—fails to account for the fact that the *economic reality* of an inherited IRA is fundamentally different from that of a personal IRA. As the Seventh Circuit emphasized, “the money used to be ‘retirement funds’ but isn’t now.” The funds are no longer held for the beneficiary’s retirement; they are a liquid asset subject to mandatory distribution and immediate taxation. To treat such funds as exempt would be to “shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption,” a result Congress did not intend.

The Clarks’ reliance on the word “retirement” in “inherited IRA” is equally unpersuasive. The term “IRA” in the Internal Revenue Code refers to the *source* of the funds, not their current economic function. Just as a trust established for a beneficiary’s education is not exempt from creditors’ claims simply because it was once used for that purpose, an inherited IRA is not exempt merely because it once served as a retirement vehicle. The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt assets based on their *past* status; it exempts those that *serve* a retirement purpose *now*. As the Seventh Circuit noted, “the account remains a tax-deferral vehicle until the mandatory distribution is completed, but distribution precedes the owner’s retirement.” That is the decisive point.

Moreover, the Clarks’ argument would create a perverse incentive: individuals could avoid bankruptcy by transferring retirement assets to heirs, knowing those assets would be shielded from creditors regardless of the beneficiary’s intent or use. This would undermine the Bankruptcy Code’s goal of providing a fresh start to debtors while ensuring that assets are not shielded from creditors simply because they were once used for retirement. As the Supreme Court has cautioned, “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525 (1987). Congress chose to exempt only those funds that are *currently* dedicated to the debtor’s retirement—not those that have been converted by law into vehicles for immediate distribution.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is instructive. Under §522(b)(3)(A), a debtor may exempt a “domicile” only if it was the debtor’s home for at least 730 days before filing. A house inherited from a parent is not exempt simply because it once served as the parent’s home. The exemption depends on *current use*, not *past ownership*. The same principle applies here: an inherited IRA is not exempt because it once served as a retirement fund, but because it currently serves that function. It does not.

Finally, the Clarks’ claim that the Seventh Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split is overstated. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Chilton* is not only inconsistent with the statutory text and economic reality of inherited IRAs, but also with the Seventh Circuit’s own precedent in *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1985), which held that exemptions depend on the debtor’s *current* interest in property, not the prior owner’s use. The conflict is not one of interpretation, but of policy—and the Seventh Circuit’s interpretation is the correct one.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
