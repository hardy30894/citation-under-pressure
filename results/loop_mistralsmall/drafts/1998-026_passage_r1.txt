**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the failure to advise him of his right to appeal was harmless. Rule 32(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires the district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2) (1992). However, this Court has long held that a procedural error does not warrant reversal unless it prejudiced the defendant. *See United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 253-54 (1940) ("[A]buse of discretion here, for no prejudice has been established. Hence this case falls within the well established rule that neither this Court nor the Circuit Court of Appeals will review the action of a federal trial court in granting or denying a motion."); *see also Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) ("being taken in the absence of the accused, and after the case is called for trial, which involves his substantial rights. The requirement is not that he must be personally present at the trial by the jury, but 'at the trial.' The Code, we have").

Here, the District Court found that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied because Petitioner knew of his right to appeal. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) ("If, when all is said and done, the conviction is sure that the error did not influence the jury, or had but very slight effect, the verdict and the judgment should stand."); *see also Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 82 (1935) ("not going to stop him because the question includes the Court. I will let him answer it.").

This principle is well-established in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Bruno v. United States*, 308 U.S. 287, 293 (1939) ("into the discussions or deliberations of the jury in any manner.' The trial judge declined this request, saying 'I feel that I've already covered that.' The exception to this denial having been saved, the Circuit Court of Appeals found no error in the refusal"), the Court held that "[a]n error which does not affect substantial rights is not ground for reversal." Similarly, in *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("to remind tha the question is not whether guilt may be spelt out of a record, but whether guilt has been found by a jury according to the procedure and standards appropriate for criminal trials in the federal courts. Accordingly, we cannot treat the"), the Court stated that "[a]n error which does not prejudice the rights of the accused is not ground for reversal." These cases confirm that harmless-error review applies to procedural violations, including those under Rule 32(a)(2).

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly emphasized that the harmless-error standard is not limited to constitutional errors but applies to all procedural violations. *See, e.g., United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 63 (2002) (citing *United States v. Lane*, 474 U.S. 438, 449 (1986), for the proposition that "[a]ny error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded."); *see also United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993) ("Rule 52(a) states: 'Harmless error. Any error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded.'").

The District Court’s finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is dispositive. This Court has long recognized that a defendant’s knowledge of his rights can render a procedural error harmless. *See, e.g., United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954), that "[t]he purpose of a procedural rule is to promote the proper administration of justice, and if that purpose is otherwise served, the rule may be deemed satisfied." The District Court’s factual finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is entitled to deference under *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985), and this Court’s decision in *United States Gypsum Co. v. United States*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) ("[A] finding is 'clearly erroneous' when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.").

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
