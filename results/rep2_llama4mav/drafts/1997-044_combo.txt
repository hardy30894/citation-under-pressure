## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to provide a fresh start for honest debtors, but they are not a haven for those who have committed fraud. The question presented here is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." This provision is rooted in the principle that a debtor who has obtained property or services through fraud should not be allowed to discharge the debt arising from that fraud. As the Supreme Court held in Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1877), debts incurred through fraud are not dischargeable in bankruptcy.

The Court has consistently held that the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions should be construed narrowly to achieve this purpose. In Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court stated that the exceptions to discharge "should be confined to those plainly expressed" in the statute. Here, § 523(a)(2)(A) plainly excepts debts obtained by actual fraud from discharge.

The lower courts have applied this principle to hold that debts arising from fraudulent conduct are nondischargeable. In Brown v. Gore, 134 F.2d 741 (3d Cir. 1943), the Third Circuit Court of Appeals held that a debt incurred through the debtor's fraudulent misrepresentations was not dischargeable. Similarly, in In re Pulver, 146 F. Supp. 496 (W.D.N.Y. 1956), the district court held that a debt arising from the debtor's fraudulent conduct was excepted from discharge under the Bankruptcy Act.

The respondent, Edward S. Cohen, committed actual fraud by charging excessive rents to his tenants, Hilda de la Cruz and others, in violation of the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen had made an implicit representation regarding the rent he charged, and that he had acted with reckless indifference to the truth of that representation. This finding is supported by the evidence, and it establishes that Cohen committed actual fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A).

The damages awarded to the tenants, including treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, are also nondischargeable. As the Court held in Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), a debt that is not dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code includes not only the original debt but also any damages or penalties arising from that debt. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are a direct result of Cohen's fraudulent conduct and are therefore nondischargeable.

Furthermore, the principle that a debtor who has committed fraud should not be allowed to benefit from their wrongdoing is well-established. In Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234 (1934), the Court stated that the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to provide a fresh start for honest debtors, not those who have engaged in fraudulent conduct. Cohen's debt to the tenants is not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A), and the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are a proper remedy for his fraudulent conduct.

The decision of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed, as it correctly held that Cohen's debt to the tenants is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are not a means for Cohen to escape liability for his fraudulent conduct.

In Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473 (1904), the Court held that a liability arising from a willful and malicious injury is not dischargeable in bankruptcy. Similarly, in McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138 (1916), the Court held that a debt arising from a fraudulent conversion of property is not dischargeable. These cases demonstrate that the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions have long been interpreted to except debts arising from fraudulent or willful conduct from discharge.

In conclusion, the debt incurred by Cohen through his fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable, as they arise from Cohen's fraudulent conduct. The decision of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
