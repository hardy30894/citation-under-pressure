ARGUMENT

I. The Controlled Substances Act’s Temporary Scheduling Provision Supplies an Intelligible Principle and Therefore Does Not Effect an Unconstitutional Delegation.

Article I, Section 1 vests “All legislative Powers herein granted” in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. This Court has long held that Congress may obtain assistance from the coordinate branches provided it lays down “an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The principle must be “sufficiently definite and precise to enable Congress, the courts, and the public to ascertain” whether the delegatee has followed it. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944).

Section 811(h) meets this standard. It authorizes temporary placement of a substance on Schedule I only upon a finding that scheduling “is necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The Attorney General must consider three specific statutory factors—history and current pattern of abuse, scope and significance of abuse, and risk to public health—drawn from the eight factors enumerated in § 811(c). The temporary order expires after one year (extendable by six months), § 811(h)(2), and the Secretary of Health and Human Services must be given notice and an opportunity to comment, § 811(h)(4). These constraints are at least as definite as those upheld in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372-79 (1989), and Yakus, 321 U.S. at 420-27.

That the delegated power affects criminal liability does not alter the analysis. This Court has sustained delegations authorizing criminal sanctions when an intelligible principle guides the exercise of discretion. See, e.g., United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-22 (1911); Yakus, 321 U.S. at 418-19. The two 1935 decisions striking down delegations involved statutes containing “no standard whatever.” Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 430 (1935); see also A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935). Section 811(h) supplies multiple, concrete standards.

II. The Attorney General Lawfully Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), effectuates that authority by assigning to the DEA Administrator “[a]ll functions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970.” Such subdelegation is presumptively valid when the statute permits it and the subdelegatee remains subject to the same statutory constraints. See United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512-14 (1974); Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co., 331 U.S. 111, 121-22 (1947). The Administrator’s temporary scheduling orders are therefore authorized and reviewable under the same intelligible-principle framework that governs the Attorney General.

Because the statute supplies an intelligible principle and the subdelegation is statutorily authorized, the convictions should be affirmed.
