The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The answer is no.

Punitive damages are available under Title VII if the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The plain language of the statute focuses on the defendant's state of mind, not the egregiousness of its conduct. This interpretation is consistent with the common law understanding of punitive damages, which has long allowed for such awards when a defendant acts with "malice, fraud, oppression, or wilfulness." Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893).

The Supreme Court has recognized that punitive damages are intended to punish the defendant and deter similar conduct in the future. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886). To achieve this purpose, the Court has held that punitive damages may be awarded when a defendant acts with "actual malice" or "reckless disregard" for the rights of others. St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727, 731 (1968); Henry v. Collins, 380 U.S. 356, 357 (1965); New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 280 (1964).

In the context of employment discrimination, the Court has applied a similar standard. In Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), the Court held that punitive damages were available under state law when the defendant's conduct was "wanton" or "reckless." Id. at 161. Similarly, in Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris, 122 U.S. 597 (1887), the Court upheld an award of punitive damages when the defendant's servants acted with "bad intent" and in pursuance of an "unlawful purpose." Id. at 609.

The legislative history of § 1981a, which governs punitive damages under Title VII, is consistent with this interpretation. Representative Edwards stated that punitive damages are available under § 1981a "to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981." 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991). Under § 1981, courts have long allowed punitive damages when a defendant acts with "malice or reckless indifference." See, e.g., Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886); Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893).

Furthermore, the Court has held that the amount of punitive damages awarded should be guided by the "nature of the case, the character and extent of injury likely to result from disregard of duty, and all the attendant circumstances." Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society, 320 U.S. 238, 242 (1943) (quoting Alabama Water Service Co. v. Harris, 221 Ala. 516, 519 (1933)). This standard is consistent with the focus on the defendant's state of mind under § 1981a.

In Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58 (1897), the Court upheld an award of punitive damages under state law, noting that such damages are intended to "punish the guilty, and as an example to deter others from offending in like manner." Id. at 86. Similarly, in Inglehart v. Stansbury, 151 U.S. 68 (1894), the Court recognized that punitive damages may be awarded when a defendant's conduct is "wanton" or "reckless." Id. at 77.

In Ginzburg Et Al. v. Goldwater, 396 U.S. 1049 (1969), Justice Black dissented from the denial of certiorari, noting that the award of punitive damages in a libel case must be guided by the "actual malice" standard of New York Times Co. v. Sullivan. Id. at 1051. This standard is similar to the "malice or reckless indifference" standard under § 1981a.

In conclusion, the plain language of § 1981a, combined with the common law understanding of punitive damages and the relevant case law, demonstrates that an employer's conduct need not be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The focus is on the defendant's state of mind, specifically whether it acted with "malice or with reckless indifference" to the plaintiff's federally protected rights.
