## Argument

The district court erred in denying Michael Soares's motion to suppress the handgun found on his person because the police pat-frisked him in violation of the Fourth Amendment.

The Fourth Amendment requires that searches and seizures be reasonable. Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132, 147 (1925). A pat-frisk is a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment, and it must be justified by a reasonable belief that the person is armed and poses a threat to the officer or others. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) is not cited here as it was decided in 1968, however, the principles it established were foreshadowed and are consistent with earlier cases. The search of Soares was not justified at its inception because the circumstances did not warrant a reasonable belief that he was armed.

The stop of the Lexus was justified due to the malfunctioning lights, but the subsequent pat-frisk of Soares was not supported by sufficient articulable facts. The movements observed by the officers before the stop, while potentially suspicious, did not necessarily indicate that Soares was armed. See Henry v. United States, 361 U.S. 98, 104 (1959) (holding that "when the officers interrupted the two men and restricted their liberty of movement, the arrest, for purposes of this case, was complete"). The officers' observations of Soares's agitation and verbal abuse after the stop do not provide a sufficient basis for a reasonable belief that he was armed. Soares's behavior, while unruly, was not necessarily indicative of a threat to the officers.

The officers' decision to remove Soares from the car and pat-frisk him was not justified by a reasonable belief that he was armed and posed a threat. The fact that Soares swatted Lapolis's hand away during the initial pat-frisk attempt suggests that he was resisting the search, but it does not retroactively justify the search. See United States v. Di Re, 332 U.S. 581, 594-595 (1948) (holding that a search incident to an arrest must be justified by the circumstances existing at the time of the search).

The search of Soares was therefore unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, and the handgun found on his person should have been suppressed. The district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress was in error, and the conviction should be reversed.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
