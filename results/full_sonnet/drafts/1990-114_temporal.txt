**ARGUMENT**

**I. Missouri's Choice to Retire Its Judges at Seventy Does Not Violate the ADEA.**

Missouri's authority to structure its own judiciary lies at the core of state sovereignty. "The power to locate its own seat of government . . . and to appropriate its own public funds for that purpose[] are essentially and peculiarly state powers." *Coyle v. Smith*, 221 U.S. 559, 580 (1911). The power to decide who shall sit as a judge, and for how long, is no less essentially a state function than the power to locate a capital. See *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 434 (1920) (recognizing the States' reserved authority over matters of internal governance); *New York v. United States*, 326 U.S. 572, 580–81 (1946) (states retain sovereign control over their own governmental instrumentalities). Congress may of course override such state prerogatives, but not by implication. When a federal statute is said to intrude upon a domain "traditionally left to the States," this Court "start[s] with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947); accord *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 96 (1945) (federal statutes touching sensitive areas of state authority should not be read to work such intrusions absent unmistakable congressional intent).

The ADEA contains no clear statement subjecting appointed state judges to its terms. To the contrary, its text excludes any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Petitioners' judicial office falls squarely within that exclusion. Judging is not the mechanical application of settled rules; it is, and has long been understood to be, an exercise of judgment that makes law where none previously existed. This Court has recognized that the common law administered by state courts is itself a species of judicially declared policy, evolving through the decisions of the judges who administer it. *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78–79 (1938) (the "law" applied in diversity cases is that "declared by [the State's] Legislature in a statute or by its highest court" through the exercise of judicial judgment). A judge who fills the interstices of constitutions, statutes, and precedent is exercising precisely the kind of discretionary, policy-laden authority Congress meant to exempt. Nothing in § 630(f)'s text confines the exemption to the executive and legislative branches; had Congress wished to reach so deeply into a State's control over its own courts, it would have said so with the clarity *Rice* demands. It did not, and this Court should not supply by inference what Congress omitted by design.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Rational-Basis Scrutiny Under the Equal Protection Clause.**

Age classifications in state employment schemes are reviewed under the most deferential standard known to equal protection doctrine. A classification "having some reasonable basis does not offend" the Equal Protection Clause "merely because it is not made with mathematical nicety or because in practice it results in some inequality." *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911). "A statutory discrimination will not be set aside if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it." *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961). A state legislature—or, as here, the citizens of Missouri acting through their constitution—need not "reflect sociological insight, or shifting social standards, any more than it requires them to keep abreast of the latest scientific standards" to sustain a classification of this kind. *Goesaert v. Cleary*, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948). It is enough that "there is an evil at hand for correction, and that it might be thought that the particular legislative measure was a rational way to correct it." *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483, 488 (1955).

Missouri's mandatory retirement age is amply justified on this record. The State could rationally conclude, as have other jurisdictions regulating vital public offices, that a fixed retirement age avoids the "unpleasantness" and difficulty of case-by-case evaluations of declining judicial capacity, ensures orderly succession, and ties important adjudicative decisions to judges whose faculties remain undiminished by age. Cf. *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs*, 330 U.S. 552, 563–64 (1947) (upholding a State's practical judgments about qualifications for an office charged with public safety). "The Constitution does not require things which are different in fact or opinion to be treated in law as though they were the same," *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940), and a State may rationally treat judicial tenure differently from other public employment given the unique demands the office places on its holders. Nor is it this Court's role to weigh the wisdom of that choice: "we refuse to sit as a 'super-legislature to weigh the wisdom of legislation.'" *Ferguson v. Skrupa*, 372 U.S. 726, 730 (1963). Because Missouri's rule "is not invalid under the Constitution because it might have gone farther than it did," *Metropolitan Casualty Ins. Co. v. Brownell*, 294 U.S. 580, 584 (1935), the provision comfortably survives rational-basis review.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
