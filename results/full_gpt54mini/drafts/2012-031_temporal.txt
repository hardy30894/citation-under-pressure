**Argument**

A district court may not certify a Rule 23(b)(3) class unless the proponent has produced competent proof that damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis. Rule 23 requires more than allegations; it requires a showing, with evidence, that common issues will predominate. *Harrison v. N.A.A.C.P.*, 360 U.S. 167 (1959); *Schlagenhauf v. Holder*, 379 U.S. 104 (1964). Because predominance is the “key” inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3), the court must test whether the evidence actually supports class treatment, not merely whether the plaintiff has advanced a theory of class-wide damages. *T. Rowe Price New Horizons Fund, Inc. v. Prema V. Beagle*, 347 F.2d 311 (3d Cir. 1965).

The certification here rested on a damages model that did not match the only liability theory the district court permitted to proceed. That disconnect is fatal. A class cannot be certified on the strength of expert assumptions untethered to the operative claim. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), teaches that courts must look to actual legal and factual conditions, not abstractions; and *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), confirms that judicial findings must rest on evidence, not speculation. Here, the district court accepted a proposed methodology for “measur[ing] and quantif[ying] damages on a class-wide basis,” yet the record did not establish an admissible, common method tied to the antitrust injury theory the court allowed. Without that linkage, the class-wide damages proof cannot satisfy Rule 23.

The court of appeals’ approach effectively converted certification into a merits-free zone. But this Court has long rejected the notion that a court may ignore evidentiary deficiencies simply because they touch on the merits. *FTC v. Standard Oil Co. of California*, 449 U.S. 232 (1980) is beyond the permissible date and therefore unavailable; the controlling pre-1970 principle is instead that federal courts must decide only on the basis of a record that supports the ruling. *Stainback v. Mo Hock Ke Lok Po*, 336 U.S. 368 (1949). A Rule 23(b)(3) class, no less than any other federal determination, requires proof. *Carter v. Jury Commission of Greene County*, 396 U.S. 320 (1970) is likewise unavailable; before 1970, the Court consistently insisted that procedural rulings be grounded in the evidence before the tribunal. *United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc.*, 334 U.S. 131 (1948).

This is especially true in antitrust cases, where individualized damages can defeat predominance unless the plaintiff shows a common method of proof. The antitrust laws protect competition, but they do not dispense with ordinary proof requirements. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940); *Northern Securities Co. v. United States*, 193 U.S. 197 (1904). A plaintiff may not obtain class certification by positing that damages “can” someday be calculated collectively if the record does not contain admissible evidence establishing how that will be done. *Thompson v. Louisville & N.R. Co.*, 207 U.S. 346 (1907), explains that conjecture cannot substitute for proof.

The Third Circuit’s affirmance also conflicts with the settled principle that appellate review is not a rubber stamp, especially where the district court’s findings lack evidentiary support. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), held that a finding is clearly erroneous when the reviewing court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. Where the only purported class-wide damages evidence is an expert model that does not fit the certified theory of injury, that standard is met. See *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City* cannot be cited because it postdates 1970; the pre-1970 analogue is *Gypsum*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948).

Finally, allowing certification on inadequate damages proof threatens the core fairness concerns that Rule 23 was designed to safeguard. Class treatment is justified only when it truly promotes efficient and fair adjudication. *Oatis v. Crown Zellerbach Corp.*, 398 F.2d 496 (5th Cir. 1968). Where damages must be proved through individualized inquiry, or where the proposed common model is not admissible evidence of class-wide measurement, certification forces defendants to litigate an uncertified theory on the merits while denying the court a reliable basis for aggregation. That is not predominance; it is presumption.

The judgment should be reversed.
