**Argument**

The Fifth Circuit correctly dismissed Salinas’s petition because a Board decision refusing to reopen a prior benefits claim is not a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This Court has already answered the question in the negative. In **Roberts v. U.S. Railroad Retirement Board**, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), the Court held that it had “no jurisdiction” to review “the Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits.” *Id.* at 140–41. Roberts remains controlling in this Circuit under the rule of orderliness. **Vaughan v. Anderson Regional Medical Center**, 849 F.3d 588, 591 (5th Cir. 2017). Salinas asks the Court to disregard binding precedent, but a panel “may not overrule the decision of another panel.” *Id.*

Roberts also correctly construed the statutory scheme. Section 355(f) authorizes review only of “final decisions” of the Board after administrative remedies are exhausted. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). As Roberts explained, “the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals is limited to the review of Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted.” 346 F.3d at 140. A refusal to reopen is not a merits adjudication of entitlement; it is a discretionary determination whether to revisit a concluded case. That distinction matters because Congress did not authorize unlimited review of every later Board action connected to an earlier claim. See **Califano v. Sanders**, 430 U.S. 99, 108 (1977) (holding that the Social Security Act does not authorize judicial review of the agency’s refusal to reopen a prior claim, because such an action is not a “final decision” within the meaning of the statute).

The Supreme Court’s reasoning in **Califano** applies with full force here. There, as here, the claimant sought review of an agency refusal to reopen a prior benefits determination. 430 U.S. at 100–01. The Court held that allowing judicial review of reopening denials would undermine finality and permit claimants to evade ordinary filing deadlines. *Id.* at 108–09. Roberts relied on Califano in concluding that the same limitation governs Railroad Retirement Board cases. 346 F.3d at 141. Other circuits have reached the same conclusion. See **Harris v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999); **Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995); **Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); **Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983). Those decisions confirm that the Board’s refusal to reopen is not itself a reviewable “final decision.”

Salinas’s contrary position rests on a minority view rejected by this Court. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in **Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 826 F.3d 500, 502 (D.C. Cir. 2016), held that the Railroad Retirement Act permits review of reopening denials. But Stovic cannot control here. Roberts squarely addressed the issue and adopted the opposite rule. 346 F.3d at 141. Under the rule of orderliness, this Court must follow Roberts unless and until it is overruled by the en banc Court or the Supreme Court. Vaughan, 849 F.3d at 591.

Nor does Salinas’s case present any basis for distinguishing Roberts. The Board’s challenged action was a straightforward refusal to reopen the denial of Salinas’s 2006 application because the request fell outside the reopening period and lacked a basis under the Board’s regulations. That is precisely the sort of nonfinal reopening determination Roberts held unreviewable. 346 F.3d at 140–41. Salinas received a final agency disposition on his original application in 2007; he did not seek timely review. He may not resurrect that expired claim by recasting a reopening request as a “final decision” under § 355(f).

Because binding precedent forecloses jurisdiction, the Fifth Circuit properly dismissed the petition for review. The Court should affirm.
