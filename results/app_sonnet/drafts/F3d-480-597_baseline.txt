ARGUMENT

I. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Nieves-Castaño Knew the Weapon Had the Characteristics of a Machine Gun

A conviction may stand only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, "any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), that includes the mens rea element the Supreme Court identified in Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600 (1994). There, the Court held that a defendant charged with possessing an unregistered machine gun must be shown to have known "of the features of his weapon that brought it within the scope of the Act"—namely, that it fired automatically—rather than merely that he knowingly possessed a "firearm." Id. at 602, 619. The Court expressly distinguished knowledge of possessing a gun from knowledge of the gun's automatic-fire capability, reasoning that many commonly owned weapons "traditionally have been widely accepted as lawful possessions" and that criminalizing their possession without proof of that specific knowledge would "impose criminal sanctions on a class of persons whose mental state ... makes their actions entirely innocent." Id. at 610, 615.

The record here does not satisfy that standard. The only evidence of Nieves-Castaño's state of mind was her post-arrest statement that she had once looked into the golf bag, seen a rifle, and "knew it was an AK-47." That statement establishes, at most, that she recognized the weapon's make or model designation—not that she knew it possessed the automatic-fire mechanism that distinguishes a machine gun from the many lawfully-owned semi-automatic rifles that are commonly marketed and referred to under the same "AK-47" name. Staples itself cautioned that guns bearing similar external appearances may or may not be machine guns, and that the government must prove actual knowledge of the internal firing mechanism, not simply familiarity with a colloquial designation. 511 U.S. at 602. No witness testified that Nieves-Castaño ever examined the weapon's firing mechanism, fired it, or was told by anyone that it fired automatically. On this record, no rational juror could find beyond a reasonable doubt that she knew the rifle would fire more than one shot per trigger pull. See Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319.

II. The Jury Instructions Failed to Require the Jury to Find the Mens Rea That Staples Demands

Compounding the evidentiary gap, the district court's instructions on the § 922(o) count did not require the jury to find that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon possessed automatic-fire capability. Staples requires that the jury be told the government must prove knowledge of "the physical properties of the weapons that render them dangerous," 511 U.S. at 615 n.11, not merely knowing possession of a gun in a golf bag. Because the instructions permitted the jury to convict on proof that Nieves-Castaño knowingly possessed "a firearm" without separately finding she knew of its automatic-fire mechanism, the instructions relieved the government of an essential element of the offense. That is precisely the error Staples forecloses, and it requires reversal of the § 922(o) conviction.

III. Section 922(q)(2)(A) Is Unconstitutionally Vague Because It Fails to Specify How the 1000-Foot School Zone Is to Be Measured

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that criminal statutes "give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited" and provide "explicit standards for those who apply them" so as to avoid arbitrary enforcement. Grayned v. City of Rockford, 408 U.S. 104, 108-09 (1972). A law is void for vagueness if it fails on either score, particularly where, as here, criminal liability turns. Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983) (vagueness doctrine requires laws to provide "minimal guidelines to govern law enforcement"). This Court has recognized that criminal statutes must give fair warning of the conduct they proscribe. See also United States v. Lanier, 520 U.S. 259, 265 (1997) ("a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime").

Section 922(q)(2)(A) makes it a crime to possess a firearm "in a school zone," defined as an area "within a distance of 1,000 feet from the grounds of a public, parochial or private school." 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(25)(B). But the statute is silent on how that distance is measured—whether "as the crow flies" in a straight line, by pedestrian route, by street distance, or by some other metric. This ambiguity is not academic: depending on the method chosen, a given residence may fall inside or outside the zone. Because ordinary citizens have no way to determine, from the statute's text, whether their conduct is lawful, and because law enforcement and courts are left to improvise measurement rules on an ad hoc basis, the statute "impermissibly delegates basic policy matters to policemen, judges, and juries for resolution on an ad hoc and subjective basis." Grayned, 408 U.S. at 108-09. As applied to Nieves-Castaño, whose apartment's precise distance from the nearest school depended entirely on which unstated measurement method the government's witness employed, the statute is unconstitutionally vague.

IV. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish the School-Zone Element

Even apart from the vagueness problem, the government's proof of distance was insufficient under Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319. The witness who testified to the distance between Nieves-Castaño's building and the school did not identify the measurement method used, leaving the jury without a reliable basis to find beyond a reasonable doubt that the residence fell within the statutory zone. That uncertainty, layered on the statute's own silence, means no rational juror could have found the school-zone element proven beyond a reasonable doubt.

For these reasons, both convictions should be reversed.
