Petitioner asks whether the Equal Protection Clause reaches a “class of one” claim when a plaintiff alleges that she was intentionally singled out for irrational treatment. The answer is yes.

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause protects against arbitrary governmental discrimination, not merely discrimination against members of a formally identified class. The Court has long recognized that constitutional injury can arise when government action is directed at a single person in a way that is wholly unjustified. In Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the Court invalidated discriminatory administration of an ordinance that was fair on its face but applied “with an evil eye and an unequal hand.” Id. at 373-74. The principle is not limited to race or other suspect classifications; it is that the government may not, consistent with equal protection, choose one person for arbitrary burdens while exempting others similarly situated. See id.; Truax v. Corrigan, 257 U.S. 312, 331-32 (1921) (Equal Protection Clause protects against arbitrary denial of the “equal protection of the laws” in the enforcement of legal rights); Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield, 247 U.S. 350, 352-53 (1918) (unequal assessment violates equal protection where it is not the result of mere mistake). 

That understanding accords with decisions recognizing that equal protection forbids purposeful and irrational discrimination even when only a single party is affected. In Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission, 488 U.S. 336 (1989), the Court explained that equal protection is offended when government departs from a uniform practice without a rational basis and imposes markedly different treatment on similarly situated persons. Id. at 345-46. Earlier cases are to the same effect. See Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County, 260 U.S. 441, 445-47 (1923) (intentional and systematic assessment disparity violates equal protection); Iowa-Des Moines Nat’l Bank v. Bennett, 284 U.S. 239, 247-48 (1931) (state may not “deliberately” tax one party at a higher rate than others similarly situated); Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam) (recognizing that equal protection claims may be brought by a class of one).

The Seventh Circuit’s “spiteful effort to get him” formulation was too cramped because it made animus the exclusive basis for relief. Equal protection doctrine does not require proof of subjective spite if the plaintiff shows intentional differential treatment without a rational basis. In F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia, 253 U.S. 412 (1920), the Court held that classifications must rest on “some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation.” Id. at 415. Where government singles out one property owner for an additional burden—here, a 33-foot easement when other applicants were asked for 15 feet—the constitutional question is whether the distinction is rationally related to a legitimate objective. Cf. Sunday Lake Iron Co., 247 U.S. at 352-53; Sioux City Bridge Co., 260 U.S. at 445-47. 

That is especially true where, as here, the government allegedly conditioned a basic utility connection on an extra land concession unrelated to any legitimate municipal need. This Court has repeatedly required the government, when acting under color of law, to proceed evenhandedly and not by arbitrary selection. See Yick Wo, 118 U.S. at 373-74; Truax, 257 U.S. at 331-32; Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal, 488 U.S. at 345-46. The Equal Protection Clause would be reduced to a dead letter if a municipality could avoid review simply because its discrimination targeted one household rather than a formally defined group.

The Village’s contrary rule would also conflict with the Court’s longstanding refusal to let government evade constitutional limits through individualized favoritism or hostility. In McFarland v. American Sugar Refining Co., 241 U.S. 79 (1916), the Court rejected an equal protection challenge only because the plaintiffs failed to show unlawful discrimination; the decision confirms, however, that equal protection analysis turns on whether the challenged action irrationally singles out a party. Id. at 86-87. Likewise, in Olech, 528 U.S. at 565, the Court held that a complaint alleging intentional treatment different from that accorded others similarly situated stated a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment below therefore should be reversed.

The rule Petitioner urges is modest and faithful to precedent: where a plaintiff alleges that government intentionally treated her differently from others similarly situated and there is no rational basis for the difference, she states an equal protection claim even though she belongs to no broader class. That rule is compelled by Yick Wo, Truax, Sunday Lake Iron, Sioux City Bridge, Royster Guano, and Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal, and it is the only rule that gives effect to the Constitution’s guarantee of equal protection of the laws.
