**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that Exemption 2 of the Freedom of Information Act shields any “predominantly internal” agency document whose disclosure might risk circumvention of agency regulation. That standard is unfaithful to the statutory text, which exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The ESQD arc maps are not personnel rules or practices; they are technical safety calculations about the effects of explosions. They do not govern the employment relationship or prescribe employee conduct. They are, as the Navy’s own manual states, “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy.” The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 violates the cardinal rule that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and “must be narrowly construed.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989); *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976).

**I. The Plain Text of Exemption 2 Does Not Cover the ESQD Maps**

Exemption 2 contains three limiting terms: “internal,” “personnel,” and “rules and practices.” The ESQD maps satisfy none. They are not “internal” in the sense of housekeeping matters; they concern the safety of the surrounding community. They are not “personnel” because they do not relate to agency employees as employees. They are not “rules and practices” because they are not directives governing conduct; they are data and maps. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test effectively reads “personnel” out of the statute. Under that test, any internal document—from engineering plans to budget projections—could be withheld if disclosure might help someone circumvent agency regulation. Congress did not intend such a sweeping exemption.

This Court’s decision in *Rose* is dispositive. There, the Court considered whether Exemption 2 covered Air Force Academy honor and ethics codes. The Court held that the codes were not exempt because they were not “merely internal” matters of no “genuine and significant public interest.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The Court emphasized that Exemption 2 was designed to protect “trivial” administrative matters such as “parking facilities, lunchroom privileges, and sick leave.” *Id.* at 363. The ESQD maps are anything but trivial. They reveal the potential blast ranges of explosive ordnance stored near populated areas. The public has a profound interest in knowing whether the Navy’s storage practices endanger their lives and property.

**II. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Rewrites the Statute**

The Ninth Circuit adopted the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc). That decision is inconsistent with *Rose*. In *Rose*, this Court rejected the argument that Exemption 2 covers all internal agency matters, and it specifically limited the exemption to “personnel rules and practices.” 425 U.S. at 362-63. *Crooker* attempted to reconcile the words “related” and “solely,” but it ignored the word “personnel.” The result is a standard with no basis in the statute. This Court has never endorsed *Crooker*, and the Ninth Circuit’s decision below is the first time a court of appeals has applied it to non-personnel technical data.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on its own prior decision in *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Hardy* held only that “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2.” *Id.* at 656. That case concerned a raids and searches manual—quintessential personnel rules governing law enforcement conduct. *Hardy* did not hold that all internal documents are exempt, and it did not involve technical safety data. The Ninth Circuit’s extension of *Hardy* to the ESQD maps is a dramatic and unjustified expansion.

**III. The Risk of Circumvention Cannot Expand the Exemption**

The Ninth Circuit treated the “risk of circumvention” as the touchstone of Exemption 2. But that risk is a necessary condition for the so-called “high 2” category, not a sufficient one. The document must first fall within the statutory phrase “internal personnel rules and practices.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The ESQD maps do not. The Navy’s own declaration states that the maps are used “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” That is a far cry from a rule governing employee conduct. The Ninth Circuit’s approach collapses the two-step inquiry into a single question: would disclosure risk circumvention? That is not the law.

**IV. The Navy’s Security Concerns Are Addressed by Other Exemptions**

The government’s national security concerns do not justify rewriting Exemption 2. Congress provided specific exemptions for classified information and law enforcement records. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), (b)(7)(F). The Navy did not classify the ESQD maps, and it conceded they were not compiled for law enforcement purposes. The Navy cannot use Exemption 2 as a catch-all for information it would prefer to keep secret. As this Court has stated, FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. If the Navy believes the maps should be secret, it must classify them or invoke an applicable exemption. It cannot stretch Exemption 2 beyond its plain meaning.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
