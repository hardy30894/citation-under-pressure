**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S APPOINTED JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

This Court must determine whether Congress, in the ADEA, clearly and unequivocally intended to intrude upon the States’ traditional authority to regulate their judiciaries. *See United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Because the tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance that States have historically controlled, the ADEA should not be read to preempt Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision unless Congress explicitly manifested that intent. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 54 (1st Cir. 1988). The statutory text demonstrates that Congress did no such thing.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person … chosen by [an elected official] to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level … with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri’s appointed state judges fall squarely within the “appointee on the policymaking level” exception.

Appointed judges indisputably engage in policymaking. As the First Circuit observed, “[p]olicymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55. In Missouri, the Supreme Court has established common law for the entire State, *see Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and has resolved far-reaching ethical and public policy questions, *see Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc). Moreover, Missouri judges exercise considerable supervisory and administrative policymaking authority. The Chief Justice and presiding judges possess general supervisory powers over the court system, and the Missouri Supreme Court adopts rules governing the professional conduct of the judiciary and bar. These responsibilities place judges on the “policymaking level.”

Petitioners’ argument that the exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees lacks merit. If that were true, none of the four exemptions in § 630(f) would apply to the judicial branch in States where judges are appointed. It is “highly unlikely that Congress intended this result.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460-61 (D. Mass. 1988). The identity of powers and responsibilities between appointed and elected judges further supports this conclusion. Appointed and elected judges perform identical functions; only the method of selection differs. It would be anomalous to exempt elected judges—who are directly accountable to voters—while subjecting appointed judges—who are often insulated from the political process—to the ADEA.

Thus, because appointed state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level,” the ADEA does not extend to them, and Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is not preempted.

**II. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require that all state classifications be stricken simply because they draw lines at a particular age. The test for age-based classifications is rational basis review. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 314 (1976) (per curiam). Under this deferential standard, the classification “must be upheld against equal protection challenge if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis for the classification.” *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993).

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision easily satisfies this standard. The State could rationally conclude that judges, unlike other public employees, perform work that “makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir.), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). A mandatory retirement age “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged … judges” and avoids the “anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence.” *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). Such a clear rule also “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges.” *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 629. Furthermore, a mandatory age limit “makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary, thereby making the judiciary more representative of society.” *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146.

Because rational bases support Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision, the equal protection claim fails.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
