**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s state judges are not “employees” under the ADEA because they are “appointees on the policymaking level” within the meaning of 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603-06 (8th Cir. 1990). This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the ADEA, as well as the Supreme Court’s instruction that exemptions under the statute must be construed narrowly. *See* *EEOC v. Wyoming*, 460 U.S. 226, 231-32 (1983) (exemptions under the ADEA are to be narrowly construed).

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit properly recognized that judges exercise policymaking authority by interpreting and applying the law, filling gaps in statutes and constitutions, and establishing rules of decision that guide future conduct. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604-05 (citing *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988)). This is not merely mechanical application of settled law; it is the exercise of judgment and discretion that shapes legal policy. *Id.* at 604. As the First Circuit explained, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55.

Moreover, many Missouri judges exercise significant policymaking responsibilities in administering the state’s judicial system. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 605. The Chief Justice of the Missouri Supreme Court and other high-ranking judges supervise the courts, adopt rules governing judicial conduct, and resolve disputes within the judiciary. *Id.* These are quintessentially policymaking functions. *See also Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145 (Mass. 1988) (holding that judges are policymakers because their decisions create law and guide future conduct).

Petitioners’ argument that judges are not policymakers because they decide cases between litigants is unavailing. *See* Brief for Petitioners at 10. The ADEA does not limit the policymaking exception to executive or legislative officials; it applies to “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit correctly rejected the notion that the exception applies only to the executive and legislative branches. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 605. To hold otherwise would render the exception meaningless for state judges, a result Congress could not have intended. *Id.*

**II. THE ADEA DOES NOT SUPERSede MISSOURI’S CONSTITUTIONAL MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION BECAUSE CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY AND UNEQUIVOCALLY EXPRESS ITS INTENT TO PREempt STATE JUDICIAL RETIREMENT LAWS.**

Even if state judges were “employees” under the ADEA, the statute does not preempt Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision because Congress did not clearly and unequivocally express its intent to do so. *See* *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602-03 (citing *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971)). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that, in areas traditionally regulated by the states—such as the structure and operation of their judiciaries—federal preemption must be “clear and manifest.” *See, e.g., Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 460 (1991) (quoting *Atkinson v. Sinclair Ref. Co.*, 370 U.S. 238, 241 (1962)).

The ADEA contains no express reference to state judges or judicial retirement laws. *See* 29 U.S.C. §§ 621-634. The statute’s silence on this issue is particularly significant given that Congress amended the ADEA in 1978 and 1986 to expand its coverage, yet did not address state judicial retirement laws. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 603. This omission strongly suggests that Congress did not intend to preempt such laws. *Id.*

The Eighth Circuit’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Atkinson*, the Court held that federal labor laws did not preempt state laws governing the selection and tenure of state judges. 370 U.S. at 241. Similarly, in *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, this Court recognized that states have a strong interest in regulating the qualifications and tenure of their judges. 501 U.S. at 460. The absence of clear congressional intent to preempt state judicial retirement laws compels the conclusion that the ADEA does not apply to Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges survives rational-basis review under the Equal Protection Clause. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606-07. The provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests, including ensuring the competence and efficiency of the judiciary, reducing delays in the administration of justice, and maintaining public confidence in the courts. *Id.* at 606 (citing *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam)).

This Court has repeatedly held that age-based classifications are constitutional if they are rationally related to a legitimate government purpose. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges is no different. The provision ensures that judges retire at an age when physical and mental faculties may begin to decline, thereby safeguarding the integrity and efficiency of the judicial system. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606. The Eighth Circuit correctly recognized that these interests are legitimate and that the provision is rationally related to them. *Id.*

Petitioners’ arguments to the contrary are unpersuasive. They contend that mandatory retirement is unnecessary because other mechanisms exist to remove incompetent judges. *See* Brief for Petitioners at 25. However, this Court has rejected similar arguments in the past. *See Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314 (upholding mandatory retirement for state troopers despite the existence of other disciplinary mechanisms). The existence of alternative methods does not render the state’s chosen approach irrational. *Id.*

Moreover, Missouri’s provision promotes public confidence in the judiciary by ensuring that judges share the experiences and understandings of the majority of adult citizens. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606 (citing *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146). This is a rational and legitimate state interest. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed. The ADEA does not apply to Missouri’s state judges, and the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.
