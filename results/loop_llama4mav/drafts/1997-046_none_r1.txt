The respondent, Eugene Woodard, has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not apply to clemency proceedings because they are not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. As established by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925), clemency is an act of "mercy" exercised by the executive as a matter of discretion. This principle is reinforced by United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871), where the Court held that the President's power to grant reprieves and pardons is "unlimited" and "cannot be controlled or modified by Congress." The Court has consistently recognized that clemency decisions are not subject to judicial review.

Moreover, the creation of a clemency procedure by a state does not create a protected liberty interest. The Supreme Court's decision in Murray v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. 272, 277 (1856), established that a statutory procedure does not necessarily create a constitutional right. Similarly, in Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 603-04 (1896), the Court characterized a pardon as a "private" act of the executive that is not subject to due process requirements. The distinction between the adjudicatory process and clemency proceedings is further underscored by United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. 150, 161 (1833), where the Court described a pardon as "an act of grace" separate from the judicial process. Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. 307, 311 (1856), also held that the President's power to grant reprieves and pardons is a "constitutional duty" not subject to judicial oversight.

The respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is also without merit because the Ohio clemency procedure does not compel him to waive his rights. According to Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 111-12 (1908), the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated when a person is not compelled to testify against themselves. The respondent is not required to participate in the clemency interview and is free to assert his Fifth Amendment privilege if he chooses to participate. The Court of Appeals' remand for further proceedings is therefore premature and unnecessary, as a federal court should not intervene in a state proceeding unless there is a clear constitutional violation, as per Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496, 500-01 (1941).

In conclusion, the respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims should be reinstated.
