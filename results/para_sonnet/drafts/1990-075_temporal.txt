# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 811(h) Supplies an Intelligible Principle and Therefore Is a Constitutional Delegation of Authority

Article I vests "all legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but this Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not bar Congress from enlisting executive assistance in filling in the details of a regulatory scheme. As the Court explained in *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), Congress may delegate authority to the Executive so long as it "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Id.* at 409. That standard, not a demand for legislative micromanagement, has governed this Court's delegation jurisprudence since *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), which upheld a delegation to the President to suspend trade concessions upon a finding that a foreign nation's duties were "reciprocally unequal and unreasonable." *Id.* at 692-93.

Section 811(h) comfortably satisfies this test. Congress specified the precise finding the Attorney General must make—that scheduling is "necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety"—and directed him to consider enumerated statutory factors bearing on a substance's history, current pattern, scope, and duration of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. These are not "negligible standards." *Cf. Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation to fix commodity prices that were "fair and equitable" and would effectuate the purposes of the Emergency Price Control Act, because "it is a sufficient safeguard that the Congress has stated the legislative objective, has prescribed the method of achieving that objective... and has established standards for the guidance of the [agency's] action"). Just as the price-control standards in *Yakus* were adequate because they channeled administrative discretion toward a defined statutory goal, so too does § 811(h) cabin the Attorney General's temporary-scheduling power to a narrow class of substances presenting an imminent hazard, for a maximum period of eighteen months.

This Court has consistently sustained delegations far less confined than this one where Congress was responding to problems demanding technical expertise and rapid administrative response. In *Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator*, 312 U.S. 126 (1941), the Court upheld a delegation authorizing the Administrator to set minimum wages "consistent with... economic and financial conditions" and other broadly framed statutory objectives, observing that "the standards prescribed... are sufficiently definite and precise to enable Congress, the courts and the public to ascertain whether the Administrator... has conformed to those standards." *Id.* at 144. Likewise, in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the Court upheld the Home Owners' Loan Act's grant of authority to the Board to regulate the organization of federal savings and loan associations "under such rules and regulations as it may prescribe," reasoning that where "standards of a technical trade or occupation" are involved, and where "the field is technical, and the industry, in itself complex," Congress may leave substantial discretion to the regulator. *Id.* at 250. Congress's regulation of controlled substances—an area of shifting chemical formulations engineered specifically to evade existing law—presents precisely the kind of technical, rapidly evolving field in which this Court has permitted broad delegations. *See Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 424 (upholding delegation where "the essence of the task assigned to [the agency] was to weigh the relevant conflicting considerations and to reach an adjustment"); *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946) (upholding standard requiring corporate structures "not... unduly or unnecessarily complicate" as sufficiently definite).

Nor does the fact that violation of a temporary schedule carries criminal penalties alter the analysis. In *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742 (1948), the Court upheld a delegation authorizing recapture of "excessive profits," a standard whose violation triggered substantial financial liability, explaining that the "wide range" of judgment necessarily left to administrators reflects "a practical adjustment... to the exigencies of... government." *Id.* at 785-86. The Attorney General's temporary-scheduling power is narrower still: it is time-limited, substantively confined to statutorily enumerated factors, and requires notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. This is far more circumscribed than the delegations sustained in *United States v. Rock Royal Co-operative*, 307 U.S. 533, 574 (1939) (upholding delegation to fix milk prices "in the public interest"), or in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943) (upholding delegation to regulate broadcast licensing according to the "public interest, convenience, or necessity").

## II. The Attorney General Was Authorized to Subdelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA

Congress separately authorized the Attorney General to delegate his statutory functions to subordinate officers. Where Congress has so provided, this Court has recognized that the head of an executive department may distribute his statutory responsibilities among subordinate officials without offending the delegation doctrine, for the officer exercising the power remains answerable to, and removable by, the delegating authority. *See Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co.*, 331 U.S. 111, 121 (1947) (upholding subdelegation of price-control authority, reasoning that Congress may authorize an agency head to redelegate functions to be performed by subordinates acting in his name). The Attorney General's redelegation to the DEA Administrator—an officer within the Department of Justice charged with enforcing precisely these statutes—reflects the same permissible administrative arrangement this Court has long approved.

The convictions below should be affirmed.
