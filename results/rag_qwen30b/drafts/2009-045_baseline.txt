**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is the “most useful starting point” for determining a reasonable fee. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983); *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 898 (1984). This method is designed to reflect the value of legal services in the marketplace, including the skill, experience, and effort of counsel. To permit an enhancement based solely on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” would undermine the lodestar’s foundational principle: that a reasonable fee is already determined by the market rate and the hours reasonably expended.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to affirm a $4.5 million enhancement—nearly 43% of the total fee award—on the basis of “superb quality of counsel’s representation” and “exceptional results” directly contravenes *Blum v. Stenson*, the controlling precedent. In *Blum*, the Supreme Court rejected the notion that “the quality of representation” or “the results obtained” could serve as an independent basis for increasing the lodestar. 465 U.S. at 899. The Court emphasized that “the lodestar is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” and that “upward adjustments are not warranted merely because the attorney performed well or achieved a favorable outcome.” Id. at 898. The Court made clear that enhancements are permissible only in “rare” and “exceptional” cases, and then only when supported by “specific evidence” that the lodestar itself fails to reflect the exceptional nature of the work or the success achieved. Id. at 898–901.

Here, the district court’s enhancement rested not on specific evidence, but on the judge’s own subjective assessment of counsel’s performance—“the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case”—and his personal experience as a 58-year veteran of the bar. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This is not the kind of “specific evidence” required by *Blum*. The court did not identify any particular hours that were unusually burdensome, nor did it explain how the hourly rates charged failed to reflect the exceptional skill of counsel. Instead, it relied on a self-serving, ex parte evaluation of performance—effectively allowing the judge to testify on behalf of the plaintiffs. This violates the fundamental principle that fee awards must be based on evidence in the record, not on the judge’s personal impressions.

The dissenting opinion in the Eleventh Circuit correctly identifies the flaw: the district court’s enhancement was not supported by “specific evidence” in the record, but rather by “unidentified and unreviewable personal experience.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1251 (11th Cir. 2008) (Tjoflat, J., dissenting). The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against such judicial subjectivity. In *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986), the Court stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” The Court did not permit enhancements based on a judge’s personal admiration for counsel. Rather, it required a record that demonstrates why the lodestar, as calculated, fails to reflect the true value of the services rendered.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Those cases did not hold that enhancements based on quality or results are mandatory or even permissible absent specific evidence. *NAACP* vacated a fee award for failure to address the plaintiffs’ “exceptional success” justification, not because such a justification was valid, but because the district court had failed to consider it. 812 F.2d at 1336–37. *Norman* reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in assessing results, not because it approved enhancements based on performance. 836 F.2d at 1306. Neither case held that a district court may enhance a fee based on a judge’s personal evaluation of counsel’s skill.

The dissent’s suggestion that enhancements may be appropriate in “unpopular” or “controversial” cases—such as those involving death threats—is irrelevant here. The record shows no such adversity. The plaintiffs were foster children in a class action, and the litigation was not unpopular. The district court’s enhancement was not justified by any such exceptional circumstance. It was based solely on the judge’s personal view of counsel’s performance.

The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the idea that a fee award should be enhanced simply because the outcome was favorable or the representation was good. In *Gisbrecht v. Barnhart*, 535 U.S. 789, 798 (2002), the Court reaffirmed that “the lodestar method is the starting point for determining a reasonable fee.” It is not a starting point that can be adjusted upward at the judge’s discretion based on subjective impressions.
