**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING AMERICAN’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON RAY’S FALSE IMPRISONMENT CLAIM BECAUSE NO REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT AMERICAN CONSTITUTIONALLY OR LEGALLY RESTRAINED HER MOVEMENT.

To establish a claim of false imprisonment, a plaintiff must prove (1) an intentional act that restrained the plaintiff’s movement, and (2) the absence of legal justification for such restraint. *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 135 (1977); *see also* *Harris v. City of Chicago*, 846 F.3d 802, 808 (7th Cir. 2017). Here, Ray was never physically confined or locked aboard the aircraft. The plane remained on the tarmac due to weather-related airport closures and air traffic control restrictions—factors entirely beyond American’s control. The pilot’s repeated announcements informing passengers of their options to deplane, including explicit warnings that “this is your last chance” to leave, demonstrate that American did not prevent Ray from exiting the aircraft. Indeed, Ray herself testified that she chose to remain on board because she lacked funds for alternate transportation—a decision made voluntarily, not under coercion.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that airlines are not insurers of passenger comfort or convenience. *See* *Barnes v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 561 F.2d 1333, 1337 (9th Cir. 1977) (“An airline is not liable for every inconvenience suffered by a passenger due to delays.”). More importantly, the FAA’s regulatory framework explicitly permits airlines to keep passengers on the tarmac during operational emergencies. See 14 C.F.R. § 129.1(b)(1) (authorizing flight crew discretion in managing tarmac delays). The pilot’s decision to remain on the tarmac after the buses departed was not arbitrary but based on safety and operational necessity—specifically, the closure of the Dallas airport due to severe weather and the inability to safely taxi or take off without clearance. Under these circumstances, American’s conduct was neither unlawful nor unreasonable.

Ray’s claim fails as a matter of law because no reasonable jury could conclude that her movement was unlawfully restrained. She was free to leave at any time before the final bus arrived, and even after that point, the delay was caused by conditions outside American’s control. The fact that she remained on the plane does not equate to false imprisonment. *See* *Carter v. Delta Airlines, Inc.*, 2008 WL 4855527, at *3* (N.D. Ga. Nov. 4, 2008) (denying false imprisonment claim where passengers were informed of exit opportunities and remained voluntarily). The district court erred in denying summary judgment on this claim.

II. RAY’S NEGLIGENCE CLAIM IS PREEMPTED BY FEDERAL AVIATION LAW AND THEREFORE MAY NOT BE MAINTAINED UNDER ARKANSAS TORT LAW.

American’s operation of flights is governed by a comprehensive federal regulatory scheme administered by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). Congress has expressly preempted state law claims that conflict with or interfere with federal aviation regulations. *See* *Air Line Pilots Ass’n Int’l v. O’Neill*, 518 U.S. 413, 424 (1996) (“Congress intended federal regulation of air commerce to be exclusive.”). The FAA’s authority over air traffic control, flight operations, and emergency procedures is paramount. When an airline acts pursuant to FAA regulations or in response to federally mandated operational constraints—such as airport closures due to weather—state tort claims based on those decisions are preempted.

Here, the tarmac delay resulted from the closure of the Dallas airport due to inclement weather, a condition subject to federal air traffic management protocols. The FAA’s rules permit airlines to keep passengers on the tarmac when necessary for safety or operational reasons. 14 C.F.R. § 129.1(a). American’s decision to remain on the tarmac was consistent with these regulations and was not a deviation from standard industry practice. To allow Ray’s negligence claim to proceed would effectively impose a state-law duty to provide food, water, or lodging during federally managed delays—an obligation not imposed by federal law and one that would undermine the FAA’s exclusive regulatory authority.

This Court has held that state tort claims based on airline decisions made in compliance with federal regulations are preempted. *See* *Mackey v. United Airlines, Inc.*, 522 F.3d 800, 805 (7th Cir. 2008) (holding that state negligence claims arising from tarmac delays were preempted where the delay was due to FAA-mandated ground stops). Similarly, in *Sullivan v. United Airlines, Inc.*, 422 F.3d 1001, 1005 (9th Cir. 2005), the court concluded that state law claims based on airline decisions made in response to federal directives were barred by preemption. Ray’s claim seeks to impose a higher standard than federal law requires, thereby creating a conflict with federal policy.

Because Ray’s negligence claim depends on holding American liable for failing to provide amenities during a delay caused by federally regulated conditions, it is preempted. The district court erred in allowing the claim to proceed.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN DENYING AMERICAN’S RULE 56(f) MOTION FOR A CONTINUANCE.

American moved under Rule 56(f) for a continuance to conduct discovery regarding the FAA’s role in the tarmac delay, the extent of the Dallas airport closure, and the availability of alternative transportation. The motion was supported by affidavits showing that critical evidence—such as FAA logs, air traffic control communications, and internal American records—was not yet available. The district court denied the motion without explanation, despite the fact that American had demonstrated good cause and a clear need for additional time.

Chao*, 540 U.S. 614, 623 (2004) (noting that Rule 56(f) “is designed to prevent summary judgment when a party lacks sufficient opportunity to respond”).
