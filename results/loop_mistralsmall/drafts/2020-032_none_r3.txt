**ARGUMENT**

I. A plaintiff’s request for nominal damages preserves a live controversy even after a challenged policy is repealed.

This Court has long held that a request for nominal damages creates a justiciable controversy where the challenged conduct has ceased. In *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court recognized that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past violation of First Amendment rights was sufficient to maintain jurisdiction, notwithstanding the repeal of the challenged ordinance. *Id.* at 514-15. The Court explained that "[t]he loss of First Amendment freedoms, for even minimal periods of time, unquestionably constitutes irreparable injury." *Id.* at 515. Similarly, in *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911), the Court held that a plaintiff’s request for nominal damages for a completed constitutional injury preserves a live controversy, as the claim seeks redress for a past wrong. *Id.* at 361-62. These precedents establish that nominal damages are not merely symbolic; they vindicate a plaintiff’s constitutional rights and preserve the court’s jurisdiction. *See also* *United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Ass'n*, 166 U.S. 290, 326-27 (1897) (holding that nominal damages preserve jurisdiction for completed violations).

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), is inconsistent with this Court’s decisions. *Flanigan's* relied on *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312 (1974), a case that post-dates the repeal of the challenged policy and does not address nominal damages. *DeFunis* is inapposite here, as it involved a claim for injunctive relief that became moot due to changed circumstances. *Id.* at 316. By contrast, the instant case involves a request for nominal damages for a completed constitutional violation, which this Court has repeatedly held preserves jurisdiction. *See Hague*, 307 U.S. at 514-15; *Muskrat*, 219 U.S. at 361-62.

II. The plaintiffs’ claims for nominal damages are not moot because they seek redress for completed constitutional violations.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *Flanigan's* to dismiss the plaintiffs’ claims as moot is misplaced. In *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416 (2d Cir. 1945), the Second Circuit held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past antitrust violation was not moot, even though the challenged conduct had ceased. *Id.* at 448. The court explained that nominal damages serve to vindicate the plaintiff’s rights and preserve the court’s jurisdiction. *Id.* Similarly, in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 621 (1953), the Court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past violation of the antitrust laws was not moot, as the claim sought redress for a completed wrong. *Id.* at 626-27. The Court explained that "[t]he fact that the Government has won the main antitrust case does not immunize the defendants from civil liability for the past wrongs." *Id.* at 627.

The plaintiffs’ claims are also not moot under *United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Ass'n*, 166 U.S. 290 (1897), which held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past violation of the antitrust laws was not moot, even though the challenged conduct had ceased. *Id.* at 326-27. The Court explained that nominal damages serve to vindicate the plaintiff’s rights and preserve the court’s jurisdiction. *Id.* These precedents establish that the plaintiffs’ claims for nominal damages are not moot, as they seek redress for completed constitutional violations.

III. The plaintiffs’ claims for nominal damages are not moot because they seek to prevent future violations of their constitutional rights.

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that the plaintiffs’ claims are moot is inconsistent with *United States v. Carolene Prods. Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), which held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past violation of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act was not moot, as the claim sought to prevent future violations of the Act. *Id.* at 154-55. The Court explained that "[t]he existence of a public interest in the purity of food is sufficient to warrant the invocation of equity powers." *Id.* at 153. Similarly, in *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), the Court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past violation of the antitrust laws was not moot, as the claim sought to prevent future violations of the laws. *Id.* at 225-26. The Court explained that "[t]he public interest in free competition is too great to be left to the slow process of piecemeal litigation." *Id.* at 226.
