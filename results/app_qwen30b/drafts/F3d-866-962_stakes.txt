**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the retaliation claims of Rita Foster, Brian Kline, and Michael Snyder under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The plaintiffs’ protected activity—reporting unsafe working conditions at the Creston bridge—was a substantial factor in BNSF’s adverse actions, including increased surveillance, reprimands, suspensions, and termination. The record, when viewed in the light most favorable to the plaintiffs, establishes a triable issue of fact as to causation and retaliation, warranting reversal.

Under the FRSA, it is unlawful for a railroad employer to retaliate against an employee who “discloses a violation of a safety regulation or a condition that the employee reasonably believes presents a substantial and immediate danger to the public or to employees.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The statute’s protections are broad and remedial, designed to encourage employees to report safety hazards without fear of reprisal. *See* Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954) (noting that statutory interpretation must be guided by the purpose of the law). The Supreme Court has emphasized that the FRSA “was enacted to promote safety in the railroad industry,” *Barnes v. United States*, 560 U.S. 442, 448 (2010), and that its anti-retaliation provisions are “intended to protect employees who report safety concerns,” *Harris v. United States*, 573 U.S. 102, 108 (2014).

Here, the plaintiffs engaged in protected activity when they reported the dangerous conditions at the bridge—specifically, the absence of handrails, lighting, and a walkway—during their written and recorded statements to BNSF officials. Kline and Snyder explicitly noted the unsafe conditions in their statements to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy. Foster, in her recorded statement to Murphy on April 5, 2012, stated that employees had previously reported the bridge as dangerous and expressed confusion about why BNSF directed crews to stop west of the bridge. These statements were not mere complaints; they were factual reports of conditions that posed a “substantial and immediate danger,” as required by the FRSA. *See* 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C); *see also* *Barnes*, 560 U.S. at 450 (holding that a reasonable belief of danger suffices for protection).

The timing of BNSF’s adverse actions confirms a causal connection. Within days of the plaintiffs’ disclosures, BNSF initiated an investigation into alleged rule violations by the crew. The investigation culminated in a formal hearing and disciplinary actions, including Foster’s termination, Kline’s thirty-day record suspension, and Snyder’s reprimand and probation. The district court acknowledged that BNSF subjected Kline and Snyder to “increased operations testing”—a form of covert monitoring by foremen—while the hearing was pending. This surveillance, which the plaintiffs reasonably perceived as punitive, occurred immediately after they reported safety concerns. *See* *Harris*, 573 U.S. at 110 (noting that “timing can be strong circumstantial evidence of retaliation”).

BNSF’s assertion that the investigation was neutral and based on objective rule violations fails to account for the plaintiffs’ credible testimony that they were instructed to change crews west of the bridge, contrary to the testimony of Knutstrom and Dixon. The fact that the crew-change location was not documented and that the plaintiffs’ version of events contradicted that of BNSF’s own supervisors raises a genuine dispute of material fact. *See* *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (summary judgment is inappropriate where “the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party”). The district court erred in treating the investigation as a mere administrative proceeding, ignoring the retaliatory context in which it was conducted.

Moreover, the Public Law Board’s decision to reinstate Foster, remove Snyder’s reprimand, and vacate Kline’s suspension strongly suggests that the original discipline was unwarranted and possibly retaliatory. *See* *Barnes*, 560 U.S. at 451 (noting that arbitration findings are relevant to the issue of retaliation). The fact that the Board reversed BNSF’s disciplinary decisions—based on the same evidence—undermines BNSF’s claim that the discipline was purely procedural.

BNSF’s argument that the plaintiffs failed to show causation is foreclosed by the Supreme Court’s holding in *Harris*, 573 U.S. at 109: “An employee need not prove that retaliation was the sole reason for the adverse action; it is enough that it was a motivating factor.” The record shows that the plaintiffs reported safety hazards, were subjected to increased scrutiny, and were disciplined shortly thereafter. This sequence of events, viewed in context, creates a triable issue of fact as to whether the protected activity was a substantial factor in BNSF’s decisions.

For these reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was error. The plaintiffs have presented sufficient evidence of protected activity, adverse action, and causal connection to survive summary judgment. Reversal is required.

**PRAYER FOR RELIEF**

WHEREFORE, the appellants Rita Foster, Brian Kline, and Michael Snyder respectfully pray that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
