The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of the statute, combined with a long line of judicial precedent, supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts certain retirement funds from the bankruptcy estate. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The key issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary because it is not held for their retirement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. In Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), the Court addressed the issue of exemptions in bankruptcy proceedings. The Court held that state law exemptions are not automatically incorporated into the Bankruptcy Act, and that the Act's provisions should be carefully considered in determining the scope of exemptions. Id. at 297.

Similarly, in Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court discussed the principles guiding the interpretation of exemptions. The Court noted that exemptions are to be considered in light of the statute's purpose and context. 

In Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court emphasized the importance of adhering to the principles of equity jurisprudence when interpreting the Bankruptcy Act. The Court stated that "the provisions of the law relating to bankruptcies are to be liberally construed in favor of the bankrupt." However, this does not mean that exemptions should be extended beyond their plain meaning.

Other courts have also recognized the importance of carefully construing exemptions. In Re Lippitt, 229 F. 854 (D.C. Ohio 1915), the court considered the interpretation of exemptions under the Bankruptcy Act. See also Schwartz v. Holzman, 69 F.2d 814 (2d Cir. 1934); In re White, 61 F.2d 966 (2d Cir. 1932); Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181 (1902).

In Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, the Supreme Court noted that the Bankruptcy Code is designed to provide a fair and equitable distribution of a debtor's assets among creditors. 186 U.S. at 190. Exempting inherited IRAs from the bankruptcy estate would be inconsistent with this goal, as it would allow debtors to shield assets that are not truly retirement funds.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with this long line of precedent. By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate, the court ensured that the Bankruptcy Code is applied in a fair and equitable manner. The decision should be affirmed.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is supported by a long line of judicial precedent and is consistent with the principles guiding the interpretation of exemptions under the Bankruptcy Code. The decision should be affirmed, and inherited IRAs should not be exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy.
