The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. As the Supreme Court held in Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), interpreting the statute to permit indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns. To avoid these concerns, the Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, subject to federal court review. Id. at 682.

The Court's decision in Zadvydas was guided by the principle of statutory construction that "a statute must be construed, if fairly possible, to avoid raising serious constitutional doubts." Clark v. Martinez, 543 U.S. 371, 381 (2005) (citing United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909)). In Zadvydas, the Court applied this principle to § 1231(a)(6), holding that the statute's "reasonable time" limitation is necessary to avoid infringing on the alien's due process rights. 533 U.S. at 689.

The Fifth Circuit's pre-Zadvydas decision in this case, which held that the government may detain a resident alien based on danger to the community or risk of flight while good faith efforts to effectuate the alien's deportation continue, is no longer good law. Zadvydas v. Underdown, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). The Supreme Court's decision in Zadvydas superseded this holding, establishing that detention beyond a reasonable period is not authorized by the statute.

The "reasonable time" limitation is not a fixed period, but rather a flexible standard that depends on the circumstances of the individual case. As the Court noted in Zadvydas, "if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute." 533 U.S. at 699-700. In determining whether removal is reasonably foreseeable, the habeas court should consider the statute's basic purpose, namely assuring the alien's presence at the moment of removal. Id.

The Court's decision in Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is distinguishable from the present case. Mezei involved an alien who had been stopped at the border and had not effected entry into the United States. In contrast, Zadvydas and Ma are resident aliens who have been admitted to the United States and are subject to removal proceedings. As the Court noted in Zadvydas, "the distinction between an alien who has effected an entry into the United States and one who has never entered runs throughout immigration law." 533 U.S. at 693.

The government's argument that the statute authorizes indefinite detention is also contrary to the principles established in Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71 (1992), and Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715 (1972). In Foucha, the Court held that a state's detention of an insanity acquittee beyond a certain period without a showing of mental illness or dangerousness violates due process. 504 U.S. at 83. Similarly, in Jackson, the Court held that a state's commitment of a defendant found incompetent to stand trial must be justified by a legitimate state interest, such as restoring the defendant's competency. 406 U.S. at 738.

In this case, the government's detention of Zadvydas beyond the 90-day removal period is not justified by a legitimate state interest. As the District Court found, Zadvydas "will never be deported because there is no place to send him." Zadvydas v. Caplinger, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997). Under these circumstances, continued detention is not authorized by the statute and violates Zadvydas' due process rights.

The Supreme Court's decision in Demore v. Kim, 538 U.S. 510 (2003), is also distinguishable from the present case. Demore involved a challenge to the mandatory detention provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1226(c), which applies to aliens who are deportable due to certain criminal convictions. In contrast, Zadvydas involves a challenge to the post-removal-period detention statute, § 1231(a)(6). As the Court noted in Demore, "the detention at issue in this case [§ 1226(c)] is of a very different character" from the detention at issue in Zadvydas. 538 U.S. at 527-28.

In conclusion, the post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute contains an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, which is necessary to avoid infringing on the alien's due process rights. As the Supreme Court held in Zadvydas, if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is not authorized by the statute.
