The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. The correct interpretation of the OCSLA is that state law applies only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with" federal law. The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "applicable" in this context, but the Fifth Circuit has developed a test that requires a "significant void or gap" in federal law before state law can be adopted as surrogate federal law. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

The Ninth Circuit rejected this "gap-filling" approach, holding that state law can apply as long as it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. However, this interpretation ignores the purpose of the OCSLA, which was to provide a comprehensive body of law for the outer Continental Shelf. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). The OCSLA was enacted to address the jurisdictional dispute between the federal government and adjacent states over the outer Continental Shelf, and Congress intended for federal law to be the primary source of law governing the area.

The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, leaving no room for state law to fill gaps. The district court correctly held that the FLSA precludes the application of California's wage and hour laws to Newton's claims. The FLSA's savings clause, which allows for more protective state laws, does not create a basis for applying state law on the outer Continental Shelf. Rather, it is a recognition that state laws may provide additional protections, but it does not override the comprehensive federal scheme.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is inconsistent with the Fifth Circuit's precedent and undermines the uniformity of federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. The correct interpretation of the OCSLA is that state law applies only when there is a gap in federal law, and the FLSA's comprehensive coverage of wage and hour claims means that California's laws do not apply to Newton's claims.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated.
