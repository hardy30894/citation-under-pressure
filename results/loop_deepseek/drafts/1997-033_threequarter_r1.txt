**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s order retaining this case for trial under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) directly contravenes the plain command of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That statute provides that after coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings, each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel. The word “shall” is mandatory. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955); *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). Congress left no room for a transferee court to decide otherwise. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation—not the district court—is the sole entity authorized to remand. Section 1407(a) thus strips the transferee court of any power to retain a case for trial.

The district court’s invocation of § 1404(a) cannot override this specific statutory mandate. When a specific statute and a general one conflict, the specific governs. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must give effect to plain language). Section 1407(a) is a precise, limited grant of authority for pretrial proceedings only; § 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision. The latter cannot be used to circumvent the former’s explicit remand requirement.

Moreover, § 1404(a) authorizes transfer only “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 342–44 (1960). A district court cannot “transfer” a case to itself; that is not a transfer but a retention. *Hoffman* held that the transferee district must be one where the action “might have been brought” originally. A court already possessing the case cannot satisfy that condition because the action is already there. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 58–59 (1949), similarly recognized that § 1404(a) is a venue statute, not a grant of power to reassign cases for trial in defiance of other statutory commands.

The legislative history of § 1407 confirms that Congress intended to limit multidistrict transfers to pretrial proceedings. The statute’s structure—requiring remand at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings—reflects a policy choice that plaintiffs retain the right to trial in their chosen forum. *See United States v. National City Lines*, 334 U.S. 573, 588–89 (1948) (venue statutes strictly construed; courts may not expand them by implication). The district court’s self-assignment undermines that choice.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and cases like *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. *Pfizer* was decided after the statute’s enactment and does not bind this Court. More importantly, no pre-1970 case supports the proposition that a transferee court may use § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial. To the contrary, *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616–17 (1964), emphasized that § 1404(a) is a procedural mechanism to change venue, not to override specific statutory directives.

Finally, the district court’s order is an egregious error that warrants correction. Mandamus is appropriate to confine a lower court to its lawful jurisdiction. *Ex parte Fahey*, 332 U.S. 258, 260 (1947). The plain language of § 1407(a) leaves no discretion. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (when statute’s language is plain, the sole function of courts is to enforce it). The district court had no authority to retain this case for trial. This Court should vacate the transfer order and direct remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
