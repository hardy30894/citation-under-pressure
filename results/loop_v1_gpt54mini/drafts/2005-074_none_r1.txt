Fernandez-Vargas respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not apply to him because Congress did not clearly make the reinstatement statute reach aliens who had already reentered and remained in the United States before IIRIRA’s effective date. The Supreme Court has long held that statutes are presumed to operate prospectively absent a clear statement to the contrary. United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806); United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806); Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534-35 (1922); United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928). That presumption is at its strongest where retroactive application would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct. Id.; see also United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926).

Section 241(a)(5) did more than alter procedure. It authorized automatic reinstatement of a prior removal order and eliminated access to relief that otherwise might have been available. A statute may not be construed to upset settled expectations or impose a new disability based on past events unless Congress has made that intent unmistakable. Shwab, 258 U.S. at 534-35; Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964); United States v. Hudson, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 34 (1812). Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry occurred in 1982, long before IIRIRA. For nearly fifteen years thereafter, he lived under a legal regime in which adjustment of status remained at least potentially available. To read § 241(a)(5) to extinguish that possibility because of a pre-enactment reentry is to read the statute retroactively.

The government may contend that the statute targets continuing unlawful presence rather than past reentry. But the Court has rejected efforts to avoid retroactivity by relabeling the operative past event. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994), the Court explained that a rule is retroactive when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Earlier cases are to the same effect. Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304, 314-16 (1945); White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545, 554 (1903); Railroad Commission v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 216 U.S. 579, 584-85 (1910). Here, the critical event is Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry. Section 241(a)(5) uses that completed event to impose a new legal consequence years later. That is classic retroactive effect.

Even if the Court concludes that Congress intended § 241(a)(5) to reach pre-IIRIRA reentrants, the statute still cannot be applied to Fernandez-Vargas because doing so is impermissibly retroactive. The Court has repeatedly warned against statutes that impose “new burdens” on completed transactions or disturb “settled expectations.” Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134, 147-48 (1938); Hartung v. People of State of New York, 22 N.Y. 95, 101 (1860). Fernandez-Vargas did not merely remain in the country after 1997; he had already reentered, lived here for years, and then pursued adjustment through his marriage to a United States citizen. The government’s reading would erase, after the fact, the legal significance of those pre-1997 circumstances.

Nor can the government avoid retroactivity by characterizing the statute as regulating only future consequences of past misconduct. The Court has long distinguished prospective regulation from legislation that revives or newly burdens antecedent acts. Ogden v. Blackledge, 2 Cranch 272, 275 (1805); Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 2 Gall. 105, 139-40 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). Section 241(a)(5) falls on the wrong side of that line because its application depends entirely on the fact that Fernandez-Vargas reentered before the statute existed.

Finally, the Court should construe the statute to avoid serious fairness concerns that arise when Congress eliminates all meaningful relief without fair notice. Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 391-92 (1798); Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276-77 (1856). Fernandez-Vargas could not have ordered his affairs in 1982 to avoid a statute enacted fifteen years later. The Court should therefore hold that § 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date, and that applying it here would be impermissibly retroactive.
