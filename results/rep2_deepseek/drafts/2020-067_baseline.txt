**Argument**

The court of below properly confined its plain-error review to the record, and this Court should affirm. The touchstone of plain-error analysis is whether an error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). That inquiry necessarily looks to the evidence a jury actually considered, not extra-record materials. Permitting appellate courts to go outside the trial record would transform plain-error review into a free-ranging evidentiary hearing foreign to the role of an appellate court.

This Court has consistently held that plain-error review examines the trial record as it existed at the time of the alleged error. In *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 225 (2019), the Court recognized that a defendant challenging a conviction for the first time on appeal must “show that the error ‘actually prejudiced’ his chances of acquittal” by “point[ing] to evidence in the record that would allow a jury to find in his favor.” *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004). That “evidence” is the evidence adduced at trial, not evidence the government might have marshaled had it known the error would occur. The Court has never suggested that the government may supplement the record on appeal to cure a deficiency in its case-in-chief.

The Eleventh Circuit faithfully applied this principle. It examined the trial record—including Greer’s stipulation to prior felony convictions and the testimony presented to the jury—and determined that the jury could not have found Greer lacked knowledge of his felon status. *See Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2098 (2021) (noting that in a typical felon-in-possession case, “if a defendant has been convicted of multiple felonies, it is often ‘easy’ to conclude that the defendant knew he was a felon”). The court examined the indictment, the plea colloquy, and the evidence introduced at trial. It did not rely on evidence outside the record to determine prejudice.

Greer invites this Court to require appellate courts to ignore the trial record and speculate about what evidence the government might have presented. That approach is inconsistent with *Olano*, which instructs that plain-error review focuses on the “proceedings” below, not hypothetical proceedings. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. It also conflicts with *Dominguez Benitez*, which requires a defendant challenging a guilty plea on plain-error review to “show a reasonable probability that, but for the error, he would not have entered the plea.” 542 U.S. at 83. That showing depends on facts in the existing record, not facts that could have been developed.

Importantly, this Court has already rejected a similar argument in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002). There, the Court held that a reviewing court “may consider the whole record” in conducting plain-error review. *Id.* at 59. “Whole record” means the entire trial record, not evidence that could have been introduced. The Court expressly distinguished between the record on appeal and matters outside it: “The record in each case will be different, but the plain-error court must consider the whole record—the entire record—in deciding whether the defendant has been prejudiced.” *Id.* at 64 n.5 (emphasis added). If the Court meant to authorize consideration of matters outside the record, it would have said so. It did not.

Finally, Greer’s proposed rule would undermine finality and judicial economy. If appellate courts could consider extra-record evidence, the government would have an incentive to introduce such evidence in every case, turning plain-error review into a mini-trial. The better course is to adhere to the settled rule: the reviewing court examines the record before the district court, and if the error affected substantial rights, the court may then consider whether to exercise its discretion to notice the error. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736-37.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied plain-error review. Its judgment should be affirmed.
