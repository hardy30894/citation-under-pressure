**ARGUMENT**

I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to reentries completed before its April 1, 1997 effective date.

Congress did not clearly direct that the reinstatement bar reach aliens whose unlawful reentry predated IIRIRA. Absent such an “express command,” statutes are presumed to operate only prospectively. Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994). The text of § 241(a)(5) speaks only of an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally,” without temporal limitation or reference to pre-enactment conduct. This silence is decisive. See INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 317 (2001) (requiring “clear” statement before construing immigration statutes to attach new disabilities to completed acts).

II. Application of § 241(a)(5) to pre-IIRIRA reentries is impermissibly retroactive.

Even if the statute were ambiguous, its application here would be retroactive. A statute operates retroactively when it “attaches a new disability” to conduct “completed before its enactment.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269. Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry was lawful under then-existing law; he could seek adjustment of status after ten years of continuous presence. Section 241(a)(5) now extinguishes that opportunity and mandates reinstatement without hearing. This “new legal consequence” attached solely because of pre-enactment conduct. Id. at 270; see also Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997) (elimination of defense constitutes retroactive effect).

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on the absence of a “protectable expectation” misapplies St. Cyr. There the Court held that the elimination of discretionary relief available at the time of the underlying conduct triggers the presumption against retroactivity, regardless of whether the alien had a vested right. 533 U.S. at 321. Fernandez-Vargas’s eligibility for adjustment was precisely such a “settled expectation.” Because Congress supplied no clear statement overcoming the presumption, § 241(a)(5) cannot be applied to him.
