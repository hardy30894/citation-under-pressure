ARGUMENT

When a design patent covers only a component of a larger article, the patentee may recover only those profits fairly attributable to the patented component. This Court has long required apportionment in such circumstances. In Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), the Court held that where the patent “is only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits,” the patentee “is only entitled to recover that part of the net gains” and must “give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits . . . between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” Id. at 614-15. The same rule appears in Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480 (1854), where the Court rejected an instruction permitting recovery “as great damages for the infringement of the patent in his second count as if he had proceeded on both counts,” because the patent covered only an improvement. Id. at 490.

Design patents receive identical treatment. Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), held that a design-patent plaintiff cannot recover the “entire profit from the manufacture and sale of the carpet” absent proof that the design supplied the entire value; only “the value which the designs contributed” is recoverable. Id. at 444-45. Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), reaffirmed that rule, insisting that damages reflect “not merely the value which the designs contributed to the carpets.” Id. at 17. McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co., 141 U.S. 459 (1891), likewise required comparison of the patented improvement against “what was known and open to the public,” rejecting recovery measured by the whole machine. Id. at 463-64.

The burden rests on the patentee. Westinghouse placed on the plaintiff the duty to furnish “reliable and tangible” evidence of apportionment or to show that “the entire value of the whole machine . . . is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” 225 U.S. at 615. Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390 (1940), applied the same principle to profits, directing that the owner receive “only that part of the profits found to be attributable to the use of the copyrighted material as distinguished from what the infringer himself has supplied.” Id. at 396. Lehnbeuter v. Holthaus, 105 U.S. 94 (1882), confirmed that a design patent’s validity and utility do not automatically entitle the patentee to the profits of the entire article. Id. at 96-97.

Sperry Gyroscope Co. v. Arma Engineering Co., 271 U.S. 232 (1926), and F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Contemporary Arts, Inc., 344 U.S. 228 (1952), underscore that recovery remains limited to the proven contribution of the protected element. Because Apple’s design patents cover only selected features of multifunctional smartphones, the award must be confined to the profits fairly traceable to those features.
