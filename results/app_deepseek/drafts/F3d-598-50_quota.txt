**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred by Admitting Prejudicial Opinion Testimony That Vouched for Witnesses, Stated Legal Conclusions, and Interpreted Evidence**

The jury heard two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant opine on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, on whether probable cause existed, and on whether certain evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants’ case. This testimony violated three fundamental evidentiary prohibitions.

First, a witness may not vouch for the credibility of another witness. *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 1988) (prohibiting testimony that “express[es] a personal belief in the credibility of a witness”). The ADAs and lieutenant repeatedly stated that Ramos and Rivera were truthful, directly invading the jury’s province. *See United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18 (1985) (condemning “prosecutorial vouching” as improper).

Second, a witness may not testify in the form of legal conclusions. *United States v. Bilzerian*, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991) (holding that “testimony constituting a legal conclusion is inadmissible”). The ADAs opined that probable cause existed to arrest and charge Cameron—a pure legal determination reserved for the jury. *See United States v. Duncan*, 42 F.3d 97, 101 (2d Cir. 1994) (excluding expert testimony that “usurp[s] the jury’s function”).

Third, a witness may not interpret evidence that jurors can equally well analyze. *United States v. Mejia*, 545 F.3d 179, 197 (2d Cir. 2008) (excluding testimony that “merely tells the jury what result to reach”). The lieutenant testified that certain photographs “supported” the officers’ account, improperly weighing evidence for the jury. *See United States v. Rea*, 958 F.2d 1206, 1216 (2d Cir. 1992) (excluding testimony that “invade[s] the province of the jury”).

**II. The Erroneous Admission Was Not Harmless**

The improper testimony went to the two central issues: credibility and probable cause. Because the government’s case rested entirely on the officers’ word against Appellants’, the error was not harmless. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946) (error is harmful if it “substantially influenced” the verdict). The ADAs and lieutenant—perceived as neutral government officials—gave the jury a false imprimatur of reliability. *See United States v. Brown*, 776 F.2d 397, 400 (2d Cir. 1985) (reversing where improper vouching “may have tipped the scales”). A new trial is required.

**III. The District Court Also Erred in Its Jury Instructions**

Although the Court remands for a new trial, Appellants preserve two instructional errors. First, the court should have instructed that, under New York law, the offense of obstructing governmental administration requires that the officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *People v. Lupinacci*, 191 A.D.2d 779, 780 (3d Dep’t 1993) (holding that “the legality of the underlying arrest is an element”). Without this instruction, the jury could convict Higgenbottom even if the arrest of Cameron was unlawful.

Second, Appellants were entitled to a punitive damages instruction. A jury could infer malice from evidence that Ramos and Rivera fabricated a story. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages allowed for “reckless or callous disregard” of rights). The district court’s refusal to give this instruction was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with this Court’s opinion.
