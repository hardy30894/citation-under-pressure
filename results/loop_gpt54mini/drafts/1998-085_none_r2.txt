Respondent’s punitive-damages claim fails because Title VII does not authorize punishment whenever a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination. Congress permitted punitive damages only where the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights” of the plaintiff. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That text requires a heightened culpable state of mind, and it does not turn every unlawful employment decision into an “egregious” case warranting exemplary damages.

The common law confirms that punitive damages are exceptional. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court recognized punitive damages as an extraordinary remedy, not an automatic incident of liability. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818), the Court explained that exemplary damages depend on aggravated fault rather than mere wrongdoing. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), likewise treated punitive damages as available only upon a showing of serious culpability. Those decisions reflect the longstanding rule that punishment follows aggravated misconduct, not simple proof that a defendant acted unlawfully.

The Court’s civil-rights precedents point in the same direction. Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880), held that racial exclusion from juries violates the Constitution, but it did not suggest that every discrimination violation carries punitive sanctions. Virginia v. Rives, 100 U.S. 313 (1880), and Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339 (1880), condemned discriminatory state action, yet neither decision equated liability with punishment. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), similarly established unlawful discrimination without implying automatic exemplary damages. These cases show that proof of discrimination and proof of punitive entitlement are distinct inquiries.

That distinction is critical here. Petitioner showed, at most, that ADA unlawfully chose one candidate over another. The jury awarded back pay, which fully compensates the proven injury. But punitive damages are different: they are reserved for conduct sufficiently aggravated to justify punishment. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), and Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251 (1946), demonstrate that damages law distinguishes ordinary compensation from enhanced recovery. Nothing in those decisions suggests that exemplary damages follow automatically from liability.

Petitioner’s position would collapse Congress’s separate requirement of “malice or reckless indifference” into the bare fact of intentional discrimination. Courts must avoid construing statutory language to render terms superfluous. If every intentional Title VII violation qualified for punitive damages, the statute’s limiting language would do no work. See Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339 (1880); Yick Wo, 118 U.S. 356 (1886). The better reading is that Congress preserved the traditional rule: punitive damages require proof of aggravated misconduct, not merely a discriminatory result.

The common-law authorities are especially instructive because they show that exemplary damages have always depended on the nature of the wrong. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, the Court declined to impose punishment absent the necessary degree of fault. In Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101, the Court again emphasized the limited and exceptional character of punitive recovery. And in Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, the Court treated punitive damages as a distinct remedy for conduct more blameworthy than that necessary to establish liability. Those principles fit Title VII’s text far better than petitioner’s rule.

Accordingly, the court below correctly refused to allow punitive damages on the record here. Petitioner proved intentional discrimination, but not the sort of aggravated, punishment-worthy conduct that the statute requires. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
