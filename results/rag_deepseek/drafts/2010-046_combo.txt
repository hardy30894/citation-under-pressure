The PLRA does not bar the population-reduction order because it is the least intrusive means necessary to remedy ongoing Eighth Amendment violations. The Constitution requires that prisons provide adequate medical care. As this Court recognized in *United States v. Muniz*, 374 U.S. 150, 155 (1963), the Bureau of Prisons must “provide suitable quarters and provide for the safekeeping, care, and subsistence of all persons charged with or convicted of offenses.” That duty extends to state prisons through the Eighth Amendment. In *Sweeney v. Woodall*, 344 U.S. 86, 87 (1953), the Court acknowledged that a prisoner may assert that “his past confinement had amounted to cruel and unusual punishment.” Such claims are justiciable, and federal courts have authority to remedy them.

The three-judge court found that overcrowding is the primary cause of unconstitutional conditions. That finding is entitled to deference. In *United States v. Jones*, 345 U.S. 377, 378 (1953), the Court held that it “cannot re-examine the information and construe it de novo.” Similarly, this Court should defer to the district court’s factual findings and its conclusion that no alternative remedy would suffice.

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn” and the “least intrusive means” to correct the violation. Here, the court considered alternatives—including expanded medical facilities and increased staffing—and found them inadequate. The order is specifically tailored to the constitutional violation. This Court has “constantly emphasized the fundamental importance of the writ of habeas corpus in our constitutional scheme.” *Johnson v. Avery*, 393 U.S. 483, 485 (1969). The same fundamental importance attaches to the Eighth Amendment right to adequate medical care. Courts must protect that right even when it requires state action. In *Houghton v. Shafer*, 392 U.S. 639, 640 (1968), this Court granted certiorari and reversed a dismissal of a prisoner’s civil rights claim, demonstrating that prison officials are subject to constitutional constraints.

The PLRA does not make statutory remedies exclusive. In *United States v. Demko*, 385 U.S. 149, 150 (1966), the Court held that “compensation under 18 U.S.C. § 4126 was not his exclusive remedy” for injuries suffered in prison. Likewise, the PLRA’s procedural requirements do not preclude a remedy that is necessary to cure a constitutional violation. The order here is not a “prisoner release order” as defined by the PLRA; it is a remedy for unconstitutional conditions. The state’s power to classify prisoners does not override constitutional rights. As *Finley v. California*, 222 U.S. 28, 30 (1911), noted, “Manifestly there could be no extension of the term of imprisonment as a punishment for crimes they might commit.” The state cannot use its classification power to perpetuate unconstitutional conditions.

Finally, the state’s Eleventh Amendment immunity is not violated because the state consented to the receivership and the underlying orders. Federal authority over prisoners is paramount. In *Ponzi v. Fessenden*, 258 U.S. 254, 260 (1922), the Court stated that “no state court could assume control of his body without the consent of the United States.” Here, the federal court properly exercised its authority to remedy constitutional violations.

The PLRA does not bar the order. The district court acted within its equitable power to enforce the Eighth Amendment. The judgment should be affirmed.
