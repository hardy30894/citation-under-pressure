ARGUMENT

I. SECTION 241(a)(5) DOES NOT REACH ALIENS WHO REENTERED THE UNITED STATES BEFORE THE STATUTE'S 1997 EFFECTIVE DATE

Congress does not write retroactive statutes by accident. When Congress has intended a deportation provision to reach pre-enactment conduct, it has said so expressly. In the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, Congress specifically provided that certain deportation grounds applied "notwithstanding (1) that any such alien entered the United States prior to the date of enactment of this Act, or (2) that the facts, by reason of which any such alien belongs to any of the classes enumerated in subsection (a), occurred prior to the date of enactment of this Act." Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685, 686 (1957) (quoting INA § 241(d)). This Court in Lehmann relied on that explicit language to hold that a new deportation ground for pre-Act crimes applied despite a general savings clause. Id. The companion case, Mulcahey v. Catalanotte, 353 U.S. 692 (1957), confirms the point: it was only because Congress had spoken with that unmistakable clarity that a deportation ground could reach conduct—there, a 1925 narcotics conviction—that predated the statute creating deportability. Id. at 692-93.

Section 241(a)(5) contains no such clause. It says nothing about aliens who reentered before its 1997 effective date, and it contains none of the express backward-reaching language Congress used elsewhere in the very same title of the INA. Congress's demonstrated capacity to draft explicit retroactivity language when it wishes to do so, and its failure to do so here, is telling. Where Congress knows how to reach backward and does not, courts should not do it for them.

This reading is reinforced by the settled canon that ambiguity in a deportation statute is resolved in the alien's favor. In Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Errico, 385 U.S. 214 (1967), this Court confronted "the issue is whether the statute saves from deportation an alien who misrepresents his status for the purpose of evading quota restrictions, if he has the necessary familial relationship to a United States citizen or lawful permanent resident." Id. at 214. This Court read the exemption generously, in favor of the alien and family unity, rather than adopting the narrower construction advanced by the government. The same interpretive charity forecloses reading silence in § 241(a)(5) as an implicit command to reach back nineteen years and erase relief that was available when Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982.

II. RETROACTIVE APPLICATION OF SECTION 241(a)(5) TO FERNANDEZ-VARGAS'S 1982 REENTRY IS IMPERMISSIBLE

Even if the statute's text could be stretched to cover a pre-1997 reentry, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas attaches a new and disabling legal consequence—total ineligibility for any relief—to a completed act taken nineteen years before the statute existed, thereby upsetting the settled expectations he built during his lawful residence, marriage, and family life.

The relief Fernandez-Vargas sought, adjustment of status, has long depended on exactly the kind of accumulated equities that reinstatement now wipes away. In Mrvica v. Esperdy, 376 U.S. 560 (1964), this Court examined an alien's eligibility for discretionary adjustment under INA § 249 and the "prerequisite continuous residence in the United States" that such relief required. Id. at 561. The statutory scheme has always tied relief to years of residence, family ties, and good conduct—not to snap judgments made at the moment of an old, uninspected entry. Congress structured suspension of deportation the same way: an alien could obtain relief where deportation "would result in serious economic detriment to a citizen or legally resident alien who is the spouse, parent, or minor child of such deportable alien." United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy, 353 U.S. 72, 73 (1957). Likewise, in Foti v. Immigration & Naturalization Service, 375 U.S. 217, 218 (1963), a petitioner was found ineligible for suspension because his deportation would not result in "exceptional and extremely unusual hardship." Fernandez-Vargas's marriage to a United States citizen and his application to adjust status fit squarely within this tradition of individualized relief that Congress built into the INA precisely to account for equities accrued over years of residence.

Reinstatement collapses that individualized inquiry into an automatic, retroactive bar. That is a sharp break from this Court's oldest understanding of the deportation power, which contemplated that the deciding official, after a hearing, "may order a release or deportation, as in his judgment the case may warrant." Low Wah Suey v. Backus, 225 U.S. 460, 468 (1912). Congress's later enactments preserved discretionary, case-by-case adjustment relief, including for aliens seeking to adjust status under 8 U.S.C. § 1153(a)(7). See Cheng Fan Kwok v. Immigration & Naturalization Service, 392 U.S. 206, 207 (1968). Retroactively foreclosing that relief for a reentry that occurred fifteen years before § 241(a)(5) was enacted—when Fernandez-Vargas could not have known his reentry would later be transformed into an absolute bar to any relief—imposes exactly the kind of new disability on completed conduct that this Court's retroactivity precedents forbid.

For these reasons, § 241(a)(5) should not be applied to reinstate the 1981 deportation order against Fernandez-Vargas based on his 1982 reentry.
