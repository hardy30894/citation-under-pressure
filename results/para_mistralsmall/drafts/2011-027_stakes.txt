**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Equal Footing Doctrine by Determining Navigability at Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine provides that when a state enters the Union, it takes title to the beds and banks of rivers that are navigable for title purposes within its borders. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 28 (1935). This Court has long held that the test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was capable of supporting interstate or foreign commerce at the time the state entered the Union. *See Packard v. Baird*, 84 U.S. 168, 177 (1872) ("The true test of the navigability of a river is its capacity for valuable public use as a highway of commerce at the time the state was admitted into the Union."); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931) ("The test of navigability is the capacity of the river to be used as a highway for commerce of a substantial and commercial character at the time the state was admitted into the Union.").

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this test by examining whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 2011 MT 333, ¶ 70, 368 Mont. 1, 293 P.3d 1183. The court relied on historical evidence, including government surveys, territorial records, and contemporaneous accounts, to determine navigability at statehood. *Id.* at ¶¶ 71-75. This approach is consistent with this Court’s precedent, which permits the use of historical evidence to establish navigability at the time of statehood. *See, e.g., United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 28 (relying on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood).

PPL’s contention that the court should have considered post-statehood evidence is unavailing. The equal footing doctrine is clear: title vests at statehood based on the river’s navigability at that time. *Packard*, 84 U.S. at 177. Post-statehood evidence is irrelevant to this inquiry. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75 ("The test is not what the river is today, but what it was at the time the state was admitted into the Union.").

**II. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt Montana’s Ability to Seek Compensation for PPL’s Use of State-Owned Riverbeds.**

PPL argues that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s claims under the Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA) because FERC’s licensing authority displaces state regulation of riverbeds. This argument fails. The FPA expressly preserves state authority over state-owned lands, including riverbeds. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946) ("The Federal Power Act establishes a dual system of control between the states and the federal government."); *see also Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state property rights).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the FPA does not preempt Montana’s claims. *PPL Montana*, 2011 MT 333, ¶¶ 14-15. The FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the use of state-owned lands, and it does not divest states of their property rights. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76. PPL’s reliance on *Niagara Mohawk* is misplaced, as that case addressed federal preemption of state regulation of rates, not state property rights. *Niagara Mohawk*, 347 U.S. at 249-51.

**III. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar Montana’s Claims.**

PPL contends that the federal navigational servitude preempts Montana’s claims because it extinguishes state title to riverbeds underlying navigable waters. This argument is incorrect. The navigational servitude does not divest states of their title to riverbeds; it merely ensures that navigable waters remain open to interstate commerce. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) ("The federal navigational servitude is the power of Congress to ensure that navigable waters remain open to interstate and foreign commerce."). States retain their title to riverbeds, subject to the servitude. *Id.*

The Montana Supreme Court properly recognized that the navigational servitude does not preempt Montana’s claims. *PPL Montana*, 2011 MT 333, ¶ 11. The servitude does not extinguish state title or bar compensation claims for the use of state-owned riverbeds. *See United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941) (holding that the servitude does not divest states of their title to riverbeds).

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine by determining navigability at statehood, and its decision is consistent with this Court’s precedent. The FPA and the federal navigational servitude do not preempt Montana’s claims. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
