ARGUMENT

Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not apply to an alien whose reentry occurred before its 1997 effective date. The statute effects a substantive change in the consequences of prior illegal reentry, and Congress did not clearly intend such retroactive operation. See Mulcahey v. Catalanotte, 353 U.S. 692, 694 (1957) (savings clause preserves “status” of nondeportability arising from pre-Act conduct); Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957) (same; § 241(d) does not override savings where prior law conferred immunity); Mrvica v. Esperdy, 376 U.S. 560, 563-64 (1964) (departure under pre-Act order does not forfeit continuous-residence rights absent explicit provision).

Even if the text reaches pre-1997 reentries, the application is impermissibly retroactive because it attaches new disabilities to completed acts. The reinstatement bar eliminates all forms of relief previously available, thereby “chang[ing] the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date.” See INS v. Errico, 385 U.S. 214, 216 (1967) (construing § 241(f) to avoid retroactive forfeiture of relief); Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193, 195 (1958) (parolees retain rights under pre-Act law absent clear contrary command).

This Court has long required an express statement before immigration statutes divest accrued rights. Low Wah Suey v. Backus, 225 U.S. 460, 466 (1912) (deportation provisions operate prospectively unless Congress “otherwise specifically provided”); Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48, 51 (1955) (finality language does not eliminate review of rights fixed under prior law); Foti v. INS, 375 U.S. 217, 220 (1963) (discretionary relief determinations reviewable to protect pre-existing entitlements); Cheng Fan Kwok v. INS, 392 U.S. 206, 210 (1968) (ancillary orders affecting substantive rights subject to full review); United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy, 353 U.S. 72, 75 (1957) (suspension eligibility judged under law in force at time of application).

Petitioner’s 1982 reentry therefore remains governed by the law then in effect, and the Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding must be reversed.
