**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Bora Corp. because genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding whether the vessel breached its “active operations” duty under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b). The Supreme Court in *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos* established that a vessel owes three duties to longshoremen: the turnover duty, the duty to avoid negligence in areas under its control, and the duty to intervene when it has actual knowledge of a dangerous condition that the stevedore is not correcting. 451 U.S. 156, 167–79 (1981). Here, the vessel’s crew left a 100-pound snatch block in the “down” position, blocking cargo removal and creating a hazard. That condition—the block’s improper placement—was affirmatively created by the vessel’s crew, triggering the duty to avoid active negligence. *See Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co.*, 512 U.S. 92, 98 (1994) (vessel must exercise reasonable care to prevent injury from conditions under its control).

**A. The vessel created a dangerous condition and had actual knowledge of it.**

The record shows that the vessel’s crew operated the block-and-pulley system and left the snatch block in the down position. This was a deviation from standard practice, which required the blocks to be returned to the upright position and tied off after hatch covers were opened. The crew’s own actions thus created a dangerous condition—the block rested on cargo, impeding removal and threatening the safety of longshoremen working in the wing area. *See Turner v. Costa Line*, 804 F.2d 257, 260 (3d Cir. 1986) (vessel is liable for injuries caused by its own active negligence in leaving equipment in a hazardous state). The vessel had actual knowledge of the block’s position because the crew itself had placed it there. *See Bender v. Consolidated Rail Corp.*, 990 F.2d 101, 105 (3d Cir. 1993) (vessel’s actual knowledge of a dangerous condition, combined with a reasonable opportunity to correct it, gives rise to a duty to intervene).

**B. The vessel had a duty to intervene before Serbin attempted to move the block.**

Once the vessel knew the block was left down, it had a duty to take reasonable steps to protect longshoremen. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 175–76. The vessel could have sent a crew member to reposition the block, warned Serbin not to move it, or secured the area. Instead, it did nothing. *See Davis v. Portline Transportes Maritime, Int’l*, 16 F.3d 532, 538 (3d Cir. 1994) (vessel breaches duty by failing to correct a known hazard that the stevedore cannot reasonably avoid). Serbin’s decision to move the block himself—to avoid delaying the unloading—does not absolve the vessel of its own negligence. *See Martinez v. Korea Shipping Corp.*, 903 F.2d 606, 609 (9th Cir. 1990) (longshoreman’s reasonable response to a vessel-created hazard does not break the causal chain). The vessel cannot shift its duty to the longshoreman by simply waiting for him to act. *Green v. Crow*, 831 F.2d 71, 73 (5th Cir. 1987) (vessel must take affirmative steps when it knows the stevedore is not addressing a danger).

**C. The dangerous condition was not so open and obvious as to negate the vessel’s duty.**

While Serbin saw the block was down, the hazard of moving it—the block’s weight, the lack of safe footing, and the risk of falling—was not equally apparent. The vessel, which designed and operated the equipment, knew the block weighed 100 pounds and required special handling. *See Coker v. M/V Patria*, 869 F.2d 290, 293 (5th Cir. 1989) (vessel may be liable for a hidden danger even if the condition itself is visible). Moreover, the vessel’s own crew had the expertise and equipment to move the block safely; Serbin did not. The district court erred in concluding that the condition was “open and obvious” as a matter of law, because the severity of the risk and the feasibility of the vessel’s intervention are disputed facts. *See Treadway v. United States*, 690 F.2d 372, 376 (3d Cir. 1982) (vessel’s duty to warn of latent dangers extends to hazards that a reasonable longshoreman might not fully appreciate).

**D. Summary judgment was improper because genuine issues of material fact remain.**

The record reveals factual disputes: whether the vessel’s crew negligently left the block down, whether the vessel had a reasonable opportunity to correct it before Serbin acted, and whether Serbin’s decision to move the block was reasonable under the circumstances. The district court, by granting summary judgment, improperly resolved these disputes in the vessel’s favor. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (summary judgment appropriate only when no reasonable jury could find for the nonmovant). Even if Serbin were contributorily negligent, the Longshore Act applies comparative fault principles—his negligence does not bar recovery but merely reduces damages. *See* 33 U.S.C. § 905(b); *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 178 n.23.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
