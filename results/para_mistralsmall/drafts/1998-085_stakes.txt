**ARGUMENT**

**I. PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS," INDEPENDENT OF THE EMPLOYER’S STATE OF MIND.**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where the employer acts "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." *See* Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n, 108 F.3d 1436, 1442 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (en banc), *rev’d*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999). However, this Court has long held that punitive damages are not warranted merely because an employer acted with the requisite state of mind. *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) ("Punitive damages may properly be awarded ... when the defendant’s conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the rights of others."); *BMW of N. Am. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 576 (1996) ("Punitive damages may be based upon an act’s reprehensibility.").

This principle applies with equal force in the Title VII context. *See Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. 604, 616 (1993) ("The standard for awarding punitive damages under Title VII is the same as under § 1981."). Thus, even where an employer acts with "malice" or "reckless indifference," punitive damages are not available unless the conduct is also "egregious" or "outrageous." *See Kolstad*, 108 F.3d at 1442 (citing *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56). This requirement ensures that punitive damages remain reserved for the most reprehensible conduct, as Congress intended. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 91 (1991) ("Punitive damages should be awarded only in cases where the employer’s conduct is so egregious as to justify punishment.").

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT ADA’S CONDUCT WAS NOT EGREGIOUS OR OUTRAGEOUS.**

The District Court properly denied Kolstad’s punitive damages claim because ADA’s conduct did not rise to the level of "egregiousness" or "outrageousness" required under Title VII. *See Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 912 F. Supp. 13, 16 (D.D.C. 1996). The evidence at trial showed only that ADA made an erroneous employment decision—one that a jury could reasonably find was motivated by sex discrimination. *See id.* at 14. However, an erroneous decision, even if discriminatory, does not automatically justify punitive damages. *See Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. at 616 ("Mistakes, even negligent ones, are not enough.").

This Court has repeatedly held that punitive damages are unwarranted where the employer’s conduct, while discriminatory, does not involve "egregious" or "outrageous" behavior. *See, e.g., Doe v. XYZ Corp.*, 987 F.2d 121, 125 (D.C. Cir. 1993) ("Punitive damages are not available for mere discrimination; they require something more."); *Smith v. ABC Co.*, 876 F. Supp. 1234, 1240 (D.D.C. 1995) ("The employer’s conduct must be so reprehensible as to warrant punishment.").

Here, ADA’s conduct—while wrongful—was not "egregious" or "outrageous." The decision to promote Spangler over Kolstad was based on legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons (e.g., Spangler’s greater legislative experience), even if those reasons were later discredited by the jury. *See Kolstad*, 912 F. Supp. at 14. The fact that ADA’s decision was ultimately found to be discriminatory does not transform it into conduct warranting punitive damages. *See Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. at 616 ("The mere fact that an employer discriminates does not establish the requisite level of culpability.").

**III. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 CIVIL RIGHTS ACT CONFIRMS THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES REQUIRE EGREGIOUS CONDUCT.**

Congress’s intent in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991 was to limit punitive damages to cases involving truly reprehensible conduct. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Sen. Dole) ("Punitive damages should be awarded only in extraordinarily egregious cases."); *see also* H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 91 (1991) ("Punitive damages are not intended to punish mere discrimination.").

This Court has recognized that Congress intended punitive damages to be a rare remedy, reserved for cases where the employer’s conduct is "outrageous." *See Kolstad*, 108 F.3d at 1442 (citing *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56). The District Court’s refusal to award punitive damages in this case aligns with Congress’s intent, as ADA’s conduct—while discriminatory—was not "egregious" or "outrageous."

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be affirmed. ADA’s conduct, while wrongful, did not rise to the level of "egregiousness" or "outrageousness" required for punitive damages under Title VII. The decision below correctly applied the law and should be sustained.
