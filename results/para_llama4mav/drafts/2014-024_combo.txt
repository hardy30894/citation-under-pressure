The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because the trial court's jury instruction on the standard for a "true threat" was erroneous. The instruction, which required only an objective intent to threaten, was insufficient to convict Elonis under § 875(c).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech, even speech that is unpopular or disturbing, unless it falls within a narrow category of exceptions, such as "true threats." See Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (establishing that speech can be restricted if it poses a "clear and present danger"). However, the Court has also emphasized the importance of intent in determining whether speech constitutes a true threat. In Frohwerk v. United States, 249 U.S. 204 (1919), the Court considered the intent behind the defendant's speech in determining whether it was a true threat.

The requirement of intent is rooted in the common law understanding of criminal liability, which generally requires a culpable mental state. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250-51 (1952) (noting that "crime, as a general concept, is composed of an evil-meaning mind with an evil-doing hand"). This principle is particularly relevant in cases involving speech, where the risk of chilling protected expression is high. See Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 259 (1937) (emphasizing the need for clarity in statutes regulating speech to avoid chilling protected expression).

In cases involving threats, the intent behind the statement is crucial in determining whether it is a true threat. See Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam) (reversing a conviction under a federal threat statute because the defendant's statement was made in a political context and was not intended as a threat). The context and circumstances surrounding the statement are also important in determining its meaning. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-10 (1940) (reversing a conviction for breach of the peace because the defendant's speech, while potentially provocative, was not intended to incite violence).

The Third Circuit's decision in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee that it would be interpreted as a threat, is inconsistent with these principles. By focusing solely on the objective reasonableness of the statement, the Kosma standard fails to account for the defendant's subjective intent.

Other cases have also highlighted the importance of considering the context and intent behind a statement. See Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616, 627-28 (1919) (Holmes, J., dissenting) (arguing that the defendant's intent and the context of their speech should be considered in determining whether it poses a clear and present danger). The objective standard adopted by the Third Circuit is also at odds with the Supreme Court's emphasis on the importance of scienter in criminal cases. See United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, 251-52 (1922) (noting that "the knowledge of the accused" is often a critical element in determining criminal liability).

In light of these precedents, the trial court's instruction, which required only an objective intent to threaten, was insufficient to convict Elonis under § 875(c). To convict someone of transmitting a threat, the government should be required to prove that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten the victim. This approach is more consistent with the First Amendment's protection of speech and the common law understanding of criminal liability.

The Third Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Elonis' conviction should be vacated.
