# ARGUMENT

**THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR TITLE NAVIGABILITY DOES NOT REQUIRE COURTS TO FIX NAVIGABILITY EXCLUSIVELY AT THE MOMENT OF STATEHOOD**

## I. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Vests Title in the State Based on the River's Navigable Character, Not a Frozen Evidentiary Snapshot

Under the equal-footing doctrine, each new State acquires title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders upon admission to the Union. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845). This Court has consistently held that title to riverbeds depends on the river's navigability, a question governed by federal law. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931). The touchstone of this inquiry is whether the river was, in its ordinary condition, "used, or susceptible of being used, in [its] ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871).

Respondent does not dispute that title vests as of statehood. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75. But the timing of vesting does not dictate the *evidentiary method* by which navigability must be proved. Nothing in this Court's precedent requires a trial court to freeze its inquiry solely to the literal date of admission, ignoring evidence of the river's physical character and capacity for use drawn from the years immediately surrounding statehood. To the contrary, this Court has repeatedly approved reliance on evidence of use both before and after the critical date as probative of the river's navigable *capacity* at statehood.

## II. This Court's Precedent Permits Courts to Consider Evidence of Use Before and After Statehood as Probative of Navigability

In *The Montello*, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430 (1874), this Court examined evidence of navigation occurring after the relevant territorial period to determine whether the Fox River was navigable, explaining that the river's "capability of use by the public for purposes of transportation and commerce" was the relevant test, not merely a snapshot of actual use on a single date. *Id.* at 441-42. Similarly, in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), this Court considered testimony regarding the historical use of the Des Plaines River extending over decades to assess its susceptibility to navigation, recognizing that "the natural and ordinary condition" of a river must be assessed using the fullest available historical record. *Id.* at 122.

This approach is consistent with *United States v. Utah*, where the Court examined evidence of navigation on various stretches of the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers occurring both before and after Utah's admission in 1896, holding certain segments navigable based on that broader record while finding others non-navigable. 283 U.S. at 82-87. The Court did not require exclusive proof of use precisely coincident with the date of statehood; rather, it treated the entire body of evidence—including post-statehood surveys and use—as bearing on the river's inherent capacity. *Id.* at 76 ("the crucial question is whether the river in its natural state was ... used or susceptible of use").

Likewise, in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), this Court relied on a wide array of historical materials, including government surveys, explorer accounts, and testimony concerning use across a span of years, to determine the navigable status of the Red River for equal-footing purposes. *Id.* at 585-86.

## III. Segment-by-Segment Analysis Using the Full Historical Record Is Consistent With This Court's Precedent

Respondent recognizes that navigability for title purposes must be assessed on a segment-by-segment basis rather than for an entire river as a whole. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 77 (finding some stretches of the rivers navigable and others not). The State's proof below did precisely this: it presented segment-specific historical evidence—reports, survey records, and travel accounts—bearing on whether each stretch of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers was capable of supporting commercial navigation in its ordinary and natural condition at the time of Montana's admission in 1889.

That such evidence necessarily draws on records compiled over a span of years does not offend the constitutional test. As this Court observed in *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77 (1922), the determination of navigability for title purposes is inherently a historical inquiry requiring courts to look to the "natural and ordinary condition" of the waterway, drawing upon whatever reliable evidence exists to reconstruct that condition. *Id.* at 87. Contemporary accounts, government surveys, and later observations of unchanged river conditions are probative of what the river's condition was at the statehood date, particularly where, as here, no living witness can testify firsthand to conditions in 1889.

## IV. Petitioner's Proposed Rule Would Contradict This Court's Practical, Evidence-Based Approach

Petitioner's insistence on a rigid, single-moment temporal test finds no support in *Pollard*, *The Daniel Ball*, or their progeny. Rather, this Court has treated navigability for title purposes as a practical inquiry into the river's physical capacities, sensibly informed by the best available evidence—regardless of whether that evidence originates in the precise year of statehood. *See Economy Light*, 256 U.S. at 121-22 ("the test of navigability ... is a question of fact, to be determined by the ordinary uses ..."). Requiring exclusive contemporaneous proof would in many cases make the equal-footing doctrine unenforceable, since few 19th-century rivers were subject to systematic observation.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
