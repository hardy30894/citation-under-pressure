The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law to the outer Continental Shelf only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California's wage and hour laws apply to Newton’s claims arising on the outer Continental Shelf, as long as they are "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law, without requiring a gap in federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. However, this provision must be read in light of the Supreme Court's interpretation in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), and Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981). While Rodrigue established that state law applies to the outer Continental Shelf as surrogate federal law, it did not eliminate the requirement that there be a gap in federal law for state law to fill.

In Gulf Offshore, the Supreme Court noted that the OCSLA's choice of law provision is an "explicit choice-of-law provision" that requires a court to consider the content of both potentially applicable federal and state law. 453 U.S. at 480. This implies that state law is not to be applied when federal law already governs the subject matter.

The Fifth Circuit has consistently held that state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf only when there is a gap in federal law. In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steamship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is to be applied as surrogate federal law only when necessary to fill a "significant void or gap" in federal law. This test has been reaffirmed in subsequent Fifth Circuit decisions, such as Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990).

In this case, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), expressly allows for more protective state minimum wage and overtime laws, but this does not render the FLSA incomplete or create a gap for state law to fill. As the Supreme Court has noted, the FLSA is a comprehensive statute that governs minimum wage and overtime claims. See Long Island Care at Home, Ltd. v. Coke, 551 U.S. 158 (2007); Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc., 450 U.S. 728 (1981).

Therefore, the Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California's wage and hour laws apply to Newton's claims arising on the outer Continental Shelf, without requiring a gap in federal law. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, and there is no gap for state law to fill. Newton's claims should be governed solely by the FLSA, and the district court's dismissal of his state law claims was proper.
