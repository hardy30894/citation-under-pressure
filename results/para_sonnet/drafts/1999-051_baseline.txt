# ARGUMENT

## I. A PROCEDURALLY DEFAULTED CLAIM OF INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF APPELLATE COUNSEL MAY SERVE AS CAUSE TO EXCUSE THE DEFAULT OF ANOTHER CLAIM.

### A. This Court Has Already Held That Constitutionally Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Constitutes Cause.

In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), this Court held that "[s]o long as a defendant is represented by counsel whose performance is not constitutionally ineffective under the standard established in *Strickland v. Washington*," a procedural default caused by counsel's errors is not attributable to the defendant. *Id.* at 488. Rather, "if the procedural default is the result of ineffective assistance of counsel, the Sixth Amendment itself requires that responsibility for the default be imputed to the State." *Id.* This principle rests on the elementary proposition, reaffirmed in *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), that "[a]ttorney error that constitutes ineffective assistance of counsel is cause" for a procedural default, while only attorney error falling short of constitutional deficiency is not. *Id.* at 754.

Here, both the District Court and the Sixth Circuit found, after full review of the state-court record, that Carpenter's appellate counsel performed deficiently and that the deficiency prejudiced Carpenter within the meaning of *Strickland*. That finding is the beginning and the end of the "cause" inquiry required by *Murray v. Carrier*. Nothing in that decision, or in *Coleman*, conditions the cause determination on whether the ineffective-assistance claim could itself independently support a grant of the writ.

### B. Carpenter Satisfied the Exhaustion Requirement of *Murray v. Carrier* by Presenting His Ineffective-Assistance Claim to the State Courts.

*Murray v. Carrier* does impose one procedural prerequisite: because "the exhaustion doctrine... generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default," a petitioner must first give the state courts the opportunity to address the ineffectiveness claim. 477 U.S. at 489. That requirement exists to protect "the States' interest in correcting their own mistakes," *id.*, and Carpenter fully honored it.

Carpenter did not smuggle his ineffective-assistance claim into federal court without ever presenting it to Ohio's courts. He filed an application under Ohio Appellate Rule 26(B) asking the Ohio Court of Appeals to reopen his direct appeal precisely because his appellate counsel had failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence. The state court considered and rejected that application, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. That is exactly the "opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of its prisoners' federal rights" that this Court has always required, see *Duncan v. Henry*, 513 U.S. 364, 365 (1995) (per curiam) (quoting *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971)). The fact that the state court disposed of the claim on timeliness grounds does not mean the claim was not "presented"; it means only that the State's own procedural rule, not any omission by Carpenter, closed the door. Having done everything *Murray v. Carrier* asked of him, Carpenter should not be penalized further merely because the state court's timeliness ruling created a second layer of default.

### C. Imposing an Independent Cause-and-Prejudice Requirement on the Cause Claim Itself Would Produce an Endless Regress That This Court's Precedents Do Not Contemplate.

The "cause and prejudice" standard of *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), and *Murray v. Carrier* is a rule governing access to review of the merits of a habeas claim—it is not itself a freestanding claim for relief requiring its own showing of cause and prejudice. If a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim used as "cause" must first surmount an independent cause-and-prejudice hurdle, then logic demands a showing of cause for that default, and cause for the cause for that cause, ad infinitum. Nothing in *Coleman v. Thompson* or *Strickler v. Greene*, 527 U.S. 263 (1999), suggests that this Court intended to construct such an infinite regress. To the contrary, *Strickler* treated the "cause" inquiry as a discrete threshold question separate from any requirement that the cause-showing itself be procedurally pristine. 527 U.S. at 282–89.

Moreover, the constitutional guarantee underlying the cause exception—the right to effective assistance of appellate counsel recognized in *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985)—would be hollow if a petitioner could vindicate it only by first satisfying an independent procedural gauntlet unrelated to the merits of the ineffectiveness finding. *Evitts* held that "a first appeal as of right... is not adjudicated in accord with due process of law if the appellant does not have the effective assistance of an attorney." *Id.* at 396. Once a federal court determines, applying *Strickland*, that this guarantee was violated, the resulting default is—in the words of *Murray v. Carrier*—properly "imputed to the State," 477 U.S. at 488, without further procedural conditions.

### D. The Sixth Circuit Correctly Applied These Principles.

Because Carpenter presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio courts as *Murray v. Carrier* requires, and because the courts below correctly found that claim meritorious under *Strickland*, the Sixth Circuit properly held that the claim constituted cause excusing the default of Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court should affirm.
