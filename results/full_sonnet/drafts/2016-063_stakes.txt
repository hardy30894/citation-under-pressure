# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of the Civil Service Reform Act Directs Mixed-Case Appeals to District Court Regardless of the Ground for the Board's Dismissal

The Civil Service Reform Act establishes that "cases of discrimination subject to the provisions of section 7702" shall be filed in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). Section 7702 defines a mixed case by reference to the employee's allegations, not by reference to whether the Board ultimately validates its own jurisdiction. A case is "appealable to the Merit Systems Protection Board" and alleges "that a basis for the action was discrimination," 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), when the employee presents such a claim to the Board—full stop. Nothing in the statutory text conditions the forum for judicial review on the *outcome* of the Board's jurisdictional inquiry.

This Court's decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2013), confirms that reading. There, the Court held that "when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds—the same way it might dismiss any other kind of case—the availability of judicial review in district court follows naturally." *Id.* at 50. The Court reasoned that Congress created "a unified process" for mixed cases, *id.* at 44, and that "[g]iving the Board's procedural rulings the same treatment as its merits rulings . . . makes sense of the statute Congress wrote." *Id.* at 50. Critically, the Court's rationale was not limited to timeliness dismissals. The Court explained that "§ 7702's mixed-case provisions" apply whenever an employee "complain[s] of a personnel action serious enough to appeal to the MSPB, and alleg[es] that the action was based on discrimination." *Id.* at 44. A jurisdictional dismissal predicated on voluntariness—as in Mr. Perry's case—no less involves an employee complaining of an action she alleges was serious enough to appeal, combined with an allegation of discrimination. The Board's *disagreement* with that characterization does not change the case's mixed character; it simply produces an adverse ruling, just as a timeliness dismissal does.

The Federal Circuit and the D.C. Circuit's decision below rest on a distinction between "jurisdictional" and "procedural" dismissals that finds no footing in the statutory text. *Perry v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 829 F.3d 760 (D.C. Cir. 2016) (relying on *Powell v. Dep't of Def.*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998)). But *Kloeckner* itself rejected forum-shifting based on the *type* of Board ruling. The Court emphasized that the CSRA "does not appear to contemplate that a case should be routed to different courts depending on the ground—procedural or substantive—on which the MSPB disposed of it." *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50. The same logic forecloses routing cases to different courts depending on whether the Board's non-merits ruling is labeled "jurisdictional" or "procedural." Both are threshold rulings that avoid reaching the discrimination claim; both nonetheless arise from a case the employee brought as, and alleged to be, a mixed case.

## II. The D.C. Circuit's Jurisdictional/Procedural Distinction Is Unworkable and Contrary to *Kloeckner*'s Rationale

*Kloeckner* grounded its holding in the practical unworkability of bifurcated review. The Court observed that splitting review "between two tribunals" would "protract" proceedings and impose "arbitrary" line-drawing burdens on employees who "may have no way of knowing, ahead of the Board's ruling," which court will ultimately have authority to review the decision. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 55-56. The Court further explained that requiring "a claimant to guess" the proper forum, or to "identify the precise basis for the Board's ruling" before filing, "would be an unforgiving way to organize a legal regime." *Id.* at 55.

Those concerns apply with equal force here. The Board's ultimate jurisdictional determination often turns on contested factual questions—here, the voluntariness of Mr. Perry's settlement agreement—that are frequently intertwined with the merits of the discrimination claim itself. Mr. Perry alleged that he was coerced into settlement *because of* discrimination and retaliation. Whether his retirement was "voluntary" for jurisdictional purposes and whether it was the product of discriminatory coercion are not neatly severable inquiries. Under the D.C. Circuit's rule, an employee filing a single mixed-case appeal cannot know in advance which court will review an adverse Board ruling, because that forum depends entirely on how the Board eventually characterizes its own decision—as a jurisdictional dismissal or a merits ruling. This is precisely the "wasteful" and "bifurcated process" that *Kloeckner* held Congress did not intend. *Id.* at 56.

Moreover, *Kloeckner* explained that Congress's goal in enacting § 7702's mixed-case scheme was to spare employees "the need to bring the same claims across two tribunals," ensuring that "judicial review of the discrimination claim would occur exclusively in district court." *Id.* at 44-45. That policy is not confined to procedural dismissals. An employee whose case is dismissed on jurisdictional grounds may raise the identical discrimination allegations in district court that she would have raised had the Board reached the merits. Forcing her instead into the Federal Circuit—a court without authority to adjudicate discrimination claims under Title VII, the ADEA, or the Rehabilitation Act—defeats the very purpose of the mixed-case exception.

## III. Powell Cannot Survive Kloeckner

The D.C. Circuit believed itself bound by *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), reasoning that *Kloeckner* addressed only "procedural" dismissals. *Perry*, 829 F.3d at 764-65. But this Court's unanimous opinion nowhere suggests that its holding was limited to timeliness rulings as opposed to other threshold, non-merits dispositions. The Court's repeated references to "procedural grounds" reflected the particular facts of *Kloeckner*—a timeliness dismissal—not a considered limitation excluding jurisdictional dismissals from the same rule. The Court's reasoning, resting on the structure and purpose of § 7702's "unified" mixed-case scheme, *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 44, applies equally to jurisdictional dismissals premised on voluntariness. This Court should so hold and reverse the judgment below.
