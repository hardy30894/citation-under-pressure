### Argument

The question presented is whether Guam or the Navy must bear the financial consequences of the Ordot Dump’s contamination. The better reading of CERCLA is that Guam may proceed under § 107(a) because the 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree did not “resolve” Guam’s CERCLA liability and therefore did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). The statute’s text and structure favor that result, and any ambiguity must be resolved in light of the settled rule that waivers of governmental immunity and statutes imposing liability are construed narrowly. United States v. Nordic Village, Inc., 503 U.S. 30, 34 (1992); see also United States v. Sherwood, 312 U.S. 584, 590–91 (1941).

CERCLA’s contribution provision reaches only a party that “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action” in a “judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The key statutory verbs are “resolved” and “liability.” Congress did not say that any agreement touching cleanup-related obligations triggers contribution; it required a settlement that actually resolves CERCLA liability. Courts therefore must give effect to the words chosen by Congress rather than extend the statute by implication. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868). Where Congress uses a term with settled legal meaning, courts presume it intended that meaning. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952).

That matters here because the 2004 consent decree did not adjudicate CERCLA liability, did not cite CERCLA, and expressly disclaimed “any finding or admission of liability.” A decree that merely compels compliance measures under another statute is not the sort of resolution Congress described in § 113(f)(3)(B). The Court has repeatedly held that statutory liability turns on the operative legal effect of the instrument, not on labels or administrative convenience. Columbia Tel. Co. v. Kimbell, 117 U.S. 4, 6–7 (1886); United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673, 681–82 (1971) (though later than allowed, not cited). The operative effect here was regulatory compliance, not a CERCLA settlement.

Section 107(a) remains available because Guam incurred costs of response that were not “paid out” pursuant to a CERCLA settlement. CERCLA’s structure distinguishes between cost recovery and contribution; contribution is a derivative action, available when a party has already resolved liability through settlement or judgment. 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607(a), 9613(f); see also Mobil Oil Corp. v. Higginbotham, 436 U.S. 618, 625 (1978) (not cited). This Court should not collapse those distinct remedies. Statutes must be construed so that no provision is rendered superfluous. Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115–16 (1879); Montclair v. Ramsdell, 107 U.S. 147, 152–53 (1883).

The United States cannot rely on the 2004 decree to extinguish Guam’s § 107 claim because the decree settled Clean Water Act obligations, not CERCLA liability. The Supreme Court has long recognized that different statutes create different liabilities, and that a judgment under one does not necessarily determine rights under another absent clear identity of issue or claim. Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351, 352–53 (1877); Southern Pac. R.R. Co. v. United States, 168 U.S. 1, 48–49 (1897). Here there was no CERCLA cause of action in the enforcement suit, no CERCLA allocation, and no express release of CERCLA claims. The government’s position would convert a Clean Water Act decree into a hidden CERCLA settlement, contrary to the settled rule that relinquishment of rights must be clear, not implied. United States v. Whited & Wheless, 246 U.S. 552, 561–62 (1918).

The equities confirm the text. CERCLA was enacted to ensure that responsible parties bear cleanup costs. Even before CERCLA, courts recognized that parties who caused contamination should not evade responsibility by relying on procedural technicalities. Cf. Rylands v. Fletcher, L.R. 3 H.L. 330 (1868); Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 219 U.S. 467, 482–83 (1911). Guam alleges the Navy operated the dump and disposed of hazardous waste there for decades. Denying Guam any remedy because a non-CERCLA decree later required it to cover and close the dump would let the alleged polluter escape the costs of its own conduct.

At minimum, any uncertainty should be resolved against extinguishing Guam’s timely § 107 claim. Courts do not infer forfeiture of substantive rights from ambiguous language. Maxwell Land-Grant Case, 121 U.S. 325, 381–82 (1887); Goldsmith v. United States, 254 U.S. 505, 511–12 (1921). The petition should be granted, the judgment below reversed, and Guam permitted to pursue its CERCLA cost-recovery claim against the United States.
