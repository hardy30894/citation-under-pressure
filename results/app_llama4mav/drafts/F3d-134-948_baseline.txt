ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Selam's Habeas Corpus Petition on the Compulsory Process Claim

The Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA") guarantees tribal court defendants the right "to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This Court has held that the right to compulsory process is "essential to a fair trial." United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal, 458 U.S. 858, 867 (1982) (quoting Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967)). To establish a violation of this right, Selam must show that he was deprived of material and favorable testimony. See United States v. Bahamonde, 445 F.3d 1225, 1229 (9th Cir. 2006).

The record shows that Selam provided his attorney, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of five potential character witnesses. Although Leno-Baker attempted to contact these witnesses, Selam failed to assist her. However, the tribal judge and Leno-Baker advised Selam of his right to compel the appearance of witnesses on his behalf before the trials. Despite this advisement, Selam did not request the judge to subpoena any witnesses. The district court held that Selam was not denied his right to compulsory process because he failed to request subpoenas for his witnesses.

However, the Supreme Court has held that a defendant's failure to request a subpoena does not necessarily waive his right to compulsory process. See Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 409-10 (1988). The Court must consider whether the defendant's failure to request a subpoena was a knowing and voluntary waiver of his right. Id. at 418. In this case, the record does not establish that Selam knowingly and voluntarily waived his right to compulsory process. Therefore, the district court erred in denying Selam's habeas corpus petition on this claim.

II. The District Court Erred in Holding that Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. However, this Court has held that a petitioner's failure to exhaust a claim in tribal court does not necessarily bar federal habeas corpus review. See Miranda v. Castro, 292 F.3d 1063, 1065 (9th Cir. 2002). To bar review, the tribal court must have had a "full and fair opportunity" to consider the claim. Id.

In this case, the tribal court of appeals did not have a full and fair opportunity to consider Selam's Confrontation Clause claim because it was not raised on appeal. However, the Supreme Court has held that a petitioner's failure to raise a claim in state court can be excused if the petitioner can show cause and prejudice. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Similarly, this Court should consider whether Selam can show cause and prejudice for his failure to raise the Confrontation Clause claim in the tribal court of appeals.

The Confrontation Clause of the ICRA guarantees tribal court defendants the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). The Supreme Court has held that this right is essential to a fair trial. See Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 61 (2004). In this case, the five-year-old victim did not testify in the second trial, and her statements were introduced through four witnesses. This raises serious concerns about Selam's right to confrontation.

In conclusion, the district court erred in denying Selam's habeas corpus petition on both the compulsory process and Confrontation Clause claims. This Court should reverse the district court's judgment and remand for further proceedings.
