# ARGUMENT

## I. Federal Courts Possess Broad Equitable Authority to Fashion Structural Remedies Adequate to Eliminate an Established, Ongoing Constitutional Violation.

A court sitting in equity has always possessed authority to mold its remedy to the scope of the wrong before it. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." *Id.* at 329. That flexible authority is not confined to individual relief; it extends to structural decrees reaching the systemic conditions that produce a violation. *See In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593 (1895) (recognizing the broad power of a federal court to enter relief adequate to secure compliance with federal law).

Nor is such relief static once entered. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court confirmed that a court retains continuing power to supervise its own decree and adjust it as circumstances require, so long as any modification remains tethered to the violation the decree was designed to correct. *Id.* at 114-15. The three-judge court's order here is precisely this kind of calibrated equitable remedy: after extensive fact-finding, the court determined that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the ongoing Eighth Amendment violations, considered less intrusive alternatives, and retained jurisdiction to adjust the order as conditions changed. The PLRA's requirement that relief be "narrowly drawn" and represent "the least intrusive means" necessary does not displace this traditional framework—it codifies it. A remedy addressed to the specific overcrowding shown to cause the violation is proportional equitable relief in its purest form.

## II. This Court's Desegregation Precedents Confirm That District Courts May Order Specific, Systemic Relief When Necessary to Eliminate an Entrenched Constitutional Violation.

The State's contention that a population-reduction order is inherently too intrusive cannot be squared with this Court's repeated approval of comparably far-reaching remedies where state officials failed for years to correct an adjudicated constitutional wrong. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), this Court remanded the desegregation cases "to take such proceedings and enter such orders and decrees consistent with this opinion as are necessary and proper to admit to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis," *id.* at 301, entrusting the district courts with broad discretion to design remedies commensurate with the violation—even where compliance required restructuring the operation of state institutions.

That discretion was substantive, not nominal. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court sustained extraordinary remedial measures against continued official resistance, explaining that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out." *Id.* at 234. In *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court held that once such a violation is established, responsible officials bear "the affirmative duty to take whatever steps might be necessary" to eliminate it "root and branch." *Id.* at 437-38. And in *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145 (1965), this Court confirmed that a federal court confronting an entrenched constitutional violation "has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past as well as bar like discrimination in the future." *Id.* at 154.

These precedents establish a consistent principle: when a coordinate branch of state government has, over years of litigation, failed to remedy an adjudicated constitutional violation, a federal court does not exceed its authority by ordering specific, quantifiable relief—whether expressed as pupil assignment ratios or, as here, a population ceiling calibrated to design capacity. The order below is directly analogous to the remedial decrees this Court has consistently sustained and should likewise be sustained.

## III. The Order Is a Proportionate Response to an Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment's Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment.

The gravity of the underlying violation confirms the propriety of the remedy. The Eighth Amendment, binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), draws its meaning from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long recognized that punishment inflicting needless suffering offends the Constitution regardless of its formal legality, and that courts must assess the proportionality of penal conditions to legitimate objectives. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910). A prison system so overcrowded that inmates are systematically denied adequate medical and mental health care—as the district court found after extensive evidentiary hearings—inflicts exactly the kind of needless, unredressed suffering the Eighth Amendment forbids.

Nor may the State escape the consequences of its own prolonged noncompliance by invoking the PLRA's limits as a shield. In *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), this Court held that a State's own hostility to a federal constitutional mandate cannot be permitted to erode the mandate itself, and that the vindication of constitutional rights cannot be made contingent on the convenience of the officials obligated to secure them. *Id.* at 16-19. The same principle governs here: the State cannot invoke the burdens of compliance—burdens flowing directly from its own years of inaction—as grounds for invalidating the only remedy the record shows will cure the violation.

## CONCLUSION

The population order is narrowly drawn, reflects the least intrusive means available to correct a violation of constitutional dimension, and is fully consistent with this Court's settled equitable and Eighth Amendment jurisprudence. The judgment below should be affirmed.
