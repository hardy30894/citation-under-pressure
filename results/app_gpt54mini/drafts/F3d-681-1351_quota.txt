**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in holding that the ’880 application may claim the April 13, 1989 filing date of the ’566 application. Section 120 gives the benefit of an earlier filing date only when each intervening application contains a written description adequate to support the later claims and “the later application contains or is amended to contain a specific reference to the earlier filed application.” 35 U.S.C. § 120; see **Gosteli v. Nakashima, 239 F.3d 1344, 1349-50 (Fed. Cir. 2001)**. Continuity is strictly construed because the statute creates an exception to the ordinary rule that a patent applicant is limited to the filing date of the application on which it relies. **MPEP?** No, authority only: **Hollmer v. Harari, 681 F.3d 1351, 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2012)**; **Eli Lilly & Co. v. Board of Regents of the University of Washington, 334 F.3d 1264, 1267-68 (Fed. Cir. 2003)**.

I. **Harari cannot bootstrap the ’579 application through ambiguous incorporation language.**

The critical question is not whether the ’579 application was discernible somewhere in the record, but whether the intervening ’838 and ’768 applications themselves clearly incorporated it such that § 120 continuity is satisfied. The Board’s contrary approach conflicts with **In re Fritch, 972 F.2d 1260, 1264-65 (Fed. Cir. 1992)**, which held that incorporation by reference must be clear and that the incorporated material becomes part of the host document only to the extent expressly identified. Likewise, **Advanced Display Systems, Inc. v. Kent State University, 212 F.3d 1272, 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2000)** recognizes that incorporation by reference is effective only when the host document “clearly identify[ies]” the material to be incorporated. An ambiguous reference does not satisfy the statute.

Here, the only original language is that “[t]he disclosures of the two applications are hereby incorporated by reference.” J.A. 1204. But the statement identifies one application by serial number and the other only as “filed on the same day as the present application.” That wording is, at best, contingent on a date comparison that does not appear in the later intervening applications. Under **Harari v. Hollmer, 602 F.3d 1348, 1352-54 (Fed. Cir. 2010)**, the “reasonable examiner” test was limited to whether a preliminary amendment introduced new matter at the initial filing stage; it did not authorize the Board to treat a later, different statutory inquiry as satisfied by a vague reference that must be clarified by extrinsic filings. See also **Zenon Environmental, Inc. v. U.S. Filter Corp., 506 F.3d 1370, 1377-78 (Fed. Cir. 2007)** (incorporation by reference requires a clear identification of the material incorporated).

II. **The Board improperly relied on extrinsic documents to cure a deficient chain of priority.**

Section 120 requires the continuity showing to appear in the chain of applications, not to be reconstructed later from surrounding papers. **Applied Materials, Inc. v. Advanced Semiconductor Materials America, Inc., 98 F.3d 1563, 1578 (Fed. Cir. 1996)**, explains that priority depends on compliance with the statute’s formal requirements. A later applicant may not “fill in” an omission in an earlier application by pointing to materials outside the application chain. See also **Medtronic CoreValve, LLC v. Edwards Lifesciences Corp., 741 F.3d 1359, 1366-67 (Fed. Cir. 2014)** (priority cannot rest on post hoc reconstruction of disclosure); **Droplets, Inc. v. E*TRADE Bank, 887 F.3d 1309, 1318-19 (Fed. Cir. 2018)** (written-description support must be found in the application relied on for priority).

The Board’s ruling also conflicts with the settled principle that incorporation by reference is not a license to evade § 112’s disclosure requirements. **X2Y Attenuators, LLC v. International Trade Commission, 757 F.3d 1358, 1362-63 (Fed. Cir. 2014)**, explains that incorporated material must still be described with sufficient clarity to place the public on notice. Here, the Board accepted a priority chain in which the operative reference to the ’579 application existed only by inference from a date and a later-filed transmittal sheet. That is not the “specific reference” Congress required. 35 U.S.C. § 120; **Medtronic CoreValve, 741 F.3d at 1366-67**.

III. **Without valid § 120 continuity, the ’880 application cannot antedate Hollmer.**

Because the ’880 application is undisputedly entitled at most to the December 20, 1996 filing date of the ’708 application, it cannot predate Hollmer’s conception unless the Board’s priority holding stands. But the Board’s decision rests on an erroneous legal standard and an impermissibly elastic use of incorporation by reference. The proper remedy is reversal with instructions to deny Harari the benefit of the ’566 filing date and to enter judgment for Hollmer and Cleveland. See **Hollmer v. Harari, 681 F.3d 1351, 1356-57 (Fed. Cir. 2012)**; **Gosteli, 239 F.3d at 1350**; **Eli Lilly, 334 F.3d at 1267-68**.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse.
