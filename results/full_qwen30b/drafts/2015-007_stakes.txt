**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death is sufficient to impose the death penalty, and under which the jury is not required to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 122 S.Ct. 2428, 153 L.Ed.2d 556 (2002), did not require Florida to alter its capital sentencing procedures in the manner urged by petitioner. Nor does the Eighth Amendment demand that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances or determine mental retardation as a matter of law. Florida’s system remains constitutionally sound under binding precedent and the evolving standards of decency that inform the Eighth Amendment.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Find Mental Retardation or to Unanimously Recommend Death**

Petitioner’s claim that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to determine whether he is mentally retarded—thereby barring execution—misapprehends the scope of *Ring*. In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that “the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find each fact necessary to impose a sentence of death.” 536 U.S. at 592, 122 S.Ct. 2428. The Court emphasized that “the presence of an aggravating factor” in Arizona’s capital sentencing scheme “is an element of the greater offense of first-degree murder,” and thus must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. at 598, 122 S.Ct. 2428. The Court did not hold, however, that every mitigating or exempting factor—such as mental retardation—must be determined by a jury. Indeed, the Court explicitly reserved the question of whether “a defendant’s mental condition” is an “element” of the offense. Id. at 598 n.10, 122 S.Ct. 2428.

The Court later confirmed that *Ring* does not require a jury to find mental retardation. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 122 S.Ct. 2242, 153 L.Ed.2d 335 (2002), the Court held that executing mentally retarded individuals violates the Eighth Amendment. But the Court made clear that the *determination* of mental retardation is a matter for the trial court, not the jury. The Court stated that “the States are free to define the term ‘mental retardation’ and to establish procedures for determining whether a defendant meets that definition.” 536 U.S. at 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242. Florida’s three-pronged test—requiring a full-scale IQ of 70 or below, adaptive deficits, and onset before age 18—is consistent with this framework. The trial court, not the jury, is responsible for making the ultimate determination of mental retardation under Florida law. This is not a violation of the Sixth Amendment, because mental retardation is not an “element” of the offense of first-degree murder, nor is it a fact that increases the penalty beyond the statutory maximum. See *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490, 120 S.Ct. 2348, 147 L.Ed.2d 435 (2000) (holding that only facts that increase the penalty beyond the statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury).

Moreover, the Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury recommendation of death. The Supreme Court has never held that a capital sentencing scheme must require unanimity. In *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 355, 136 S.Ct. 616, 193 L.Ed.2d 504 (2016), the Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme—under which a non-unanimous jury recommendation suffices—violated the Sixth Amendment. But that decision was based on the specific facts of the case and the Florida Supreme Court’s prior interpretation of *Ring*. The Court concluded that Florida’s scheme “fails to satisfy the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee” because the jury’s recommendation was not “the sole basis” for the death sentence. 577 U.S. at 364, 136 S.Ct. 616. However, the Court did not hold that *all* non-unanimous capital sentencing schemes are unconstitutional. Rather, it reaffirmed that the jury must be the sole fact-finder on aggravating circumstances. The Florida Supreme Court’s decision in this case, which upheld the trial court’s determination that Hurst is not mentally retarded and that the jury’s non-unanimous recommendation was constitutionally sufficient, is consistent with the Court’s precedent and does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Prohibit Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme**

Petitioner’s Eighth Amendment claim rests on the premise that Florida’s outlier status—being the only state that allows a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death—renders its scheme cruel and unusual. But the Eighth Amendment does not require states to conform to the practices of other jurisdictions. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the Eighth Amendment is not a mirror of public opinion.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101, 78 S.Ct. 590, 2 L.Ed.2d 630 (1958). The Constitution “must be interpreted in light of the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” but it does not require uniformity across states. *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592, 97 S.Ct. 2861, 53 L.Ed.2d 982 (1977).
