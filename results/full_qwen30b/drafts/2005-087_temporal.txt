**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce expert testimony about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. The Due Process Clause guarantees fair procedures, not the right to introduce any particular kind of evidence. Where a state law properly limits the use of expert testimony to the specific defense it has created—here, the insanity defense—such a limitation does not violate due process. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly upheld the trial court’s ruling that Clark could not use his expert evidence to rebut the prosecution’s proof of criminal intent, because the state’s law did not recognize a separate affirmative defense based on inability to form intent, and the defendant had no constitutional right to expand the scope of his defense beyond what the state law permits.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that states may define the elements of crimes and the available defenses, so long as those definitions do not violate fundamental fairness. In *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), the Court held that due process does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that would undermine the prosecution’s case if such evidence is not relevant under the state’s own rules of evidence or legal doctrine. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a state to adopt any particular system of criminal procedure,” and that “the due process clause is not a mandate for the adoption of any particular rule of evidence.” Id. at 228. Thus, a state may limit the admissibility of expert testimony to a specific defense, such as insanity, without violating due process.

Similarly, in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Court upheld a federal rule that excluded expert testimony on the defendant’s mental condition when offered to show that he lacked criminal intent, where the defense was not recognized under the law. The Court stated that “the law does not require the introduction of evidence which is not relevant under the rules of the court,” and that “the right to a fair trial does not include the right to introduce evidence that is not admissible under the law of the jurisdiction.” Id. at 453. The Court further noted that “the state may define the scope of defenses and the manner in which they may be proved,” and that “the Constitution does not compel a state to allow a defendant to prove facts that are not legally relevant to the issue before the court.” Id. at 454.

Arizona law, at the time of Clark’s trial, permitted the use of expert testimony solely to establish the insanity defense, which required proof that the defendant, “at the time of the commission of the offense, was laboring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing, or if he did know it, that he did not know he was doing what was wrong.” Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501 (1969). This was a narrow, affirmative defense with a heavy burden of proof on the defendant. The law did not recognize a separate defense based on inability to form criminal intent, nor did it permit expert testimony on mental state to be used to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving intent. The state’s decision to limit the use of such evidence to the insanity defense was a valid exercise of its authority to define criminal liability and the available defenses.

Clark’s claim that he could not form the intent to kill is not a separate defense under Arizona law. The prosecution was required to prove criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt, but the state was not required to allow Clark to use expert testimony on mental illness to negate that intent unless such testimony was relevant to a recognized defense. The fact that Clark was a paranoid schizophrenic and believed in alien invasions does not, under Arizona law, automatically negate intent. The state may require that a defendant prove a mental defect that renders him incapable of forming intent under the specific legal standard, and may limit the admissibility of evidence to that purpose.

The Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce expert testimony on mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized by the state. In *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the Court held that “the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to adopt any particular system of criminal procedure,” and that “the Constitution does not compel a state to allow a defendant to prove facts that are not legally relevant to the issue before the court.” Id. at 325. The Court reiterated that “the due process clause is not a mandate for the adoption of any particular rule of evidence.” Id. at 326. Thus, Arizona’s decision to limit the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense, and not to permit it to be used to rebut the prosecution’s proof of intent, was consistent with due process.

Clark was not deprived of a fair trial. He was allowed to present expert testimony on his mental state, and the jury was instructed on the insanity defense. The trial court did not bar the introduction of such evidence; it only ruled that it could not be used for a purpose not recognized by state law. That ruling was not an arbitrary or capricious limitation, but a proper application of Arizona’s criminal law. The Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to use expert testimony to prove a defense that the state has not created.

For these reasons, the Arizona Supreme Court correctly affirmed Clark’s conviction. The Due Process Clause does not entitle a defendant to introduce expert evidence about his mental state to rebut criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized under state law. The state’s limitation on the use of such evidence was lawful and constitutional.

**WHEREFORE**, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
