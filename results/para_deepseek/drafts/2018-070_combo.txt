**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) adopts state law as surrogate federal law only to fill a gap in federal law, not whenever state law is merely “applicable and not inconsistent.” The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statute’s text, history, and this Court’s precedent.

**I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Approach**

OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision declares that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is inherently limiting. It directs courts to adopt state law only when federal law does not already supply a rule of decision. This Court has long recognized that similar language in the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13, which adopts state law for federal enclaves “when not made punishable by any enactment of Congress,” operates as a gap-filler. *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293 (1958). The OCSLA’s parallel structure compels the same conclusion. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365–66 (1969) (holding that OCSLA incorporates state law only where federal law does not provide a remedy).

**II. This Court’s OCSLA Precedent Confirms the Gap-Filling Principle**

In *Rodrigue*, the Court held that state wrongful-death law applied under OCSLA because the Death on the High Seas Act did not cover accidents on fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA “was not designed to displace state law” but to “fill the gaps” left by federal law. *Id.* at 357. The Court rejected the argument that state law could apply whenever it was not inconsistent with federal law; instead, it required a showing that federal law was “inadequate” to address the claim. *Id.* at 365. The Fifth Circuit’s pre-1970 decision in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), correctly distilled this principle: state law applies under OCSLA only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law.

**III. Federal Enclave Jurisprudence Reinforces the Gap-Filling Requirement**

OCSLA treats fixed platforms as “federal enclaves” within an adjacent state. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365. This Court has consistently held that state law applies in federal enclaves only when federal law is silent. In *Pacific Coast Dairy v. Department of Agriculture*, 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943), the Court stated that state law “cannot be enforced” on a federal enclave if it “conflicts with federal law,” but also that state law “does not apply of its own force” and is adopted only “to the extent that Congress has not occupied the field.” *Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co.*, 304 U.S. 518, 530 (1938), similarly held that state law on an enclave “is not effective unless adopted by Congress.” And *James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 148 (1937), explained that state law “may be applied” only “in the absence of federal legislation.” The OCSLA’s adoption of the enclave model thus incorporates this gap-filling logic.

**IV. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a “comprehensive” regulatory scheme that “leaves no room” for state wage-and-hour law on the OCS. *Powell v. United States Cartridge Co.*, 339 U.S. 497, 515 (1950). The FLSA establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements, and it expressly permits states to enact more protective laws. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). But that savings clause does not create a gap; it merely preserves state authority over workers *within the states’ territorial jurisdiction*. On the OCS, where state law applies only as surrogate federal law, the savings clause is irrelevant. *See Kirschbaum v. Walling*, 316 U.S. 517, 523 (1942) (FLSA’s coverage is “broad” and “comprehensive”). Because the FLSA already provides a complete set of wage-and-hour protections for OCS workers, there is no “void or gap” for California law to fill. *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036.

**V. The Legislative History Confirms Congress’s Intent to Fill Gaps, Not to Import State Law Wholesale**

Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The purpose was to supply rules for “matters not covered by federal law,” such as property, tort, and contract law. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 357. Wage-and-hour regulation, by contrast, was already covered by the FLSA. *See United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 125 (1941) (FLSA is a “comprehensive” exercise of Congress’s commerce power). Congress did not intend to duplicate federal protections with state law.

**VI. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Undermine Uniformity**

Adopting state law whenever it is “not inconsistent” would subject OCS operations to a patchwork of 50 state wage-and-hour regimes, defeating the uniformity that OCSLA was designed to achieve. *See United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35 (1947) (recognizing the need for “uniform federal law” on the OCS). This Court has consistently rejected such fragmentation. *Standard Oil Co. v. California*, 291 U.S. 242, 248 (1934) (federal enclave law must be “uniform”); *Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. at 293 (Assimilative Crimes Act ensures “uniformity” by filling gaps only).

**Conclusion**

The OCSLA permits state law only when federal law leaves a gap. The FLSA leaves no such gap. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
