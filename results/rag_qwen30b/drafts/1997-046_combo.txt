**ARGUMENT**

I. An inmate possesses a constitutionally protected liberty interest in the clemency process, and Ohio’s failure to afford due process in that proceeding violates the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects “life or liberty” interests that are not merely created by statute, but are “independent of any state procedure.” *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 558 (1974), but the Supreme Court has long recognized that such interests may arise from the very structure of a state’s criminal justice system. In *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935), the Court emphasized that “the obligation to guard and enforce every right secured by [the Constitution]” rests equally on state and federal courts. When a state establishes a mechanism for reviewing the finality of a death sentence—particularly one that is “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant,” *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985)—it creates a process that implicates due process protections. Though *Evitts* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is rooted in earlier precedent, including *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), which held that the Due Process Clause applies to “each step” of a criminal adjudication when that step is “an integral part of the system” for determining guilt or punishment. The Ohio clemency process, which occurs after trial, appeal, and sentencing, is not a mere afterthought. It is the final opportunity for a death row inmate to present mitigating evidence, to explain his conduct, and to seek mercy from the executive branch. To deny due process at this stage is to render the prior stages of adjudication meaningless.

The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that the executive clemency power is not a mere grace, but a constitutional component of the criminal justice system. In *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950), the Court upheld the authority of the Governor to determine the sanity of a death row inmate, noting that “the determination of petitioner’s sanity by the Governor supported by the report of physicians had met the standards of due process.” Though the Court found no constitutional right to a judicial hearing, it did not deny that the process itself was subject to due process scrutiny. The Court observed that “a person legally convicted and sentenced to death had no statutory or constitutional right to a judicially conducted or supervised ‘inquisition or trial’ on the question of insanity,” but this statement was not a rejection of due process—it was a recognition that the process, though administrative, must still comport with constitutional standards. The same logic applies here: if the Governor may constitutionally determine sanity, then he may also constitutionally consider mitigating evidence in a clemency proceeding, provided that the process is fair.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long acknowledged that the executive’s power to grant clemency is not absolute and may be subject to constitutional constraints. In *United States v. Murray*, 275 U.S. 347, 355 (1928), the Court stated that “in view of the existence of provisions for parole and executive clemency, it would seem unlikely that Congress would have intended to make the district court’s power to suspend sentence coextensive with the power of the executive to grant clemency.” This recognition of clemency as a distinct and constitutionally significant function of government underscores the importance of ensuring that such proceedings are not arbitrary. If the executive’s power is to be meaningful, it must be exercised in a manner consistent with due process.

II. The Ohio clemency process violates the Fifth Amendment by compelling a waiver of the right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects an individual from being “compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” This right is not forfeited simply because a person is incarcerated. In *United States v. Page*, 137 U.S. 673, 678 (1891), the Court held that “the approval of the proceedings, findings and sentence of the court-martial were approved by the President,” and that “the orders subsequently issued thereon are presumed to be his, and not those of the Secretary by whom they are authenticated.” This principle—that the final decision-maker must act in a manner consistent with constitutional rights—applies with equal force to clemency proceedings. When the Ohio Adult Parole Authority offers a voluntary interview, it creates a situation in which an inmate must choose between speaking and risking self-incrimination, or remaining silent and forfeiting the opportunity to present mitigating evidence. This is not a true choice—it is a Hobson’s choice.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may not condition the receipt of a benefit on the waiver of a constitutional right. In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 230 (1914), the Court held that “the acceptance of bribes with intent to influence official action” is a crime, and that “the Government may not be influenced by such corrupt means.” The same principle applies here: the State may not condition the opportunity to seek clemency on the surrender of a fundamental constitutional right. The Ohio regulation provides that “any statement given by the prisoner at the interview shall be considered at the clemency hearing.” This creates a direct incentive to speak, and a direct risk of self-incrimination. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that such conditions are impermissible. In *Johnson v. Massachusetts*, 390 U.S. 511, 513 (1968), the Court stated that “the record relevant to the constitutional claims now asserted is insufficient to permit decision of those claims,” but it did not dismiss the claim outright. It recognized that the voluntariness of a confession, and the fairness of the process, must be examined. Similarly, here, the risk of self-incrimination is real and present, and the process is not insulated from constitutional scrutiny.
