**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act and Warrants Deferential Review Under *Chevron***

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status is arbitrary and capricious. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983). The Act nowhere prescribes a standard for polling, and the Board’s interpretation conflicts with the statute’s text and structure. *See NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co.*, 416 U.S. 267, 275 (1974) (agency interpretations must be consistent with the Act’s purposes). The Board’s approach renders polling a “marginally useful” tool, *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), despite its potential to resolve disputes over union support without the delay and disruption of an RM election. *See Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967) (noting polling’s utility in avoiding unnecessary elections).

The Board’s standard also contravenes the Act’s policy favoring prompt resolution of representation disputes. *See 29 U.S.C. § 159(c)(1)* (authorizing expedited elections). By requiring employers to meet the same evidentiary threshold for polling as for withdrawing recognition, the Board discourages employers from using polling to verify their doubts before taking adverse action. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987) (noting the three options available to employers with reasonable doubt). This approach is irrational, as polling serves a distinct purpose: to obtain “more certain, precise information” about employee support. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063.

The Board’s interpretation is owed no deference under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), because it is not a permissible construction of the Act. *See id.* at 843. The Board’s policy is inconsistent with the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships, as it forces employers to either withdraw recognition prematurely or risk an unfair labor practice charge. *See id.* at 844. The Board’s approach is also unsupported by the statute’s text, which does not distinguish between polling and other methods of testing union support. *See 29 U.S.C. § 159(c)(1)* (authorizing elections but not prohibiting polling).

**II. The Board Erred in Concluding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt**

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board improperly discounted employee statements and misapplied its own precedent.

First, the Board erred in excluding statements from employees hired after January 25, 1991, the date Allentown Mack sent its letter refusing recognition. *See id.* at 490 (reviewing Board’s factual findings for substantial evidence). The Board’s reliance on the hire date is arbitrary, as it ignores the temporal proximity of the statements to the poll and the employer’s reasonable doubt. *See NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (Board must consider all probative evidence).

Second, the Board improperly discounted statements from employees who expressed dissatisfaction with the union during job interviews. *See Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978) (employee statements during interviews are unreliable). However, the Board ignored the context of these statements: employees were told the new company would be non-union, and their remarks were not necessarily indicative of anti-union sentiment. *See id.* The Board’s refusal to credit these statements is unsupported by substantial evidence. *See Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488.

Third, the Board improperly discounted hearsay statements from employees reporting anti-union sentiment among their coworkers. *See Westbrook Bowl*, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989) (hearsay statements are unreliable). However, the Board failed to explain why these statements lacked probative value, particularly when corroborated by the poll results. *See id.* The Board’s rejection of this evidence is arbitrary and capricious. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs.*, 463 U.S. at 43.

Finally, the Board’s reliance on the presumption of continuing majority status is misplaced. *See Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 37-41. The presumption is rebuttable, and the Board’s failure to consider the unique circumstances of a successor employer—where the bargaining unit was reduced and employees were hired from a previously represented unit—renders its finding unsupported by substantial evidence. *See id.* at 41 n.8.

**Conclusion**

The Board’s polling standard is arbitrary and inconsistent with the Act, and its factual findings are unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should grant the petition for review and vacate the Board’s order.
