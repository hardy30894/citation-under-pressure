The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), effectively overruled the D.C. Circuit's precedent in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and dictates that judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 (CSRA) governs judicial review of MSPB decisions. 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq. As a general matter, review of Board decisions lies in the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, an exception exists for "[c]ases of discrimination," which are reviewable in federal district court. Id. § 7703(b)(2). The statute defines these cases as ones in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal laws. Id. § 7702(a)(1).

The Supreme Court in Kloeckner held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court. 568 U.S. at 56. Although Kloeckner did not directly address jurisdictional dismissals, its reasoning applies with equal force to such cases. The Court's interpretation of the statutory language in § 7702(a)(1) is controlling. The phrase "action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board" does not turn on the Board's ultimate decision about its jurisdiction. Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 54-55.

The Federal Circuit's attempt to distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals in Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), is unpersuasive. The distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not supported by the statutory text. See Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 54-55. Moreover, the Board's authority to waive procedural defects, 5 C.F.R. § 1201.12, does not necessarily mean that jurisdictional dismissals are different in kind.

Perry's case is a mixed case, as he alleges that the Census Bureau's actions were discriminatory and appealed to the MSPB. The Board's dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not change the fact that Perry's case is a mixed case. Under Kloeckner, judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal lies in district court.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Powell was misplaced, as Kloeckner effectively overruled that decision. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner provides a clear interpretation of the CSRA, and the D.C. Circuit should have followed it. Therefore, this Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit's decision and hold that judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's case lies in district court.
