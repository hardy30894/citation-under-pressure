ARGUMENT

I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Reentries Predating Its Enactment.

The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), took effect April 1, 1997. Its text addresses an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally” after a prior removal order. Because the statute contains no explicit command that it reach conduct completed before that date, the presumption against retroactivity controls. Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994) (“[C]ongressional enactments and administrative rules will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.”). The same presumption applies to immigration statutes. INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001).

The government’s contrary reading would require the Court to infer retroactive reach from ambiguous language—an inference forbidden by the canon. See Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp., 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988). Legislative history supplies no clear statement overcoming the presumption. Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer, 520 U.S. 939, 946 (1997). Accordingly, the statute governs only post-enactment reentries.

II. Even If Applicable, the Statute Effects an Impermissible Retroactive Change.

If the statute reaches pre-1997 reentries, it attaches new legal consequences to Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 conduct by eliminating all forms of relief previously available. Such a change is retroactive. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269–70. The inquiry turns on whether the provision “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” Id. at 280.

Before 1997, an alien unlawfully present could seek adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The reinstatement bar destroys that opportunity. This elimination of a substantive avenue of relief constitutes the precise type of retroactive effect condemned in St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321 (“[T]he elimination of any meaningful avenue of relief would render the statute impermissibly retroactive.”). See also Martin v. Hadix, 527 U.S. 343, 357–58 (1999); Lindh v. Murphy, 521 U.S. 320, 325 (1997).

Fernandez-Vargas’s 2001 application for adjustment rested on a marriage and long-term residence that existed well before the statute’s effective date. The new bar therefore operates on “transactions already completed.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 280. No “clear congressional intent” authorizes that result. Bradley v. School Bd., 416 U.S. 696, 711 (1974). The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on the absence of a “protectable expectation” misapplies the Landgraf test, which focuses on the change in legal consequences, not subjective expectations. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 322.

The statute therefore cannot be applied to Fernandez-Vargas without violating the presumption against retroactivity.
