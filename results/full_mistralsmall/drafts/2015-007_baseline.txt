**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that Arizona’s sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it permitted a judge, rather than a jury, to find the aggravating factors necessary to impose the death penalty. *Id.* at 609. The Court reasoned that those factors “operate as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,” and thus must be found by a jury. *Id.* at 609 (quoting *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)).

Florida’s scheme is fundamentally different. Unlike Arizona, Florida does not permit a judge to find aggravating factors. Instead, the jury must unanimously find at least one statutory aggravating factor beyond a reasonable doubt before the trial court may consider imposing the death penalty. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2015); *see also* *Hurst v. State*, 18 So. 3d 975, 1008 (Fla. 2009). The jury’s role is not merely advisory; it is the constitutionally required factfinder for the aggravating factors that make a defendant eligible for the death penalty. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647-48 (1990) (plurality opinion) (upholding Arizona’s scheme where the jury found aggravating factors and the judge made the ultimate sentencing decision).

Respondent concedes that *Ring* invalidated judicial factfinding in capital sentencing, but Florida’s scheme does not involve such factfinding. The jury’s unanimous finding of an aggravating factor satisfies the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee. *See* *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275, 277-78 (1993) (holding that a jury verdict must be unanimous and beyond a reasonable doubt to satisfy the Sixth Amendment).

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme also does not violate the Eighth Amendment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that states have broad discretion in designing their capital sentencing schemes, so long as they comply with constitutional minima. *See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 195 (1976) (plurality opinion) (upholding Georgia’s guided discretion statute).

The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. In *McKoy v. North Carolina*, 494 U.S. 433 (1990), the Court held that a state could not require a jury to find mitigating factors unanimously, reasoning that unanimity was not constitutionally compelled. *Id.* at 444. By parity of reasoning, Florida’s requirement that the jury unanimously find aggravating factors does not violate the Eighth Amendment. *See also* *Walton*, 497 U.S. at 650-51 (upholding Arizona’s scheme where the jury found aggravating factors unanimously).

Moreover, Florida’s scheme provides heightened reliability in capital sentencing. The jury’s unanimous finding of an aggravating factor ensures that the death penalty is imposed only in the most aggravated cases. *See* *Tuilaepa v. California*, 512 U.S. 967, 973 (1994) (explaining that the Eighth Amendment requires “individualized consideration” of the defendant and the crime). Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement by mandating jury unanimity on at least one statutory aggravating factor.

**III. FLORIDA’S TREATMENT OF MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

Florida’s treatment of mental retardation also comports with the Eighth Amendment. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids the execution of mentally retarded defendants. However, the Court left to the states the manner in which this constitutional restriction is enforced. *Id.* at 317.

Florida has adopted a clear and administrable test for mental retardation, requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant meets all three prongs of the statutory definition. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4) (2015); *see Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009). This test is consistent with *Atkins* and does not violate the Eighth Amendment. *See Hall v. Florida*, 572 U.S. 701, 720 (2014) (upholding Florida’s statutory IQ cutoff of 70 for mental retardation determinations in the capital context).

Hurst’s claim that Florida’s scheme violates the Eighth Amendment because it does not permit the jury to determine mental retardation as an absolute bar to execution is unavailing. The trial court, not the jury, makes the ultimate determination of whether a defendant is mentally retarded under Florida law. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4). This allocation of decisionmaking authority is consistent with the Supreme Court’s recognition that states have flexibility in designing their capital sentencing schemes. *See Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 195.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
