**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed because this Court has never held that enhancements for superior performance and exceptional results are categorically prohibited under federal fee-shifting statutes. To the contrary, this Court has consistently recognized that such enhancements remain available in the “rare” and “exceptional” case.

**I. THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT PERMITS ENHANCEMENTS FOR SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS IN APPROPRIATE CASES**

In *Blum v. Stenson*, this Court expressly rejected the argument that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” 465 U.S. 886, 901 (1984). The Court recognized that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)). While the Court declined to approve an enhancement in that case because the applicants “offered no evidence” of exceptional results, the Court explicitly left the door open for future cases. *Id.* at 900-01. The Court stated that an upward adjustment may be justified in “the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and that the results were “exceptional.” *Id.* at 899.

In *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) (“*Delaware Valley I*”), this Court again confirmed that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). The Court did not overrule *Blum* or categorically eliminate enhancements for superior performance. Instead, the Court clarified that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” *id.* at 566 (emphasis added), leaving open the possibility of extraordinary cases where enhancement remains appropriate. *Id.* at 566-68.

The Court further noted that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” *id.* at 566, but did not hold that it includes *all* such factors in every case. The caveat “most, if not all” preserves the possibility that in some cases the lodestar may not fully capture the value of extraordinary representation.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS DISCRETION BASED ON SPECIFIC EVIDENCE IN THE RECORD**

The district court did not enhance the fee based on a general sense that counsel performed well. Rather, the court made detailed findings supported by specific evidence. The court observed that plaintiffs’ counsel “achieved an extraordinary result” in obtaining “sweeping and comprehensive injunctive relief” that transformed Georgia’s foster care system. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court found that the quality of representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Id.* at 1286. These findings were based on the court’s “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Id.*

This Court has long recognized that because “the district court has close and intimate knowledge of the efforts expended and the value of the services rendered,” it is entitled to “substantial deference” in fee determinations. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437. The district court’s firsthand observation of counsel’s performance is precisely the kind of “specific evidence” that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require. No rule prohibits a district court from relying on its own observations of counsel’s performance, especially when those observations are set forth in detailed findings.

**III. THE DECISIONS OF THE CIRCUITS CONFIRM THAT ENHANCEMENTS REMAIN AVAILABLE**

Every Court of Appeals to consider this issue has concluded that enhancements for exceptional performance and results remain permissible under this Court’s precedent. *See Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008); *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). These decisions uniformly hold that an enhancement may be appropriate where the applicant provides specific evidence of exceptional performance and results.

This uniform body of appellate authority reflects a correct understanding of this Court’s precedent. The alternative—a categorical ban on enhancements—would contradict the express language of *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* and would deprive district courts of a necessary tool to ensure that fee awards are truly “reasonable.”

**CONCLUSION**

This Court should affirm the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that an enhancement for superior performance and exceptional results remains available under federal fee-shifting statutes in the rare and exceptional case where the record supports it.
