**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language and legislative intent of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that California’s wage and hour laws apply on the OCS so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute and ignores binding precedent. The correct rule is that state law may only be applied on the OCS to fill a *gap* in federal law—absent such a gap, federal law governs exclusively. This principle is rooted in the structure, history, and precedent of OCSLA, and is consistent with the Supreme Court’s repeated insistence that federal statutes are not to be supplanted by state law unless necessary to complete the federal scheme.

**I. The OCSLA’s Choice of Law Provision Is Not a Blanket Adoption of State Law, But a Narrow Mechanism to Fill Gaps in Federal Law**

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a general invitation to apply any “applicable and not inconsistent” state law—regardless of whether federal law already governs the subject—contradicts the Act’s purpose and the settled understanding of its operation. The provision does not create a default rule of state law application; rather, it functions as a *gap-filling mechanism*. As the Supreme Court held in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835, 23 L.Ed.2d 360 (1969), the OCSLA “incorporates state law only to the extent that it is necessary to fill a gap in federal law.” The Court emphasized that the Act was not intended to “supplant” federal law but to “supplement” it where federal law was silent or inadequate. Id. at 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Tallentire v. United States*, 477 U.S. 208, 218, 106 S.Ct. 2485, 91 L.Ed.2d 174 (1986), where the Court stated that the OCSLA’s choice of law provision “does not automatically apply state law to every issue arising on the OCS.” Instead, the provision “applies only when federal law is silent or inadequate.” Id. at 218, 106 S.Ct. 2485. The Court further explained that the “special relationship with the shore community” that justifies the application of state law “does not support claims brought pursuant to § 1333(a)(2)(A) regardless of the location of the accident.” Id. at 218–19, 106 S.Ct. 2485. Thus, the mere fact that California law is “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law is insufficient; the law must be *needed* to fill a gap.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—endorsing a “no gap, no application” rule—ignores this foundational principle. By holding that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent, the court effectively renders the FLSA’s comprehensive framework superfluous. But as the Supreme Court has long held, “Congress does not intend to displace state law unless it is necessary to do so.” *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 398, 107 S.Ct. 2425, 96 L.Ed.2d 318 (1987). The FLSA is not a mere floor; it is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour standards for all employees, including those on the OCS. See *29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq.* The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, explicitly permits states to impose *more protective* standards, but it does not require federal courts to apply such laws when federal law already provides a complete remedy. The presence of a savings clause does not transform federal law into a mere baseline—especially when, as here, the federal law is fully operative and comprehensive.

**II. The Federal Law Is Not Inadequate; The FLSA Provides a Complete and Exclusive Remedy**

The district court correctly concluded that the FLSA is a “comprehensive statutory scheme” that “leaves no room for state law to address wage and hour grievances.” This conclusion is supported by *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), which established that state law may only be applied on the OCS “to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. The Fifth Circuit’s test—requiring a “significant void”—has been consistently applied in the context of OCSLA and is the correct standard. See *Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc.*, 713 F.3d 208, 214 (5th Cir. 2013); *Demette v. Falcon Drilling Co.*, 280 F.3d 492, 505 n.2 (5th Cir. 2002) (DeMoss, J., dissenting). The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of this test, without meaningful justification, undermines decades of settled precedent.

The FLSA is not silent on meal periods, overtime, or wage payment.
