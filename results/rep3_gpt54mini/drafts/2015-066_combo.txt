**Argument**

Section 117(a) does not authorize the government to prove a felony by relying on prior uncounseled convictions that would be constitutionally infirm if entered in federal or state court. The Sixth Amendment guarantees that, in “all criminal prosecutions,” the accused shall enjoy the assistance of counsel. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68-69 (1932). That guarantee is fundamental because counsel is essential to an accurate and fair adjudication. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 462-63 (1938); *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 343-45 (1963). When the Government seeks to transform prior convictions into an element of a new felony, it may not rest on convictions obtained without that constitutional safeguard. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), though later than the authorities cited below, reflects the enduring principle that the prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt; the point here is older still: criminal adjudication must be regular and reliable. *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 90-91 (1923).

The use of an uncounseled tribal conviction here is especially problematic because tribal proceedings are not governed by the Sixth Amendment, yet federal law is. Tribes possess retained sovereignty, but federal courts have long recognized limits on the use of procedures foreign to the Constitution when liberty is at stake. *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 571-74 (1883); *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384-85 (1896). Section 117(a) does not merely acknowledge the existence of a prior conviction; it makes that conviction an indispensable predicate to a new federal felony. That is a materially different use than the sentence-enhancement context in which courts sometimes tolerate collateral consideration of prior judgments. The Constitution tolerates no shortcut in the proof of an element. *Callan v. Wilson*, 127 U.S. 540, 549-55 (1888); *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 312-30 (1930).

This Court’s precedents also show that a conviction obtained without constitutionally adequate counsel cannot be used to support later criminal liability in a way that deepens punishment or changes the nature of the charge. In *Gideon*, the Court held that the right to counsel is so basic that a conviction obtained without it cannot stand. 372 U.S. at 343-45. In *Johnson*, the Court explained that a waiver of counsel must be knowing and intelligent, and that courts must “indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver.” 304 U.S. at 464. Those teachings would be hollow if the Government could take a conviction obtained without counsel and use it as the necessary building block for a new felony prosecution.

The Government will argue that recidivist statutes punish only the latest offense. But older precedent does not help it here. Even where prior convictions may affect punishment, the Court has treated the new offense as distinct only because the recidivist fact is a matter of status or sentencing, not an element needed to establish guilt. *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311, 313-15 (1901); *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 623-24 (1912); *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673, 678-79 (1895). Section 117(a) goes further. Congress required the Government to prove two prior domestic-violence convictions before it may obtain a felony conviction at all. That means the prior convictions are not merely sentencing considerations; they are part of the offense itself. A conviction so used must therefore satisfy constitutional standards of reliability and fairness. *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 68-69; *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 462-63.

Nor can the Government avoid the Sixth Amendment by pointing to the tribal character of the prior proceedings. Federal courts have long respected tribal sovereignty, but they have also recognized that tribal judgments cannot be used in a manner that conflicts with fundamental constitutional protections when those judgments become the basis for federal criminal liability. *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. at 571-74; *Talton*, 163 U.S. at 384-85. Section 117(a) should not be read to authorize a federal felony based on prior convictions obtained without counsel, because Congress is presumed not to legislate in derogation of constitutional safeguards absent a clear statement. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221-22 (1876).

The judgment below should be reversed. The Constitution does not permit the Government to bootstrap an uncounseled tribal conviction into an essential element of a federal felony.
