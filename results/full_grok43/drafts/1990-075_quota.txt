ARGUMENT

I. The temporary scheduling provisions of the Controlled Substances Act supply an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General’s exercise of delegated authority.

Article I, Section 1 vests legislative power in Congress, yet “Congress may delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Court has upheld delegations containing far less specific guidance. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947); Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952).

Section 811(h) directs the Attorney General to consider three enumerated factors drawn from § 811(c)—history and pattern of abuse, scope and significance of abuse, and risk to public health—before finding an imminent hazard. Temporary placement is limited to eighteen months, requires notice in the Federal Register, and obliges consideration of the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ comments. These constraints mirror the goals, factors, and limits the Court found sufficient in Mistretta. 488 U.S. at 376-77.

Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), are inapposite. Those statutes supplied “no criterion to govern the President’s course.” Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 415. The CSA, by contrast, prescribes both substantive standards and procedural safeguards.

II. The Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is statutorily authorized.

Congress expressly empowered the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General exercised that authority in 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). Subdelegation of this character has long been sustained when the governing statute permits it. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-94 (1892); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-17 (1911). Nothing in § 811(h) withdraws the general delegation power or requires personal exercise by the Attorney General.

Because the statute supplies an intelligible principle and the subdelegation rests on express congressional authorization, the Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power.
