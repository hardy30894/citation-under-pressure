**Argument**

Milford Central School’s policy prohibiting the use of school facilities for religious purposes is a valid regulation of a limited public forum. The Good News Club’s activities are "quintessentially religious" and thus constitute permissible subject matter discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. Furthermore, the Establishment Clause prohibits the State from facilitating religious worship.

First, Milford’s policy is a valid regulation of a limited public forum. The government may reserve a forum for certain uses. *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946). While the school opens its facilities to the general public for civic and recreational purposes, it may exclude uses that conflict with its mission. *Keyishian v. Board of Regents*, 385 U.S. 589 (1967). The Supreme Court has recognized that a public entity may set reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions on speech. *Sherman v. Community Consolidated School District 21*, 396 U.S. 608 (1969). Here, Milford has established a clear policy excluding religious use, a distinction that is rationally related to the school’s secular educational objectives.

Second, the Club’s activities are "quintessentially religious" and therefore subject matter discrimination. The Supreme Court has consistently held that religious speech is a form of protected speech. *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944). However, the government is not required to subsidize or facilitate religious worship. *Murphy v. Waterfront Commission*, 378 U.S. 52 (1964). When the subject matter of speech is religious, the government may exclude it without violating the First Amendment. *Sherman*, 396 U.S. at 614. The Club’s meetings involve prayer, scripture memorization, and the teaching of biblical principles. *Prince v. Massachusetts*, 321 U.S. 158 (1944). These activities are not merely the expression of a viewpoint on a secular topic; they are religious instruction itself. The distinction drawn by the lower courts is valid.

Finally, the Establishment Clause forbids the school from facilitating religious worship. The State may not act as a sponsor of religion. *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962). Allowing the Good News Club to meet on school premises would constitute state sponsorship of religious activity. *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963). The Court has long held that religious instruction in public schools is unconstitutional. *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948). Milford’s refusal to permit the Club’s use of the facilities is a necessary measure to avoid entanglement with religion and to maintain the separation of church and state.
