# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Imposes a Causal Requirement, Not a Situs Requirement.

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to the "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). By its terms, the provision asks only whether the injury was causally connected to shelf operations. It says nothing about where the employee's body happened to be when the injury occurred. This Court has long held that where a statute's language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Congress's word choice confirms that a causal test, not a geographic one, governs. Section 1333(b) twice uses causal phrasing—"resulting from" and "as the result of"—rather than locational phrasing such as "occurring on" the Shelf. Congress knew how to draft a geographic limitation when it wanted one: it defined the Shelf itself by reference to "submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters." 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The absence of comparable locational language in § 1333(b) is not an oversight for courts to correct; it reflects a deliberate choice that this Court should respect rather than override. See Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 485.

## II. OCSLA and the Longshore Act Are Remedial Statutes That Must Be Construed to Serve Their Coverage Purposes.

Congress enacted OCSLA to ensure that extraction of the Nation's offshore resources would not proceed in a legal vacuum, extending to fixed structures on the Shelf a body of law—including compensation for injured workers—adequate to that undertaking. Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969). That remedial purpose cannot be reconciled with a construction that strips coverage from a worker whose fatal injury was causally bound up with shelf operations merely because the fatal blow landed onshore rather than offshore.

This Court's broader Longshore Act jurisprudence confirms that coverage provisions receive expansive, purpose-driven construction. In Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114 (1962), the Court construed the Act to reach as far as Congress's power over maritime injuries reasonably permitted, rejecting a cramped reading that would have left injured workers without any remedy. In South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), the Court resolved coverage questions at the margins of the Act's reach with close attention to its underlying remedial purpose, deferring to administrative determinations that extended rather than withheld coverage. Id. at 260. And this Court has repeatedly held that doubts about the scope of workers' compensation statutes should be resolved in favor of coverage for the injured worker and his family. Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953).

That deference is especially appropriate here, given that Congress built the Longshore Act's remedial scheme around specialized administrative adjudication designed to serve the statute's protective ends. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 39-40 (1932). Nothing in that scheme supports adopting the most restrictive available reading of a coverage provision when a causally grounded reading better serves the statute's evident purpose.

## III. This Court's Functional, Causal Approach to Longshore Act Coverage Governs the Meaning of "As the Result Of."

Cardillo v. Liberty Mutual Insurance Co., 330 U.S. 469 (1947), demonstrates that this Court gives the Longshore Act's causal language—there, "arising out of and in the course of employment"—a broad, practical construction, sustaining coverage on reasonable inferences drawn from the employment relationship rather than rigid formalism about the precise moment or place of injury. Id. at 478-80. Section 1333(b)'s parallel causal phrase, "as the result of," calls for the same functional inquiry.

This Court's decision in O'Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc., 340 U.S. 504 (1951), is directly on point. The Court there rejected any requirement of a tight causal or physical nexus between the precise nature of the claimant's duties and the accident, holding instead that "all that is required is that the obligations or conditions of employment create the zone of special danger out of which the injury arose." Id. at 507. The Court cautioned against a construction of workmen's compensation coverage so "narrow" that it would defeat the statute's protective purpose. Id. at 506.

Juan Valladolid's employment placed him within precisely such a zone of shelf-related risk. He worked as a roustabout stationed principally aboard the Hogan platform, and his periodic duty of consolidating scrap metal generated by offshore operations—performed at a facility built specifically to receive and process crude oil extracted from the Shelf—was an integral incident of that offshore employment. The connection between his death and shelf operations is direct, not incidental. Under the functional approach this Court applied in O'Leary and Cardillo, that connection satisfies § 1333(b) without regard to which side of an arbitrary three-mile line his body happened to occupy at the moment of injury.

## IV. Isolated, Unnecessary Statements in Opinions Addressing Different Provisions Do Not Control.

To the extent petitioners rely on stray language from an opinion addressing an entirely different OCSLA provision, that reliance is misplaced. This Court has long recognized that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and that such expressions, "if they go beyond the case, . . . ought not to control the judgment in a subsequent suit when the very point is presented for decision." Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821). This Court reaffirmed that principle in Humphrey's Executor v. United States, explaining that unconsidered dicta are not to be treated as binding resolutions of questions the Court was never asked to decide. 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). A passing remark offered without briefing or analysis of the precise question presented here—whether § 1333(b) imposes a situs-of-injury requirement distinct from its situs-of-operations requirement—cannot override the statute's plain causal text or the remedial construction this Court has consistently applied to compensation legislation of this character.

## CONCLUSION

Because § 1333(b) asks only whether an injury resulted from operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf, and because this Court's precedents require causal, remedial statutory language to be construed in favor of coverage, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
