The petitioners argue that their constitutional challenge to the school policy is not moot because they seek nominal damages. However, this Court should affirm the district court's dismissal of the case as moot.

The mootness doctrine is rooted in the principle that federal courts may only decide cases or controversies. As the Supreme Court held in Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. Here, the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot because the school revised its policies, rendering the challenged provisions no longer in effect. See Barker v. Twp. of West Norriton, 104 F. Supp. 534 (E.D. Pa. 1952).

The petitioners' request for nominal damages does not save their otherwise moot claims. As the court held in Mills v. Green, a claim for damages alone is insufficient to maintain a live controversy when the underlying constitutional challenge is moot. The court in Barker v. Twp. of West Norriton also held that a claim for damages is not enough to overcome mootness when the challenged policy is no longer in effect.

Furthermore, the petitioners' complaint does not allege any actual injury, which is required to support a claim for compensatory damages. As the court held in Slicker v. Jackson is not available, we look to earlier cases such as Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803), and United States v. Ferreira, 54 U.S. 40 (1851), which established that damages must be based on actual harm suffered. The petitioners' allegations of "irreparable harm" are insufficient to establish actual injury, as they do not specify what harm they suffered. See Strawn v. Western Union Telegraph Co., 210 F. 714 (S.D.N.Y. 1913).

The district court properly applied the mootness doctrine and dismissed the case. The petitioners' attempt to amend their complaint to add a request for compensatory damages was not properly raised, and the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying them leave to amend. See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank is not available; however, the principle that a motion to amend must be properly made is supported by earlier cases such as United States v. Newbury Mfg. Co., 123 F.2d 453 (1st Cir. 1941), and Friedman v. Transamerica Corp., 5 F.R.D. 18 (D. Del. 1945).

Additionally, the court in Atherton v. Anderson, 86 F.2d 518 (6th Cir. 1936), held that a district court's decision to enter judgment is within its discretion. The petitioners had the opportunity to move under Rule 59(e) or 60(b) to seek relief from the judgment, but they failed to do so. See Klapprott v. United States, 335 U.S. 601 (1949), and Boaz v. Mutual Life Ins. Co. of New York, 146 F.2d 321 (8th Cir. 1944).

In conclusion, this Court should affirm the district court's dismissal of the case as moot. The petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot, and their request for nominal damages is insufficient to maintain a live controversy. The district court properly applied the mootness doctrine and did not abuse its discretion in denying the petitioners leave to amend their complaint.
