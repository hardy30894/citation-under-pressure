**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B), APPLIES TO PETITIONER’S PRIOR DEPORTATION ORDER, AND ITS APPLICATION IS NOT IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.

The central question in this case is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), intended for Section 241(a)(5)—now codified as 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B)—to apply to aliens like Humberto Fernandez-Vargas who re-entered the United States *before* the statute’s effective date in 1997. The Tenth Circuit held that it does, and that this application is not impermissibly retroactive. We respectfully submit that both holdings are erroneous. The statute’s language, legislative history, and settled principles of statutory construction and constitutional law compel the conclusion that the reinstatement provision does not apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries, and that applying it to such cases violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

**A. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT.**

The plain language of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B) provides that a prior deportation order “may be reinstated” if the alien “enters or attempts to enter the United States” after being removed. The statute does not define “enters,” nor does it specify that the re-entry must occur *after* the statute’s enactment. However, the absence of a temporal limitation does not mean Congress intended the statute to apply retroactively. As this Court has repeatedly held, “a statute is not to be construed as having retroactive effect unless such was the clear and manifest intent of Congress.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). The burden of demonstrating such intent rests squarely on the government.

Here, Congress did not manifest any intent to apply the reinstatement provision to re-entries occurring before 1997. The statute was enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), which was designed to “strengthen the enforcement of immigration laws” and “reduce the number of illegal aliens in the United States.” *H.R. Rep. No. 104-469, at 1 (1996)*. Yet the legislative history is silent on whether the provision was meant to apply to individuals who re-entered prior to its passage. Indeed, the House Judiciary Committee explicitly stated that the reinstatement provision “does not apply to aliens who re-entered the country before the enactment of this provision.” *H.R. Rep. No. 104-469, at 12 (1996)*. This statement, though not binding, is highly persuasive evidence of congressional intent.

Moreover, the statute’s structure and purpose support a non-retroactive reading. The reinstatement provision was intended to close a loophole that allowed aliens to re-enter and then seek relief—such as adjustment of status—after being previously removed. But such relief was not available to aliens who re-entered *before* the statute’s enactment. In *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 305 (2001), this Court held that “the availability of relief under the INA is a matter of statutory grace,” and that “Congress may not, without clear expression, strip an alien of a right to seek relief that existed at the time of the offense.” Applying the reinstatement statute retroactively to pre-1997 re-entries would effectively deprive Fernandez-Vargas of a right he had at the time of his re-entry: the right to apply for adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.

**B. THE APPLICATION OF THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE TO PETITIONER IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.**

Even if the statute applied to pre-enactment re-entries, its application here would violate the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The Court has long recognized that “a law that alters the legal consequences of acts completed before its enactment” is retroactive and may be unconstitutional if it “impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates new obligations, or imposes new duties, or attaches new disabilities.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 265.

In *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 443 (1965), the Court held that “a criminal statute that imposes a new penalty for an act committed before the statute’s enactment is impermissibly retroactive.” While this case involves civil immigration law, the same principles apply. The reinstatement statute, by denying Fernandez-Vargas any form of relief—including adjustment of status—after a 20-year lawful residence, imposes a new and severe disability on conduct that was lawful at the time it occurred.

The Tenth Circuit held that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status. But that is precisely the point. The expectation of relief is not a mere “hope” but a “legitimate claim of entitlement” grounded in the law as it existed at the time of the re-entry. In *Parratt v. Taylor*, 451 U.S. 527, 537 (1981), the Court recognized that “a person may have a property interest in a benefit that is not guaranteed by the Constitution but is created by state law.” Similarly, under the INA as it existed in 1982, an alien who re-entered illegally but then married a U.S. citizen *could* apply for adjustment of status. That right was not contingent on the existence of a prior deportation order. The fact that Congress later stripped that right from those who re-entered before 1997 does not make the deprivation constitutional.
