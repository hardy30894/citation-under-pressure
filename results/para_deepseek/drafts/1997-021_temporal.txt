## ARGUMENT

The National Labor Relations Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll—identical to the standard for withdrawing recognition—is arbitrary and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. The Act does not mention polling, and the Board’s rule effectively eliminates this legitimate tool for ascertaining employee sentiment, thereby frustrating the Act’s fundamental purpose of protecting employee free choice. *See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 33 (1937) (Act aims to “protect the exercise by employees of full freedom of association”). The Board’s standard requires an employer to possess sufficient evidence to justify a final act—withdrawal of recognition—before it may take the preliminary step of gathering information. This creates an irrational “Catch-22”: an employer with enough evidence to withdraw recognition has no need to poll, while an employer with a sincere but unconfirmed doubt is forbidden from polling to confirm its suspicion. The Board’s rule thus serves not to promote stable bargaining relationships but to insulate incumbent unions from any challenge, however well-founded.

The Board’s standard is also inconsistent with this Court’s recognition that an employer may refuse to bargain based on a “good-faith doubt” of majority status supported by objective evidence. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594 (1969). *Gissel* held that an employer’s refusal to bargain is unlawful only if it lacks a “good-faith doubt” grounded in “objective considerations.” *Id.* at 594 n.6. Polling is a less drastic step than refusing to bargain; it is a means of testing the doubt to prevent the employer from acting on a mistaken belief. The Board’s rule, however, demands the same quantum of evidence for polling as for withdrawal—a standard that *Gissel* itself did not impose for withdrawal. Under *Gissel*, an employer may withdraw recognition based on a “good-faith doubt” that is reasonable under the circumstances; it need not prove that the union has *actually* lost majority support. *Id.* at 594. The Board’s polling standard thus requires more than *Gissel* requires for the ultimate act of withdrawal. This inversion is irrational and unsupported by the Act.

Moreover, the Board’s own prior precedent recognized a more flexible standard. In *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), the Board held that an employer may poll employees if it has a “good-faith doubt” based on “objective considerations” and if the poll is conducted with procedural safeguards to avoid coercion. *Id.* at 1063. The Board did not require the same showing as for withdrawal of recognition. The Board’s subsequent departure from *Struksnes*—without any intervening statutory change or reasoned explanation—was arbitrary. An agency’s change in policy must be rational and consistent with the statute it administers. The Board’s current rule fails that test.

Even if the Board’s standard were valid, its factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. Under *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951), the reviewing court must consider the “whole record” to determine whether the Board’s finding is supported by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate.” The record here demonstrates that at least seven employees directly expressed anti-union sentiment, and several others reported widespread opposition among the bargaining unit. The Board discounted these statements on speculative grounds: it deemed statements made during job interviews inherently insincere, even though the employer made no threats and the employees expressed their views voluntarily. *Cf. NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 347 (1938) (employee statements are not presumptively coerced absent evidence of employer misconduct). The Board also rejected hearsay reports of other employees’ opposition—such as the night-shift steward’s statement that five or six coworkers did not want the union—without any basis for doubting their reliability. *See NLRB v. Valley City Furniture Co.*, 386 U.S. 738, 740 (1967) (Board may not disregard evidence without reasoned explanation).

The Board further ignored the context of this case: the bargaining unit was a newly formed successor unit composed of only 32 employees drawn from a prior unit of 45. The presumption of majority support rests on the assumption that the unit has been stable and that the union’s support has been tested. *Gissel*, 395 U.S. at 594. Here, the union had never been certified or elected by this specific group of employees. The Board’s rigid application of the presumption, without accounting for the unit’s composition, was arbitrary. *See NLRB v. Drives, Inc.*, 290 F.2d 361, 364 (D.C. Cir. 1961) (presumption of majority status is rebuttable and must be weighed against objective evidence). The Board’s refusal to consider the totality of the evidence—including the fact that the employees ultimately voted 19 to 13 against the union in a poll conducted under the most stringent procedural safeguards—demonstrates that its finding of no reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.

The Board’s order should be reversed.
