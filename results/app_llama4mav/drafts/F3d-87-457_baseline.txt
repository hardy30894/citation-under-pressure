## Argument

The Florida League of Professional Lobbyists, Inc. (the "League") challenges the constitutionality of Chapter 93-121, Laws of Florida, which regulates lobbying activities in the state. Specifically, the League contests the statute's disclosure requirements and its ban on contingency-fee lobbying. We argue that the statute's disclosure provisions are overbroad and facially invalid, and that the ban on contingency fees is unconstitutional in light of recent Supreme Court precedent.

### I. The Disclosure Provisions are Overbroad and Facially Invalid

The statute's disclosure requirements are overly broad and burdensome, chilling protected First Amendment activity. The Supreme Court has held that disclosure requirements can be unconstitutional if they are not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest. See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64-68 (1976) (striking down certain disclosure requirements under the Federal Election Campaign Act). The disclosure provisions at issue here require lobbyists to disclose all lobbying expenditures, including those made by their principals, and to categorize these expenditures in a non-exclusive list of categories. Fla.Stats. §§ 11.045(3)(a). This level of disclosure is not narrowly tailored to serve the state's interest in preventing corruption or undue influence.

The breadth of the disclosure requirements is particularly problematic because it encompasses activities that are not necessarily related to lobbying, such as "research" and "publications." Id. This could chill legitimate First Amendment activity, such as grassroots lobbying or public education campaigns. See NAACP v. Alabama, 357 U.S. 449, 462-63 (1958) (holding that compelled disclosure of NAACP membership lists was unconstitutional because it could chill associational activity). The availability of an administrative procedure for seeking clarification does not cure the overbreadth, as it places the burden on the lobbyist to seek clarification rather than on the state to draft a more precise statute. See id. § 11.045(4).

### II. The Ban on Contingency Fees is Unconstitutional

The statute's ban on contingency fees is also unconstitutional. While the Supreme Court has upheld similar bans in the past, see, e.g., Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483, 488 (1955), more recent cases have cast doubt on the constitutionality of such restrictions. See, e.g., Edenfield v. Fane, 507 U.S. 761, 774-75 (1993) (striking down a ban on in-person solicitation by certified public accountants). The Court has increasingly scrutinized restrictions on commercial speech, applying a more rigorous standard of review. See Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. v. Public Serv. Comm'n, 447 U.S. 557, 566 (1980).

The ban on contingency fees restricts the ability of lobbyists to engage in commercial speech, and the state has not demonstrated that this restriction is necessary to serve a compelling government interest. While the state may have an interest in preventing corruption or undue influence, a ban on contingency fees is not narrowly tailored to achieve this interest. See Edenfield, 507 U.S. at 774-75. We acknowledge that this court is bound by prior Supreme Court precedent, but we argue that the ban on contingency fees should be reevaluated in light of more recent cases.

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the state. The League has demonstrated that the statute's disclosure provisions are overbroad and facially invalid, and that the ban on contingency fees is unconstitutional. We respectfully request that this court reverse the district court's judgment and hold that Chapter 93-121, Laws of Florida, is unconstitutional to the extent it requires extensive disclosure by lobbyists and their principals and bars lobbyists from receiving fees contingent on their success in affecting legislative or executive outcomes.
