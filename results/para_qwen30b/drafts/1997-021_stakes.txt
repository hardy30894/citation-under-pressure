**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLING IS IRRATIONAL, UNREASONABLE, AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) does not prohibit employers from testing employee sentiment regarding union representation; indeed, it implicitly permits such inquiry as a means of avoiding the risk of bargaining with a minority union—a violation of § 8(a)(2). See *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). Yet the NLRB’s current standard—requiring an employer to have “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll—renders polling functionally useless and creates a regulatory absurdity: an employer may not lawfully conduct a poll unless it already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, yet that same evidence would preclude the need for a poll in the first place. This circular logic undermines the NLRA’s purpose of facilitating stable collective bargaining relationships by discouraging both unnecessary conflict and hasty decisions.

The Board’s own rationale exposes the flaw. In *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989), the Board acknowledged that polling serves a legitimate purpose: to provide employers with “more certain, precise information” about union support than their own subjective doubts. But if the employer must already possess objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition—evidence that would itself dispel uncertainty—then the poll adds no value. The Board’s insistence on this high threshold thus defeats its own stated goal of using polling as a tool for clarification. As the Court observed in *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786, 110 S.Ct. 1542, 1549, 108 L.Ed.2d 801 (1990), “the Board is charged with the primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy.” That authority includes crafting rules that are rational, consistent with statutory goals, and administratively workable. The Board’s current standard fails all three tests.

Moreover, the Board’s decision to treat polling and RM elections under identical evidentiary standards is irrational. The Board explicitly states that RM elections are the preferred method for testing union support, *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061, yet allows polling only when the employer meets the same high bar. If RM elections are superior, then polling should be subject to a lower standard—otherwise, employers will avoid polls entirely and opt for elections, which are more costly and time-consuming. The Board’s approach thus incentivizes inefficiency while undermining the very flexibility it claims to promote.

This inconsistency is compounded by the fact that the Board permits employers to withdraw recognition without any procedural safeguards whatsoever—yet demands a higher evidentiary threshold before allowing a poll. *See* *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1298 (9th Cir.1984) (“Polling is a means by which employers may avoid the risk of bargaining with a minority union”). If the risk of bargaining with a minority union is so serious as to warrant a strict evidentiary standard for polling, why is it not equally serious when an employer unilaterally withdraws recognition? The Board’s failure to reconcile these competing principles renders its standard arbitrary and capricious.

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

Under § 10(e) of the NLRA, 29 U.S.C. § 160(e), the Board’s factual findings must be supported by “substantial evidence.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 477, 71 S.Ct. 456, 459, 95 L.Ed. 456 (1951). Here, the Board concluded that Allentown Mack lacked “objective indications” of a loss of union support, relying primarily on the fact that only seven employees made statements opposing the union. But this conclusion ignores overwhelming, unrefuted evidence of declining union support.

First, the company had received direct, contemporaneous statements from multiple employees indicating dissatisfaction with the union. Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack—though not employed on January 25—had expressed anti-union sentiments during interviews prior to their hiring. While the Board excluded them based on timing, the ALJ properly considered their statements as part of the broader context of employee sentiment. More significantly, Kermit Bloch reported that the entire night shift—five or six employees—did not want the union. Though Bloch did not testify, his statement was corroborated by the absence of any contrary evidence and was made in the course of employment discussions. Similarly, Ron Mohr, the union steward, told management that “if a vote were taken, the union would lose,” and that he believed employees “didn’t want a union.” These statements, though informal, were made in the context of ongoing negotiations and reflect a clear shift in sentiment among key personnel.

The Board’s refusal to credit these statements rests on faulty legal reasoning. It dismissed Mohr’s remark as “unverified” and “off-the-cuff,” but the NLRA does not require verifiable, sworn testimony for every expression of employee sentiment. *See* *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335–36 (9th Cir.1995) (holding that dissatisfaction with union representation does not equate to opposition to representation). The Board conflates the burden of proof with the standard for admissibility.
