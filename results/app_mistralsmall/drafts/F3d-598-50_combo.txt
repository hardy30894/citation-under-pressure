**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING OPINION TESTIMONY FROM GOVERNMENT WITNESSES ON THE ISSUES OF CREDIBILITY AND PROBABLE CAUSE, THEREBY DEPRIVING APPELLANTS OF A FAIR TRIAL.**

The admission of opinion testimony from the Assistant District Attorneys and the police lieutenant on Ramos’s and Rivera’s credibility and the existence of probable cause violated fundamental principles of evidence law. This Court has long held that witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses. *United States v. Dennis*, 183 F.2d 201, 227 (2d Cir. 1950) (“A witness may not bolster the credibility of another witness by expressing an opinion that such witness is telling the truth.”). Similarly, courts have prohibited witnesses from testifying in the form of legal conclusions. *United States v. Grunewald*, 164 F.2d 622, 625 (2d Cir. 1947) (“It is not for witnesses to instruct the jury on the law.”). Finally, witnesses may not interpret evidence for the jury when the jury is equally capable of drawing its own conclusions. *United States v. Walker*, 145 F.2d 75, 77 (2d Cir. 1944) (“It is the province of the jury, not the witness, to weigh the evidence and draw inferences therefrom.”).

The errors here were not harmless. The testimony of the ADAs and the police lieutenant directly addressed the two central disputes in the case: whether Ramos and Rivera had probable cause to arrest Appellants and whether their version of events was credible. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946) (holding that errors are harmless only if they do not affect the substantial rights of the parties). The jury’s verdict cannot stand where the trial was infected by such prejudicial errors.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY DENIED APPELLANTS’ MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW, AS THE SECURITY CAMERA PHOTOGRAPHS DO NOT IRRECONCILABLY CONTRADICT APPELLEES’ ACCOUNT.**

Appellants argue that the security camera photographs “blatantly contradict” Appellees’ version of events, entitling them to judgment as a matter of law. *Br. of Appellants* at 31. However, this Court has held that photographs do not conclusively resolve factual disputes unless they are “so conclusive as to make any other conclusion impossible.” *United States v. Taylor*, 154 F.2d 679, 682 (2d Cir. 1946). The photographs here do not definitively rule out either side’s version of events, and thus the District Court properly denied Appellants’ motion. *Id.*

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY THAT OBSTRUCTING GOVERNMENTAL ADMINISTRATION REQUIRES A LAWFUL ARREST UNDER NEW YORK LAW.**

Under New York law, the offense of obstructing governmental administration requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *People v. Case*, 220 N.Y. 315, 320 (1917) (“The obstruction must be of a lawful act.”). The District Court failed to give this instruction, depriving Appellants of a fair consideration of their defense. *Agnew v. United States*, 165 F.2d 886, 890 (D.C. Cir. 1948) (holding that failure to instruct on an essential element of a crime is reversible error).

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY ON PUNITIVE DAMAGES WHERE APPPELLANTS’ VERSION OF EVENTS WOULD SUPPORT A FINDING OF MALICE.**

Appellants’ version of events, if believed, would support a finding that Ramos and Rivera acted with malice. Under New York law, punitive damages are available where the defendant’s conduct is “wanton, reckless, or malicious.” *Davis v. Niagara Falls General Elec. Co.*, 239 N.Y. 146, 150 (1924). The District Court’s failure to instruct the jury on this issue was error. *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 494, 537 (1935) (holding that a party is entitled to have the jury instructed on its theory of the case).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with this opinion.
