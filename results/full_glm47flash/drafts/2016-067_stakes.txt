**Argument**

I. The FDCPA Defines a “Debt Collector” as One Who Collects for Another, Not for Himself.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates only those who collect debts on behalf of others, not creditors collecting their own debts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). To state a claim under the FDCPA, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant was acting as a “debt collector” at the time of the alleged violation. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The Supreme Court confirmed that the Act’s definition of “debt collector” includes a person “who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] ... another.” *Id.* (quoting 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)). This definition hinges on the relationship between the collector and the creditor at the time of collection, not the status of the debt at the time of origination. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015).

Here, the complaint fails to allege that Santander was collecting debts on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. Instead, the complaint alleges that CitiFinancial sold the defaulted loans to Santander. *See* Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc., 810 F.3d 560, 563 (4th Cir. 2016). This sale transferred the right to collect the debt to Santander, making Santander the creditor. When a creditor collects its own debt, it is not acting as a debt collector. *See* S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977) (“The Committee intends the term ‘debt collector,’ subject to the exclusions discussed below, to cover all third persons who regularly collect, debts for others”).

II. The Default Status of the Debt Does Not Determine Whether an Entity Is a Debt Collector.

The petitioners argue that because the debts were in default when Santander acquired them, Santander must be a debt collector. This argument misinterprets the statutory structure. The FDCPA defines “debt collector” first, and then provides exclusions. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314. If a person does not satisfy one of the definitions in the main text of § 1692a(6), the exclusions in subsections (A)-(F) do not come into play. *Id.* The default status of the debt is relevant only to the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which protects entities servicing non-defaulted debts. It does not transform a creditor collecting its own account into a debt collector.

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition. *Henson*, 810 F.3d at 566. The court noted that the petitioners’ argument “contains several interpretational and logical flaws, such that their interpretation of the FDCPA ultimately stands in tension with its plain language.” *Id.* at 565. Because Santander collected on its own account, it was a creditor, not a debt collector, and thus not subject to the FDCPA.

III. The Complaint Failed to State a Claim Because It Did Not Alleged Santander Was a Debt Collector.

For a plaintiff to succeed on an FDCPA claim, all statutory elements must be met at the time of the prohibited conduct. *Henson*, 810 F.3d at 568. The complaint alleged that Santander began collecting on the loans it had purchased, but it did not allege that Santander was acting on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. *Id.* at 563. Instead, the complaint alleged that CitiFinancial sold the loans to Santander, leaving Santander to collect on the debts for its own account. *Id.* at 563. Because the complaint failed to allege facts demonstrating that Santander was acting as a “debt collector” as defined by § 1692a(6) when it engaged in the challenged collection practices, the district court properly granted Santander’s motion to dismiss. *Id.* at 568.
