The Ninth Circuit's holding that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence to trigger ERISA's three-year statute of limitations is contrary to the plain meaning of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) and this Court's precedent. 

ERISA's statute of limitations provision, 29 U.S.C. § 1113, requires that an action be commenced within "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." The Ninth Circuit correctly identified that the statute does not define "actual knowledge." However, it failed to properly apply this Court's guidance on the matter.

In Tibble v. Edison Int'l, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), this Court addressed the statute of limitations under ERISA but did not directly define "actual knowledge." Nonetheless, the Ninth Circuit's prior decisions and other circuits' interpretations provide insight. The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case relies on its prior holdings, particularly Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), and Waller v. Blue Cross of California, 32 F.3d 1337 (9th Cir. 1994), which suggest that actual knowledge of the transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations.

The Ninth Circuit's requirement that a plaintiff have actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence effectively adds a scienter element to the statute of limitations, which is not supported by the statutory text. As the Ninth Circuit itself noted in Blanton, "the statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law." 760 F.2d at 992. This reasoning implies that knowledge of the transaction is sufficient.

Requiring actual knowledge of the transaction alone is consistent with the purpose of the statute of limitations, which is to bar stale claims and prevent plaintiffs from sleeping on their rights. When a plaintiff has actual knowledge of the transaction, they have sufficient information to investigate potential claims. The fact that they may not understand the legal implications of the transaction is not a sufficient reason to toll the statute of limitations.

In this case, Intel disclosed all relevant information about the investments to Sulyma through various documents. Sulyma's claim that he did not read or recall reading these documents creates a factual dispute, but it does not change the fact that he had actual knowledge of the transactions. The district court erred in granting summary judgment based on a disputed fact, but the Ninth Circuit's holding on the meaning of "actual knowledge" is the primary issue.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed because its interpretation of "actual knowledge" under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) is overly broad and contrary to the statutory text and relevant case law. A plaintiff should be deemed to have "actual knowledge" of a breach or violation for purposes of the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA when they have actual knowledge of the underlying transaction, regardless of whether they understand its legal implications.
