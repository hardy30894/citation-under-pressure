**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Board’s decision denying Petitioner Manfredo M. Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application for a disability annuity constitutes a “final decision” within the meaning of 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), and is therefore subject to judicial review in this Court. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding, based on its own precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is erroneous and must be overruled. That decision misapplies the statutory framework and ignores binding Supreme Court precedent establishing that a decision denying a request to reopen a prior claim is a final, reviewable determination when it conclusively resolves a claimant’s right to benefits.

The Railroad Retirement Act, enacted in 1934, provides for judicial review of “final decisions” of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The term “final decision” is not defined in the statute, but the Supreme Court has long held that a decision is final when it “conclusively determine[s] the rights of the parties” and “ends the litigation on the merits.” *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). This standard is not met merely because a claimant has not yet received benefits; rather, it is met when the administrative agency has definitively resolved the claimant’s entitlement to benefits, either on the merits or through a procedural bar that precludes further consideration.

In *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), the Court emphasized that “a decision is final when it is the last word of the administrative agency on the matter.” The Board’s denial of Salinas’s 2006 application, which became final on February 9, 2007, was such a decision. It was not a procedural ruling, but a substantive determination that Salinas did not qualify for a disability annuity under the Railroad Retirement Act. That determination was not subject to further administrative appeal, and the Board’s subsequent refusal to reopen the claim—after the expiration of the four-year window under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2—was not a mere procedural step, but a final adjudication of Salinas’s right to benefits.

The Board’s refusal to reopen the 2006 claim is not a “non-final” administrative action, as the Fifth Circuit suggests. In *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59, 64 (1956), the Court held that a decision that “ends the administrative process” and “determines the rights of the parties” is final, even if it is not a decision on the merits of the original claim. Here, the Board’s decision not to reopen the 2006 claim definitively concluded that Salinas’s prior application would not be reconsidered, and thus effectively determined that he would not receive benefits based on that application. This is precisely the kind of decision that Congress intended to be reviewable under § 355(f).

Moreover, the Board’s refusal to reopen is not insulated from review by the doctrine of exhaustion. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that exhaustion of administrative remedies is not required where the agency has “finally determined” the claimant’s rights. The Board’s decision in this case was not merely a procedural denial; it was a final determination that Salinas’s prior claim would not be reopened, and thus that he would not be entitled to benefits based on that claim. The claimant’s right to benefits was conclusively resolved.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is misplaced. That decision, which cites *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), is not binding precedent in this Court, and more importantly, it contradicts the Supreme Court’s own interpretation of finality in administrative decisions. In *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33 (1952), the Court held that a decision is final when it “ends the administrative process” and “determines the rights of the parties.” The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim satisfies this test.

The Board’s decision also falls within the category of decisions that are “final” under the general principles of administrative law. In *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59 (1956), the Court held that a decision that “ends the administrative process” and “determines the rights of the parties” is final, even if it is not a decision on the merits. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s claim is such a decision.

Furthermore, the Board’s decision is not a mere “administrative error” or procedural ruling. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court held that a decision that “finally determines” the rights of the parties is reviewable, even if it is not a decision on the merits. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s claim is such a decision.

Finally, the Board’s decision is not insulated from review by the doctrine of exhaustion. In *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33 (1952), the Court held that exhaustion is not required where the agency has “finally determined” the claimant’s rights. The Board’s decision in this case was not merely a procedural denial; it was a final determination that Salinas’s prior claim would not be reopened, and thus that he would not be entitled to benefits based on that claim.
