**ARGUMENT**

The Second Circuit erred in affirming the District’s exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel from school facilities based solely on the religious viewpoint of its proposed film series. The District opened its property to a broad array of community groups for after-hours expressive activities, yet singled out religious speech for exclusion. This constitutes viewpoint discrimination in violation of the First Amendment.

**I. The School District Created a Limited Public Forum Open to a Wide Range of Secular Speech**

When the government designates a forum for expressive activity, it may impose content-based restrictions only if they are “necessary to serve a compelling state interest” and “narrowly drawn.” *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). Even in a limited public forum, the government may not discriminate against speech based on its viewpoint. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

Here, the District permitted use of its facilities by numerous outside organizations—including the Boy Scouts, the Southern Harmonizers, and a lecture series on parapsychology—for presentations on family values, child rearing, and community issues. By opening its doors to such a wide range of secular groups, the District created a limited public forum. Once it did so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel simply because its message was religious. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267–69 (1981) (university that opened facilities to student groups could not exclude religious groups).

**II. The Exclusion Was Viewpoint-Based, Not Content-Neutral**

The District’s policy barred “religious purposes,” but the film series at issue—Dr. James Dobson’s “Turn Your Heart Toward Home”—addressed family values and child rearing from a Christian perspective. The District allowed other groups to present secular views on the same topics. For example, the parapsychology lecture series discussed “the human unconscious, the mind, the unconscious emotional system” and made “incidental reference to religious matters,” yet was permitted. The District thus permitted secular viewpoints on family and psychology while excluding religious viewpoints on identical subjects. That is the “paradigm” of viewpoint discrimination. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (government may not “discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint”).

The Second Circuit’s reliance on the absence of prior religious uses misses the point. The District’s policy and practice excluded all religious speech, regardless of topic. But when a forum is open to secular speech on a subject, the government cannot bar religious speech on that same subject. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393–94 (1993) (reversing the decision below). The District’s “narrowly drawn” exclusion of religious groups is not content-neutral; it targets a particular perspective.

**III. Equal Access Does Not Violate the Establishment Clause**

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation does not justify viewpoint discrimination. In *Widmar*, this Court held that allowing religious groups equal access to a public forum “would not be an establishment of religion.” 454 U.S. at 273. The same principle applies here. The District’s facilities were used after hours by community groups, not by students during the school day. Permitting Lamb’s Chapel to show its film series on an equal footing with secular groups would not convey government endorsement of religion. *Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 248 (1990) (plurality opinion) (“secondary school students are mature enough to understand that a school does not endorse or support student speech that it merely permits on a nondiscriminatory basis”).

**IV. The Second Circuit’s Distinction Between Student Groups and Outside Groups Is Irrelevant**

The court below distinguished *Widmar* and *Mergens* on the ground that those cases involved student groups, not outside organizations. But the forum analysis does not turn on the speaker’s identity. Once the government opens a forum for expressive activity, it must respect the First Amendment rights of all speakers. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45. The District allowed outside groups—including the Boy Scouts and a public library lecture series—to use its facilities. Having done so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel because of its religious viewpoint.

**CONCLUSION**

The District’s exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel from school facilities violated the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be reversed.
