**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE APPELLANTS ARE EXEMPT FROM THE ADEA AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The First Circuit held that “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). The Northern District of Illinois agreed, noting that judges exercise discretion and “make law” to resolve issues previously unsettled. *EEOC v. Illinois*, 721 F. Supp. 156, 160 (N.D. Ill. 1989). The Eastern District of Virginia reached the same conclusion, finding that judges are “appointees on the policymaking level.” *Schlitz v. Virginia*, 681 F. Supp. 330, 334 (E.D. Va. 1988). The Pennsylvania Supreme Court similarly held that judges are not “employees” under the ADEA because they are “appointees on the policymaking level.” *In re Stout*, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489, 492 (Pa. 1989). The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court agreed, finding that the exception applies to judges. *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). Because the appellants perform policymaking functions, they fall within the statutory exemption and are not “employees” entitled to protection under the ADEA.

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION PASSES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW.**

The Equal Protection Clause requires only a rational basis for a state law. *San Antonio School Dist. v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 28 (1973). Missouri has a legitimate interest in assuring that its public servants are competent. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976). Mandatory retirement addresses the risk of declining faculties, as “the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979). It also promotes efficiency by reducing delays in the administration of justice caused by the death or disabling illness of sitting judges. *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 629. Furthermore, the rule eliminates the “unpleasantness of selectively removing aged... judges.” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). Finally, the rule allows for diversity in the judiciary, making it more representative of society. *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Because the state has a rational basis for its mandatory retirement provision, it does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.
