**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Disclosure Provisions Are Facially Invalid Because They Are Overbroad and Violate the First Amendment Rights of Association and Speech.**

The statute compels lobbyists to disclose all lobbying expenditures, including the source of funds, and categorizes expenditures broadly. This sweeping disclosure chills protected associational activities. The Supreme Court has long held that compelled disclosure of membership and financial support can violate the First Amendment. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. 449, 460–62 (1958) (compelled production of membership lists invalid because it would expose members to reprisals). Similarly, in *Bates v. City of Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516, 523–24 (1960), the Court struck down a disclosure ordinance because it burdened associational rights without a compelling justification. The statute here imposes even broader disclosure, covering not just membership but all expenditures, without any showing of a compelling state interest.

The state’s interest in preventing corruption is legitimate, but disclosure must be narrowly tailored. In *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625–26 (1954), the Court upheld a narrower disclosure statute that applied only to persons who directly communicate with legislators. The Florida statute goes far beyond that, requiring disclosure of all expenditures by lobbyists and principals, including research and media advertising. This overbreadth chills speech and association. *Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479, 488–90 (1960) (statute requiring teachers to list all organizational memberships overbroad). Moreover, the statute provides no exemption for de minimis expenditures or for expenditures that might reveal sensitive associational ties. The absence of such exemptions renders the statute facially invalid. *Talley v. California*, 362 U.S. 60, 64–65 (1960) (ordinance requiring identification on handbills invalid because it could deter anonymous speech). The League’s members face a choice between complying with this overbroad disclosure and risking reprisal, which is precisely the harm the First Amendment prevents. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963) (statute invalid because it “sweeps broadly” and chills protected activity).

**II. The Contingency-Fee Ban Unconstitutionally Restricts the Right to Petition.**

The statute prohibits lobbyists from receiving compensation contingent on legislative outcomes. This ban directly burdens the right to petition the government for redress of grievances, a core First Amendment freedom. *Eastern Railroad Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight*, 365 U.S. 127, 137–38 (1961) (right to petition includes efforts to influence legislation). The Supreme Court has recognized that the use of contingent fee arrangements in the context of litigation is a form of protected association and speech. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 429–31 (invalidating ban on solicitation of legal business on contingency because it restricted the right to litigate for political ends). Lobbying is similarly a means of petitioning, and the contingency fee enables groups with limited resources to engage in effective advocacy.

The state’s asserted interest in preventing corruption is insufficient to justify an outright ban when less restrictive alternatives, such as disclosure, are available. *Harriss*, 347 U.S. at 625–26 (disclosure serves to deter corruption). The old cases cited by the state, such as *Trist v. Child*, 88 U.S. 441 (1874), and *Marshall v. United States*, 176 U.S. 289 (1900), are inapposite. Those cases involved contracts to procure government action through improper means or claims against the United States, not the modern practice of lobbying as protected speech. Moreover, those decisions predate the development of modern First Amendment doctrine, which requires strict scrutiny of laws that burden core political speech. The contingency-fee ban is a content-based restriction on the manner of petitioning and cannot survive such scrutiny. Because the statute is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling interest, it is facially invalid.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. This Court should reverse and hold Chapter 93-121 unconstitutional in its disclosure and contingency-fee provisions.
