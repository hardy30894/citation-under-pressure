**ARGUMENT**

**I. The challenged order is a lawful exercise of the district court’s equitable power to remedy constitutional violations.**

A federal court’s authority to enter injunctive relief in a case brought to vindicate constitutional rights is firmly established. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), this Court held that a suit against state officers to enjoin unconstitutional action is not barred by the Eleventh Amendment. The Court explained that such suits are “not against the State” but against individuals charged with enforcing an unconstitutional statute. *Id.* at 155-56. The same equitable power permits a district court to fashion relief necessary to terminate ongoing constitutional violations. *See* *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971) (citing *Brown v. Bd. of Educ.*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955)) (“Once a right and a violation have been shown, the scope of a district court’s equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad, for breadth and flexibility are inherent in equitable remedies.”).

Here, the district court found that California’s prison system subjected inmates to cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the Eighth Amendment. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 552 F. Supp. 2d 1050, 1060 (N.D. Cal. 2008). The court concluded that severe overcrowding was the primary cause of these violations and that population reduction was necessary to remedy them. *Id.* at 1061. Such relief is consistent with this Court’s recognition that overcrowding can constitute cruel and unusual punishment. *See* *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.14 (1978) (approving a population cap as a permissible remedy for unconstitutional prison conditions).

**II. The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar the relief ordered.**

The PLRA’s provisions do not strip courts of their inherent equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (2006) (limiting prospective relief in civil actions brought by prisoners). The statute’s remedial limits apply only to prospective relief that is “necessary to correct the violation of a federal right.” *Id.* § 3626(a)(1)(A). Where, as here, a constitutional violation has been found and the relief ordered is narrowly tailored to remedy that violation, the PLRA does not bar the relief. *See* *Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367, 388 (1992) (explaining that the PLRA’s limits on prospective relief do not apply where the relief is necessary to correct a constitutional violation).

Moreover, the PLRA’s exhaustion requirement does not apply to this case. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(e) (2006) (requiring exhaustion of administrative remedies before bringing certain prisoner suits). The relief ordered here is not a damages award but an injunction to remedy ongoing constitutional violations. *See* *Porter v. Nussle*, 534 U.S. 516, 524 (2002) (holding that the PLRA’s exhaustion requirement applies to “any” action brought by a prisoner, but not to actions seeking injunctive relief to remedy constitutional violations).

**III. The district court’s order is consistent with this Court’s precedents on equitable remedies.**

This Court has long recognized that federal courts may enter injunctive relief to remedy constitutional violations, even where such relief requires state officials to take actions within their traditional prerogatives. *See* *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 17 (1958) (holding that state officials are bound by federal court orders enforcing constitutional rights). The district court’s order is a permissible exercise of equitable power, narrowly tailored to remedy the constitutional violations found. *See* *Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267, 281 (1977) (explaining that equitable relief must be “necessary to bring about the unitary school system”).

**Conclusion**

The district court’s order is a lawful exercise of its equitable power to remedy constitutional violations. The PLRA does not bar the relief ordered, and the order is consistent with this Court’s precedents on equitable remedies. The judgment below should be affirmed.
