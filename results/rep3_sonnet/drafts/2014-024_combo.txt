# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 875(c)'s Silence on Mental State Does Not Dispense With the Requirement of Subjective Intent to Threaten.

Congress's failure to specify a mental state in § 875(c) does not mean that none is required. This Court has long presumed that criminal statutes incorporate a scienter requirement even when the text is silent. In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), the Court explained that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion" but is instead "as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." *Id.* at 250. Congressional silence on intent is therefore properly read as an adoption of "the mental element" traditionally required for crime, not its elimination. *Id.* at 263.

The narrow exception to this presumption is confined to public-welfare offenses involving regulatory conduct, where the penalty is minor and the defendant is dealing with a device or substance he knows to be dangerous. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-54 (1922). Threatening another person by communication is not such an offense; it is a true crime carrying a felony sentence, targeting the very kind of expressive conduct this Court has long treated with special constitutional solicitude. Where, as here, a broad or objective reading of a criminal statute would render it constitutionally suspect, this Court has construed the statute to require heightened intent in order to avoid that infirmity. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the plurality construed the term "willfully" into a federal civil-rights statute precisely because "the requirement of a specific intent to do a prohibited act may avoid those consequences to the accused which may otherwise render a vague or indefinite statute invalid." *Id.* at 101-02 (plurality opinion). The same logic compels a subjective-intent reading of § 875(c): absent proof that the speaker meant his words as a threat, or knew they would be understood as one, an individual may be convicted for a communication he never intended as menacing—precisely the kind of criminal liability without a blameworthy mental state that *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225, 228 (1957), forbids consistent with due process.

## II. The First Amendment's True-Threat Doctrine Requires Proof That the Speaker Subjectively Intended to Threaten.

True threats are punishable only because they fall within a narrow category of speech "which have never been thought to raise any Constitutional problem," *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571-72 (1942)—a category defined by the speaker's own purpose, not merely the listener's reaction. This Court's decision in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), confirms that the constitutional line separating unprotected threats from protected expression turns on the character of the speaker's statement in context, not simply on how a reasonable listener might construe it. The Court there held that a statement made "in the context of a political debate" amounted to no more than "a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition," *id.* at 708, and could not be punished as a threat consistent with the First Amendment. *Watts* thus rejected the notion that speech loses its constitutional protection merely because some listeners might take it as menacing; the speaker's own intent and the context of the utterance are central to the inquiry.

That approach is consistent with this Court's broader First Amendment jurisprudence, which has repeatedly insisted that restrictions on speech be tethered to the speaker's intent before liability may attach for words that cause fear or provoke reaction in others. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court cautioned that the State's legitimate interest in preventing "coercion, intimidation, [and] harassment" must be reconciled with the constitutional protection afforded to even provocative expression. *Id.* at 310. Similarly, in *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494 (1951), the plurality's approval of criminal liability for advocacy rested expressly on proof that the defendants "intended to overthrow the Government," *id.* at 511, not merely that their words might have been understood by others to counsel force. An objective standard—one asking only what a "reasonable person" would think, without any inquiry into what the defendant meant—collapses this essential distinction and punishes speakers for the misunderstandings, oversensitivities, or misperceptions of their audience rather than for their own culpable purpose.

## III. An Objective Standard Would Chill Protected Speech.

Finally, a negligence-based standard threatens to suppress a swath of protected expression, because speakers of hyperbole, satire, and rap lyrics—forms of expression this Court has recognized are frequently misunderstood—will be forced to self-censor rather than risk prosecution for words a jury might later deem threatening in hindsight. This Court has consistently held that First Amendment freedoms "need breathing space to survive," *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963), and that the risk of erroneous suppression of protected speech requires procedural and substantive safeguards, including a heightened burden tied to the speaker's own state of mind. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525-26 (1958). As this Court recognized in the analogous context of bookseller liability, dispensing with a scienter requirement "tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter," *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959), because speakers will restrict their own expression far beyond what the law actually forbids. The same chilling danger justifies requiring proof of subjective intent under § 875(c).
