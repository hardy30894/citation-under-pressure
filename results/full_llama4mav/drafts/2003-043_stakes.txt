The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years after the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, both a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. United States v. Galletti, 298 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir. 2002).

The IRS's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. The Code's assessment provision, 26 U.S.C. § 6203, requires the IRS to record the liability of the "taxpayer" in its office. The regulation interpreting this provision states that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This language indicates that the assessment must be made against a specific taxpayer, and an assessment against a partnership does not satisfy this requirement with respect to its individual partners.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not support the government's position. While the court held that a partner was liable for taxes assessed against the partnership, it did so in the context of a refund action, not a collection action. The district court's opinion in Young, which this Court affirmed, suggests that the IRS would need to assess the tax against the individual partner or obtain a judgment against them to collect the tax. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,049 (S.D.Cal.1959).

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), supports the respondents' position. In Coson, the court held that an assessment against a partnership was insufficient to create a lien on the property of an individual partner. While the court ultimately affirmed on other grounds, the district court's opinion, which was not disturbed on appeal, held that an assessment against a partnership does not permit the IRS to collect the tax from an individual partner without a separate assessment or judgment against that partner. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Under California law, a creditor may not collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The IRS has not obtained such a judgment against the respondents, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Therefore, the IRS's claim against the respondents is barred, and the bankruptcy court's disallowance of the claim was proper.
