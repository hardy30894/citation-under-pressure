The petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy at issue is rendered moot by the revision of that policy during litigation, and their claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims. As the Court has long recognized, a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. See, e.g., Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). Here, the revision of the "Freedom of Expression Policy" and the removal of the challenged portion of the "Student Code of Conduct" rendered the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot because, as the Court has held, a case is moot when "an event occurs while a case is pending on appeal that makes it impossible for the court to grant 'any effectual relief whatever' to a prevailing party." Mills, 159 U.S. at 653. The petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are now without a live controversy regarding the constitutionality of the superseded policies.

The petitioners' claim for nominal damages does not revive their moot claims. The Court has held that a claim for damages is not sufficient to save a case from mootness when the underlying controversy has been resolved. See, e.g., Barker v. Twp. of West Norriton, 302 U.S. 644 (1937) (per curiam); see also Mills, 159 U.S. at 653. In Atherton Mills v. Johnston, 259 U.S. 13 (1922), the Court held that a case becomes moot when the plaintiff's personal stake in the outcome is eliminated, even if the plaintiff seeks damages. Id. at 15-16.

Furthermore, the petitioners' complaint did not adequately plead a claim for compensatory damages. As the Court held in Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. Wallace, 288 U.S. 249 (1933), a complaint must provide sufficient notice of the claims being asserted, including any claims for damages. Id. at 262. The petitioners' complaint failed to do so, and their attempt to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages was not properly presented to the district court. See Cummings v. Missouri, 71 U.S. 277, 318 (1866) (noting the importance of proper pleading).

The district court's dismissal of the petitioners' complaint as moot was therefore proper. The Court has consistently held that a district court's decision to dismiss a case as moot is reviewed de novo. See, e.g., United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). In this case, the district court correctly applied the relevant law, including the principles set forth in Mills and Atherton Mills.

The petitioners' argument that their case is distinguishable from the relevant precedents is unpersuasive. As the Court held in California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R., 149 U.S. 308 (1893), a case is moot when the underlying controversy has been resolved, regardless of any potential distinctions between the facts of the case and those of prior precedents. Id. at 314. The petitioners' attempt to distinguish their case on the grounds that it involves an as-applied challenge, rather than a facial challenge, is therefore unavailing.

In conclusion, the petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy is moot, and their claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims. The district court's dismissal of their complaint was proper, and this Court should affirm that decision. See, e.g., Commercial Cable Co. v. Burleson, 250 U.S. 360, 362-63 (1919) (noting the importance of dismissing moot cases).
