**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tribal Court Denied Selam His Right to Compulsory Process Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), guarantees that “[n]o Indian tribe in exercising powers of self-government shall . . . deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right . . . to compel the attendance of necessary witnesses.” This right is fundamental to a fair trial. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) (holding that the right to compulsory process is “essential to due process”). Selam provided his lay Tribal Spokesperson with a list of five potential character witnesses. The spokesperson located three, but two said their testimony would be damaging. Selam then failed to assist further, and no subpoenas were issued. The tribal judge and spokesperson advised Selam of his right to compel witnesses, but Selam—a layperson without legal training—did not request subpoenas. This does not constitute a knowing and intelligent waiver. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of constitutional rights must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). Selam’s inaction, coupled with the spokesperson’s failure to secure favorable witnesses, effectively denied him the opportunity to present a defense. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (the right to present witnesses is “a fundamental element of due process”). The tribal court had an affirmative duty to ensure Selam understood and could exercise his compulsory process right, especially given the absence of trained counsel. *Settler v. Lameer*, 507 F.2d 231, 237 (9th Cir. 1974) (tribal courts must provide “fundamental fairness” in criminal proceedings). Because Selam was not afforded meaningful assistance and the court did not intervene, the denial of compulsory process violated ICRA.

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding That Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim by Failing to Exhaust Tribal Remedies.**

Selam raised his Confrontation Clause claim for the first time in his federal habeas petition. The district court held that he waived the claim by not presenting it to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. This ruling was erroneous for two reasons.

First, exhaustion of tribal remedies is not an absolute jurisdictional bar. *National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe*, 471 U.S. 845, 856-57 (1985) (exhaustion is a prudential requirement, not a jurisdictional one). Federal courts may excuse exhaustion where tribal remedies are inadequate or where requiring exhaustion would be futile. *Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 16 n.8 (1987). Here, the tribal court of appeals dismissed Selam’s appeal without a hearing, suggesting that further tribal review would have been futile. Moreover, Selam’s compulsory process and confrontation claims are closely intertwined—both concern the right to present and challenge evidence. The tribal court’s rejection of his compulsory process claim effectively foreclosed any meaningful opportunity to raise the related confrontation issue.

Second, Selam’s failure to raise the confrontation claim was not a knowing waiver. He was represented by a lay Tribal Spokesperson, not a licensed attorney. The spokesperson likely lacked awareness of the distinct Confrontation Clause right under ICRA. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464. The Supreme Court has recognized that ICRA rights are enforceable in federal habeas proceedings, *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 66-67 (1978), and that the Confrontation Clause under ICRA guarantees the right to face accusers, *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1382, 1386 (9th Cir. 1989). Selam’s conviction rested heavily on hearsay testimony from the five-year-old victim, who did not testify. The tribal court admitted statements through four witnesses without any opportunity for cross-examination. This directly implicates the Confrontation Clause. *See Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1386 (ICRA’s confrontation guarantee “affords a criminal defendant the right to be confronted with the witnesses against him”). Selam’s failure to raise this claim earlier should not bar federal review where the tribal proceedings were fundamentally unfair and he lacked effective representation.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of habeas relief and remand for further proceedings.
