**Argument**

Under this Court’s precedents, the answer is no: a “reasonable” fee under a federal fee-shifting statute is ordinarily the lodestar, and it may not be enhanced solely because counsel did excellent work or obtained excellent results. The Supreme Court has repeatedly said that the lodestar “is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 564 (1986), and that “the quality of representation” and “results obtained” are generally already reflected in the reasonable hourly rate and reasonable hours component of the calculation. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 898-900 (1984). An enhancement based only on those factors would therefore duplicate what the lodestar already captures. Id. at 898-900.

Blum squarely rejected the notion that superior quality or results may justify a fee increase in the ordinary case. The Court explained that “the ‘quality of representation’ generally is reflected in the reasonable hourly rate,” and that “the ‘results obtained’ generally will be subsumed within other factors used to calculate a reasonable fee.” Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900. For that reason, the Court held that the district court erred in increasing the fee based on those considerations absent “specific evidence” that the lodestar would otherwise be inadequate. Id. at 899. The Court did not authorize a free-standing enhancement for excellent lawyering; it required evidence that the lodestar itself failed to compensate for the services rendered. Id. at 898-901.

Delaware Valley confirmed that point. There, the Court reiterated that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance in order to serve the statutory purpose of enabling plaintiffs to secure legal assistance.” Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-66. The plurality further stated that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar.” Id. at 566. That is the controlling rule here: quality and results are ordinarily reflected in the lodestar itself, and they do not support an additional multiplier merely because the litigation was successfully prosecuted. Id. at 565-68.

Nor does Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), help respondents. Hensley held that where a plaintiff achieves “excellent results,” counsel should recover a “fully compensatory fee,” ordinarily measured by reasonable hours and rates. Id. at 435-36. But Hensley did not hold that the fee may then be enhanced again because the result was excellent. To the contrary, the Court directed courts to adjust the lodestar downward where success is limited and to use the lodestar method to arrive at a fee that is “reasonable in relation to the results obtained.” Id. at 434-37. Hensley thus reinforces, rather than undermines, the proposition that results are to be accounted for in the lodestar analysis itself. Id.

Respondents may rely on language in Blum and Delaware Valley mentioning “rare” or “exceptional” cases. See Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-901; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565. But those passages do not establish a rule authorizing enhancement solely for quality and results. Rather, they emphasize that any departure from the lodestar requires “specific evidence” that the lodestar does not already compensate counsel adequately. Blum, 465 U.S. at 899; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565. In other words, rarity is not a license to double count; it is a demand for proof. Id. Respondents offered none beyond the asserted excellence of counsel’s performance and the favorable outcome. That is not enough under Blum. 465 U.S. at 899-900.

This Court’s later decision in City of Burlington v. Dague underscores the same principle. There, the Court rejected contingency enhancements because they would duplicate factors already reflected in the lodestar and would produce unpredictability incompatible with fee-shifting statutes. City of Burlington v. Dague, 505 U.S. 557, 562-67 (1992). The same reasoning applies here. If the lodestar already reflects counsel’s skill, prevailing market rates, and the success achieved, then an additional enhancement for those same reasons would be impermissible double counting. Id.; Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be reconciled with these cases. To say that a district court may enhance a lodestar merely because counsel’s “skill, commitment, dedication, and professionalism” produced “exceptional” results is to make enhancement turn on the very factors Blum said are ordinarily subsumed in the lodestar. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-900. The statute requires a “reasonable” fee, not a premium for excellence already priced into the market rate. Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-66.

Because respondents seek an enhancement based solely on quality of performance and results obtained, the award cannot stand. The proper fee is the lodestar, no more and no less. Blum, 465 U.S. at 897-900; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 564-68; Dague, 505 U.S. at 562-67.
