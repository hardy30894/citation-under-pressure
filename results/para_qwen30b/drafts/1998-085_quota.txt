**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” *independent of its state of mind* to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language and legislative intent of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, particularly 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), which authorizes punitive damages when a defendant acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” The Court should hold that *no* independent showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct is required beyond a showing of *malice* or *reckless indifference*. The lower courts erred in imposing such a heightened standard, and the respondent, Carole Kolstad, is entitled to a jury determination on punitive damages under the statutory framework Congress enacted.

### I. THE STATUTORY TEXT REQUIRES NO “EGREGIOUSNESS” STANDARD BEYOND MALICE OR RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE

The text of § 1981a(b)(1) is unambiguous: punitive damages are available “if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language does not condition punitive damages on conduct that is “egregious,” “outrageous,” or “extraordinarily egregious.” Rather, it conditions them on the defendant’s *mental state*—specifically, malice or reckless indifference. The absence of any independent objective standard—such as “egregiousness”—in the statutory text forecloses the creation of one by judicial fiat.

This Court has long held that when a statute’s language is clear, courts must give effect to that language without adding qualifications not found in the text. *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989) (“When the statutory language is unambiguous, our inquiry ends.”). The Court has also emphasized that “the presence of a particular word in a statute does not necessarily mean that the word is intended to have a special or technical meaning.” *Barnhart v. Peabody Coal Co.*, 537 U.S. 149, 160 (2003). Here, Congress chose the terms “malice” and “reckless indifference”—terms with well-established meanings in tort law—and did not include any additional threshold of “egregiousness.” To impose such a requirement would be to rewrite the statute.

### II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY CONFIRMS THAT CONGRESS INTENDED A LOWER STANDARD THAN “EGREGIOUSNESS”

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 confirms that Congress intended to make punitive damages available in a broad range of intentional discrimination cases, not only in “extraordinarily egregious” ones. The House Report explicitly stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “are available to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991). This statement is dispositive: § 1981 has long permitted punitive damages upon a showing of malice or reckless indifference, *without* requiring “egregious” conduct. *See, e.g.,* *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677, 694 (1979) (recognizing punitive damages under § 1981 for intentional discrimination); *Browder v. City of Chicago*, 817 F.2d 437, 442 (7th Cir. 1987) (affirming punitive damages under § 1981 where defendant acted with “malice”).

Moreover, the Senate Report, while noting that Congress intended to “deter intentional discrimination,” did not suggest that punitive damages should be limited to “extraordinarily egregious” cases. S. Rep. No. 102-166, at 10 (1991). Instead, it emphasized that the purpose of punitive damages was to “deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace,” id., which would be undermined by a narrow “egregiousness” standard.

### III. THE LOWER COURTS’ “EGREGIOUSNESS” STANDARD CONTRADICTS PRECEDENT ON PUNITIVE DAMAGES IN CIVIL RIGHTS CASES

The lower courts’ imposition of an “egregiousness” standard is inconsistent with this Court’s precedent on punitive damages in civil rights contexts. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 50 (1983), the Court held that punitive damages under § 1983 are available when the defendant’s conduct is “malicious or reckless,” not merely negligent. The Court rejected the idea that “a showing of mere negligence” suffices, but also rejected the notion that “egregiousness” must be proven independently of the defendant’s state of mind. *Id.* at 50–51. The Court emphasized that “the availability of punitive damages under § 1983 is not limited to cases in which the defendant’s conduct is ‘outrageous’ or ‘egregious’ in the sense of being beyond the pale of acceptable conduct.” *Id.* at 51.

Similarly, in *City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.*, 453 U.S. 247, 271 (1981), the Court held that punitive damages under § 1983 are not limited to cases involving “outrageous” conduct, but may be awarded when the defendant’s conduct reflects “malice or reckless indifference.” The Court rejected the idea that “the mere fact that a defendant’s conduct was intentional or reckless” is insufficient—precisely the standard the lower courts applied here. *Id.* at 271.
