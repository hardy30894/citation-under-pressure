# ARGUMENT

## I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE CONSTITUTION ESTABLISH FIXED AND EXCLUSIVE CRITERIA FOR CONGRESSIONAL SERVICE THAT STATES MAY NOT SUPPLEMENT

The Constitution specifies that a Representative must be twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state from which elected. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. Senators must be thirty years old, citizens for nine years, and inhabitants of their state. *Id.* § 3, cl. 3. These are not illustrative minima but an exhaustive enumeration. As this Court recognized in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Framers intended the qualifications for Congress to be fixed, uniform standards, and Congress itself lacks power to add to them. The Court there held that "the Convention intended that the qualifications for members of Congress be fixed in the Constitution and that the exclusion power granted to the House was not a general power to exclude for any reason." *Id.* at 540. If Congress—the body most directly interested in its own membership—cannot alter these qualifications, individual states possess no greater authority to do so. To hold otherwise would permit fifty states to create fifty different sets of eligibility standards for national office, destroying the very uniformity the Framers sought.

This structural logic finds firm support in *Powell*'s recognition that the qualifications clauses reflect a deliberate constitutional choice. The Framers debated and rejected proposals that would have permitted additional qualifications, including property requirements and other restrictions. Having settled on a fixed list, the Framers did not leave the door open for states to reintroduce, through the backdoor of ballot access, the very restrictions the Convention rejected. As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly observed below, "the action finally taken by the framers of the constitution, following exhaustive debates, is strong evidence that term limits for senators and representatives was rejected." That determination reflects the constitutional text's design: Congress is a national institution, and its members "partake of the same national character as the President of the United States."

The Tenth Amendment does not rescue Amendment 73. States retain only those powers not surrendered to the national government or reserved by the Constitution's structure. But the power to regulate the qualifications of federal officeholders was never a power the states possessed to reserve. Representatives and Senators are officers of the national government, not the state government; their existence and qualifications spring from the Federal Constitution itself, not from state law. As this Court explained in analogous contexts of federal supremacy, when the Constitution speaks with specificity on a subject central to the structure of federal governance, the states cannot use reserved powers to circumvent that specificity. Amendment 73 does exactly that: it attempts to add a de facto fourth qualification—not having served three House terms or two Senate terms—to the three qualifications the Constitution establishes.

## II. ARKANSAS'S "BALLOT ACCESS" MECHANISM IS A TRANSPARENT ATTEMPT TO EVADE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES AND CANNOT BE SUSTAINED ON THAT BASIS

Petitioners contend that because Amendment 73 merely restricts *ballot access* rather than directly barring service, it escapes the Qualifications Clauses' prohibition. This is a distinction without constitutional substance. In *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780 (1983), this Court recognized that restrictions on ballot access implicate fundamental associational and voting rights precisely because ballot access is the practical mechanism through which citizens exercise their right to choose their representatives. A state cannot achieve through ballot exclusion what it is forbidden from achieving through direct disqualification. The Arkansas Supreme Court properly rejected this formalism, concluding that "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp"—and, more importantly, too fine a point to satisfy constitutional scrutiny. A term-limited candidate barred from the ballot is as surely excluded from congressional service as one formally declared ineligible; the practical effect on the citizens' choice and the candidate's rights is identical.

## III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT SECTION 3'S INFIRMITY VOIDS THE ENTIRE AMENDMENT

Even if this Court were to entertain doubts about the qualifications analysis, the severability question independently supports affirmance. Arkansas law requires courts to ask "whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion." *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly found that sections 1, 2, and 3 were "not only inextricably linked" but "systemically fused in such a manner that each ceased to have a separate existence for voting purposes." Voters were presented a single, undifferentiated choice—term limits for state and federal officials bundled together under a unified preamble describing all affected officeholders collectively as "elected officials." There is no evidentiary basis in this record for concluding that Arkansas voters would have approved state-only term limits had section 3 been excised. The proponents of the amendment, who "obviously knew that section 3 was of questionable constitutionality," bore the burden of establishing severability and failed to do so.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
