ARGUMENT

I. The Equal Protection Clause Redresses Discrimination Against Persons as Members of a Class, Not the Idiosyncratic Treatment of a Single Property Owner.

The Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection guarantee has always been understood as a shield against government action that treats "persons in similar circumstances" unequally because of their membership in a disfavored group. In Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), this Court explained that "class legislation, discriminating against some and favoring others, is prohibited," but that regulation limited in its application does not offend the Amendment so long as, "within the sphere of its operation," it "affects alike all persons similarly situated." Id. at 32. The wrong the Clause addresses is discrimination against a class of persons, not a single citizen's disagreement with a discretionary municipal decision.

Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), confirms the point. There this Court condemned a licensing scheme "applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances." Id. at 373-74. The discrimination condemned in Yick Wo was directed at Chinese laundry operators as a group; the "unequal hand" was unequal precisely because it singled out an identifiable class for adverse treatment while sparing others outside that class. Nothing in Yick Wo suggests that a single, isolated instance of asserted favoritism toward one neighbor and not another states a constitutional claim.

This Court's later decisions reinforce that class-based discrimination is the touchstone. In Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court held that "[t]he unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." Id. at 8. Snowden further cautioned that the Fourteenth Amendment does not convert every "erroneous or discriminatory" act of a state official into a constitutional wrong absent proof that the discrimination was directed at the plaintiff because of his membership in some class entitled to protection. Id. at 8-9.

Equal protection review of economic and regulatory classifications has likewise proceeded on the premise that a class, not an individual grievance, is the unit of analysis. In F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia, 253 U.S. 412 (1920), the Court stated that a classification "must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike." Id. at 415 (emphasis added). And in Tigner v. Texas, 310 U.S. 141 (1940), the Court recognized that "[c]lassification is the essence" of regulation and that some line-drawing is inevitable and constitutionally tolerable. Id. at 147. Railway Express Agency v. New York, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), likewise upheld a traffic regulation that burdened one kind of advertising but not another, explaining that equal protection does not demand that "all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." Id. at 110. These cases confirm that municipal line-drawing—inevitably producing differences between one property owner and the next—is not itself an equal protection violation; something more, a showing of class-based or invidious motivation, has always been required.

Respondent alleged none of this. She did not claim that the Village singled her out because of race, because she belonged to any disfavored group, or because of any policy applicable to a defined class of similarly situated owners. She claimed only that she, individually, received different treatment than certain unidentified neighbors. Under Barbier, Yick Wo, Snowden, Royster Guano, and Tigner, that bare assertion of differential treatment—wholly untethered to class membership—does not state a claim upon which the Equal Protection Clause affords relief.

II. Municipalities Possess Broad Discretion to Impose Individualized Conditions on Connections to Municipal Water Systems, and Variation in Those Conditions Does Not Offend the Constitution.

This Court has long recognized that municipalities exercise substantial discretion in regulating access to and use of municipal water systems, including the terms on which individual property owners may connect. In Consumers' Co. v. Hatch, 224 U.S. 148 (1912), the Court considered a dispute over the conditions a water company could impose on a connection to municipal service and recognized that regulatory bodies may set terms for service connections according to the circumstances presented by particular applicants. Similarly, in Boise Artesian Hot & Cold Water Co. v. Boise City, 230 U.S. 84 (1913), the Court acknowledged the authority of municipal or quasi-municipal bodies to fix the terms of water service and to adjust those terms to the practical necessities of the system. And in Skaneateles Water Works Co. v. Skaneateles, 184 U.S. 354 (1902), this Court underscored the broad latitude municipalities retain in constructing, operating, and expanding their own water systems, holding that a village's exercise of its own governmental discretion over its water supply worked no constitutional violation even though it diminished the value of a private company's competing works. Id. at 358.

These precedents establish that the terms on which a municipality extends water service—including the width of an easement required for a particular connection—are matters of engineering and administrative judgment committed to the municipality's discretion, not matters converted into constitutional claims merely because two parcels received different easement conditions. Absent an allegation that the Village's demand rested on Olech's membership in some disfavored class, the Equal Protection Clause supplies no cause of action.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
