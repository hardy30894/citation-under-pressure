**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard for “true threats” violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech and contravenes settled principles of criminal liability that demand proof of mens rea. The Court has long held that a conviction for a crime must rest on proof that the defendant possessed a culpable mental state, and that the First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a narrow category of unprotected expression. The “true threat” exception, while recognized, is not a license to convict based on the speaker’s conduct alone, but only when the speaker *intends* to threaten. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—permitting conviction based solely on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening—transforms the First Amendment into a license for punishment of speech that is merely offensive, alarming, or even bizarre, without regard to the speaker’s actual intent. This is impermissible under the Constitution and the precedents of this Court.

The foundational principle that criminal liability requires proof of a culpable mental state is deeply rooted in American jurisprudence. In *Lee v. Mississippi*, 332 U.S. 742 (1948), the Court held that a confession obtained through duress is inadmissible because it is not “voluntary” under the Due Process Clause. The Court emphasized that “the confessions were the result of pressure overcoming Rogers’ powers of resistance and were not voluntary on his part.” Id. at 745. This principle—that a conviction cannot stand unless the defendant’s conduct reflects a culpable state of mind—applies with equal force to the crime of threatening. If a defendant is to be punished for threatening another, the government must prove not only that the statement was threatening in form, but that the defendant *intended* it to be so. To hold otherwise would be to punish speech based on its effect on the listener, not on the speaker’s intent—a doctrine that would render the First Amendment meaningless.

The Court has consistently required that the government prove a defendant’s intent in cases involving speech that may be punished. In *Sexton v. California*, 189 U.S. 319 (1903), the Court considered whether a threat to accuse another of a crime could constitute extortion. The Court held that the crime required proof that the threat was made “with the intent to extort money,” and that the mere existence of a threat, without such intent, did not constitute the offense. Id. at 324. The Court reasoned that “the threat to accuse another of crime is the material matter in the state statute,” but that “it is not material that the threat was pointed at a crime made such only by the Federal statute.” Id. The Court’s focus was on the *intent* behind the threat, not its objective effect. This principle is directly applicable here: a threat under § 875(c) must be one made with the intent to injure another, not one that merely happens to be perceived as threatening by a reasonable person.

The Court has also long recognized that threats are not punishable unless they are made with a purpose to intimidate or harm. In *Brown v. Pierce*, 74 U.S. 205 (1869), the Court held that a contract procured by “fear of loss of life; (2) fear of loss of limb; (3) for fear of mayhem; (4) for fear of imprisonment” is voidable. Id. at 212. The Court emphasized that only threats of “death, or of great bodily harm, or unlawful imprisonment” could overcome a person’s will and render a contract voidable. Id. The Court’s reasoning was that “such threats are held not to be of a nature to overcome the mind and will of a firm and prudent man.” Id. This principle underscores the necessity of a subjective element: a threat must be one that is *intended* to cause fear of serious harm, not one that merely *causes* fear in a sensitive listener. The same logic applies to criminal threats: the law does not punish speech that is merely alarming, but only that which is intended to threaten.

The Court has further held that the government must prove intent in cases involving threats to public officials. In *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101 (1942), the Court considered a habeas petition in which the petitioner claimed he was coerced into pleading guilty by threats from an FBI agent. The Court noted that the petitioner alleged that the agent threatened to “throw petitioner out of a window and ‘beat me up’.” Id. at 102. The Court did not inquire whether a reasonable person would have feared for his safety, but rather whether the threat was *made with the intent to coerce*. The Court’s focus was on the agent’s intent, not the petitioner’s reaction. This demonstrates that the law treats threats as crimes only when the speaker intends to harm or intimidate.

The Court has also recognized that the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or disturbing. In *Iron Mountain & Helena Railroad v. Johnson*, 119 U.S. 608 (1887), the Court held that a party cannot be forced to surrender possession of property by “force, by violence, or by terror.” Id. at 615. The Court emphasized that the law’s purpose was “to prevent disturbances of the public peace, to forbid any person righting himself in a case of that kind by his own hand and by violence.” Id. The Court did not punish speech that caused fear, but only conduct that involved actual violence or the threat of it. This distinction is critical: the law protects the right to speak, even if the speech is threatening in tone, unless the speaker *intends* to threaten.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard for “true threats” is inconsistent with these principles. It permits conviction based on the listener’s perception, not the speaker’s intent. This is precisely the kind of speech regulation the Court has rejected. In *Cole v.
