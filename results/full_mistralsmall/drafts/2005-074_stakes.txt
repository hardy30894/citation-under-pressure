**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-Entered Before Its Effective Date**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (the “reinstatement statute”) provides that it applies only to aliens who “enter[ed] the United States without inspection after having been ordered removed.” The statute does not specify whether it applies to re-entries occurring before its 1997 effective date. However, this Court has consistently held that statutes affecting substantive rights should not be applied retroactively unless Congress has clearly indicated such an intent. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994)*. Here, Congress did not expressly state that § 1231(a)(5) applies to pre-enactment re-entries. Thus, the statute must be construed to apply only prospectively.

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Congress intended § 1231(a)(5) to apply to all illegal re-entries, regardless of when they occurred—is unsupported by the statutory text or legislative history. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 n.12 (1987)* (courts must not assume Congress intended retroactive application absent clear evidence). The reinstatement statute’s structure further supports this interpretation: it authorizes the Attorney General to “reinstate” a prior removal order, which presupposes that the prior order was valid at the time of re-entry. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5)*. If the statute applied to pre-1997 re-entries, it would effectively rewrite the legal consequences of past conduct, a quintessential retroactive application. *See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70*.

**II. Applying § 1231(a)(5) to Pre-Enactment Re-Entries Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if § 1231(a)(5) could be read to apply to pre-1997 re-entries, doing so would violate the Constitution’s prohibition on retroactive legislation. A law is retroactive if it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270*. Here, Petitioner’s 1982 re-entry was a completed act long before § 1231(a)(5) was enacted. Applying the statute to him would impose new legal consequences—denial of all forms of relief and automatic removal—based on that past conduct.

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that Petitioner lacked a “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status. *See Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales, 438 F.3d 1196, 1202 (10th Cir. 2006)*. This Court has recognized that reliance interests may arise from settled expectations, including those created by long-standing administrative practices. *See INS v. Yang, 519 U.S. 26, 32 (1996)* (noting that aliens who entered before 1990 relied on pre-IIRIRA rules). Petitioner’s 19-year residence in the U.S., his marriage to a citizen, and his bona fide application for adjustment of status created a legitimate expectation that he would have an opportunity to seek relief. *See INS v. Phinpathya, 464 U.S. 183, 189 (1984)* (recognizing that aliens may develop equities during prolonged residence).

The government’s reliance on *Harisiades v. Shaughnessy, 342 U.S. 580 (1952)*, is misplaced. In *Harisiades*, the Court upheld deportation of aliens who had re-entered after prior deportations, but the statute at issue there applied to all re-entries, regardless of when they occurred. *Id. at 584*. By contrast, § 1231(a)(5) was enacted decades later and targets a specific class of conduct—post-1997 re-entries. Its retroactive application to pre-enactment re-entries is unconstitutional.

**III. The BIA’s Construction of § 1231(a)(5) Is Entitled to No Deference**

The Tenth Circuit accorded Chevron deference to the BIA’s interpretation of § 1231(a)(5). *See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. NRDC, 467 U.S. 837 (1984)*. However, where an agency’s interpretation raises serious constitutional questions, courts must reject it. *See Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Fla. Gulf Coast Bldg. & Constr. Trades Council, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988)*. The BIA’s construction of § 1231(a)(5) to apply retroactively implicates constitutional concerns under the Due Process Clause. Thus, no deference is owed, and the statute must be construed in favor of Petitioner. *See INS v. Aguirre-Aguirre, 526 U.S. 415, 424-25 (1999)*.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the decision below and hold that § 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who re-entered the U.S. before its 1997 effective date.
