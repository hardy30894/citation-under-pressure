The Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings that complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, and therefore “do not violate the Sixth Amendment.” Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746–47 (1994) (citing Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). Because the Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes,” Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008), the absence of appointed counsel created no constitutional infirmity that could be imported into a subsequent federal prosecution.

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penalize[s] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; see also United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). The government must prove the existence of the prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt, In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), but it need not relitigate their validity. A conviction obtained without appointed counsel remains valid when no sentence of imprisonment was imposed, Scott, 440 U.S. at 373–74, and even when imprisonment was imposed the underlying conviction itself is not rendered void, Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654, 661–62 (2002). Tribal-court judgments are therefore entitled to the same recognition as other judgments that satisfy the governing law at the time they were rendered.

Tribal sovereignty supplies an independent reason for this result. “Indian tribes are ‘distinct, independent political communities’” that retain “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders.” United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 322, 323 (1978); see also United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004). Federal courts accordingly extend comity to tribal judgments and “respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems.” Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987); see also Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (warning against “unnecessary judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance”). Conditioning the use of tribal convictions on the provision of counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment would impose precisely the procedural uniformity that these precedents forbid.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule rests on an unwarranted extension of United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), and a misreading of Nichols that treats an element of a recidivist offense as though it were a sentencing factor subject to heightened reliability scrutiny. Nichols itself rejected that distinction for purposes of the Sixth Amendment. 511 U.S. at 748. Because Bryant’s tribal convictions were valid when obtained and remain valid today, their use as predicates under § 117(a) does not violate the Constitution.
