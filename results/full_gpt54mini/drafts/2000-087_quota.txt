The Court should hold that 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued custody is not authorized. Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 689, 699, 701 (2001).

That construction follows the Court’s settled obligation to avoid grave constitutional doubts. In United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957), the Court construed an immigration statute narrowly to avoid “serious constitutional questions.” The same principle governed in Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932), and is especially apt where prolonged civil detention is at stake. See also Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988). As the Court explained in Zadvydas itself, reading § 1231(a)(6) to permit detention without foreseeable removal would raise “serious constitutional concerns.” 533 U.S. at 682.

The constitutional problem is fundamental. Freedom from bodily restraint is “at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause.” Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992); see also Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997). Civil confinement is permissible only when it is linked to a valid regulatory purpose and accompanied by adequate limiting standards. Addington v. Texas, 441 U.S. 418, 425-27 (1979). Here, once the government cannot effect removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, detention ceases to serve the statute’s basic purpose of ensuring the alien’s presence at removal and becomes, in practical effect, imprisonment without end. That is precisely the type of confinement the Due Process Clause disfavors. See Foucha, 504 U.S. at 82-83; Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972).

The government’s contrary reading cannot be squared with precedent on immigration detention. In Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 534-36 (1952), the Court upheld detention of deportable aliens because Congress had authorized confinement to prevent flight and protect the public in aid of ongoing proceedings. But Carlson did not approve detention after removal becomes unrealistic and the custody no longer bears a reasonable relation to the anticipated deportation. Nor does Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 212-16 (1953), help the government; Zadvydas distinguished Mezei because it involved an alien stopped at the border, not a lawful permanent resident who had effected entry. 533 U.S. at 693-94. The Court likewise recognized in Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896), that immigration-related detention remains subject to constitutional limits.

The government’s position also conflicts with the Court’s general due process cases limiting executive detention. In Zadvydas, the Court relied on Foucha and Jackson to confirm that civil detention cannot continue when the asserted justification has evaporated. 533 U.S. at 690-91. The Court has repeatedly required a strong justification for restraints on liberty even in sensitive regulatory settings. See Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. at 363; Addington, 441 U.S. at 425. A statute that allowed potentially permanent detention of removable aliens whose countries will not accept them would exceed those bounds.

Nor can the government convert a reasonable-removal statute into a blank check by invoking Congress’s plenary immigration power. While Congress has broad authority over admission and removal, that power does not permit indefinite civil confinement absent clear authorization. See Fiallo v. Bell, 430 U.S. 787, 792 (1977); INS v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919, 940-41 (1983). Zadvydas held that Congress did not speak clearly enough to authorize indefinite detention, and the Court properly construed the statute to avoid the constitutional issue. 533 U.S. at 689-90, 696-97.

The Court should therefore reaffirm the rule it announced: after six months, if the alien shows good reason to believe there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the government must rebut that showing. Id. at 701. That rule preserves the statute’s purpose, respects due process, and avoids the “serious constitutional threat” posed by indefinite detention. Id. at 699-700.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
