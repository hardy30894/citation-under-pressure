**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

The court of appeals correctly held that Woodard has no constitutionally protected interest in the clemency process itself. *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) (“[P]ardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.”). Clemency is an act of grace, not a right. *Id.* at 465. Ohio law vests the Governor with “almost unfettered discretion” to grant or deny clemency. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994). No state statute or regulation creates a mandatory entitlement to clemency. *See Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979) (liberty interest arises only from “language of an unmistakably mandatory character”). Because Woodard lacks any state-created liberty interest, the Due Process Clause does not attach. *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 224 (1976) (“[T]he Constitution does not guarantee that a prisoner will be placed in any particular prison.”); *Olim v. Wakinekona*, 461 U.S. 238, 249 (1983) (same).

The court of appeals erred by importing due process protections from *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), which addressed a first appeal as of right—a stage the State made “an integral part of the … system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.” *Id.* at 393. Clemency is not part of the adjudicatory process. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. Unlike appeals, clemency is “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* The “great distance” between trial and clemency, as the court of appeals acknowledged, confirms that no due process scrutiny is warranted. *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600, 610 (1974) (no right to counsel for discretionary appeals because they are not part of the adjudicatory process). Even if some minimal process were conceivable, *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995), requires an “atypical and significant hardship” to create a liberty interest; clemency proceedings impose no such hardship.

**II. The Voluntary Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

Woodard’s Fifth Amendment claim is both meritless and premature. The challenged regulation gives the inmate the *option* to request a personal interview. Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07; Death Penalty Clemency Procedure, Part IV C.2. No inmate is compelled to participate. The Fifth Amendment prohibits only *compelled* self-incrimination. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 429 (1984) (“[A] witness’s ‘choice’ to speak … is not compelled unless the government threatens to impose a penalty for silence.”). Woodard faces no penalty for declining the interview. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 317-18 (1976) (no compulsion where inmate could remain silent in prison disciplinary hearing). The regulation does not require a waiver of Fifth Amendment rights; an inmate may say as much or as little as he wishes. *See United States v. Grayson*, 438 U.S. 41, 54 (1978) (voluntary statements are not compelled).

The court of appeals remanded for speculation about hypothetical future proceedings. But the claim is “premature.” *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 324. No interview has occurred, and no statement has been used. *Murphy*, 465 U.S. at 429 (no actual compulsion where “the threat of revocation of probation” was not present). Woodard’s complaint merely pleads a legal conclusion that he “will be irreparably harmed.” That is insufficient. *See Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (conclusory allegations need not be accepted). The district court properly dismissed the Fifth Amendment claim as speculative.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Woodard has no protected life or liberty interest in clemency, and the voluntary interview option does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
