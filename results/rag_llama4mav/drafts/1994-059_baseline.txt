The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, found in Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements that candidates must meet to be eligible for congressional office. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, these provisions are not merely suggestive but are definitive and exhaustive. See Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969).

In Powell, the Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member based on qualifications other than those specified in the Constitution. The Court reasoned that the Framers' decision to enumerate specific qualifications in the Constitution was a deliberate choice to preclude additional requirements. The Court's analysis underscores that the Qualifications Clauses are not merely minimum requirements but rather the sole criteria for determining eligibility for congressional office.

The attempt by Arkansas to impose term limits on its congressional delegation through Amendment 73 contravenes this constitutional framework. By preventing individuals who have served a certain number of terms from appearing on the ballot, Amendment 73 effectively adds a qualification for congressional office not enumerated in the Constitution. This action is beyond the authority of the states, as the Constitution vests Congress with the power to regulate the "Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives," but does not permit states to alter the qualifications for office. U.S. Const., Art. I, § 4, cl. 1; see also Oregon v. Mitchell, 400 U.S. 112 (1970).

The historical context and the constitutional text make clear that the Framers intended to establish a uniform set of qualifications for congressional office, applicable across all states. Permitting states to impose additional qualifications would undermine this uniformity and potentially lead to a patchwork of different requirements across the country. The Constitution's silence on state-imposed term limits, combined with its explicit enumeration of qualifications, indicates that such limits are not permissible.

The Supreme Court has consistently recognized the importance of maintaining the constitutional structure and the division of powers between the federal government and the states. In this context, allowing states to impose term limits on congressional candidates would disrupt the balance established by the Constitution. See Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. 232. The Court has emphasized that the Constitution's provisions regarding congressional elections are designed to ensure a certain level of uniformity and federal oversight. See Barry v United States Ex Rel. Cunningham, 279 U.S. 597 (1929).

In conclusion, Amendment 73's imposition of term limits on Arkansas's congressional delegation is unconstitutional because it adds a qualification for office not specified in the Constitution. The Qualifications Clauses are exclusive, and states lack the authority to alter them. The circuit court's determination that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the U.S. Constitution should be affirmed.
