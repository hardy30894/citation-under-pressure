# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court's Factual Findings Regarding Common Proof of Damages Are Entitled to Substantial Deference and Were Not Clearly Erroneous

A district court's findings underlying a class certification order, like any other factual findings, may be disturbed only if "clearly erroneous." *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). This Court has repeatedly cautioned that a reviewing tribunal may not substitute its own judgment for that of the fact-finder simply because it might have weighed conflicting evidence differently. *See Commissioner v. Duberstein*, 363 U.S. 278, 291 (1960) (the "primary weight" in such determinations "must be given to the conclusions of the trier of fact"). Where, as here, the trial court has heard live testimony, examined dozens of expert reports, and conducted a four-day evidentiary hearing before reaching its conclusion, its findings deserve the same deference this Court has long accorded factfinders who "hear the evidence" and evaluate the credibility of competing experts. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (a reviewing court must respect the factfinder's "informed judgment" even where reasonable minds might differ). Comcast asks this Court to do precisely what *Universal Camera* forbids—reweigh competing expert testimony on damages methodology because it prefers Comcast's expert to Dr. McClave. That is not the role of an appellate court.

## II. Antitrust Law Has Never Required Certainty or Precision in Proving the Amount of Damages, Only a Just and Reasonable Basis for Estimation

Comcast's central premise—that class certification is improper unless plaintiffs first prove damages can be calculated with exactitude—inverts nearly a century of antitrust remedial doctrine. This Court held in *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931), that once the fact of damage from an antitrust violation is established, "the jury may make a just and reasonable estimate of the damage based on relevant data, and render its verdict accordingly." Precision has never been the antitrust plaintiff's burden. *See Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (damages may be inferred "from the relevant facts and circumstances of the case" even without exact proof).

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251 (1946), explaining that although "damages may not be determined by mere speculation or guess," it is "enough that the evidence show the extent of the damages as a matter of just and reasonable inference, although the result be only approximate." *Id.* at 264–65. The wrongdoer, this Court held, "is not entitled to complain that they cannot be measured with the exactness and precision that would be possible if the case, which he alone is responsible for making, were otherwise." *Id.* at 265. Comcast, having engaged in the very "clustering" conduct the District Court found deterred overbuilder competition, cannot now demand of Dr. McClave's regression methodology a certainty this Court has never required even at trial, let alone at the class-certification stage. *See also Hartford-Empire Co. v. United States*, 323 U.S. 386, 409 (1945) (courts possess broad remedial flexibility in fashioning relief for antitrust injury).

If a jury may return a verdict on damages evidence that is merely a "just and reasonable inference," then a district court, applying a preponderance standard at certification, plainly may find that a common regression methodology—subjected to four days of adversarial testing—suffices to show damages are capable of class-wide measurement. The District Court did exactly what *Bigelow* and *Story Parchment* permit: it examined whether Plaintiffs' methodology provided a reasonable, non-speculative means of quantifying the overcharge attributable to Comcast's clustering scheme, and it found that it did. That finding, resting on record evidence rather than guesswork, satisfies every standard this Court has ever articulated for proof of antitrust damages.

## III. The Presence of Common Antitrust Injury Justifies Class Treatment Even Though Individualized Damages Calculations May Ultimately Be Required

Class treatment of antitrust claims has long been recognized as appropriate where common questions of liability and impact predominate, notwithstanding that damages may vary among class members. *See Weeks v. Bareco Oil Co.*, 125 F.2d 84, 88 (7th Cir. 1941) (permitting antitrust class litigation despite differences in individual damages because the "common question" of illegal conspiracy and impact predominated). The concern that damages calculations might later require some individualized attention is not a bar to certification; it is a matter that may be managed by the district court through subsequent proceedings. *See Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555, 566 (2d Cir. 1968) (recognizing the district court's broad authority to manage class proceedings, including bifurcation of liability and damages, to accommodate differences among class members).

Nor does the adequacy of Plaintiffs' common proof implicate the due-process concerns this Court identified in *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32 (1940). There, this Court held that class treatment offends due process only where the interests of class members are not adequately aligned or represented. *Id.* at 42–43. Here, every class member suffered the same alleged injury—supracompetitive prices flowing from Comcast's clustering scheme—and Dr. McClave's model calculates that common overcharge using data and methodology equally applicable to each member. Nothing in *Hansberry* or its antitrust progeny required more.

## CONCLUSION

The District Court, after rigorous evidentiary review, reasonably found that Plaintiffs demonstrated a common methodology capable of measuring class-wide damages consistent with this Court's antitrust damages jurisprudence. That finding was not clearly erroneous and should be affirmed.
