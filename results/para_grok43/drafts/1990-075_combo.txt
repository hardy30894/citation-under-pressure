ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General satisfies the intelligible-principle requirement. In J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), the Court held that Congress may confer authority when it lays down “an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized * * * is directed to conform.” The Act supplies such a principle by enumerating eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, 21 U.S.C. § 811(c), requiring a scientific evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, id. § 811(b), and limiting temporary scheduling to substances posing “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” id. § 811(h)(1). These constraints mirror those upheld in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420, 426 (1944), where Congress directed the Price Administrator to fix prices that “effectuate the purposes” of the statute while considering enumerated economic factors.

The same principle governs the temporary-scheduling provision. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), confirmed that Congress may authorize the Executive to ascertain facts that trigger statutory consequences. Here the Attorney General merely determines whether a substance meets congressionally prescribed criteria; the criminal prohibition itself remains fixed by statute.

Subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is likewise valid. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court sustained the transfer of functions within the Executive Branch when the statute permitted the officer to whom authority was originally delegated to act “through such persons as he shall designate.” The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), rests on identical statutory language, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Wayman v. Southard, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825), recognized that Congress may leave “to those who are to act under such general provisions to fill up the details.” The DEA’s exercise of temporary-scheduling power is precisely such detail-filling.

Earlier decisions confirm that the complexity and urgency of the subject matter support broader delegation. Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), upheld standards for tea inspection that required expert judgment; United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911), sustained regulations governing forest reserves whose violation was made criminal by statute. Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), likewise approved delegation in a field “where the knowledge relevant to the regulation was largely in the possession of the regulatees.” The rapid evolution of designer drugs presents an analogous situation.

Because the statute supplies an intelligible principle, authorizes subdelegation, and operates in a domain demanding administrative flexibility, the Third Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
