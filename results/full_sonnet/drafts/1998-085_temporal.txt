**ARGUMENT**

**Punitive damages under Title VII, like punitive damages at common law, may be awarded only for conduct that is itself egregious — not merely for conduct accompanied by a particular state of mind.**

When Congress authorized punitive damages for intentional discrimination in the Civil Rights Act of 1991, it legislated against the backdrop of a century-old body of common law defining when such damages are appropriate. This Court has long held that punitive, or "exemplary," damages are reserved for a narrow category of wrongs distinguished not merely by the defendant's subjective awareness of risk, but by the aggravated character of the conduct itself. That common-law understanding should inform the standard this Court adopts here, and it forecloses petitioner's position that "malice" or "reckless indifference" alone, untethered to any independent showing of egregiousness, suffices to support an award of punitive damages.

In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852), this Court explained that juries may award damages "having in view the enormity of [the defendant's] offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff," and made clear that such damages are appropriate only "when the injury has been wanton or malicious, or gross fraud has intervened." *Id.* at 371. The touchstone was not simply what the defendant knew or intended, but the *enormity* — the aggravated quality — of the act itself.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), holding that exemplary damages require more than the ordinary wrongful conduct sufficient to establish liability. The Court there distinguished compensatory damages, which redress the plaintiff's loss, from punitive damages, which punish conduct rising to the level of "wanton" disregard of the rights of others. *Id.* at 493. Liability for the underlying wrong and liability for punishment were treated as analytically distinct inquiries — the latter demanding an independent showing of aggravation, not simply an inference drawn from the state of mind necessary to establish the former.

*Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), confirms the same principle. There this Court sustained an instruction permitting the jury to award damages "by way of punishment" only where the defendant's conduct was "malicious," "wanton," or otherwise so reckless as to evince "a total disregard of the rights of the plaintiff." *Id.* at 562-63. The Court's focus was on the character of the wrong — its "wantonness" — as a substantive limitation on punitive awards, not merely a restatement of the intent element of the underlying tort.

Finally, in *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), this Court described punitive damages as "a punishment for the wrong done" that requires an "aggravation of the injury" beyond the elements of the wrong itself. *Id.* at 108. Because punitive damages exist to punish "aggravated misconduct," the Court held that they cannot properly rest on the same showing that suffices to establish the underlying violation. *Id.* Notably, the Court also refused to impute exemplary liability to a corporate employer absent misconduct by, or ratification by, those in a position of real authority — reflecting the same insistence that punishment be reserved for conduct that is truly blameworthy, not merely technically unlawful. *Id.* at 109.

These decisions establish a consistent common-law rule: punitive damages are not simply damages for intentional wrongdoing with a culpable state of mind; they are damages for wrongdoing whose *character* — its wantonness, oppression, or maliciousness — sets it apart from ordinary unlawful conduct. Congress is presumed to have legislated with this settled common-law backdrop in mind when it authorized punitive damages "if the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The statutory terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" should therefore be read consistently with the common-law tradition from which they were drawn — as terms that describe egregious conduct, not as a freestanding mental-state inquiry divorced from the nature of the act itself.

Collapsing the punitive-damages inquiry into a pure state-of-mind test would also produce anomalous results inconsistent with this Court's traditional treatment of exemplary damages as an extraordinary remedy. Under petitioner's view, any employer found liable for intentional discrimination — the state of mind necessary to establish liability in the first place — could automatically be exposed to punitive damages so long as it was aware, in the abstract, that discrimination is unlawful. But *Arms* and *Barry* make clear that liability for the wrong and liability for punishment are not coextensive; the latter demands an independent showing that the conduct itself was sufficiently aggravated to warrant punishment, not merely that the actor knew or should have known its conduct was wrongful.

For these reasons, respondent respectfully submits that this Court should hold that punitive damages under Title VII require proof that the employer's conduct was egregious or outrageous, consistent with the common-law principles that have long governed the availability of exemplary damages, as reflected in *Day*, *Arms*, *Barry*, and *Prentice*.
