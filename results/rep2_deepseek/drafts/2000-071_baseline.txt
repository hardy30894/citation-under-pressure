**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because It Excluded Religious Worship, Not a Religious Viewpoint on a Secular Subject**

Milford’s community-use policy created a limited public forum for secular activities, not a traditional or designated public forum. “Nothing in the Constitution requires the Government freely to grant access to all who wish to exercise their right to free speech on every type of Government property without regard to the nature of the property or to the disruption that might be caused by the speaker’s activities.” *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 799-800 (1985). Once a government entity creates a limited public forum, it may restrict speech based on subject matter so long as the restriction is reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995).

Milford’s policy opened school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but expressly prohibited use for “religious purposes.” This subject-matter distinction is permissible. In *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), the Court held that a school district could open its facilities for a wide range of secular uses while excluding religious uses, as long as the exclusion was not viewpoint-based. There, the school had permitted presentations on child rearing and family values from secular perspectives but denied access to a religious film series on the same topics. The Court found viewpoint discrimination because the school allowed discussion of the subject matter from *all* perspectives except the religious one. *Id.* at 393-94.

Here, by contrast, Milford did not open its forum to any group teaching morals and character from any perspective. The record shows that Milford allowed groups such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club to use its facilities—groups focused on civic, recreational, and character-building activities that are entirely secular in content. As the Second Circuit recognized, “[e]ach of these organizations focuses on the development of youth in various ways; however, the record includes nothing to indicate that any of these clubs’ activities remotely approach the type of religious instruction and prayer provided by the Club.” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000).

The Club’s proposed activities were not merely a religious *viewpoint* on morals and character; they were religious *instruction and worship*. The meeting opened with prayer, included Bible lessons centered on salvation through Jesus Christ, and concluded with an invitation for children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” for salvation. The lesson plan for “Israel Demands a King” explicitly distinguishes between “saved” and “unsaved” children and urges the unsaved to “believe on Him, trusting Him to save you.” These are not discussions of morality from a religious perspective; they are quintessentially religious exercises. When the subject matter of speech is itself religious worship or instruction, a school district that has not opened its forum for religious purposes may exclude it. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 (1981) (recognizing that religious worship and discussion are “forms of speech” but that a university could exclude them from a forum limited to secular activities).

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, the university had created a forum for *all* student publications, irrespective of viewpoint, and simply denied funding to those that “manifest[ed] a particular belief in or about a deity.” 515 U.S. at 825. That was viewpoint discrimination because the forum was open to *all* student groups. Here, Milford did not create a forum for discussion of morals and character from *any* perspective; it created a forum for secular activities only. The Club seeks to convert a secular forum into one that must include religious exercises. That is not required by the First Amendment.

**II. Exclusion Was Justified by Milford’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation**

Even if the exclusion were viewed as a speech restriction, it is justified by Milford’s compelling interest in complying with the Establishment Clause. “There is a compelling interest in maintaining an atmosphere free from sectarian disputes and in avoiding the entanglement of the school with religion.” *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394-95 (citing *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271-72). Here, the Club’s activities involved prayer, Bible instruction, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—all conducted on school premises with elementary school children who were required to attend the district’s schools. As the Court noted in *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992), “the State, in a school setting, in all its activities, is bound to maintain an attitude of ‘neutrality’ toward religion.” Permitting a religious organization to conduct inherently devotional activities with young children on school grounds risks the perception of official endorsement. *See also Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308 (2000) (holding that school sponsorship of prayer at football games violated the Establishment Clause because of the coercive pressure on students to participate).

Milford’s concern was not speculative: the Club’s materials targeted children who attended the school, and the meetings were held at a time when children were still on campus. The school had a duty to avoid even the appearance of endorsing religion. Under *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971), government action must have a secular purpose, must neither advance nor inhibit religion, and must avoid excessive entanglement. Excluding religious worship from a limited secular forum satisfies all three prongs. The policy has a secular purpose—to provide community activities without sectarian divisions; it neither advances nor inhibits religion because the Club remains free to meet elsewhere; and it avoids the entanglement that would arise from monitoring religious content.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The school district permissibly excluded religious worship from its limited forum, and that exclusion was compelled by the Establishment Clause.
