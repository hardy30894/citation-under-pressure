**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state may impose term limits on members of the United States Congress through its own constitutional amendment—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution of the United States, in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3, explicitly prescribes the qualifications for membership in the House of Representatives and the Senate. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the exclusive standards by which eligibility for federal office is determined. Any attempt by a state to add to, subtract from, or otherwise alter these qualifications violates the Supremacy Clause and the structural integrity of the federal system.

The Framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to vest in Congress the power to regulate its own membership. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542 (1875), this Court reaffirmed that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law, or state action, can impair the rights secured by the Constitution.” The qualifications for Congress are not left to the discretion of the states. They are fixed by the Constitution itself. To hold otherwise would permit a patchwork of conflicting standards across the states, undermining the uniformity essential to the functioning of a national legislature.

The Constitution provides that “No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty-five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, cl. 2. Similarly, the Constitution requires that “No Person shall be a Senator who shall not have attained to the Age of thirty Years, and been nine Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State for which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These are not mere suggestions. They are the *only* qualifications that may be imposed by the federal government. The Framers did not leave open the possibility that a state might impose additional requirements—such as term limits—on candidates for federal office.

This principle was settled in *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849), where the Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and that no state law or state action can be valid if it conflicts with it.” The Court further emphasized that “the Constitution is a complete and self-contained system,” and that “the powers of the states are limited to those not prohibited by the Constitution.” The power to regulate qualifications for federal office is not among the powers reserved to the states. It is a power expressly granted to Congress and, by implication, reserved to the national government.

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment attempts to impose a new qualification—eligibility based on the number of terms served—on candidates for the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate. This is not a mere regulation of state elections; it is a direct interference with the federal electoral process. The amendment purports to bar individuals from appearing on the ballot for federal office based on their prior service. This is not a matter of state election administration. It is a substantive restriction on who may run for Congress. Such a restriction is not within the authority of any state.

The Court has long recognized that the Constitution’s qualifications clauses are exclusive. In *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506 (1868), the Court stated that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state and federal, must conform to it.” The Court further held that “no state can, by its own laws, alter the qualifications for federal office.” To allow a state to impose term limits would be to permit a state to rewrite the Constitution by administrative fiat. That is not the role of the states. It is not the function of state constitutions to amend the federal charter.

Moreover, the argument that the amendment merely affects ballot access is legally immaterial. The effect of the amendment is to disqualify candidates from federal office based on a criterion not found in the Constitution. Whether the disqualification is labeled “ballot access” or “eligibility” does not change its substance. The Supreme Court has consistently held that “the right to vote is not a mere privilege, but a fundamental right secured by the Constitution.” *Yarbrough v. United States*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884). To deny a candidate the right to appear on the ballot based on a state-imposed term limit is to infringe upon that right. The Constitution does not permit such a restriction.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that the amendment violates the U.S. Constitution. The amendment attempts to impose a qualification not found in the Constitution. It is not a regulation of state elections. It is an attempt to alter the federal qualifications for Congress. That power belongs exclusively to the people of the United States, acting through the constitutional amendment process. No state may usurp that power.

The dissent’s reliance on severability is misplaced. The amendment was presented as a single, unified package. The voters were not given a choice between state term limits and federal term limits. They were asked to approve or reject the entire measure. The Preamble refers to “elected officials” without distinction. The ballot title and summary lumped state and federal officeholders together. The amendment was not a series of independent provisions. It was a single initiative with a single purpose: to impose term limits on all elected officials, federal and state alike. To sever the federal provisions from the state ones after the fact is to rewrite the will of the voters. It is to allow the sponsors of the amendment to benefit from a constitutional violation while pretending that the remainder is valid.

The principle is clear: the Constitution of the United States is supreme. The qualifications for Congress are fixed by the Constitution. No state may alter them. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does exactly that. It is therefore void.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
