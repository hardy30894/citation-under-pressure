**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that Anglo-American criminal law has historically conditioned criminal liability on proof of a culpable mental state, a requirement so deeply rooted in the common law that congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement rather than to dispense with it. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–251, 263 (1952).

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter, such as regulatory violations involving inherently dangerous or heavily regulated conduct. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from the regulatory context of *Balint*; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court read a "willfully" requirement into a federal civil-rights statute to require that the defendant act "in reckless disregard of constitutional prohibitions or guarantees fairly implied from the fact that they are so often expressed and understood," 325 U.S. at 105, precisely to avoid punishing defendants for conduct they did not appreciate as unlawful. Likewise, this Court has instructed that criminal statutes touching on protected liberty must be construed narrowly, resolving doubt against extending criminal liability by inference. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." Only narrow, historically unprotected categories of speech fall outside the First Amendment's shelter, defined by this Court as speech that is "no essential part of any exposition of ideas, and [is] of such slight social value as a step to truth that any benefit that may be derived from them is clearly outweighed by the social interest in order and morality." *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). Such categories must be defined with precision, not expanded through an objective, listener-focused standard that punishes speech based on how it might be received rather than what the speaker meant to convey.

This Court has cautioned that a speech offense may not be defined so that mere audience reaction determines criminality. In *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1 (1949), the Court explained that "a function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger." *Id.* at 4. If speech may not be suppressed merely because it provokes anger or alarm in its audience, a fortiori a speaker's own words may not be transformed into an unprotected "true threat" solely because a listener, applying a reasonable-person standard, perceives them as threatening — without regard to whether the speaker intended any such meaning.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression — dark humor, hyperbole, rap lyrics, venting — because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has recognized that the First Amendment demands "breathing space" for such expression and that laws touching speech must not be construed to sweep protected activity within their reach merely because some hearers might take offense or alarm. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Where scienter requirements are absent, "the ordinance tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter," because a speaker of ordinary prudence will steer far wider of the forbidden zone than the Constitution requires. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before punishment may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense — punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium, holding that "no one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); *see also Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–102 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
