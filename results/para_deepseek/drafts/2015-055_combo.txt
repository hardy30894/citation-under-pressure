ARGUMENT

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague—does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under this Court’s well-settled retroactivity framework, a new rule of constitutional criminal procedure is not retroactive unless it is “designed to improve the fact-finding process” or is “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 636–37 (1965). The *Johnson* rule satisfies neither criterion.

**I. The *Johnson* Rule Is a New Rule of Criminal Procedure.**

*Johnson* announced a new constitutional rule. It struck down the residual clause as void for vagueness, a due process doctrine that concerns notice and the avoidance of arbitrary enforcement. *See United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954) (vagueness doctrine “requires that a penal statute define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited”). Such a rule is procedural in nature: it does not decriminalize any conduct or alter the elements of any offense. It merely changes the method by which a court determines whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony. Accordingly, the *Johnson* rule is a new rule of criminal procedure, not a substantive rule that would automatically apply retroactively.

**II. The *Linkletter* and *Stovall* Factors Counsel Against Retroactivity.**

Under *Linkletter*, this Court examines the purpose of the new rule, the reliance of law enforcement and the courts on the old rule, and the effect of retroactivity on the administration of justice. *Id.* at 637. *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293, 296 (1967), refined this inquiry, adding the factor of “the extent to which the purpose of the new rule is to enhance the reliability of the fact-finding process.” The *Johnson* rule’s purpose is to provide fair notice and to prevent arbitrary enforcement. But that purpose does not affect the accuracy of the original conviction or sentencing. As this Court recognized in *Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406, 416 (1966), a rule that “does not affect the integrity of the fact-finding process at trial” is not entitled to retroactive application. *Johnson* does not involve the reliability of evidence or the truth-seeking function of the trial; it concerns only the statutory definition of a predicate offense. Thus, its purpose is not to correct errors that undermine the correctness of the conviction.

Moreover, law enforcement and the courts heavily relied on the residual clause for decades. The clause was a settled part of federal sentencing law, and courts uniformly applied it. This Court has declined to give retroactive effect to new rules where there has been substantial reliance on the old rule. *See Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244, 249–50 (1969) (denying retroactivity to *Katz v. United States* because “law enforcement officials reasonably relied on” the prior rule). Here, the reliance was even greater, as the residual clause had been upheld by this Court in multiple decisions. Retroactive application would impose an enormous burden on the federal judiciary, requiring the resentencing of countless prisoners. That disruption would undermine the “finality of judgments” that is essential to the administration of justice. *Stovall*, 388 U.S. at 298.

**III. Collateral Review Is Not a Substitute for Direct Appeal.**

This Court has long held that habeas corpus is not a means to correct errors that could have been raised on direct appeal. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 182 (1947) (“The writ of habeas corpus is not a substitute for an appeal.”). *Johnson* was decided after Welch’s direct appeal became final. The vagueness challenge was not raised at the time, and the rule announced is not a jurisdictional defect that can be raised at any time. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376 (1880) (habeas jurisdiction limited to cases where the court lacked jurisdiction or the sentence was void). Because the *Johnson* rule does not render Welch’s conviction void, collateral relief is unwarranted.

**IV. The *Johnson* Rule Does Not Affect Basic Fairness.**

Finally, the *Johnson* rule is not “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 637. A rule that changes the definition of a sentencing enhancement does not affect the fundamental fairness of the criminal proceeding. *See Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719, 731 (1966) (denying retroactivity to *Miranda* because the rule was not “a basic constitutional right going to the very fairness of the trial”). The residual clause was not so vague that its application at the time of Welch’s sentencing rendered the proceeding fundamentally unfair. This Court has consistently denied retroactive application to new rules that do not affect the accuracy of the guilt determination. *Tehan*, 382 U.S. at 416.

For these reasons, the *Johnson* rule should not be applied retroactively. The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
