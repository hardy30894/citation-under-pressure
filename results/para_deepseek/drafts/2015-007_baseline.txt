ARGUMENT

I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment’s Jury Trial Guarantee

The Sixth Amendment requires that any fact rendering a defendant eligible for the death penalty must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002). But *Ring* did not invalidate Florida’s scheme because, unlike Arizona, Florida already affords a central role to the jury. The jury renders an advisory verdict on whether death is the appropriate sentence, and the judge then makes the ultimate findings as to aggravating and mitigating circumstances. This Court has repeatedly upheld Florida’s hybrid system against Sixth Amendment challenges. See *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984) (“Florida’s capital sentencing scheme … does not violate the Sixth Amendment.”); *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 641 (1989) (per curiam) (rejecting argument that aggravating factors must be found by the jury). In *Hildwin*, the Court held that “the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the death penalty be made by the jury.” 490 U.S. at 640–641.

*Ring* expressly declined to overrule *Hildwin* and *Spaziano* with respect to Florida. 536 U.S. at 597 n.4 (noting that those cases “are not overruled” and that “the differences between [Florida’s] scheme and the one before us” might be controlling). Under Florida law, the jury recommends a sentence by majority vote, and the judge conducts an independent weighing of aggravators and mitigators. The jury’s role is not merely advisory in the sense of being inconsequential; the judge must give the jury’s recommendation “great weight.” *Tedder v. State*, 322 So. 2d 908, 910 (Fla. 1975). That participatory role satisfies any Sixth Amendment requirement because the jury is involved in the capital sentencing decision. Unlike Arizona’s scheme, where the judge alone found aggravating factors, Florida ensures that the jury has a voice in the determination of death-eligibility facts.

Moreover, the Sixth Amendment does not mandate jury unanimity on the sentencing recommendation. This Court has never held that jury verdicts in capital sentencing must be unanimous. To the contrary, the Court has long recognized that “the States are free to devise their own methods of determining the punishment to be imposed” in capital cases. *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 259 (1976) (plurality opinion). Florida’s use of a simple majority for advisory verdicts is consistent with the historical practice of non-unanimous jury findings in sentencing. *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 410 (1972) (upholding non-unanimous verdicts in criminal cases). And the Eighth Amendment’s requirement of “heightened reliability” in capital sentencing, *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), is satisfied by the judge’s independent review and the availability of appellate proportionality review.

II. The Sixth and Eighth Amendments Do Not Require a Jury Determination of Mental Retardation

The Eighth Amendment, as interpreted in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 321 (2002), prohibits executing mentally retarded offenders, but “leaves to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. Florida has done precisely that. Under Florida law, mental retardation is not a traditional element of the offense; it is a statutory bar to execution that the defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence. *Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009). Nothing in the Sixth Amendment requires that a jury determine such a sentencing factor. In *Ring*, the Court held that the jury must find aggravating factors that “make the defendant eligible for the death penalty.” 536 U.S. at 609. Mental retardation is not an aggravator; it is a mitigating factor that, if proven, precludes imposition of the death sentence. The determination of whether a defendant is mentally retarded is a “highly subjective” judgment best left to the trial court, as the Court recognized in *Atkins*. 536 U.S. at 317.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment demand that a jury make this finding. The “heightened reliability” standard for capital sentencing requires that the sentence be “individually determined on the basis of a broad inquiry into the circumstances of the offense and the character of the offender.” *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 304 (1976) (plurality opinion). Florida’s scheme provides such an inquiry: the defendant may present mental retardation evidence to the jury as mitigation, and the judge must consider that evidence before sentencing. The trial court here conducted a full evidentiary hearing and concluded that Hurst had not proven mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. That process satisfies the Eighth Amendment’s guarantee of individualized sentencing.

Finally, Florida’s status as an outlier on jury unanimity does not render its scheme unconstitutional. This Court has consistently held that the Eighth Amendment is not a “code” for uniformly imposing procedural requirements across the States. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 176 (1976) (joint opinion). The Constitution allows for “diversity in state approaches” to capital punishment. *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 319 (1987). Florida’s procedures are rational and have been upheld for decades. There is no violation of the Sixth or Eighth Amendments.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
