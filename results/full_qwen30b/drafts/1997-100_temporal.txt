**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause, which provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb,” is not limited to trials on the underlying substantive offense. Rather, it protects against successive prosecutions for the same offense, including those that determine the severity of punishment based on prior criminal conduct. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—permitting retrial of a prior conviction allegation after an acquittal on that issue—violates the fundamental principle that a defendant may not be twice put in jeopardy for the same offense, even when the second proceeding is styled as a sentencing enhancement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies not only to trials on guilt or innocence but also to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution” for the same offense. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a criminal charge could not be retried on the same charge, even if the first trial had ended in a mistrial due to a hung jury. The Court emphasized that “the double jeopardy clause is not confined to cases where the first trial has ended in a verdict of acquittal or conviction,” but extends to “any proceeding which is a second prosecution for the same offense.” Id. at 671. The Court further explained that “the purpose of the clause is to prevent a second trial after an acquittal, whether the first trial ended in a verdict or in a mistrial.” Id. at 672. This principle is equally applicable when the “offense” is not the commission of a new crime, but the determination of a prior conviction that triggers a more severe sentence.

In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150 (1833), the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a crime could not be retried on the same charge, even though the first trial had been reversed on appeal due to a procedural error. The Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States declares that no person shall be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb. This is a fundamental principle of justice, and is not to be evaded by technicalities.” Id. at 167. The Court made clear that the protection of the Double Jeopardy Clause is not contingent on the nature of the proceeding—whether it be a trial on guilt or a determination of punishment—but on whether the proceeding constitutes a second prosecution for the same offense.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings is inconsistent with these foundational precedents. The Court’s reasoning—that the prior conviction allegation is not an “offense” within the meaning of the Clause—misconstrues the scope of the protection. The Clause does not require that the prior conviction be a “crime” in the traditional sense; rather, it protects against any proceeding that subjects a defendant to the risk of punishment for the same conduct. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court held that a defendant could not be prosecuted twice for the same offense, even when the second prosecution was based on a different statute, if the two prosecutions were for the same underlying conduct. The Court stated: “The double jeopardy clause is not limited to cases where the same statute is violated, but extends to cases where the same act or transaction is the subject of prosecution under different statutes.” Id. at 382. Here, the prior conviction allegation is not a separate crime, but a factual determination that, if true, subjects the defendant to a harsher sentence for the same underlying offense. To permit retrial on that allegation after an acquittal is to subject the defendant to a second prosecution for the same offense.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection in sentencing proceedings is misplaced. *Williams* held that due process does not require a jury trial or formal evidentiary hearing at sentencing, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court in *Williams* explicitly noted that the defendant had already been convicted of the substantive offense and that the sentencing proceeding was “not a trial on guilt or innocence.” Id. at 245. However, the Court did not suggest that the Double Jeopardy Clause was inapplicable to any sentencing determination. Indeed, the Court in *United States v. Ball* had already established that the Clause applies to any second prosecution for the same offense, regardless of whether it occurs at trial or sentencing.

The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), on the ground that it involved a capital case is unsupported by precedent. The Court in *Bullington* did not limit its analysis to capital cases. Rather, it held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a sentencing enhancement could not be retried on that enhancement, because the proceeding was “a second prosecution for the same offense.” Id. at 439. The Court emphasized that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal,” and that “the nature of the proceeding is not dispositive.” Id. at 439. The Court’s reasoning in *Bullington* is consistent with the earlier decisions in *Ball* and *Lanza*, which established that the Clause applies to any second prosecution for the same offense, regardless of the nature of the proceeding.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings is therefore contrary to settled precedent. The Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense, and a retrial on a prior conviction allegation after an acquittal constitutes such a prosecution. The judgment of the California Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
