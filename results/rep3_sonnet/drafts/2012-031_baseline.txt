# ARGUMENT

## I. Rule 23(b)(3) Requires Admissible Evidence That Damages Are Susceptible of Measurement on a Class-Wide Basis, and the Third Circuit Erred in Certifying a Class Without Such Proof

This Court has long held that "Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading standard." *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). A district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" to determine whether the prerequisites of Rule 23 have been satisfied, *id.* at 351, and this rigorous analysis "will frequently entail overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim," *id.* at 351 n.6. The court below acknowledged this standard, quoting its own precedent that class certification requires "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations" and "may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits." *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 317 (3d Cir. 2008). Yet in practice, the Third Circuit abandoned this rigor precisely where it mattered most: at the threshold question whether damages could be proven through common, admissible evidence.

Rule 23(b)(3) demands that "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). Where, as here, plaintiffs allege four distinct theories of antitrust injury—but only one theory (overbuilder deterrence) survived scrutiny at the merits stage—any methodology purporting to calculate class-wide damages must be capable of isolating damages attributable to that single surviving theory. The District Court and the Third Circuit permitted Dr. McClave's regression model to serve as "common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis," *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010), despite the fact that this model calculated damages assuming the validity of all four theories of antitrust impact, not merely the one theory ultimately certified.

This Court's decision in *General Telephone Co. of Southwest v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147 (1982), establishes that class certification requires "actual, not presumed, conformance with Rule 23(a)." *Id.* at 160. The same logic extends to Rule 23(b)(3): a district court cannot presume that a damages model is capable of isolating class-wide injury attributable to a particular theory of liability; it must determine, through rigorous analysis of admissible evidence, that the model actually does so. The Third Circuit's deferential review—finding no "clear error" in accepting a methodology that conflated four theories of harm into a single damages figure—effectively presumed conformance with Rule 23(b)(3) rather than requiring proof of it.

## II. The Court of Appeals Improperly Relied on *Anderson v. Bessemer City*'s Deferential Standard to Avoid Rigorous Scrutiny of the Damages Methodology

The Third Circuit invoked *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), for the proposition that "[w]here there are two permissible views of the evidence, the factfinder's choice between them cannot be clearly erroneous." *Id.* at 574. But *Anderson*'s deferential clear-error standard governs findings of fact after a full trial on the merits; it was never intended to relieve district courts of their gatekeeping obligation at the class-certification stage to determine, as a matter of law, whether a proposed methodology is capable of proving class-wide damages consistent with the plaintiffs' theory of liability. The question whether Dr. McClave's model was legally sufficient to establish that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis under the *single* surviving theory of antitrust impact is a question of methodology's fit to the theory of liability—a legal question reviewable de novo, not a factual finding entitled to *Anderson* deference. *See Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312 ("We review de novo whether an incorrect legal standard has been used.").

This Court's antitrust jurisprudence has consistently required that damages calculations bear a direct relationship to the theory of antitrust violation actually proven. In *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251 (1946), this Court permitted approximation of damages, but only where the "wrongful act" and its causal relationship to injury had first been established. *Id.* at 264-65. Here, by contrast, the model measured aggregate overcharges attributable to *four* theories of anticompetitive conduct, when only one theory—overbuilder deterrence—survived as a basis for class-wide liability. Nothing in *Bigelow* or its progeny sanctions certification of a class where the damages methodology fails to isolate the effects of the specific liability theory certified for class treatment.

## III. Permitting Certification Without Admissible Proof of a Workable Class-Wide Damages Methodology Undermines the Predominance Requirement's Core Purpose

The predominance inquiry exists to ensure that common questions do not merely predominate in the abstract but that the class action mechanism can actually resolve the case efficiently and fairly. *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591 (1997), instructs that Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement is "far more demanding" than Rule 23(a)'s commonality requirement, and exists precisely to test "whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation." *Id.* at 623-24. A damages model that cannot distinguish between four different theories of harm—three of which are no longer part of the case—cannot demonstrate the cohesion *Amchem* requires. If such a model suffices to satisfy predominance, then Rule 23(b)(3) imposes no meaningful constraint at all, and defendants will face class-wide liability determinations based on evidence that was never rigorously tested against the plaintiffs' actual, certified theory of the case.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
