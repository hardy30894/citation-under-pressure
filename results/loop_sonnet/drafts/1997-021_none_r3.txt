# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Application of an Identical "Reasonable Doubt" Standard to Polling, Withdrawal, and Elections Is Arbitrary and Cannot Stand.

The National Labor Relations Act says nothing about employer polling of employees; the practice, and whatever standard governs it, is entirely a creature of Board policy. This Court has long recognized the dilemma facing an employer confronted with evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support: the employer risks liability either by continuing to bargain with a union that no longer represents its employees or by refusing to bargain with one that still does. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969). Polling exists precisely to let an employer resolve that uncertainty through a measured, procedurally safeguarded inquiry rather than the blunter instrument of outright withdrawal of recognition.

An administrative rule, however, is entitled to deference only where the agency has drawn "a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962). No such connection supports the Board's insistence on an identical evidentiary showing—objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt of majority status—for three categorically different employer actions: outright withdrawal of recognition, a Board-conducted RM election, and an internal poll. The Board itself maintains that RM elections are the preferred method of testing employee sentiment precisely because they carry procedural protections that polling lacks. That premise compels a *lower* threshold for polling, not an identical one. An agency may not adopt a rule whose stated rationale points in one direction while its operative effect points in the opposite direction; a reviewing court must be able to discern that "the grounds upon which the administrative agency acted" were "clearly disclosed and adequately sustained." *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943). The Board's polling standard fails that basic test of coherence.

Nor can the Board defend the equivalence of standards as a permissible exercise of remedial discretion. The Board's authority to fashion policy under the Act must serve the Act's purposes, not administrative convenience. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941). This Court has repeatedly required the Board to accommodate the legitimate, competing interests at stake rather than mechanically apply one rule to conduct of plainly different character and consequence. *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945). A secret-ballot poll conducted with neutral supervision and no threat of reprisal poses none of the risks to employee free choice that attend an employer's unilateral withdrawal of recognition. Treating the two as evidentiary equivalents—without any reasoned explanation of why the reduced risk of a properly conducted poll warrants no reduction in the evidentiary burden—is the kind of unreasoned agency action this Court has consistently refused to sustain.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even accepting the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt cannot survive review of the record as a whole. Congress intended reviewing courts to take real responsibility for the reasonableness of Board findings, directing that "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board may not treat its chosen inference as conclusive simply because it was one of several possible readings of the evidence; the inference itself must be reasonable in light of the record taken as a whole. *Id.* at 490.

The Board's handling of the evidence here repeats the error this Court identified in *NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 291–92 (1965), where an inference of unlawful motive rested not on an even-handed reading of the record but on a predetermined conclusion superimposed on ambiguous facts. Here, the ALJ and the Board discounted every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown in turn: Ridgick's and Marsh's statements were dismissed as equivocal; Bloch's and Mohr's reports of their coworkers' views were excluded wholesale because those coworkers did not personally testify; and employees no longer on the payroll were removed from the calculus altogether. No comparable scrutiny was applied to evidence supporting the Union's position. Board findings must rest on the actual dynamics of the record, not on categorical rules that foreclose consideration of whole classes of evidence before that evidence is ever weighed. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963).

The cumulative effect of this selective winnowing is to impose an evidentiary threshold that no employer could realistically meet without first conducting the very poll the Board would forbid it from taking absent that same showing. That circularity is not the substantial evidence *Universal Camera* demands; it is precisely the outcome-driven factfinding that decision was designed to prevent. Any remedy the Board imposes, moreover, must reflect the employees' actual, current wishes rather than an administrative presumption divorced from the record. *Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944). The uncontradicted testimony of record here—substantially exceeding the seven statements the Board was willing to credit—establishes that Allentown's doubt about the Union's continuing majority support was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
