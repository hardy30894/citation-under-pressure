**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not extend Longshore Act coverage to every land-based injury that has some causal connection to offshore drilling. Congress extended coverage only for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That text limits coverage to injuries sufficiently tied to operations on the Shelf itself; it does not convert the statute into a broad, offshore-related workers’ compensation regime for injuries occurring at onshore processing sites.

Statutory text must be applied as written. The Court has long held that “[w]here the words of a law are plain, and the meaning clear, we have no right to speculate upon the intentions of the legislature.” **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**. In **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**, the Court likewise explained that courts must enforce statutory language according to its terms. And **United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820)** cautions that a statute “is not to be extended by construction.” Read in light of those settled principles, § 1333(b) cannot be stretched to cover an injury occurring onshore at a processing facility simply because offshore production made the facility necessary.

The Court has also long recognized that compensation statutes are bounded by the jurisdictional facts Congress chose. In **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932)**, the Court upheld a compensation scheme by emphasizing the statute’s defined jurisdictional limits. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court enforced the statutory line Congress drew rather than enlarging coverage on equitable grounds. Those decisions confirm that courts may not substitute a general causation inquiry for the specific statutory trigger Congress enacted here.

Respondent’s theory also conflicts with the settled rule that remedial statutes, however beneficent, may not be rewritten under the banner of liberal construction. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**, teaches that a court may not depart from statutory text because a broader reading seems more consistent with the statute’s purpose. **Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 416 (1932)**, likewise confirms that courts must honor Congress’s chosen limits. And **Ex parte McNiel, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 236, 242 (1871)** holds that statutory conditions are not waived by implication. Here, Congress did not say “any injury connected in any way to offshore operations.” It said injury “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That choice of words matters.

The respondent’s reading would erase the statute’s geographic anchor. The phrase “on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies the operations that generate coverage, and the resulting injury must be tied to those operations in a meaningful way. The injury here occurred when Mr. Valladolid was working at La Conchita, an onshore oil-processing facility, and was crushed by a forklift while consolidating scrap metal. That location and activity are far removed from offshore drilling operations themselves. Treating such an accident as covered would make the statute’s reference to the Outer Continental Shelf largely surplusage, a result the Court has rejected. See **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**; **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**.

The Court’s compensation decisions reinforce the same point. In **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 281 U.S. 574, 580-81 (1930)**, the Court recognized that compensation coverage depends on the legislative boundary Congress enacted. And in **Baltimore Mail S.S. Co. v. Fawcett, 269 U.S. 247, 251-52 (1925)**, the Court declined to extend a maritime compensation scheme beyond its statutory design. Those cases confirm that courts may not broaden coverage based on policy preferences or sympathy for a claimant.

In short, § 1333(b) covers injuries that arise from operations on the Outer Continental Shelf; it does not cover every onshore injury that would not have happened but for offshore work. Because Mr. Valladolid was injured onshore at a processing facility, the claim falls outside the statute. The judgment should be affirmed.
