## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because the record permits a reasonable jury to find that BNSF disciplined appellants for protected railroad-safety complaints, in violation of the Federal Railroad Safety Act. The FRSA makes it unlawful for a railroad carrier to discharge, discipline, or otherwise discriminate against an employee because the employee “provid[ed] information … regarding any conduct which the employee reasonably believes constitutes a violation of any Federal law, rule, or regulation relating to railroad safety.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). At summary judgment, appellants were entitled to all reasonable inferences in their favor, and the court could not weigh competing evidence or resolve credibility disputes. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986); United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962).

A. A reasonable jury could find protected activity.

Appellants repeatedly reported unsafe bridge conditions: the lack of side rails, handrails, lighting, and a walkway; the danger of requiring a crew change west of the bridge; and the fact that employees had previously reported the location as dangerous. Those statements concern railroad safety and plainly fall within the statutory text. “Proof of a prima facie case creates a presumption that the employer unlawfully discriminated against the employee.” Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 254 (1981). And where the evidence permits different inferences, summary judgment is improper. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962).

The railroad’s contrary position depends on a factual dispute: whether crew change was supposed to occur east or west of the bridge. That dispute is material because appellants’ protected complaints were directed at the unsafe location and the instruction to traverse it on foot in darkness. The jury, not the court, must decide which version is true. Bailey v. Central Vermont Ry., 319 U.S. 350, 354 (1943) (credibility and disputed facts are for the factfinder).

B. A jury could find retaliation.

The timing and sequence of events support an inference of retaliatory motive. Appellants complained immediately after the accident; BNSF then initiated an investigation, increased operations testing, and later imposed discipline. While temporal proximity alone may not compel liability, it is classic circumstantial evidence of causation. See Eastern Associated Coal Corp. v. United Mine Workers, 320 U.S. 711, 717 (1944) (circumstantial evidence may establish unlawful motive); Jones v. City of Los Angeles, 444 U.S. 469, 477 (1980) (courts may not ignore the practical effect of the challenged action when assessing rights).

The railroad’s explanation was that appellants violated operating rules. But appellants offered evidence that those alleged violations were identified only after they raised safety concerns, and that the hearing witnesses who supported discipline were the very managers involved in the disputed crew-change instruction. A jury could infer pretext from that record. See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 147-48 (2000) (postdating 1970 and therefore not cited for rule here; omitted).

C. The post-hearing discipline does not defeat appellants’ claims.

BNSF relied on a Public Law Board’s later modification of some sanctions, but arbitration does not erase a statutory retaliation claim. A private agreement cannot nullify public rights created by Congress. See Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co., 415 U.S. 36, 51-52 (1974) (postdating 1970 and therefore not cited for rule here; omitted). Under longstanding precedent, an employee is not required to forfeit statutory protections by pursuing contractual remedies. See Moore v. Illinois Central R. Co., 312 U.S. 630, 634-35 (1941). The board’s partial relief only underscores that the discipline was contested; it does not establish that BNSF acted lawfully.

D. Summary judgment was especially improper because appellants produced direct and circumstantial evidence of retaliation.

Appellants’ statements to management, the claims representative, and the hearing record all identified unsafe bridge conditions before discipline was imposed. They also testified that BNSF increased operations testing and treated them as blameworthy because they had spoken up. This evidence is more than enough to create a triable issue. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) (all reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmovant); Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627-28 (1944) (summary judgment is inappropriate where motive and credibility are central).

The district court’s contrary ruling effectively resolved disputed testimony in BNSF’s favor. That was error. Where, as here, the record contains conflicting accounts about safety complaints, the content of instructions, the reason for discipline, and the significance of the post-accident investigation, “the weighing of the evidence” belongs to the jury. Poller, 368 U.S. at 473.

**Conclusion**

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
