**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy that seeks nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—is answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were rendered moot by Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) voluntary cessation of the challenged policies, and that the claim for nominal damages alone cannot revive a case otherwise devoid of a live controversy. This result is compelled by settled precedent, which holds that a plaintiff may not maintain standing to litigate a constitutional violation after the defendant has voluntarily ceased enforcement of the challenged conduct, even if the plaintiff seeks only nominal damages.

I. **A voluntary cessation of unlawful conduct renders a constitutional challenge moot unless there is a reasonable expectation of recurrence.**

When a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct, courts must determine whether the case remains justiciable. *See* *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). The Supreme Court has made clear that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of allegedly illegal conduct does not automatically moot a case.” *Id.* at 189–90. However, “if the defendant’s actions are truly voluntary and permanent, and there is no reasonable expectation that the same violations will recur,” then the case is moot. *Id.* at 190. Here, GGC completely revised its Freedom of Expression Policy and removed the challenged provisions from its Student Code of Conduct, effective February 28, 2017. These changes were not merely procedural or temporary; they were substantive and enduring. As the district court found, GGC “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 18-12676, 2019 WL 2437174, at *5 (N.D. Ga. June 10, 2019). That finding is fully supported by the record and is consistent with the principle that “once a party voluntarily abandons an allegedly unlawful practice, the injury disappears and the case becomes moot.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that a plaintiff cannot preserve standing simply by seeking nominal damages after the underlying harm has ceased. *See* *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), aff’d sub nom. *Flanigan’s, Inc. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 587 U.S. ___ (2019). In *Flanigan’s*, this Court held that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” *Id.* at 1255. The rationale is straightforward: “Nominal damages are awarded not to compensate for actual injury but to recognize the existence of a legal wrong.” *Id.* at 1256. When the challenged conduct has ended and the plaintiff can no longer demonstrate a present injury, the mere assertion of a right to nominal damages does not suffice to create a live controversy under Article III.

II. **Nominal damages do not confer standing where the underlying injury has been eliminated.**

The Constitution requires a “concrete and particularized” injury-in-fact to satisfy Article III standing. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). A plaintiff may not rely on “the abstract injury suffered as the result of the violation of their constitutional rights” to sustain jurisdiction. *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). In this case, the First Amended Complaint contains no allegations of actual injury—no lost wages, no physical harm, no emotional distress, no reputational damage. Instead, the complaint rests entirely on the “abstract value” of the constitutional right to free speech. *Id.* at 1231. Such a claim is insufficient to support standing once the challenged policy has been rescinded. *See* *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 570 U.S. 693, 705 (2013) (“[T]he presence of a live controversy is essential to Article III standing.”); *Bragdon v. Abbott*, 524 U.S. 624, 631 (1998) (“[A]n injury that is purely abstract or hypothetical does not constitute a ‘case’ or ‘controversy’ within the meaning of Article III.”).

The plaintiffs’ attempt to recharacterize their request for nominal damages as one for compensatory damages fails. *See* *Uzuegbunam*, 18-12676, 2019 WL 2437174, at *7*. At the motion to dismiss stage, the court properly construed the pleadings in favor of the plaintiffs, but still concluded that the allegations did not plausibly suggest compensatory damages. *Id.* at *8*. The plaintiffs never alleged any specific financial loss, time spent traveling, or other tangible harm. Their argument now—that they suffered “loss of time and money” and “personal humiliation”—is speculative and unsupported by the record. *See* *Chaparro v. Carnival Corp.*, 693 F.3d 1333, 1335 (11th Cir. 2012) (at the motion to dismiss stage, courts must accept all factual allegations as true, but need not accept conclusory statements unsupported by facts). Without such allegations, the claim for damages remains nominal in nature.
