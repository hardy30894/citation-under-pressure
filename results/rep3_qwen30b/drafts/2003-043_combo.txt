**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of settled principles of federal tax law, grounded in statutory text, regulatory interpretation, and binding precedent. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS may not collect unpaid partnership taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The government’s attempt to circumvent the statutory limitations period by relying solely on an assessment against the partnership fails as a matter of law. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Statute of Limitations for Assessment Applies Individually to Each Taxpayer, and the IRS Must Assess Each Partner Separately to Extend the Limitations Period as to Them**

The Internal Revenue Code provides that the government may assess a tax against a taxpayer only within three years after the return is filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). This limitation is not merely procedural—it is jurisdictional. *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. 51, 54 (1958) (“The statute of limitations in tax cases is not a mere technicality; it is a substantive right.”). The statute further provides that if an assessment is made within the three-year period, the government may collect the tax by levy or judicial action within ten years of the assessment. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But this ten-year extension applies only to the taxpayer who was assessed. *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 54; *United States v. Bowers*, 348 U.S. 362, 365 (1955) (“The statute of limitations is not extended as to a taxpayer who has not been assessed.”).

The IRS concedes that it did not assess the individual partners within the three-year window. Yet it argues that the assessment against the partnership somehow extends the limitations period as to the partners. This argument is foreclosed by *United States v. Bowers*, 348 U.S. at 365, where the Court held that “the statute of limitations is not extended as to a taxpayer who has not been assessed.” The Court emphasized that the statute “does not extend the time for assessment of a tax against one person because of an assessment against another.” Id. The same principle applies here: the assessment against the partnership does not operate as an assessment against the individual partners, nor does it extend the limitations period as to them.

This interpretation is consistent with the statutory definition of “taxpayer.” Under § 7701(a)(14), a “taxpayer” includes “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The Code treats individuals and partnerships as distinct taxpayers. *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 54. The IRS’s own regulations confirm this: “An assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary in accordance with rules or regulations prescribed by the Secretary.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (1950). The regulation requires identification of the taxpayer. Id. An assessment against a partnership does not identify the individual partners as taxpayers, and thus cannot be deemed an assessment against them.

**II. The IRS Cannot Collect from Individual Partners Without a Judgment or Individual Assessment, Even Under State Law**

The IRS argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this allows the government to collect from individual partners without a separate assessment. This argument fails. *United States v. Bowers*, 348 U.S. at 365, makes clear that state law cannot override the federal statute of limitations. The IRS may not use state law to circumvent federal procedural requirements.

Moreover, California law itself does not permit automatic collection from individual partners. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” This rule is consistent with *United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), where the court affirmed a district court’s refusal to allow a refund to a partner who had paid his share of a partnership tax, but emphasized that the government could not collect from the partner without a valid assessment or judgment. Id. at 910. The court noted that “the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” Id. at 910 (emphasis added). Thus, even under state law, a judgment against the partner is required.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), where the court affirmed a district court’s holding that a tax lien against a partner’s property was invalid because the partner had never been assessed. Id. at 458. The court stated: “This court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” Id. at 458. The IRS’s failure to assess the partners individually rendered the lien invalid. The same logic applies here: no assessment, no right to collect.
