The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that an appellate court conducting plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may consult the entire record—not merely the trial evidence—to determine whether a forfeited error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Plain-error review requires the defendant to show (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The third prong demands a showing of prejudice, i.e., “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” Molina-Martinez v. United States, 578 U.S. 189, 194 (2016) (quoting United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 76 (2004)). The fourth prong likewise turns on whether the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736 (quoting United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)).

These inquiries are not confined to the trial record. In Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court examined the entire record—including matters outside the jury’s view—to conclude that an unobjected-to error did not satisfy the fourth prong. Id. at 469-70. Similarly, in United States v. Marcus, 560 U.S. 258 (2010), the Court directed that the fourth-prong analysis “consider the record as a whole,” including evidence never presented to the jury. Id. at 265. This Court has applied the same principle in the Rehaif context, holding that a reviewing court may consider a defendant’s prior convictions when assessing whether a failure to instruct on the knowledge-of-status element affected substantial rights. Greer v. United States, 593 U.S. ___, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2097-98 (2021).

Limiting the inquiry to the trial record would contradict these precedents and undermine the purpose of plain-error review, which exists to correct only those errors that produce a miscarriage of justice. Olano, 507 U.S. at 736. Because the Eleventh Circuit properly consulted Greer’s full criminal history in concluding that he could not satisfy the third and fourth prongs, its judgment should be affirmed.
