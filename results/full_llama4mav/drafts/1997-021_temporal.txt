The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern the practice of polling employees about their support for an incumbent union. However, the NLRB has established a standard for polling, which requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status." This standard is too strict and renders employer polls useless. As the Fifth Circuit noted in NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 413 F.2d 1084 (5th Cir. 1969), an employer should be allowed to poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition." 

In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support. Several employees made statements to the new owners suggesting that the union had lost the support of bargaining-unit members generally. The NLRB, however, discounted the statements of several employees, including Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, who expressed anti-union sentiments during job interviews. The NLRB's decision to discount these statements is inconsistent with its own precedent, as seen in cases such as NLRB v. John S. Barnes Corp., 146 F.2d 66 (7th Cir. 1944), where the court held that an employer's good faith doubt can be based on statements made by employees.

The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The statements made by employees, including Ridgick and Marsh, demonstrate that Allentown Mack had a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status. As the Supreme Court noted in Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), the NLRB's findings must be supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. In this case, the record as a whole supports Allentown Mack's claim that it had a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status.

The NLRB's standard for polling is overly restrictive and fails to account for the interests of employers. As the Sixth Circuit noted in NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (6th Cir. 1965), an employer has a legitimate interest in determining whether a union still represents a majority of its employees. By allowing employers to poll their employees with substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, the NLRB can strike a balance between the interests of employers and employees.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the NLRA. The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. This Court should reverse the NLRB's decision and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful.
