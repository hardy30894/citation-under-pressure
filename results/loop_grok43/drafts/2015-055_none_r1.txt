The rule that a criminal statute may not stand when it fails to provide fair notice of the conduct it punishes must apply to all convictions resting upon it. This follows because the Constitution is the supreme law, and any statute repugnant to it is void from the beginning. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803). A judgment imposing punishment under such a statute exceeds the lawful authority of the court and cannot be permitted to stand. Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 119 (1866).

Due process requires that every criminal statute give adequate notice of what it forbids. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 99 (1908). When a statute is later declared incapable of constitutional application because it supplies no such notice, the defect is fundamental and reaches the validity of every sentence imposed under it. Boyd v. United States, 116 U.S. 616, 631 (1886). The same principle of fundamental fairness that voids the statute in the first instance requires that it not be applied to prior convictions. Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937); Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46, 54 (1947).

New rules that protect against arbitrary or vague criminal liability have always been given retroactive effect on collateral review. Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 17 (1956); Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963). Where the constitutional defect goes to the integrity of the criminal process itself, the judgment cannot survive. Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 639 (1965). The same result follows when the statute is declared incapable of any constitutional application. Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 727 (1966).

Retroactive application is required even on collateral attack when the new rule concerns the substantive reach of a criminal statute. Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967); Witherspoon v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 510, 521 n.20 (1968). A sentence imposed under an unconstitutionally vague provision is no less void than one imposed without counsel or without notice of the charges. Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952); Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932).

Because the residual clause is void for vagueness, any sentence resting upon it exceeds the court’s lawful authority and must be set aside. Chimel v. California, 395 U.S. 752, 766 (1969). The judgment below therefore cannot stand.
