### Argument

#### I. Stelwagon failed to prove actual damages caused by any Robinson-Patman violation, so the trebled antitrust judgment cannot stand.

Section 4 of the Clayton Act permits recovery only for a plaintiff “injured in his business or property by reason of anything forbidden in the antitrust laws.” 15 U.S.C. § 15(a). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that this requires proof of fact of injury and proof that the damages claimed were caused by the antitrust violation, not by independent market forces or the plaintiff’s own business decisions. See *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 114 (1969); *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264–65 (1946); *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, 489 (1977).

Here, although the jury could find a Robinson-Patman violation, Stelwagon still had to prove antitrust injury with reasonable certainty. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562–64 (1981), is controlling. There, the Court rejected the notion that a Robinson-Patman violation alone establishes damages and held that a plaintiff must show actual injury attributable to the discrimination. Id. at 561–62. The Court emphasized that Robinson-Patman plaintiffs are not relieved of the ordinary requirement of proving “actual injury” and “some showing” of damages causally linked to the violation. Id. at 561, 563. So too here: Stelwagon proved a price spread, but not that the spread caused lost sales, lost profits, or any other measurable injury.

The record, as summarized by the district court, shows that Stelwagon’s claimed losses depended on speculation about how its business would have evolved absent the price discrimination. Speculative damages are not recoverable under the antitrust laws. See *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264; *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563–66 (1931). While uncertainty as to the precise amount of damages does not bar recovery once injury is shown, uncertainty as to whether the injury was caused by the defendant does. *Zenith Radio*, 395 U.S. at 114; *Masonite Corp. v. Norfolk & W. Ry. Co.*, 316 U.S. 265, 275–76 (1942). Stelwagon’s evidence did not bridge that gap.

Nor did Stelwagon show that any asserted decline in business was attributable to the discriminatory pricing rather than to other business decisions. Under *Brunswick*, damages must be “of the type the antitrust laws were intended to prevent” and must flow from that which makes the defendant’s conduct unlawful. 429 U.S. at 489. A plaintiff may not recover simply because a statutory violation occurred; it must prove that the violation caused a cognizable competitive loss. Id.; *Atlantic Richfield Co. v. USA Petroleum Co.*, 495 U.S. 328, 334–35 (1990). Stelwagon’s proof here did not establish that any lost profits were the product of the discriminatory sales to Standard Roofing or Celotex, rather than Stelwagon’s own inability or failure to compete on other terms.

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Rea v. Ford Motor Co.*, 497 F.2d 577, 583–84 (3d Cir. 1974), confirms that antitrust damages cannot rest on conjecture about how the plaintiff’s business would have performed in a hypothetical but-for world. And in *Murphy Tugboat Co. v. Crowley*, 467 F. Supp. 841, 853 (N.D. Cal. 1979), aff’d, 708 F.2d 580 (9th Cir. 1983), the court held that a plaintiff must provide a reliable causal connection between the violation and the claimed loss; unsupported estimates are insufficient. The same principle defeats Stelwagon’s award.

The remittitur does not cure the problem. A reduced number does not transform speculative proof into competent evidence of antitrust injury. See *J. Truett Payne*, 451 U.S. at 563–64. Because Stelwagon failed to prove actual damages caused by the alleged discrimination, the treble-damages judgment under 15 U.S.C. § 15(a) should be vacated.

#### II. At most, Stelwagon was entitled to nominal relief on the contract claim; the contract award cannot support the antitrust trebling judgment.

The district court correctly recognized that the jury’s original damages award was “unsupported by the evidence and . . . grossly excessive,” and reduced it substantially. But the Robinson-Patman award still rests on the same defective proof. Contract damages and antitrust damages are analytically distinct. See *Brunswick*, 429 U.S. at 489; *Associated Gen. Contractors of Cal., Inc. v. California State Council of Carpenters*, 459 U.S. 519, 540–45 (1983). Stelwagon’s evidence about an exclusive distributorship may bear on contract liability, but it does not establish antitrust injury from discriminatory pricing.

Indeed, the district court’s own remittitur illustrates the unreliability of the damages proof. Where a verdict depends on inference piling upon inference, judgment as a matter of law is appropriate. See *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 149–50 (2000) (courts must disregard only evidence favoring the movant, but may not sustain a verdict unsupported by a legally sufficient evidentiary basis); *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986) (a jury verdict cannot stand on mere “scintilla” of evidence). Stelwagon’s antitrust damages evidence amounted to little more than that.

#### III. The judgment should be vacated insofar as it awards treble damages under the Clayton Act.

Because Stelwagon failed to prove actual antitrust damages with the causation required by *J. Truett Payne*, *Zenith Radio*, and *Brunswick*, the Court should vacate the treble-damages portion of the judgment. The jury’s liability finding, even if sustained, cannot support a money judgment under the antitrust laws absent competent proof of injury. See *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264–65; *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 563–66; *Atlantic Richfield*, 495 U.S. at 334–35. The proper disposition is to reverse the antitrust damages award and remand for entry of judgment consistent with the absence of proved antitrust injury.
