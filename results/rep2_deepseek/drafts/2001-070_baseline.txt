ARGUMENT

I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CATEGORICALLY PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED PERSONS.

This Court squarely held in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989), that the Eighth Amendment "does not categorically prohibit" the execution of mentally retarded offenders. The Constitution leaves such individual determinations to the sentencing jury, which may consider mental retardation as a mitigating factor. *Id.* at 319-20. Nothing in the text, history, or evolving standards of decency has eroded that holding.

A. There Is No National Consensus Against Executing Mentally Retarded Persons.

The "clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values," *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370 (1989), is found in state legislation. In *Penry*, this Court observed that only two states had enacted statutes barring execution of the mentally retarded, which was "insufficient to demonstrate a national consensus." 492 U.S. at 334. Today, only a minority of states prohibit the practice, and many of those have done so only recently. The federal government, which represents the entire nation, has not enacted such a ban. A "handful of States" cannot alone establish a national consensus. *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 372. Moreover, even in states that have no formal ban, sentencing juries frequently impose death sentences on mentally retarded defendants, further demonstrating the absence of any settled societal judgment.

B. The Purposes of Capital Punishment Are Served by Executing Mentally Retarded Offenders.

The Eighth Amendment requires that punishment serve legitimate penological goals: retribution and deterrence. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 183-87 (1976) (plurality opinion). Mental retardation does not negate the moral culpability necessary for retribution. As *Penry* recognized, a defendant's reduced capacity may "diminish his blameworthiness," but the jury can weigh that factor case-by-case. 492 U.S. at 340. For the most severe offenders—those who, like Atkins committed a violent, cold-blooded murder—retribution remains a valid justification. Similarly, the deterrent effect of capital punishment is not eliminated by mental retardation. *Penry* itself noted that the "potential deterrent effect" still applies, as many mentally retarded individuals can conform their conduct to law. *Id.* Cf. *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 319 (1987) (rejecting categorical challenges to capital punishment based on statistical disparities). There is no evidence that executing mentally retarded murderers fails to deter others.

C. The Sentencing Process Adequately Accounts for Mental Retardation.

The Eighth Amendment guarantees individualized sentencing in capital cases. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion). Under *Penry*, the sentencer must be permitted to consider any relevant mitigating factor, including mental retardation. 492 U.S. at 328. Indeed, this Court mandated that Texas's capital sentencing scheme failed to allow such consideration. *Id.* at 319-20. Virginia's procedure, like many states, instructs the jury to consider all mitigating evidence. Atkins's own jury heard expert testimony that he was mildly mentally retarded but nonetheless found the aggravating circumstances outweighed mitigation. That individualized assessment is precisely what the Constitution requires. A blanket prohibition would supplant the jury's role and substitute a rigid rule for case-by-case judgment. See *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 304 (1976) (striking down mandatory death penalty for failure to individualize). The Constitution does not demand that the States adopt a categorical exemption.

D. Penry Remains Controlling Precedent.

*Penry* has not been overruled, and it squarely controls this case. The Court in *Penry* expressly rejected the argument that capital punishment of the mentally retarded is invariably cruel and unusual. 492 U.S. at 340. The reasoning of *Penry* relied on the same "evolving standards of decency" test petitioner invokes. *Id.* at 330. Since *Penry*, the Court has not retreated from its holding. Lower federal and state courts have consistently applied *Penry* to reject categorical challenges. See, e.g., *Bleuvins v. State*, 951 S.W.2d 384, 388 (Tenn. 1997). If the standard has changed, the evidence is ambiguous at best, and the States must be given latitude to experiment. *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 377 (Scalia, J., concurring). The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on *Penry*.

II. THE VIRGINIA SUPREME COURT DID NOT ERR IN AFFIRMING ATKINS’S DEATH SENTENCE.

The state court faithfully applied *Penry*, allowing the jury to consider Atkins’s mental retardation. Atkins received a full and fair sentencing hearing. The jury weighed the evidence and imposed death. Nothing in the Eighth Amendment requires a different outcome.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
