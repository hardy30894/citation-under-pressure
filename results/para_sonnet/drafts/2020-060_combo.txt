# ARGUMENT

## I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam's Liability, and Guam's Section 107 Action Therefore Remains Timely.

A judicially approved settlement must be read according to what the parties actually agreed to, not according to what a court might infer from its practical consequences. This Court has long recognized that consent instruments—unlike judgments entered after full litigation—embody only the bargain the parties struck, and courts must not enlarge that bargain by implication. In *United States v. International Building Co.*, 345 U.S. 502 (1953), this Court refused to give a consent judgment effect beyond its actual terms precisely because the underlying issues had never been adjudicated; the decree reflected agreement to specific outcomes, not a resolution of the parties' respective fault. The same logic governs here. The 2004 Consent Decree expressly recites that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." That disclaimer is not surplusage. It is the parties' chosen means of ensuring that Guam's assumption of remediation obligations would not be mistaken for an acceptance of responsibility for the Navy's decades of unlawful dumping.

Where the parties to a settlement have spoken plainly, courts should not construe the instrument to accomplish what its text disclaims. Cf. *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 115–17 (1932) (a consent decree reflects the terms the parties actually accepted, and a court may not read into it obligations or effects the parties did not adopt). Because the Decree here disclaimed any adjudication of liability, it cannot simultaneously be read to have "resolved" Guam's liability for purposes of triggering a three-year contribution clock. Guam's six-year cost-recovery action remains available.

## II. Even If the Decree's Effect Were Doubtful, Equitable Principles Forbid Treating Ambiguity as a Forfeiture of Guam's Remedy.

Limitations periods are practical devices, not talismans to be applied mechanically against an innocent party. As this Court observed in *Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson*, 325 U.S. 304, 314 (1945), "[s]tatutes of limitation find their justification in necessity and convenience rather than logic." They exist to protect against stale claims and lost evidence—concerns wholly absent where, as here, the Navy's decades-long deposit of munitions and chemicals such as DDT and Agent Orange into the Ordot Dump is a matter of established administrative record, not disputed fact requiring aged testimony.

Equity has likewise long refused to let technical timing rules reward the party responsible for the underlying wrong at the expense of the party left to clean up after it. *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392 (1946), teaches that limitations doctrines are read in light of equitable considerations designed to prevent a wrongdoer from using procedural formalism to escape accountability. Here, it is the Navy—not Guam—that built and operated the Ordot Dump, continued to use it through the Korean and Vietnam Wars, and left behind an unlined, uncapped landfill discharging contaminants into the Lonfit River. Equity does not permit that history to be erased by a hyper-technical reading of a settlement's ambiguous language.

Nor should the governing statutory scheme be construed to produce that inequitable result. This Court has repeatedly instructed that remedial legislation "should be construed broadly to effectuate its purposes." *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967). A cost-recovery framework designed to ensure that those responsible for hazardous contamination bear its costs should not be read, through interpretive doubt about a settlement's legal character, to instead immunize the responsible federal actor and place the burden permanently on the innocent territorial government.

## III. Sovereign and Equitable Principles Governing Responsibility for Pollution Confirm That the Party Who Created the Hazard Must Bear Its Cost.

This Court's early pollution jurisprudence establishes the baseline equitable principle at stake: the entity that contaminates land, air, or water under another's dominion bears responsibility for that contamination, and the injured sovereign is entitled to protection and redress. In *Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co.*, 206 U.S. 230, 237 (1907), this Court recognized that a sovereign has an interest "in all the earth and air within its domain" and may seek relief against contamination inflicted by conduct beyond its control. *Missouri v. Illinois*, 200 U.S. 496 (1906), likewise confirms the gravity with which this Court has treated the discharge of contaminants into waters used and relied upon by another sovereign entity.
