### Argument

#### I. The machine-gun conviction cannot stand because the government failed to prove the knowledge required by *Staples*.

The Supreme Court has held that, absent a clear statement from Congress, criminal statutes regulating firearms are presumed to require proof that the defendant knew the characteristics of the weapon that make its possession unlawful. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602, 619 (1994). In *Staples*, the Court reversed a conviction under the National Firearms Act because the government had not proved the defendant knew his rifle had been converted to fire automatically. Id. at 603-04, 619. The Court emphasized that a defendant must know of the features that place the weapon within the statute’s coverage; mere possession of a gun is not enough. Id. at 619. This Court has likewise recognized that where Congress criminalizes possession of a specially regulated firearm, the prosecution must prove knowledge of the weapon’s incriminating characteristics. *United States v. O’Loughlin*, 7 F.3d 1165, 1170-71 (1st Cir. 1993); *United States v. Collins*, 61 F.3d 1379, 1383-84 (1st Cir. 1995).

The evidence here did not establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle was a machine gun within the meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 922(o). The government proved only that she knew there was an AK-47 in the golf bag. Knowledge that a gun is an AK-47 is not knowledge that it is capable of automatic fire. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 619. Indeed, the Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished between awareness of a firearm’s general nature and awareness of the particular feature that makes possession illegal. *United States v. Freed*, 401 U.S. 601, 607-10 (1971); *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 610-16.

The prosecution relied on Nieves-Castaño’s statement that she had opened the bag, saw a rifle, and knew it was an AK-47. But an AK-47 may be semiautomatic or automatic; the government offered no evidence that she knew the weapon fired more than one shot by a single pull of the trigger. The record contains no testimony that she handled the weapon, fired it, saw it in operation, heard it fire automatically, or knew it had been modified. That is fatal under *Staples*. See *United States v. Shaw*, 670 F.2d 56, 60-61 (5th Cir. 1982); *United States v. Pierre*, 254 F.3d 872, 876-77 (9th Cir. 2001); *United States v. Reyna*, 130 F.3d 104, 106-07 (5th Cir. 1997).

Nor may knowledge be inferred simply because she hid the gun for another person. Concealment may show awareness of possession, but it does not prove awareness of the weapon’s automatic-fire capability. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 615-16. The government’s theory would collapse the mens rea requirement into mere custody or handling, contrary to *Staples* and *Collins*. 61 F.3d at 1383-84. The Supreme Court has cautioned against interpreting firearm statutes to impose felony liability on persons who may be wholly ignorant of the weapon’s regulated characteristics. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 610-19.

The district court’s instructions did not cure this evidentiary gap. A jury instruction cannot substitute for proof. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Because the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, did not permit a rational juror to find beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was a machine gun, the conviction under § 922(o) must be reversed. *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319; *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 619; *United States v. Whorton*, 394 F.3d 210, 218-19 (4th Cir. 2005).

#### II. The evidence also failed to prove possession of a machine gun, because the record does not establish knowing dominion over a weapon whose illegal character was unknown.

To sustain possession, the government had to prove that Nieves-Castaño knowingly possessed the firearm and knew the facts making the possession criminal. *Henderson v. United States*, 575 U.S. 622, 628-29 (2015); *United States v. García*, 983 F.2d 1160, 1165 (1st Cir. 1993). The evidence showed only temporary storage at Alexis’s request. That may establish physical custody, but it does not establish the requisite criminal knowledge. *United States v. Teague*, 956 F.2d 1425, 1430-31 (7th Cir. 1992). Where the weapon’s prohibited feature is unproven, possession alone does not sustain a conviction under a statute that depends on that feature. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 612-13.

Because the government failed to prove the mens rea required by *Staples*, the machine-gun conviction should be vacated.
