The Sixth Circuit's decision in this case is consistent with established Supreme Court precedent, and its holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim should be affirmed.

In Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Supreme Court held that "so long as a defendant is represented by counsel whose performance is not constitutionally ineffective under the standard established in Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), we discern no inequity in requiring him to bear the risk of attorney error that results in a procedural default." Murray, 477 U.S. at 488. The Court further noted that "ineffective assistance of counsel... is cause for a procedural default." Id. This principle is grounded in the notion that a defendant's right to effective assistance of counsel is a fundamental aspect of due process.

The Court has also recognized that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse a procedural default, even if the ineffective-assistance claim itself is procedurally defaulted. In Martinez v. Ryan, 566 U.S. 1 (2012), the Court held that, in certain circumstances, the ineffective assistance of post-conviction counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of a claim of ineffective assistance of trial counsel. While Martinez is distinguishable from the present case, its reasoning is instructive. The Court emphasized that the "equitable judgment" to be made is whether the prisoner can demonstrate cause and prejudice to overcome the procedural default. Id. at 14.

Here, the Sixth Circuit correctly applied this principle, holding that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted. This decision is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent, as it recognizes that a defendant's right to effective assistance of counsel is a fundamental aspect of due process, and that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance can, in certain circumstances, serve as cause to excuse another procedural default.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that a habeas petitioner's claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not itself subject to procedural default if it is raised in an initial-review collateral proceeding. See Massaro v. United States, 538 U.S. 500 (2003). In this case, Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim was raised in his application to reopen his direct appeal, which is analogous to an initial-review collateral proceeding.

The Sixth Circuit's decision is also consistent with the principle that a federal habeas court should not "trap" a petitioner in a procedural default by requiring that the underlying ineffective-assistance claim be exhausted or not procedurally defaulted before it can serve as cause to excuse another defaulted claim. See Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446, 453-54 (2000) (Breyer, J., concurring).

In conclusion, the Sixth Circuit's decision is grounded in established Supreme Court precedent, and its holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim is consistent with the principles of due process and the right to effective assistance of counsel. The judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be affirmed.
