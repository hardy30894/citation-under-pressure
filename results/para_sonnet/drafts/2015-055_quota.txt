# ARGUMENT

## Johnson Announced a New Rule of Criminal Procedure That Does Not Apply Retroactively on Collateral Review

Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), "new constitutional rules of criminal procedure will not be applicable to those cases which have become final before the new rules are announced," subject only to two narrow exceptions. *Id.* at 310. Because Johnson's rule regulates the method courts must use to adjudicate the residual clause, rather than the range of conduct or persons the ACCA may punish, it is quintessentially procedural under *Teague* and does not apply retroactively to Welch's already-final conviction.

### A. The Substantive/Procedural Line Turns on Whether the Rule Alters the Range of Conduct or Punishment, Not on the Constitutional Provision Invoked

The Court has long distinguished "rules that place, as a matter of constitutional interpretation, certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe," which apply retroactively, from rules "that regulate only the manner of determining the defendant's culpability," which generally do not. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311 (quoting *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in part)). *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348 (2004), sharpened this test: a rule is substantive only if it "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Id.* at 353. In *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998), the Court applied that principle where a subsequent decision narrowed the statutory definition of the crime itself, removing certain conduct from criminal liability altogether. *Id.* at 620.

Johnson does nothing of the kind. It leaves the ACCA's substantive definition of "violent felony" wholly intact under the enumerated-offenses and elements clauses; it merely holds that one clause—the residual clause—is too indeterminate to be judicially administered because "[i]nvoking so shapeless a provision to condemn someone to prison for 15 years to life" denies fair notice. *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591, 597 (2015). That is a holding about the *method* of adjudication—the "categorical approach" requiring courts to assess "a judge-imagined abstraction," *id.* at 598—not a holding that any category of conduct is now beyond Congress's power to punish. Sentencing courts remain free to impose the ACCA enhancement whenever a predicate offense satisfies the elements clause or enumerated-offenses clause. Johnson thus regulates *how* eligibility is determined, which is the hallmark of a procedural rule under *Schriro*.

### B. Johnson Is Analogous to Other Rules This Court Has Deemed Procedural

This case parallels *Whorton v. Bockting*, 549 U.S. 406 (2007), where the Court held that *Crawford v. Washington*'s new Confrontation Clause rule, though constitutionally grounded, was procedural because it "altered the range of permissible methods for determining whether a defendant's guilt... may be established," not the range of conduct subject to prosecution. *Id.* at 417–18. So too here: Johnson alters the permissible *methods* for determining ACCA eligibility without touching the substantive reach of the statute.

Likewise, in *Chaidez v. United States*, 568 U.S. 342 (2013), the Court held that *Padilla v. Kentucky*'s new Sixth Amendment rule regarding deportation advice was a "new rule" for *Teague* purposes because it broke new constitutional ground rather than merely applying settled principles. *Id.* at 347–49. Johnson likewise broke new ground: prior to Johnson, this Court twice upheld the residual clause against vagueness challenges. *See James v. United States*, 550 U.S. 192, 210 n.6 (2007); *Sykes v. United States*, 564 U.S. 1, 15 (2011). Johnson's abrogation of that precedent confirms it announced a genuinely new procedural rule rather than dictating an outcome required by prior law—precisely the sort of rule *Teague* was designed to withhold from collateral relief. *Sawyer v. Smith*, 497 U.S. 227, 234 (1990) ("A rule is new... if it breaks new ground or imposes a new obligation.").

### C. Johnson Does Not Satisfy Either *Teague* Exception

Even a new procedural rule may apply retroactively if it (1) places conduct beyond the State's power to punish, or (2) constitutes a "watershed rule[] of criminal procedure" implicating fundamental fairness. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311; *Saffle v. Parks*, 494 U.S. 484, 495 (1990). Johnson satisfies neither. As shown above, Johnson does not decriminalize any conduct; felon-in-possession liability under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) remains fully intact, and ACCA enhancement remains available under the surviving clauses. Nor is Johnson a "watershed" rule "without which the likelihood of an accurate conviction is seriously diminished." *Whorton*, 549 U.S. at 418 (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 313). Johnson concerns only the calculation of a sentencing enhancement for an offense of conviction that is not in dispute; it bears no relation to the accuracy of the underlying adjudication of guilt. Since *Teague*, this Court has "rejected every claim that a new rule satisfied the requirements for watershed status." *Whorton*, 549 U.S. at 417. Johnson should fare no differently.

### Conclusion

Because Johnson announced a new procedural rule that neither narrows the substantive reach of the ACCA nor qualifies as a watershed rule of criminal procedure, it does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
