**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. Petitioner Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds because the Tenth Amendment protects the sovereign interests of States, not the individual rights of private parties.

**I. A Private Criminal Defendant Has No Standing to Invoke the Tenth Amendment to Challenge a Federal Statute**

The Tenth Amendment states that "the powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. This provision operates as a "truism" that confirms the structural principle of federalism by limiting federal power vis-à-vis the States. *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It does not create individual rights enforceable by private litigants. *See Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939).

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, the Supreme Court held that private utility companies lacked standing to challenge federal action under the Tenth Amendment. The Court explained that "absent the states or their officers, [private parties] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the amendment." *Id.* at 144. The reasoning was straightforward: the Tenth Amendment reserves powers to the States, and only States (or their officers) may assert violations of that reservation. *Id.* at 143-44. Because Bond is a private individual, not a State, she cannot raise a Tenth Amendment claim. *See United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 526 (8th Cir. 2009) (holding that "a private party lacks standing to assert a Tenth Amendment claim"); *Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234 (2d Cir. 2006) (same); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33-35 (1st Cir. 2005) (same).

Bond attempts to distinguish *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* as mere dicta, but this argument fails. *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* expressly held that the private plaintiffs "lack standing *in this suit* to raise any question under the amendment." 306 U.S. at 144 (emphasis added). That holding—a jurisdictional determination—is binding precedent. *See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998) (requiring courts to resolve standing before reaching the merits). The First Circuit correctly recognized that the standing ruling in *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* is an alternative ground for the decision, not dicta. *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 34.

**II. Bond's Status as a Criminal Defendant Does Not Confer Standing to Raise Structural Claims**

Bond argues that, because she faces criminal prosecution, she has a personal stake sufficient to challenge the statute's constitutionality. This Court has recognized that a criminal defendant generally has standing to challenge a statute on grounds that it infringes her own rights. *See Bond v. United States*, 564 U.S. 211, 217 (2011). However, the Tenth Amendment claim she raises is not a personal right; it is a structural limitation on federal power designed to protect state sovereignty. *See Alden v. Maine*, 527 U.S. 706, 713 (1999) (describing federalism as "a constitutionally mandated balance of power between the States and the Federal Government"). Because Bond is not a State, she cannot invoke this structural protection. *See United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004) (holding that a criminal defendant lacked standing to assert a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal statute).

This Court's decision in *Bond v. United States* does not alter that analysis. There, the Court held that Bond had standing to challenge her conviction under the Chemical Weapons Convention because she suffered a concrete injury traceable to the statute. *Bond*, 564 U.S. at 225. But the Court expressly distinguished between standing to assert personal rights and standing to assert structural claims. *Id.* at 220 n.3 ("[W]e need not decide whether an individual may ever raise a Tenth Amendment claim."). The Court left that question open. It should now be resolved: an individual may not.

**III. Only States May Invoke the Tenth Amendment to Challenge Federal Action**

The Tenth Amendment is a "reservation to the States of all powers not delegated to the United States." *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992). States are the proper parties to assert violations of this reservation. *See Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898, 935 (1997) (entertaining a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by state officials). Where a State chooses not to object, private parties lack standing to do so on its behalf. *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35-36. Here, no State has challenged § 229. Bond cannot step into the shoes of a State to enforce structural limits that belong to the States alone.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
