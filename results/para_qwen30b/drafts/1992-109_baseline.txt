**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and its reversal must be affirmed. The record in this case reveals substantial evidence of mental impairment at the time of Moran’s waiver and pleas—evidence that triggered a good faith doubt about his competency and necessitated a hearing under established precedent. The state court’s failure to conduct such a hearing violated due process, and the post-conviction determination, based on an erroneous legal standard, cannot cure that constitutional defect.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights—such as the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial—must be “voluntary, knowing, and intelligent.” *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). This standard is not satisfied by mere formal compliance with procedural safeguards. Rather, it demands that the defendant possess the capacity to make a “reasoned choice” among the available alternatives. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981). This “reasoned choice” standard is distinct from—and more demanding than—the standard for competency to stand trial. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387, 86 S.Ct. 836, 843, 15 L.Ed.2d 815 (1966); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960) (per curiam). While the latter requires only a “rational and factual understanding” of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel, the former demands a higher level of mental functioning: the ability to comprehend the consequences of one’s decisions and to make a deliberate, informed choice among them. *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (hereinafter *Evans II*); *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518.

Here, the record demonstrates that Moran lacked the capacity for such a reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, slashing his wrists and shooting himself in the abdomen. He was still recovering from that trauma when he appeared before the court. His mental state was further compromised by the fact that he was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect the central nervous system and can impair judgment, memory, and decision-making. The court was informed that Moran was on medication, yet made no inquiry into its nature, dosage, or effects. This failure to investigate a known risk factor for diminished capacity constitutes a clear breach of due process. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973) (a court must initiate a competency hearing when there is “substantial evidence” of incompetence).

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing was not consistent with a reasoned choice. His responses to the court’s questions were largely monosyllabic, and he offered no explanation for his decision to waive counsel or to foreclose the presentation of mitigating evidence. He stated only that he wanted to prevent “any favorable evidence” from being introduced. This self-destructive motive—seeking to ensure a death sentence—suggests not rational deliberation, but a state of mind inconsistent with the ability to make a reasoned choice. The Supreme Court has recognized that a defendant’s desire to avoid mitigation is a “red flag” indicating possible incompetence. *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247, 1249 (9th Cir.1978). The court’s acceptance of such a plea without further inquiry was constitutionally deficient.

The state court’s post-conviction determination that Moran was competent is legally irrelevant. It relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier to determine competency to stand trial—evaluations that did not address the higher “reasoned choice” standard required for waiver of counsel or guilty pleas. *Evans I*, 705 F.2d at 1480; *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. The state court applied the wrong legal standard, and thus its finding cannot be presumed correct under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 857; *Evans II*, 800 F.2d at 887. Even if the presumption applied, it would not bar independent federal review of the legal standard. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 106 S.Ct. 445, 88 L.Ed.2d 405 (1985). The federal court must conduct its own de novo review of whether the waiver was knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 857.
