The petitioner's argument that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim is without merit. The Supreme Court has long held that a procedural default can be excused only by a showing of cause and prejudice. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438-439 (1963). In this case, the petitioner argues that the ineffective assistance of counsel claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.

However, the Court has also held that a claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot serve as cause to excuse another procedural default. See Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 482-483 (1953) (opinion of Reed, J.). The reasoning behind this rule is that a petitioner cannot rely on a claim that they have waived or defaulted to excuse another default. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (noting that a waiver of a constitutional right is a classic example of a procedural default).

Furthermore, the Court has established that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is subject to the same cause and prejudice analysis as any other claim. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 70-71 (1942) (holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel requires a showing of prejudice). If the ineffective assistance of counsel claim is itself procedurally defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to excuse another default. See Bute v. Illinois, 333 U.S. 640, 674-675 (1948) (noting that a petitioner's failure to raise a claim on direct appeal can result in a procedural default).

See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71-72 (1932) (emphasizing the importance of timely raising claims of constitutional error).

See Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-117 (1944). If the petitioner has failed to exhaust their state remedies with respect to the ineffective assistance of counsel claim, it cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.
