Argument

The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent, when the infringed design patent applies only to a component of a product. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits, rather than limiting damages to the profits attributable to the infringing component.

The governing statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that a design patent infringer shall be liable for "the total profit." However, this provision must be interpreted in light of the principle that damages should be commensurate with the infringement. As the Supreme Court held in Dobson v. Hartford Fire Ins. Co., 86 U.S. 3 (1873), damages for infringement should be calculated based on the "actual injury" caused by the infringement. Id. at 16. In the context of design patents, this means that damages should be limited to the profits attributable to the infringing design.

The Court has long recognized that design patents protect only the ornamental aspects of a product. See Gorham Co. v. White, 81 U.S. 511 (1871). In Gorham, the Court held that a design patent is infringed when the accused product has "the same appearance" as the patented design. Id. at 528. This principle is relevant here, as the infringed design patents apply only to specific components of Samsung's smartphones.

In Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the profits attributable to the infringing component, rather than the entire product. Id. at 614-15. Similarly, in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), the Court held that a patentee is entitled to recover only the profits attributable to the infringing feature, not the entire profit from the sale of the product. Id. at 121.

The principle that damages should be limited to the profits attributable to the infringing component is also supported by the Court's decision in Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641 (1915). In Dowagiac, the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the "advantages" derived from the infringement. Id. at 648. In the context of design patents, this means that damages should be limited to the profits attributable to the infringing design.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that a patent owner is not entitled to recover damages for non-infringing aspects of a product. See Coupe v. Royer, 155 U.S. 565 (1895). In Coupe, the Court held that a patentee is not entitled to recover damages for the entire product when only a component of the product infringes the patent. Id. at 582.

The Federal Circuit's decision in this case is inconsistent with these precedents. By affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits, the Federal Circuit effectively allowed Apple to recover damages for non-infringing aspects of Samsung's smartphones. This result is contrary to the principles established in Gorham, Westinghouse, Garretson, Dowagiac, and Coupe.

In Sinclair & Carroll Co. v. Interchemical Corp., 325 U.S. 327 (1945), the Court held that a patent owner is entitled to recover damages for infringement, but only to the extent that the infringement caused actual harm. Id. at 330. Similarly, in Enterprise Mfg. Co. of Pennsylvania v. Ladoo Co., 241 F. 664 (8th Cir. 1917), the court held that damages for patent infringement should be calculated based on the profits attributable to the infringing feature. Id. at 667.

In conclusion, the Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits. Damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent. This Court should reverse the Federal Circuit's decision and remand for a calculation of damages consistent with this principle.
