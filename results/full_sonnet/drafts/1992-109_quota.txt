# ARGUMENT

**THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD OF COMPETENCE TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL BEYOND THE STANDARD FOR COMPETENCE TO STAND TRIAL**

The Ninth Circuit held that a defendant who is competent to stand trial under *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), may nonetheless be incompetent to plead guilty or waive counsel unless he possesses a heightened capacity for "reasoned choice." This holding rests on an artificial distinction that finds no support in this Court's precedents and threatens to destabilize the settled competency framework that governs criminal proceedings nationwide. The competency standard for standing trial is sufficient, without modification, for accepting a guilty plea or waiver of counsel.

**A. This Court's Competency Standard Requires Only a Rational and Factual Understanding of the Proceedings**

In *Dusky*, this Court articulated the governing test for competency to stand trial: whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and has "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." 362 U.S. at 402. This Court reaffirmed that standard in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), explaining that competency requires "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 171 (quoting *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402). Similarly, in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court applied this same understanding-based standard without suggesting any heightened variant for particular stages of a criminal proceeding. *Id.* at 385.

Nothing in *Dusky*, *Drope*, or *Pate* suggests that a different, more demanding standard applies once a defendant decides to plead guilty or to represent himself. The *Dusky* standard already accounts for the defendant's capacity to make rational decisions in consultation with counsel; it does not stop at the threshold of trial and leave subsequent decisions ungoverned by any workable rule.

**B. A Guilty Plea Requires Only That It Be Knowing, Voluntary, and Intelligent—Not That the Defendant Possess Heightened Competence**

This Court's guilty-plea jurisprudence, exemplified by *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), asks whether the waiver of trial-related rights was made "voluntarily and knowingly." *Id.* at 242. The *Boykin* inquiry protects defendants by requiring an affirmative showing on the record that the plea was intelligently and voluntarily entered—not by superimposing a separate, higher competency threshold. The voluntariness and knowledge requirements of *Boykin* already guard against uninformed or coerced pleas; a freestanding "reasoned choice" competency test is unnecessary and doctrinally unmoored.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on decisions such as *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981), for a heightened standard does not withstand scrutiny, as those cases are circuit precedents untethered from any holding of this Court. Indeed, this Court in *Godinez* should make clear that *Dusky* competence, combined with the *Boykin* voluntariness inquiry, fully satisfies due process.

**C. A Heightened Standard for Waiver of Counsel Is Similarly Unwarranted**

The right to waive counsel and proceed pro se, recognized in *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), requires that the waiver be knowing and intelligent, but this Court did not suggest that a defendant must satisfy some elevated competency standard beyond that required to stand trial. *Faretta* focused on whether the defendant was "made aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation," 422 U.S. at 835, not on a separate psychiatric threshold of decision-making capacity.

Requiring a heightened competency standard for waiver of counsel—as opposed to a heightened *information* requirement, which *Faretta* already supplies—conflates two distinct due process concerns: (1) whether the defendant has the mental capacity to understand the proceedings, and (2) whether the defendant has been adequately informed of the consequences of forgoing counsel. The latter concern is addressed through colloquy requirements, not through a psychiatric competency standard exceeding *Dusky*.

**D. A Single Competency Standard Promotes Judicial Administrability and Respects the States' Role in Criminal Procedure**

This Court has recognized that competency proceedings implicate significant state interests in the administration of criminal justice. In *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), the Court emphasized deference to state procedural rules governing competency determinations absent a showing that a challenged practice "offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 445 (quoting *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 202 (1977)). No such tradition supports bifurcating competency into multiple standards depending on the stage or nature of the decision at hand.

Moreover, in *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986), this Court cautioned against expanding due process protections beyond what is necessary to ensure fundamental fairness, emphasizing that "[t]he Constitution ... requires no more than that the government not exploit weaknesses in the mental condition" of a defendant. *Id.* at 165. A single, coherent competency standard—one already calibrated to protect defendants' rational decision-making capacity—achieves this fairness without introducing an unworkable, undefined "reasoned choice" inquiry that lower courts and psychiatric evaluators are ill-equipped to apply consistently.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that the *Dusky* competency standard, applied in conjunction with the *Boykin* and *Faretta* colloquy requirements, satisfies the demands of due process for accepting guilty pleas and waivers of counsel.
