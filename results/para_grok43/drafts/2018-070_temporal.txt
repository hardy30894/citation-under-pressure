ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The text and structure of the statute make plain that state law supplies surrogate federal law solely where federal law leaves a gap.

In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court explained that Congress “feared that federal law standing alone would be inadequate” and therefore “decided that” existing federal law together with the law of the adjacent State would “comprise the body of law” governing the Shelf. Id. at 357. The Court emphasized that fixed platforms are treated “as though they were federal enclaves in an upland State,” id. at 355, and that state law is borrowed only to the extent necessary to furnish rules “where federal law did not apply,” id. at 358. This understanding confirms that incorporation occurs only when federal law is silent.

The same principle appears in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958). There the Court sustained the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13, which adopts state criminal law for federal enclaves “where there is no applicable federal statute.” Id. at 289. The Court stressed that the statute operates “only when the federal law is silent,” id. at 294, and does not permit state law to operate concurrently with comprehensive federal legislation. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) employs identical language and serves the identical purpose; it therefore admits state law only to fill voids in federal coverage.

The Fifth Circuit reached the same conclusion in Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Insurance Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969). Interpreting the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, the court held that state law is assimilated only when “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” exists. Id. at 1036. Because the Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive scheme of wage-and-hour regulation, no such gap remains for California law to occupy.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—would convert the Shelf into a concurrent-jurisdiction zone, contrary to the enclave model adopted in Rodrigue and the gap-filling limitation recognized in Sharpnack and Continental Oil. The district court correctly held that the FLSA leaves no room for additional state regulation. The judgment should be reversed.
