**ARGUMENT**

The District Court abused its discretion by certifying a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of the evidence before certifying a class. That analysis demands that the proponent of certification demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that each Rule 23 requirement is satisfied—including that common questions predominate. Here, the court accepted Plaintiffs’ damages model even though it failed to isolate damages attributable to the specific antitrust theory alleged—deterrence of overbuilder entry—and instead measured all overbuilder deterrence, regardless of cause. Such a model cannot provide a common methodology for measuring class-wide damages. The court’s approach conflicts with fundamental antitrust principles requiring that damages be tied to the particular violation and proven with reasonable certainty.

**I. The District Court Failed to Require That Plaintiffs’ Damages Model Be Consistent with Their Theory of Liability**

Antitrust damages must be “the result of the unlawful conduct” alleged. *Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 392 U.S. 481, 483 (1968). A plaintiff must prove “the fact of damage” and provide a reasonable basis for computation. *Id.* at 484. Here, Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, calculated damages by comparing actual Comcast prices to prices in a “but-for” world without overbuilder entry. But his model did not distinguish between overbuilder deterrence caused by Comcast’s alleged clustering and deterrence caused by other factors—such as market conditions or independent business decisions. The District Court nonetheless certified the class, holding that the model was common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis. That holding cannot be squared with *Hanover Shoe*’s requirement that damages be causally linked to the unlawful conduct. A model that measures all overbuilder deterrence, without isolating the effects of the challenged conduct, provides no assurance that damages are attributable to the antitrust violation. *See also White Motor Co. v. United States*, 372 U.S. 253, 263 (1963) (rejecting conclusory market definitions and requiring analysis of actual competitive effects).

**II. The District Court Also Erred in Accepting an Unsupported Geographic Market Definition**

The relevant geographic market is a critical element of any antitrust claim. *United States v. First Nat. Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. 665, 667 (1964) (requiring analysis of “the relevant market” to assess competitive effects). The District Court accepted the Philadelphia Designated Market Area (“DMA”) as the relevant geographic market, even though Comcast’s expert demonstrated that cable markets are local—often as small as individual franchise areas. The court’s acceptance of the DMA was based on Plaintiffs’ expert’s assertion, not on evidence that competition for cable subscribers operates on a DMA-wide basis. This error infected the entire predominance analysis. *See United States v. Radio Corp. of America*, 358 U.S. 334, 346 (1959) (requiring careful market definition before assessing antitrust impact). Without a properly defined market, Plaintiffs cannot show that common evidence will prove antitrust impact across the class.

**III. The District Court’s Reliance on a Flawed Damages Model Violated the Requirement That Common Questions Predominate**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Predominance is not satisfied merely because a damages model exists; the model must be capable of proving damages through common evidence. *See Leh v. General Petroleum Corp.*, 382 U.S. 54, 58 (1965) (emphasizing that the proponent bears the burden of proof). Here, Dr. McClave’s model required individualized inquiries into each subscriber’s location, the timing of overbuilder entry, and the extent of deterrence. These individualized issues overwhelm any common questions. *Cf. Seaboard Air Line Railroad Co. v. United States*, 382 U.S. 154, 157 (1965) (requiring analysis of competitive effects in the relevant market, not aggregate assumptions). The District Court’s conclusion that the model was “common” was clearly erroneous.

**IV. The District Court’s Approach Conflicts with the Need for Admissible Evidence at the Certification Stage**

A district court must “resolve relevant disputes” and may not rely on inadmissible evidence. *Federal Maritime Commission v. Aktiebolaget Svenska Amerika Linien*, 390 U.S. 238, 246 (1968) (requiring substantial evidence for agency findings). Here, the court accepted Dr. McClave’s expert report without assessing its admissibility under *Daubert* standards. The report contained unsupported assumptions and failed to account for alternative causes of price increases. *See Kiefer-Stewart Co. v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 211, 213 (1951) (requiring proof of concerted action, not mere speculation). By certifying the class on the basis of such evidence, the District Court abused its discretion.

**V. Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
