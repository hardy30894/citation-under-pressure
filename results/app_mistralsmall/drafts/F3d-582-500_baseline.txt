**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Erdman Failed to Accumulate Sufficient Hours to Qualify for FMLA Leave.**

A. **The FMLA’s Eligibility Standard Requires Only That an Employee Work 1,250 Hours in the Twelve-Month Period Preceding the Request for Leave.**

The FMLA defines an “eligible employee” as one who has been employed by the employer for at least 12 months and who has worked at least 1,250 hours during the 12-month period immediately preceding the commencement of the leave. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(i)-(ii). The statute does not condition eligibility on the manner in which those hours are accrued or compensated. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c) (“An employee does not meet the hours of service requirement if the employee has not worked 1,250 hours during the 12-month period immediately preceding the date when FMLA leave is to commence.”). The Third Circuit has not addressed whether extra hours worked beyond an employee’s scheduled schedule may be counted toward the 1,250-hour threshold. However, the Department of Labor’s regulations and the decisions of other circuits make clear that such hours are compensable and count toward eligibility.

In *Hodgens v. General Dynamics Corp.*, 144 F.3d 151 (1st Cir. 1998), the First Circuit held that an employee’s unapproved overtime hours could be counted toward the 1,250-hour requirement where the employer had actual or constructive knowledge of the hours worked. *Id.* at 170-71. The court reasoned that the FMLA’s purpose—to balance the demands of the workplace with the needs of families—would be undermined if employers could avoid liability by ignoring or disavowing knowledge of extra hours worked by employees. *Id.* at 171. *See also* 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c) (hours worked need not be “actually counted” by the employer to be counted toward eligibility).

Here, the record demonstrates that Nationwide had actual knowledge of Erdman’s extra hours. Her supervisor, Patty Sarno, consistently authorized payment for or “comp” time for these hours, and Erdman’s subsequent supervisor, Stella Getgen, was aware of the practice by at least January 2003. Getgen’s January 28, 2003 email admonishing Erdman for unapproved overtime implicitly acknowledged that such hours had been worked and were compensable. *See* App. 123 (“Erdman’s overtime was unapproved”). Thus, Nationwide cannot now disclaim knowledge of Erdman’s hours to defeat her FMLA eligibility.

B. **The District Court’s Reliance on *McGregor v. AutoZone, Inc.*, 180 F. Supp. 2d 566 (D.N.J. 2001), Was Misplaced.**

The District Court cited *McGregor* for the proposition that unapproved overtime cannot count toward the 1,250-hour requirement. *McGregor*, however, is not binding precedent and is distinguishable. In *McGregor*, the court held that an employee who worked unapproved overtime could not establish eligibility where the employer had no knowledge of the hours worked. *Id.* at 572. Here, by contrast, Nationwide had actual knowledge of Erdman’s extra hours and even compensated her for them. *McGregor* does not stand for the broader proposition that unapproved overtime can never count toward eligibility when the employer is aware of the hours worked.

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Interference Claim.**

A. **An Employer Interferes With an Employee’s FMLA Rights When It Denies or Discourages the Exercise of Those Rights.**

The FMLA prohibits employers from “interfer[ing] with, restrain[ing], or deny[ing] the exercise of or the attempt to exercise” any FMLA right. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1). The Third Circuit has not yet addressed whether an employer’s denial of FMLA leave based on an erroneous eligibility determination constitutes interference. However, the Department of Labor’s regulations provide that an employer may not “use the taking of FMLA leave as a negative factor in employment actions.” 29 C.F.R. § 825.220(c). *See also* *Cobb v. Contract Transport, Inc.*, 452 F.3d 543, 551 (6th Cir. 2006) (employer interfered with FMLA rights by denying leave based on an incorrect eligibility determination).

Here, Nationwide terminated Erdman’s employment shortly after she requested FMLA leave. A reasonable jury could infer that Nationwide’s stated reasons for termination were pretextual and that Erdman was fired in retaliation for requesting FMLA leave. *See* *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000) (a plaintiff may prove pretext by showing that the employer’s proffered reasons are unworthy of credence).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
