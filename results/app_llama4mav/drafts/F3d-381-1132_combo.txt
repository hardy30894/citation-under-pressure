ARGUMENT

I. THE COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT TO THE GOVERNMENT BECAUSE THE BPA'S INSTALLATION OF EXCESS FIBER OPTIC CAPACITY AND LEASING IT TO THIRD PARTIES CONSTITUTES A PHYSICAL TAKING OF TUTHILL'S PROPERTY.

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the government from taking private property without just compensation. U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical taking occurs when the government physically occupies or invades private property. See United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261-62 (1946); Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 327, 329-30 (1922). The government's actions in this case constitute a physical taking because the BPA's installation of excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties exceeds the scope of the easement and burdens Tuthill's property.

The scope of the easement is crucial in determining whether the government's actions constitute a taking. See United States v. Welch, 217 U.S. 333, 339 (1910). The easement granted to the BPA in 1953 allowed for "signal lines... necessary in connection therewith." The BPA's installation of fiber optic cables with excess capacity far beyond its current needs, and leasing the excess to third parties, is not "necessary in connection" with the transmission of electrical power. See Western Union Tel. Co. v. Pennsylvania R. Co., 195 U.S. 540, 570-71 (1904) (holding that a telegraph company's use of a railroad's right-of-way for its own purposes was not within the scope of the easement).

The government's argument that the excess capacity is for future needs is not supported by the law. See Northern Transp. Co. of Ohio v. Chicago, 99 U.S. 635, 642 (1878) (holding that a taking occurs when the government's actions interfere with the owner's use and enjoyment of the property). The fact that the BPA may need the excess capacity in the future does not justify the current burden on Tuthill's property. See Lyon v. McDonald, 117 U.S. 211, 215-16 (1886) (holding that a taking occurs when the government's actions impose a new burden on the property).

The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the scope of the easement is not relevant to the takings analysis. See United States v. General Motors Corp., 323 U.S. 373, 378 (1945) (holding that the government's invasion of private property constitutes a taking, regardless of the intent or purpose behind the invasion). The government's actions in this case are a clear example of a physical taking, as they have physically occupied Tuthill's property with excess fiber optic capacity.

Furthermore, the government's leasing of the excess capacity to third parties is a separate and distinct taking. See Peabody v. United States, 231 U.S. 530, 538-39 (1913) (holding that the government's occupation of private property for a public use constitutes a taking). The government's argument that the leasing does not increase the burden on Tuthill's property is without merit. See Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co., 260 U.S. at 329-30 (holding that a taking occurs when the government's actions interfere with the owner's use and enjoyment of the property).

In conclusion, the government's actions in installing excess fiber optic capacity and leasing it to third parties constitute a physical taking of Tuthill's property. The Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
