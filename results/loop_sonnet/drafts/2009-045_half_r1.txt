# ARGUMENT

## I. Equity Has Long Tied Attorney Compensation to the Quality of Representation and the Results Achieved

The premise underlying petitioners' position—that a "reasonable" fee is calculated solely by multiplying hours by a market rate—cannot be reconciled with nearly a century of this Court's equitable fee jurisprudence. Long before Congress enacted modern fee-shifting statutes, this Court recognized that compensation for counsel who create or protect a benefit for others must account for more than clock time.

In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), the Court approved compensating an attorney out of a common fund because his exertions produced a benefit for a class of beneficiaries, establishing that the value of legal services is measured by what counsel actually accomplished, not merely by the hours logged. This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), holding that an attorney's entitlement to compensation from a fund turns on the benefit conferred through his efforts. The Court applied the same logic in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), where it scrutinized a fee award against the results the litigation produced for the fund's beneficiaries, and again in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), where the Court recognized the equitable authority of federal courts to allow an attorney reimbursement out of a fund created or preserved by his efforts, calibrating that allowance to the value of the work performed rather than to a mechanical accounting of hours. And in *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago*, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), the Court examined the reorganization estate's benefit and the caliber of the legal work performed in assessing whether the fee awarded was fair.

These cases establish a settled principle: from the earliest exercises of federal equity jurisdiction over fee awards, courts have treated the *quality* of an attorney's performance and the *result* obtained as legitimate—sometimes decisive—components of a reasonable fee. Petitioners ask this Court to discard that tradition in favor of a rigid formula that equity never employed even in the fund cases from which the fee-shifting statutes descend.

## II. Congress Enacted Fee-Shifting Statutes to Secure Counsel Capable of Producing Exceptional Results, Not Merely to Reimburse Hours

Congress did not write § 1988 on a blank slate; it legislated against the equitable backdrop described above, with the specific goal of ensuring that civil rights plaintiffs could obtain counsel able to litigate complex, resource-intensive cases to a meaningful conclusion. This Court's decision in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400 (1968), construing an analogous fee-shifting provision in Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, confirms that purpose. The Court explained that "if successful plaintiffs were routinely forced to bear their own attorneys' fees, few aggrieved parties would be in a position to advance the public interest by invoking the injunctive powers of the federal courts." *Id.* at 402. That reasoning presupposes that fee awards must be adequate not merely to reimburse time spent, but to make possible the kind of skilled, sustained advocacy that actually vindicates the statutory right—precisely the type of advocacy the district court found here.

*Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714 (1967), reinforces the point from the other direction. This Court there declined to fashion a fee-shifting remedy absent statutory authorization, emphasizing that Congress deliberately chooses when and how to displace the American Rule to serve particular statutory ends. *Id.* at 719–20. Where, as in § 1988, Congress has made that choice specifically to enable enforcement of civil rights, courts must give that choice practical effect—including, in the rare case, through an enhancement that reflects extraordinary performance and results that the raw lodestar cannot capture.

## III. The District Court's Assessment of Counsel's Performance Warrants the Deference Traditionally Accorded to the Tribunal Closest to the Evidence

No court is better positioned than the district court to judge whether counsel's performance and the result obtained were exceptional. This Court has long recognized that the tribunal that observes the proceedings firsthand deserves deference precisely because appellate judges cannot recreate that vantage point from a paper record. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court held that a factual determination should be disturbed only when the reviewing court, examining the entire record, "is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Id.* at 395. That standard, born of the recognition that trial-level observation cannot be duplicated on appeal, applies with equal force to a district judge's assessment—informed by years presiding over this litigation—of whether counsel's advocacy and the settlement achieved were extraordinary.

## IV. This Is the Rare Case in Which Enhancement Was Warranted

The district court did not rely on generalized praise. It made detailed, record-based findings—concerning the magnitude of Georgia's foster-care crisis, the extraordinary relief obtained, and counsel's singular commitment—before concluding that the lodestar could not capture the value of what was accomplished. Consistent with the equitable principles reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, *Sprague*, and *Woods*, and the statutory purpose confirmed in *Piggie Park*, the enhancement should be affirmed.
