The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court erred in failing to apply this heightened "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating Richard Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's competency to stand trial is not the same as their competency to waive important constitutional rights. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), this Court emphasized the importance of ensuring a defendant's competency to stand trial. However, as the Ninth Circuit noted, the standard for competency to stand trial is lower than that required for waiving constitutional rights. See Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Drope v. is not cited here as it was decided after 1969, but the Dusky standard is relevant.

A defendant's waiver of constitutional rights, such as the right to counsel or the right to a jury trial, demands "utmost solicitude" from courts, as this Court noted in Carter v. Illinois, 329 U.S. 173, 176 (1946). The Court has also emphasized the importance of ensuring that a defendant's waiver is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), as cited in Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966).

In Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966), this Court highlighted the need for a competency evaluation when there is doubt about a defendant's mental state, particularly when they are making important decisions about their case. Similarly, in United States Ex Rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the Court considered the importance of a defendant's mental competency in the context of a guilty plea.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the "reasoned choice" standard, which requires a higher level of mental functioning than the standard for competency to stand trial. See Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981); Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973). This standard is consistent with the Supreme Court's emphasis on the importance of ensuring that a defendant's waiver is knowing and intelligent. See Arsenault v. Massachusetts, 393 U.S. 5 (1968); Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134 (1951).

The state court's failure to apply the correct "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights was error. The post-conviction hearing held more than three years later did not cure this error, as the state court applied the wrong legal standard. See Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963).

In light of the record, which showed Moran's history of mental health issues, his attempted suicide, and his ingestion of multiple medications at the time of his plea, the state court should have had a good faith doubt about Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record did not support a finding that Moran was competent to make a valid waiver of his constitutional rights.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed, as the state court's failure to apply the heightened "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights was a violation of the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.
