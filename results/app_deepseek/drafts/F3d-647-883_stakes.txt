## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Buckles’s § 2255 Motion as Untimely Because His Certiorari Petition Was Timely

The 90-day period for filing a petition for certiorari runs from “the date of the denial of rehearing” or “the date of the entry of the judgment.” Sup. Ct. R. 13(1), (3). When this Court recalled the mandate on June 12, 2007, it vacated the prior finality of the judgment. *See United States v. Foumai*, 910 F.2d 617, 620 (9th Cir. 1990) (recall of mandate “vacates the prior judgment” and restores the appeal to the court’s active docket). The mandate did not reissue until June 20, 2007. Until that date, no final judgment existed from which certiorari could be sought. *See Budinich v. Becton Dickinson & Co.*, 486 U.S. 196, 202 (1988) (finality requires a “decision that ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment”). Because the judgment was not final until June 20, the 90-day certiorari period began on that date and expired on September 18, 2007. Buckles filed his petition on September 5, 2007—well within that window. The Supreme Court Clerk’s contrary calculation, which ignored the recall, was erroneous. Accordingly, Buckles’s certiorari petition was timely, and his § 2255 motion—filed within one year of the denial of certiorari—was also timely. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1).

### II. Alternatively, Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling

Even if the certiorari petition was untimely, the district court should have equitably tolled the limitations period. Equitable tolling is appropriate when a petitioner shows “(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way.” *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005). Buckles satisfies both prongs.

**A. Extraordinary Circumstances**

First, the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office provided misleading information. On June 4, 2007, Buckles’s sister telephoned the Clerk’s office and was told that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” Buckles reasonably relied on this statement to believe his certiorari deadline ran from the new mandate date. Misleading advice from a court clerk constitutes an extraordinary circumstance warranting equitable tolling. *See United States v. Witherspoon*, 231 F.3d 923, 926–27 (9th Cir. 2000) (equitable tolling where court clerk gave incorrect filing deadline). The government’s own agent created the confusion, and Buckles should not bear the consequences.

Second, Buckles’s retained counsel abandoned him. Counsel failed to assist in filing a motion for appointment of counsel as required by Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), despite Buckles’s repeated requests. Attorney abandonment that leaves a prisoner without representation during a critical period is an extraordinary circumstance. *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 652 (2010) (attorney’s “serious misconduct” can justify equitable tolling). Buckles did everything he could: he wrote counsel, telephoned, and ultimately filed a pro se motion. Counsel’s inaction was beyond Buckles’s control.

**B. Reasonable Diligence**

Buckles acted diligently. He contacted counsel on the 21st day after denial of rehearing, forwarded a copy to the Clerk, filed a pro se motion when counsel failed to act, and promptly filed his certiorari petition after the mandate reissued. He also sought clarification from the Supreme Court Clerk. This conduct demonstrates the “reasonable diligence” required. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 653. The district court erred in concluding otherwise.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, the district court’s dismissal of Buckles’s § 2255 motion as untimely should be reversed.
