# ARGUMENT

## I. THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR TITLE NAVIGABILITY REQUIRES A SEGMENT-BY-SEGMENT DETERMINATION OF NAVIGABILITY AT THE TIME OF STATEHOOD

The equal-footing doctrine holds that upon admission to the Union, each new State acquired title to the beds of all waters within its borders that were navigable in fact at the time of statehood. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845). This Court has consistently reaffirmed that the touchstone of title navigability is navigability *at the time the State entered the Union*, not at some later or earlier date. *Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324 (1876); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931). In *Utah*, this Court explained that "[t]he question of navigability is thus determined according to the condition of the waterway at the time in question," making clear that the relevant temporal inquiry is fixed by the date of statehood itself. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82.

The test for navigability in fact was articulated in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1871), where this Court held that rivers are navigable in fact "when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *Id.* at 563. This capacity-based test necessarily requires an inquiry into the condition of the waterway at the relevant historical moment—statehood—because a river's susceptibility to commercial use is not static but depends on its physical characteristics as they existed at that time. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76.

This Court in *Utah* did not merely permit a segment-by-segment approach; it required one. Faced with a lengthy river system, the Court examined navigability with respect to "long stretches" separately, holding certain reaches navigable and others not, based on the particular physical characteristics of each stretch. *Id.* at 77-87. The Court's methodology confirms that navigability for title purposes cannot be assessed for an entire river as a unitary whole, nor can it be assessed without reference to the conditions prevailing at statehood. Rather, courts must examine the river "according to its capacity in its natural state" for the specific segment at issue. *Id.* at 87.

This principle finds further support in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), where the Court examined discrete portions of the Red River to determine navigability, recognizing that a single river may possess different navigability characteristics along its length. The Court's approach in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), likewise demonstrates that navigability determinations must account for the specific physical conditions of the waterway segment in question, evaluated at the pertinent historical juncture.

The temporal fixity of the statehood-navigability test is not a mere formality; it flows directly from the equal-footing doctrine's constitutional foundation. As this Court held in *Pollard v. Hagan*, the right to the soil under navigable waters passed to the States "as an incident to the sovereignty" they acquired at admission. 44 U.S. at 230. That sovereign incident vested at the moment of statehood and cannot be altered by subsequent developments—whether the construction of dams, changes in river use, or later commercial activity. *Scott v. Lattig*, 227 U.S. 229 (1913) (holding that title to riverbeds vests at statehood based on conditions then existing, unaffected by later accretion or avulsion affecting navigability). To permit a court to consider post-statehood conditions—including the very hydroelectric development at issue in this case—would allow private parties to affect title to sovereign lands through their own actions, a result flatly inconsistent with the constitutional character of the equal-footing doctrine as recognized in *Martin v. Waddell's Lessee*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 367 (1842).

The lower court's determination that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889 was thus not only permissible but constitutionally mandated. The trial court properly examined historical evidence—including accounts of fur trade navigation, steamboat commerce, and log floating—to reconstruct the condition of these rivers as they existed in 1889, precisely as this Court's precedents require. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82 (relying on historical accounts of river use to establish navigability at statehood).

## II. THE HISTORICAL EVIDENCE PROPERLY CONSIDERED BY THE TRIAL COURT SATISFIES THE STATEHOOD-ERA NAVIGABILITY STANDARD

This Court has long recognized that navigability determinations of necessity depend upon historical evidence, since the relevant inquiry concerns conditions predating current litigation by decades or centuries. In *The Montello*, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430 (1874), this Court examined historical records of fur trade and exploration to determine whether the Fox River was navigable, explaining that "the capability of use by the public for purposes of transportation and commerce affords the true criterion of the navigability of a river, rather than the extent and manner of that use." *Id.* at 441-42. The Court did not require contemporaneous eyewitness testimony but relied upon historical accounts, treatises, and records of early exploration.

Similarly, in *United States v. Utah*, this Court credited historical evidence of fur trapper navigation, early steamboat voyages, and other nineteenth-century commercial activity to establish navigability as of Utah's admission to the Union in 1896—more than three decades after the litigation commenced. 283 U.S. at 82-86. The Court's reliance on such evidence confirms that the statehood-navigability test is inherently retrospective and historical in character, and that courts must necessarily look to documentary and testimonial evidence of past conditions rather than contemporary observation.

The trial court's reliance on historical societies' records, contemporaneous newspaper accounts, and government surveys concerning the condition of Montana's rivers in 1889 was entirely consistent with this settled methodology.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
