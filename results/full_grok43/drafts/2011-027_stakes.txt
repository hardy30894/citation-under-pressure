I. The Navigability-for-Title Test Requires a Determination of Navigability at the Time of Statehood.

Under the equal-footing doctrine, “the title to the beds of navigable waters” passes to a new State “upon its admission to the Union.” *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). The constitutional test therefore “is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). Navigability in fact exists when the waters “are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). Because title vests or fails to vest at the moment of statehood, evidence of later improvements or modern use is irrelevant unless it demonstrates susceptibility at that earlier date. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926).

The inquiry proceeds on a segment-by-segment basis. A court must examine “the particular segments of the river” whose beds are claimed, rather than the river as a whole. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 77. This rule follows directly from the principle that title to submerged lands is determined by the condition of each discrete reach at statehood. *See Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26–27 (1894).

The Montana Supreme Court applied precisely this framework. It required the State to prove, through contemporaneous historical evidence, that the relevant reaches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in fact in 1889. The trial court’s findings rested on that evidence and satisfied the segment-specific, statehood-date standard mandated by *The Daniel Ball* and *United States v. Utah*. No other temporal or geographic test satisfies the equal-footing guarantee.
