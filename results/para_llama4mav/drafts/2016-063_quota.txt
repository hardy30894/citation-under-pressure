ARGUMENT

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction to review the Merit Systems Protection Board’s dismissal of Anthony Perry’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. Instead, judicial review of the Board’s decision lies in a federal district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 (CSRA) governs judicial review of MSPB decisions. 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq. As a general matter, review of Board decisions lies in the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, an exception to that rule applies to "[c]ases of discrimination," as to which review lies in federal district court. Id. § 7703(b)(2). The statute defines those cases as ones in which an employee "(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal laws. Id. § 7702(a)(1).

In Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The Court's decision in Kloeckner effectively overruled the D.C. Circuit's prior holding in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which had drawn a distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. See Harms v. IRS, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007-08 (10th Cir. 2003); Downey v. Runyon, 160 F.3d 139, 145-46 (2d Cir. 1998).

The Kloeckner Court's reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals. The statute's definition of a mixed case turns on whether the employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), not on whether the Board ultimately decides to hear the appeal. See Kloeckner, 133 S.Ct. at 604. In both procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, the employee alleges discrimination and seeks review of an adverse employment action. See Garcia v. Dep't of Homeland Sec., 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006).

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's own precedent suggests that Kloeckner's holding should be extended to jurisdictional dismissals. In Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), the court acknowledged that the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals may not be meaningful in the context of mixed cases. Id. at 1118 n.1; see also id. at 1121-22 (dissenting opinion).

Other circuits have also recognized that the Kloeckner decision undermines the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. See, e.g., Perry v. MSPB, 582 F. App'x 122, 124 n.2 (3d Cir. 2014); see also Brunson v. Nicholson, 491 F. App'x 977, 979 (11th Cir. 2012). The Ninth Circuit has held that Kloeckner's reasoning applies to jurisdictional dismissals, and that review of such decisions lies in district court. See Sloan v. West, 140 F.3d 1255, 1261-62 (9th Cir. 1998) (pre-Kloeckner); see also Elmore v. Dep't of Transp., 421 F. App'x 634, 636 (9th Cir. 2010) (applying Kloeckner to a jurisdictional dismissal).

The Supreme Court's decision in Elgin v. Department of Treasury, 567 U.S. 1 (2012), also supports the conclusion that Kloeckner's holding should be extended to jurisdictional dismissals. In Elgin, the Court held that the CSRA's review provisions are not limited to cases in which the Board has reached the merits of the employee's claim. Id. at 15-16. Rather, the Court emphasized that the statute's "comprehensive framework" for reviewing adverse employment actions applies to all cases brought under the CSRA, regardless of whether the Board ultimately decides to hear the appeal. Id. at 11-12; see also United States v. Fausto, 484 U.S. 439, 445 (1988).

In light of Kloeckner and the other authorities cited above, the D.C. Circuit's decision in this case should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to a federal district court for further proceedings. See Williams v. Dep't of Army, 715 F.2d 1485, 1491 (Fed. Cir. 1983) (en banc); Ballard v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 768 F.2d 756, 757 (6th Cir. 1985). The Federal Circuit's lack of jurisdiction over this case is consistent with the principles of judicial review under the CSRA and the Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner.
