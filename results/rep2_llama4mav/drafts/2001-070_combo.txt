The execution of Daryl Renard Atkins, who is mentally retarded, does not constitute "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that his mental retardation precludes a death sentence.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is rooted in the English Bill of Rights of 1689, which was adopted by the American colonies and later incorporated into the United States Constitution. As the Supreme Court noted in Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910), the Eighth Amendment's language is "taken from the Bill of Rights of 1688" and is intended to prohibit punishments that are "cruel and unusual."

The concept of "cruel and unusual punishment" has been interpreted by courts to prohibit punishments that are inhumane, barbaric, or excessively harsh. In Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that involve "torture" or "unnecessary cruelty." Similarly, in In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), the Court stated that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual, involving torture or a lingering death."

The execution of mentally retarded individuals is not inherently cruel or unusual. As the Court noted in Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947), the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is not a static concept, but rather one that evolves with societal norms. However, at the time of Atkins' trial, there was no national consensus against executing mentally retarded individuals, as evidenced by the fact that many states did not prohibit such executions. See Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (noting that the Eighth Amendment's meaning is "not limited to the primitive measures used by our ancestors").

The penalty phase of Atkins' trial, during which his mental retardation was presented to the jury, demonstrates that the sentencing process took into account his individual circumstances. The jury's consideration of Atkins' mental retardation as a mitigating factor is consistent with the principles enunciated in Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949), which held that a sentencing jury may consider a wide range of evidence in determining an appropriate sentence.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the states have significant latitude in determining the appropriate punishment for crimes. As the Court noted in Malloy v. South Carolina, 237 U.S. 180, 185-86 (1915), "the States are free to determine their own policies" regarding punishment, subject only to the limitations imposed by the Eighth Amendment.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the execution of Daryl Renard Atkins does not constitute "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court's decision affirming Atkins' death sentence should be upheld.
