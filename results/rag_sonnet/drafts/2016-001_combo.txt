# ARGUMENT

The court of appeals allowed Apple to recover Samsung's entire profits on complete smartphones based on infringement of design patents covering only a handful of visual features—a rounded rectangular front face, a bezel, and a grid of colorful icons. That result cannot be reconciled with a century of this Court's patent-damages jurisprudence, which has consistently required that recovery be limited to the profits attributable to what the patentee actually invented.

## I. This Court Has Long Required Apportionment Between Patented and Unpatented Features

The foundational rule of patent damages is that a patentee may recover only the profits attributable to his invention, not profits attributable to features he did not invent. In *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480 (1854), this Court reversed a damages award for infringement of a single improvement to a reaping machine because the jury had been instructed "that the measure of damages for infringing a patented improvement on a machine in public use is the same as if the defendant had pirated the whole machine." The Court held that a patentee "given no evidence to show that he could have made and sold a single machine more than he did" could not recover as though the entire machine's profits flowed from his narrow improvement. *Id.*

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co.*, 141 U.S. 459 (1891), explaining that "in estimating the profits made by the infringer of a patent, the comparison must be between the patented invention and what was known and open to the public at and before the date of the patent"—not between the infringer's entire commercial product and nothing at all.

The rule reached its fullest articulation in *Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co.*, 225 U.S. 604 (1912). There the Court explained that where "the plaintiff's patent is only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits," the patentee "must, therefore, 'give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.'" *Id.* at 614 (quoting *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120). Only where the patentee proves "that the entire value of the whole machine, as a marketable article, is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature" may he recover unapportioned profits. *Id.* A smartphone's profits are not "properly and legally attributable" to a bezel design any more than a reaper's profits were attributable to a raker's seat in *Seymour*.

## II. This Court's Design-Patent Decisions Confirm That Apportionment Applies With Equal Force to Design Patents

Apple cannot escape this body of law by characterizing its patents as design patents rather than utility patents. This Court squarely confronted that argument—and rejected it—in the *Dobson* cases. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court held that a design-patent plaintiff could not recover "the entire profit from the manufacture and sale of the carpet, as profits or damages, including all the profits from the carding, spinning, dyeing and weaving," where there was "no evidence as to" the value the design itself contributed. Because the plaintiff failed to distinguish the value of the ornamental design from the value of the underlying article, the Court permitted only nominal damages.

The following year, in *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), the Court reaffirmed that holding against the very argument Apple presses here—that design patents warrant categorically different treatment because "the result is patented" rather than "the means." The Court held that this distinction did not justify awarding "the entire profits or damages on the article containing the design," and that "only nominal damages should have been allowed" absent proof of the design's specific contribution. *Id.* Nothing in the intervening statutory history disturbs this holding; to the contrary, it confirms that a design patent claiming a component of a multicomponent product entitles the patentee only to the profits attributable to that component, precisely as with any other patent.

## III. Analogous Intellectual-Property Doctrine Confirms the Apportionment Requirement

This Court's copyright decisions independently confirm that unapportioned "total profits" awards are disfavored whenever an infringing work combines protected and unprotected elements. In *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390 (1940), the Court held that a copyright owner is entitled "only that part of the profits found to be attributable to the use of the copyrighted material as distinguished from what the infringer himself has supplied." The Court reversed an award of the infringer's entire net profits from a motion picture, even though the infringing material had been essential to the film's storyline, because the infringer had also contributed substantial value of its own. *Id.* The same logic applies with even greater force here, where the patented designs are but a handful of aesthetic features on a complex multifunction device comprising thousands of unclaimed components and technologies. See also *F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Contemporary Arts, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 228 (1952) (recognizing that Congress structured recovery to reflect actual injury rather than windfall).

## Conclusion

For these reasons, damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a multicomponent product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component. The judgment below should be reversed.
