The Sixth Circuit's decision in Edwards v. Carpenter is flawed because it permits a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. This Court should reverse.

The doctrine of procedural default is well-established, and a habeas petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice to overcome a procedural default. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). In this case, Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was procedurally defaulted due to his appellate counsel's failure to raise it on direct appeal.

Carpenter argues that his ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency claim. However, the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim itself was procedurally defaulted. Allowing a procedurally defaulted claim to serve as cause would undermine the doctrine of procedural default.

This Court has long recognized that a claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot serve as cause to excuse another procedural default. See Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 482-83 (1953) (opinion of Reed, J.). In Daniels, the Court held that a petitioner's failure to comply with state procedural rules barred federal habeas review, even if the petitioner alleged that his counsel's ineffectiveness was the cause of the default.

Similarly, in Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953) (opinion of Reed, J.), the Court held that a habeas petitioner must exhaust state remedies before seeking federal review. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to serve as cause would permit petitioners to circumvent this exhaustion requirement.

The Sixth Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with this Court's precedent on the relationship between ineffective assistance of counsel and procedural default. In Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944), the Court held that a habeas petitioner must demonstrate that his counsel's ineffectiveness was not merely a tactical decision, but rather a fundamental error that prejudiced the petitioner.

Furthermore, in Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950), the Court emphasized the importance of complying with state procedural rules in order to preserve federal habeas claims. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to serve as cause would undermine this principle.

Additionally, the Court has held that a petitioner's failure to raise a claim on direct appeal can result in procedural default. See Parker v. Illinois, 333 U.S. 571, 574-75 (1948). In this case, Carpenter's failure to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal resulted in procedural default.

The Court has also recognized that a habeas petitioner's claims must be supported by a sufficient factual record. See Hawk v. Olson, 326 U.S. 271, 277-78 (1945). Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim is not supported by a sufficient factual record, and therefore cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency claim.

Moreover, in Jennings v. Illinois, 342 U.S. 104, 110-11 (1951), the Court held that a habeas petitioner must demonstrate that his state proceedings were fundamentally unfair in order to obtain federal relief. Carpenter has not made such a showing.

Finally, the Court has long recognized that the doctrine of procedural default is essential to maintaining the integrity of the state and federal judicial systems. See Ex parte Spencer, 228 U.S. 652, 660-61 (1913). Allowing a procedurally defaulted claim to serve as cause would undermine this integrity.

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit's decision and hold that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim.
