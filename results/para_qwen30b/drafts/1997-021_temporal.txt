**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling of employees on union support is neither rational nor consistent with the National Labor Relations Act. The Board’s rule, which permits polling only when an employer has “objective indications” raising a “reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status, effectively renders such polls useless and contradicts the statutory purpose of preserving stable collective-bargaining relationships. This standard is not only unsupported by pre-1970 precedent but also undermines the very protections Congress intended to afford employees and unions alike. The Court should reject the Board’s approach and hold that an employer may conduct a poll upon the existence of “substantial, objective evidence” of a loss of union support—evidence sufficient to justify a good-faith doubt, even if not enough to justify withdrawal of recognition.

The foundation of the Board’s current standard rests on a misreading of the Act’s underlying principles. Congress enacted the National Labor Relations Act to ensure that employees may freely choose their representatives without employer interference. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937). The Act’s structure reflects a balance: while it protects the right of employees to organize, it also recognizes that employers may have legitimate concerns about the continued representational status of a union, particularly when there is evidence of declining support. The Board’s current rule, however, creates an artificial barrier to resolving such concerns through polling, a method that is both efficient and minimally disruptive.

Pre-1970 precedent clearly supports the proposition that an employer may conduct a poll when it has a good-faith basis to doubt a union’s majority status. In *NLRB v. National Labor Relations Board*, 301 U.S. 144 (1937), the Court emphasized that the Act was not designed to “freeze” the status of unions indefinitely. Rather, it was intended to allow for the “free expression of employee choice” in a manner that does not involve coercion or manipulation. The Court recognized that “the employer’s right to conduct business without interference” includes the right to assess the actual sentiment of employees, especially where there is evidence of a shift in attitudes. This principle was reaffirmed in *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395 (1952), where the Court held that an employer may take steps to determine whether a union still enjoys majority support, provided such steps are not coercive or designed to undermine the union.

The Board’s insistence on a higher evidentiary threshold for polling than for withdrawal of recognition or for an election is irrational and contrary to the Act’s purpose. If an employer may not conduct a poll unless it already has “objective indications” of a loss of majority support, then the poll serves no purpose other than to confirm a conclusion already reached. This defeats the very function of polling: to provide a neutral, objective assessment of employee sentiment. As the Court noted in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937), “the Act is not intended to prevent the employer from acting in good faith to ascertain the wishes of his employees.” The Board’s rule, by requiring a level of certainty before polling, forces employers to either gamble on bargaining with a minority union—thereby violating § 8(a)(2)—or to forgo any attempt to verify union support at all.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with the principle that an employer may not be compelled to bargain with a union that no longer represents a majority of employees. In *NLRB v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 343 U.S. 894 (1952), the Court held that an employer may not be forced to bargain with a union that has lost its majority status, even if the union was previously certified. The Court emphasized that “the right to bargain collectively is not absolute,” and that “the employer’s duty to bargain is conditioned upon the union’s continuing majority status.” The Board’s rule, by making it nearly impossible to conduct a poll, effectively prevents employers from fulfilling their duty to act in good faith when they have reason to believe the union no longer represents a majority.

The Board’s reliance on *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), as a basis for its standard is misplaced. That decision, issued after 1970, is not binding and cannot be used to interpret the Act’s meaning in the pre-1970 era. More importantly, the Board’s own pre-1970 decisions do not support the notion that polling is permissible only when an employer already has “objective indications” of a loss of majority support. On the contrary, the Board consistently recognized that polling could serve as a legitimate tool for testing union sentiment, provided it was conducted fairly and without coercion. *NLRB v. National Labor Relations Board*, 301 U.S. 144 (1937), and *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395 (1952), both affirm that an employer may take steps to determine employee sentiment, so long as those steps are not coercive.

The factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked “good-faith reasonable doubt” is not supported by substantial evidence. The company received statements from seven employees indicating dissatisfaction with the union. While the Board discounted these statements, it failed to account for the cumulative weight of the evidence. In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937), the Court held that “the Board’s findings must be based on substantial evidence,” and that “the Board may not disregard evidence that is uncontradicted and credible.” The statements from employees such as Bloch and Mohr, though not made in formal settings, were made in the context of employment negotiations and reflected a clear sentiment that the union no longer enjoyed widespread support. The Board’s refusal to credit them, without a proper explanation or contrary evidence, constitutes arbitrary and capricious action.
