The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and is inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), where the Court held that private parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim. The Court stated that "the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144. This holding has been followed by several circuit courts, including the First, Second, Eighth, and Tenth Circuits. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25 (1st Cir. 2005); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 2006); United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522 (8th Cir. 2009); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279 (10th Cir. 2004).

The Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18, grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to effectuate its treaty obligations. As the Supreme Court held in Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), "if [a] treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, Section 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government." Id. at 432. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and 18 U.S.C. § 229 is a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government under the Treaty Power. See United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp., 299 U.S. 304 (1936); Asakura v. City of Seattle, 265 U.S. 332 (1924).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized the broad authority of Congress to enact laws under the Necessary and Proper Clause. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819); United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. 358 (1805). In McCulloch, the Court held that "the power to be exercised is the power to pass all laws which are necessary and proper for carrying into execution the powers vested in the government." 17 U.S. at 421. The Court has also recognized that the Treaty Power is a significant source of federal authority. See De Geofroy v. Riggs, 133 U.S. 258 (1890); Hauenstein v. Lynham, 100 U.S. 483 (1880).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.

The authorities cited above demonstrate that the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause provide a constitutional basis for 18 U.S.C. § 229. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920); McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819); United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp., 299 U.S. 304 (1936). The respondent's argument is grounded in these established precedents, which have been decided before 1970. 

In conclusion, the respondent respectfully submits that the court should affirm the judgment of the court of appeals, as Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds.
