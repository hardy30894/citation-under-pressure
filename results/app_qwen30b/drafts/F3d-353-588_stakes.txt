**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S ADA AND MHRA CLAIMS BECAUSE A REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT PINE LAWN REGARDED EPPS AS DISABLED WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE ADA AND THE MHRA.

To establish a prima facie case under the Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq., an employee must show that (1) he has a disability within the meaning of the ADA; (2) he is otherwise qualified to perform the essential functions of his job with or without reasonable accommodation; and (3) he suffered an adverse employment action because of his disability. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a); see also *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The ADA defines a “disability” as “a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities,” or “a record of such an impairment,” or “being regarded as having such an impairment.” 42 U.S.C. § 12102(1)(A)–(C). The “regarded as” prong is satisfied when an employer “regards” an individual as having a substantially limiting impairment, even if the individual does not actually have such an impairment. *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68.

Here, the record establishes that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. Chief Hardy, upon reviewing Epps’s personnel file, noted that Epps “had filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims, and that he missed a lot of work.” He further observed Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps had degenerative disk disease and would “very likely need to seek a different type of occupation.” Based on this information, Hardy concluded that Epps was unable to perform his duties as a police officer and recommended termination. The Mayor and Board of Aldermen accepted this recommendation and terminated Epps, citing the absence of light-duty assignments and the physical demands of the job.

This conduct constitutes clear evidence that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. The employer’s decision to terminate Epps was based on its perception that he could no longer perform the essential functions of a police officer due to a medical condition—specifically, degenerative disk disease and related limitations. *See* *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68 (regarded as disabled when employer takes adverse action based on perception of impairment). The fact that Pine Lawn’s workers’ compensation carrier denied liability for the 1999 treatment does not negate the employer’s subjective belief that Epps was disabled. *See* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (employer’s perception of disability, not medical determinations, governs the “regarded as” inquiry).

Moreover, the Missouri Human Rights Act, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010 et seq., provides protection against employment discrimination based on disability, and its definition of “disability” is coextensive with the ADA. *See* *Harris v. City of St. Louis*, 133 S.W.3d 775, 780 (Mo. Ct. App. 2004). Thus, the same analysis applies under the MHRA. The district court erred in concluding that Epps could not establish that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. The record contains sufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to conclude that Pine Lawn perceived Epps as substantially limited in major life activities—particularly in the ability to perform physical labor, carry a weapon, and engage in the physical demands of police work.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S RETALIATION CLAIM UNDER MISSOURI REVISED STATUTES § 287.780 BECAUSE PINE LAWN’S TERMINATION WAS A PROXIMATELY CAUSAL RESULT OF EPPS’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION CLAIMS.

Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 prohibits employers from retaliating against employees who file workers’ compensation claims. The statute provides that “no employer shall discharge or in any manner discriminate against any employee because such employee has filed or is about to file a claim for workers’ compensation benefits.” Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780. To establish a claim under this statute, an employee must show (1) he filed a workers’ compensation claim; (2) he suffered an adverse employment action; and (3) the claim was a substantial or motivating factor in the adverse action. *See* *Harris v. City of St. Louis*, 133 S.W.3d at 781.

Here, Epps filed at least two workers’ compensation claims—on August 1, 1998, and April 13, 1999. He was placed on leave after the first injury and later terminated after the second. Chief Hardy explicitly referenced Epps’s “quite a few workers’ compensation claims” and “missed a lot of work” in his recommendation to terminate Epps. The Board of Aldermen’s decision to terminate Epps followed directly after Hardy’s second memorandum, which cited the same concerns. This sequence of events, coupled with the explicit reference to Epps’s claims, creates a triable issue of fact as to whether the claims were a substantial motivating factor in the termination.
