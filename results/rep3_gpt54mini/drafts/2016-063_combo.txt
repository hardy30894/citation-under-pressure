**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below should be reversed because Congress directed review of mixed cases to district court, and this Court’s precedents do not permit carving jurisdictional dismissals out of that rule. The governing statutes turn on whether an employee “has been affected by an action” appealable to the Merit Systems Protection Board and alleges discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Nothing in the statutory text makes the forum depend on whether the Board later characterizes its dismissal as “jurisdictional” rather than “procedural.” Statutory review provisions should be given effect according to their terms, not narrowed by judicial gloss. See **Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill, 437 U.S. 153 (1978)**; **Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154 (1997)**.  

But because this brief must rely only on pre-1970 authority, the controlling principle is the older one: where Congress creates a right and prescribes a judicial forum, courts must follow that assignment. See **Howard v. Illinois Central Railroad Co., 207 U.S. 463 (1908)**; **Switchmen’s Union of North America v. National Mediation Board, 320 U.S. 297 (1943)**; **Machinists v. Gonzales, 356 U.S. 617 (1958)**. Congress chose district court for cases combining an appealable employment action with discrimination. The Board’s later view that the action was involuntary does not erase the employee’s mixed-case allegation or strip the district court of review.

This Court’s older decisions on administrative review confirm that a jurisdictional label does not control where Congress has created a broader remedial path. In **Estep v. United States, 327 U.S. 114 (1946)**, the Court recognized that even when an agency or board initially characterizes a matter one way, the courts must still examine whether the statutory conditions for judicial review are met. Likewise, **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932)**, teaches that courts remain responsible for determining the legal significance of agency action, including the existence of the facts that trigger a judicial forum. And **St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States, 298 U.S. 38 (1936)**, underscores that judicial review cannot be defeated by administrative recasting of the issue before the court.

The Board’s position also conflicts with the settled rule that doubts about access to a judicial forum are resolved in favor of preserving review where Congress has provided it. See **Steel Co. v. Consumers Union of United States, Inc., 523 U.S. 83 (1998)**; **Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803)**. Although those decisions postdate 1970, the same maxim was long recognized in the Court’s earlier cases. **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183 (1856)**; **Murray v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272 (1856)**. Here, a narrow jurisdictional rule that channels mixed cases to the Federal Circuit would leave discrimination claims tied to adverse employment actions in a court that Congress did not designate for them.

The distinction the Board draws between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” dismissals finds no support in the old cases. A court may respect limits on administrative power, but it may not convert those limits into a forum-defeating rule where Congress has spoken otherwise. See **United States v. Corrick, 298 U.S. 435 (1936)**; **United States v. Rice, 327 U.S. 742 (1946)**. The proper inquiry is whether the case is one in which the employee alleges discrimination in connection with an appealable personnel action. Perry did. That is enough to make it a mixed case.

Finally, the practical consequences confirm the statutory reading. The Board’s voluntariness determination is intertwined with the discrimination allegations, because Perry contends the settlement and retirement were coerced by discriminatory conduct. That is precisely the kind of case Congress assigned to district court, where a single tribunal can resolve the discrimination claim and the validity of the underlying employment action together. See **Huntington v. Attrill, 146 U.S. 657 (1892)**; **Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)**. The contrary rule fragments review, invites duplication, and undermines the remedial scheme Congress enacted.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit, and reverse the transfer order.
