I. Neither the South Carolina distribution conviction nor the Georgia arson convictions qualify as ACCA or Guidelines predicates.

The categorical approach requires comparison of the elements of a prior conviction to the generic federal definition, without regard to the facts of the offense. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). When the statute is broader than the generic offense, it cannot serve as a predicate. Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013). A court may consult Shepard documents only to determine which statutory alternative formed the basis of the conviction. Shepard v. United States, 544 U.S. 13, 26 (2005). If the statute is divisible, Mathis v. United States, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2256 (2016), supplies the divisibility test.

Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine fails both the ACCA “serious drug offense” definition, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii), and the Guidelines “controlled substance offense” definition, USSG § 4B1.2(b). South Carolina law criminalizes distribution without requiring proof that the substance was a federally controlled substance at the time of the offense. See United States v. McKennon, 814 F.3d 205, 209 (4th Cir. 2016). In addition, the maximum penalty prescribed by South Carolina law does not invariably reach ten years. United States v. Simmons, 649 F.3d 237, 243 (4th Cir. 2011) (en banc). Because the statute is overbroad, the conviction cannot count under either enhancement. See United States v. Aparicio-Soria, 740 F.3d 152, 157 (4th Cir. 2014) (en banc).

Furlow’s 2008 Georgia first-degree arson convictions likewise do not qualify as “violent felonies” or “crimes of violence.” Both the ACCA and Guidelines enumerate “arson,” yet Georgia’s statute reaches conduct that does not satisfy the generic definition of arson, which requires a malicious or knowing burning of the property of another. See United States v. Knight, 606 F.3d 171, 174 (4th Cir. 2010). Georgia law permits conviction on a showing of reckless disregard, rendering the offense broader than generic arson. United States v. Chacon, 533 F.3d 942, 946 (8th Cir. 2008); see also Begay v. United States, 553 U.S. 137, 144-45 (2008) (requiring purposeful conduct for enumerated offenses). The residual clause cannot supply an alternative ground after Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591, 597 (2015). Because the Georgia statute is indivisible and overbroad, the convictions are not proper predicates. United States v. White, 836 F.3d 437, 443 (4th Cir. 2016).

Without three qualifying predicates, Furlow is not an armed career criminal under the ACCA and not a career offender under the Guidelines. The district court’s contrary rulings must be reversed.
