**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Protects Against Discrimination Against Definable Classes of Persons, Not Against Every Instance of Allegedly Disparate Treatment of a Single Individual.**

From its inception, this Court's equal protection jurisprudence has been anchored in the concept of *class*. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court held that "the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." *Id.* at 8. That formulation presupposes a set of persons "entitled to be treated alike"—a class—against which the plaintiff's treatment can be measured. Respondent's theory dispenses with that predicate altogether, asking a court to find "class of one" discrimination without any group against which to compare her treatment other than an ad hoc selection of other applicants she deems comparable.

This Court's oldest equal protection decisions confirm that the Clause was designed to check invidious *classifications*, not to police every discretionary decision that happens to fall unevenly on a single citizen. In *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), the Court explained that "class legislation, discriminating against some and favoring others, is prohibited," but that regulation "which, in carrying out a public purpose, is limited in its application, if within the sphere of its operation it affects alike all persons similarly situated, is not within the amendment." *Id.* at 32. Even *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), on which lower courts have relied to extend equal protection to selective administrative enforcement, involved discrimination against Chinese laundry operators as a class—persons denied permits "with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances, material to their rights." *Id.* at 373–374. The wrong condemned in *Yick Wo* was the systematic exclusion of an identifiable group from a benefit granted to all others similarly situated—not the denial of a benefit to one person measured against a shifting universe of supposed comparators.

Respondent alleged no membership in any class. She did not claim that the Village singled out a race, a religion, a viewpoint, or any other cognizable group to which she belonged. She claimed only that the Village demanded of her a larger easement than it demanded of certain unspecified other property owners. That is, at most, a claim that a municipal administrator exercised discretion imperfectly—precisely the kind of claim *Snowden* held insufficient absent proof of purposeful discrimination against a class. 321 U.S. at 8.

**II. Recognizing a "Class of One" Equal Protection Claim Would Constitutionalize Ordinary Local Governmental Decisionmaking and Displace the Deference This Court Has Long Accorded Municipal Land-Use Judgments.**

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that equal protection does not demand mathematical precision in the treatment of individual cases. In *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court sustained a traffic regulation against an equal protection challenge, observing that "it is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." *Id.* at 110. If a State need not eliminate every instance of unevenness in the regulation of an entire industry, it follows a fortiori that a single municipal employee's negotiation of an easement with a single homeowner cannot be elevated to a constitutional violation merely because another homeowner, at another time, negotiated a different result.

That principle has special force in the domain of municipal land-use administration, where this Court has long recognized that officials must be given room to exercise judgment property by property. In *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926), the Court held that a municipality's exercise of its police power over land use will be overturned only if it is "clearly arbitrary and unreasonable, having no substantial relation to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare." *Id.* at 395. Easement negotiations attendant to extending water service—turning on topography, soil conditions, existing infrastructure, and the individual configuration of each parcel—are quintessential exercises of that same discretion. If every discrepancy between the terms offered to one property owner and those offered to another gives rise to a federal constitutional claim, then federal courts will be transformed into boards of review for every water hookup, building permit, and zoning variance in the country. Nothing in this Court's equal protection decisions before 1970 supports so radical a transformation of the Clause's function.

**III. Without a Cognizable Class, There Is No Judicially Manageable Standard for Determining Who Is "Similarly Situated."**

The class requirement is not a formality; it supplies the benchmark equal protection analysis requires. *Barbier* itself measured constitutionality by whether a law "affects alike all persons similarly situated." 113 U.S. at 32. Where, as here, the plaintiff defines the comparison class herself, after the fact, by pointing to whichever other property owners received more favorable terms, the "similarly situated" inquiry collapses into a standardless second-guessing of individualized administrative decisions—precisely the outcome *Snowden* forecloses by requiring proof of purposeful discrimination against an identifiable class. 321 U.S. at 8.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
