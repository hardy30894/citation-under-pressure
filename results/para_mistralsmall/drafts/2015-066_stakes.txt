**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Applies to Tribal Court Convictions Used as Predicate Offenses in Federal Prosecutions Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right ... to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right attaches at all “critical stages” of a criminal prosecution, *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 224 (1967), and applies to all “prosecutions” in which a defendant faces potential imprisonment, *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 31 (1972). The Supreme Court has made clear that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is not limited to federal or state prosecutions but extends to any proceeding where a defendant is “haled into court” and faces the risk of imprisonment. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342-43 (1963); *see also* *Argersinger*, 407 U.S. at 37 (holding that the right to counsel applies to misdemeanor prosecutions where imprisonment is imposed).

Tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses under § 117(a) are no exception. The statute criminalizes domestic assault by a defendant with “at least two separate prior occasions” of qualifying convictions in “Federal, State, or Indian tribal court proceedings.” 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). These predicate convictions are not mere sentencing enhancements; they are essential elements of the federal offense. *See* *Richardson v. United States*, 526 U.S. 813, 817 (1999) (holding that prior convictions used to elevate a federal offense to a felony are elements of the crime). Thus, the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to these tribal court proceedings because they are part of the “prosecution” for purposes of § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), and its progeny correctly recognized this principle. *Ant* held that uncounseled tribal court convictions could not be used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to such proceedings. *Id.* at 1394-96. This rule is consistent with the Supreme Court’s precedents, which have repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited by the forum in which the prosecution occurs. *See, e.g., Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342-43; *Wade*, 388 U.S. at 224.

II. **Nichols v. United States Does Not Authorize the Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses.**

The government relies on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), to argue that uncounseled tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). However, *Nichols* is inapposite. In *Nichols*, the Supreme Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could be used to enhance a defendant’s sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, even though the conviction itself did not violate the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 746-47. The Court reasoned that sentencing enhancements are not part of the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution and thus do not implicate the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel. *Id.* at 748.

*Nichols* does not apply here because § 117(a) does not involve a sentencing enhancement. The predicate convictions are elements of the federal offense, not merely factors considered at sentencing. *See Richardson*, 526 U.S. at 817. The Supreme Court has distinguished between sentencing enhancements and elements of an offense, holding that the former do not require proof beyond a reasonable doubt, while the latter do. *Id.* at 817-18. Because the predicate convictions in § 117(a) are elements of the offense, they must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, and the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies.

Moreover, *Nichols* itself acknowledged the distinction between sentencing and guilt phases. The Court noted that sentencing courts could consider the reliability of uncounseled convictions under the preponderance of the evidence standard, but it did not address whether such convictions could be used as elements of an offense proven beyond a reasonable doubt. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747-48. The Court’s silence on this issue underscores that *Nichols* does not authorize the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

III. **The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Is Not Limited to Federal or State Proceedings.**

The government argues that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings because the Bill of Rights does not expressly apply to Indian tribes. However, this argument misapprehends the nature of § 117(a). The statute does not rely on the tribal court’s compliance with the Sixth Amendment; it relies on the fact of the conviction itself. *See Richardson*, 526 U.S. at 817. The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to the federal prosecution under § 117(a), not to the tribal court proceedings. *See Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342-43 (holding that the Sixth Amendment applies to all criminal prosecutions, regardless of the forum in which the defendant was originally convicted).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state prosecutions. In *Gideon*, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment applies to state prosecutions, even though the Fourteenth Amendment was the vehicle for incorporation. *Id.* at 342-43. Similarly, the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions because the federal government is the one seeking to rely on those convictions. *See* U.S. Const. amend. VI (“In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right ... to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.”).

IV. **The Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Violates Due Process.**

Even if the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel does not apply to tribal court proceedings, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Due Process Clause. The Supreme Court has held that the use of unreliable prior convictions to enhance a sentence violates due process. *See United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 447 (1972) (holding that a sentencing court may not rely on constitutionally invalid prior convictions to enhance a sentence). The same principle applies here: the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions, which are presumptively unreliable because they were obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment, violates due process.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized this principle in *Ant*, holding that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions violates due process. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394-96. This rule is consistent with the Supreme Court’s precedents, which have emphasized that the reliability of prior convictions is a critical concern in recidivist statutes. *See, e.g., Custis v. United States*, 511 U.S. 485, 490-91 (1994) (holding that a defendant may collaterally attack prior convictions used as predicate offenses under the Armed Career Criminal Act if they were obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment).

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *Nichols* does not authorize the use of such convictions, and the Due Process Clause prohibits their use if they are unreliable. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Ant* and its progeny correctly recognized these principles, and this Court should affirm.
