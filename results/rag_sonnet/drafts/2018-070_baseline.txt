# ARGUMENT

## THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF LANDS ACT ADOPTS STATE LAW AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW ONLY TO FILL GAPS IN FEDERAL COVERAGE

The Ninth Circuit's holding—that state law applies on the Outer Continental Shelf ("OCS") whenever it is not affirmatively inconsistent with federal law, without regard to whether federal law already occupies the field—cannot be reconciled with the text, structure, and purpose of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA"), 43 U.S.C. §§ 1331–1356b, or with this Court's precedents construing it.

### A. OCSLA's Text and Structure Confirm That Federal Law Governs the OCS, With State Law Serving Only a Gap-Filling Function

OCSLA declares that "[t]he Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States are extended to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and to all artificial islands and fixed structures" erected thereon "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1); *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). This provision does not merely extend federal law alongside state law—it establishes federal law, and federal law alone, as the governing sovereign authority, treating the OCS as though it were "an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction." § 1333(a)(1).

Only in the immediately following subsection does OCSLA turn to state law, and it does so in explicitly subordinate terms: state law applies only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." § 1333(a)(2)(A); see *Pacific Operators Offshore, LLP v. Valladolid*, 565 U.S. 207, 211–12 (2012). The word "applicable" does independent work in this formulation. If Congress meant simply that state law governs unless it conflicts with federal law, the "not inconsistent" clause alone would suffice. The additional requirement that state law be "applicable" signals that state law is borrowed only where it is *needed*—that is, where federal law has not already spoken to the subject.

This structural reading is reinforced by OCSLA's mechanism for adopting state law: it does not preserve concurrent state regulatory authority but instead *converts* qualifying state law into federal law. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) declares that applicable and consistent state laws "are declared to be the law of the United States" for the relevant OCS areas. § 1333(a)(2)(A); accord *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97, 101 (1971) (quoting same). State law is not applied as state law; it is assimilated as a surrogate for federal law where federal law is otherwise silent. That assimilation function has no purpose where comprehensive federal law—like the FLSA—already occupies the field.

### B. This Court's Precedents Confirm the Gap-Filling Function of OCSLA's Choice-of-Law Provision

This Court's decisions in *Rodrigue* and *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), demonstrate that OCSLA's choice-of-law provision operates as a gap-filler, not as a general savings clause for state regulation.

In *Rodrigue*, the Court confronted a scenario in which the exclusive federal admiralty remedy—the Death on the High Seas Act—could not reach injuries occurring on fixed platforms because those platforms were not vessels and the deaths did not arise from traditional maritime activity. 395 U.S. at 359–60. Only because federal maritime law left this vacuum did the Court hold that OCSLA permitted incorporation of Louisiana's wrongful-death statute "as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]." *Id.* at 366. Critically, the Court explained that the absence of an applicable federal remedy "remove[d]" the obstacle to state-law incorporation. *Id.* The clear implication is that had a comprehensive federal remedy existed, no such obstacle would have needed removing—state law would have been foreclosed.

*Gulf Offshore* likewise instructs that courts applying OCSLA's choice-of-law provision must first assess "the content of both potentially applicable federal and state law" to determine whether state law is needed to supply a rule of decision. 453 U.S. at 480. The Court's analysis proceeded by asking whether federal common law already supplied an answer to the disputed issue—not merely whether state law conflicted with an existing federal rule. *Id.* This sequencing—federal law first, state law only where federal law is inadequate—is incompatible with the Ninth Circuit's approach, under which state law applies automatically unless it happens to conflict with existing federal provisions.

### C. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Wage-and-Hour Law to Fill

The FLSA is not a partial regulatory scheme with silent gaps respecting overtime, minimum wage, and meal-period-adjacent compensation structures; it is a comprehensive code addressing precisely the subject matter Newton seeks to import from California law. The FLSA regulates minimum wages, § 206, overtime compensation, § 207, and even expressly addresses when meal periods constitute compensable working time through longstanding Department of Labor regulations that this Court has treated as authoritative elaborations of the statute's coverage. *See Long Island Care at Home, Ltd. v. Coke*, 551 U.S. 158, 165 (2007) (recognizing Department of Labor's authority to define scope of FLSA coverage through regulation); *Christensen v. Harris County*, 529 U.S. 576, 587 (2000).

Where Congress wished to permit more generous state regulation to coexist with the FLSA, it said so expressly, through the savings clause in 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). That clause demonstrates that Congress considered, and specifically addressed, the relationship between federal and state wage law—undercutting any suggestion that the FLSA left an inadvertent "void or gap" for OCSLA to fill. The comprehensive nature of the FLSA's wage-and-hour scheme, including its own savings mechanism, forecloses the need for OCSLA to borrow California law as a federal surrogate.

### Conclusion

Because OCSLA adopts state law only to address matters left unaddressed by federal law, and because the FLSA comprehensively occupies the field of wage-and-hour regulation relevant here, California law does not apply to Newton's claims arising from his work on the OCS. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
