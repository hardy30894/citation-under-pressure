ARGUMENT

I. Punitive Damages at Common Law Required Conduct Beyond Mere Intentional Wrongdoing

This Court has long recognized that punitive or exemplary damages serve to punish the defendant and deter similar misconduct. But from the earliest days of the common law, those remedies were reserved for conduct that was not merely intentional, but also independently egregious or outrageous. A plaintiff could not recover punitive damages simply by proving that a defendant intended the act that caused injury; something more—some element of “grossness” or “outrage”—was essential.

In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851), this Court stated that punitive damages were permissible where the trespass was “wanton and malicious, or gross and outrageous.” The conduct itself had to bear indicia of extraordinary blameworthiness beyond the bare intention to commit the wrong. Similarly, in *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858), the Court permitted punitive damages only for “gross neglect, willful misconduct, or actual malice”—each a standard that demands more than simple intent.

*Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), reiterated that punitive damages may be awarded only “in cases of gross negligence, or where the act is willful, malicious, or wanton.” The term “wanton” itself connotes a degree of recklessness that is indifferent to the consequences, but even that state of mind, under *Arms*, is not sufficient unless the conduct is also “gross”—a qualitative judgment about the severity of the defendant’s actions. Indeed, *Missouri Pacific Ry. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885), upheld a state statute allowing exemplary damages only “when the wrong is done willfully, or is the result of that entire want of care which would raise a presumption of a conscious indifference to consequences”—again linking the remedy to the character of the conduct, not just the actor’s intent.

II. The “Malice or Reckless Indifference” Standard in Section 1981a Incorporates the Common Law Requirement of Egregious Conduct

The Civil Rights Act of 1991, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), permits punitive damages only when the plaintiff demonstrates that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” Congress used common-law terms of art. This Court applies the settled meaning those terms had at common law unless Congress indicates otherwise. At common law, “malice” in this context meant not merely the intentional violation of a right, but “that state of mind which is actuated by improper motives,” *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), and the conduct had to be “oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious,” *id.* at 106. Similarly, “reckless indifference” required conduct that was “so wanton and reckless as to manifest a gross disregard of the rights of others,” *St. Louis, Iron Mountain & Southern Ry. v. Williams*, 251 U.S. 63, 67 (1919). In each case, the severity of the conduct—not merely the actor’s mental state—was an independent element.

*Scott v. Donald*, 165 U.S. 58, 86 (1897), reinforces this principle. There, the Court affirmed that punitive damages are “allowed only when the act is malicious or wanton,” but it evaluated the conduct itself to determine whether the statutory violation was “of such a character as to justify punitive damages.” The inquiry thus includes an assessment of how the defendant acted, not solely why.

This Court has never deviated from that dual requirement. In *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107, the Court explained that exemplary damages “are allowed only in cases of malice, fraud, oppression, or gross negligence.” Each of those terms describes a level of misconduct that transcends simple intentional wrongdoing. “Oppression” implies an act committed with a “conscious disregard of the rights of others,” *Williams*, 251 U.S. at 67, but that disregard must be demonstrated by egregarious conduct, not mere knowledge.

III. The Lower Court Correctly Required a Showing of Egregious Conduct

The district court and the court of appeals majority therefore erred in holding that proof of “malice or reckless indifference” as a state of mind, standing alone, is enough to sustain a punitive-damages award under Title VII. The common law has never adopted such a permissive standard. To hold otherwise would convert § 1981a(b)(1) into a remedy available in virtually every intentional discrimination case—because every intentional violator of Title VII necessarily knows that discrimination is unlawful. Yet Congress deliberately used language that requires “more than even [the] heightened showing” of intentional discrimination. *Humes*, 115 U.S. at 521 (awarding punitive damages only for conduct manifesting “entire want of care” equivalent to “conscious indifference”).

The text of § 1981a(b)(1) mirrors the common-law formulation, and this Court has consistently looked to the common law to inform the meaning of similar provisions. *See, e.g., Day*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Quigley*, 62 U.S. at 214; *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 106. Accordingly, to recover punitive damages, a plaintiff must demonstrate not only that the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference, but also that the discriminatory practice was independently egregious or outrageous—conduct that, in the words of this Court, is “gross,” “wanton,” or “oppressive.”

Because the evidence in this case did not satisfy that higher threshold, the district court properly declined to submit the punitive-damages claim to the jury. The judgment of the court of appeals on that issue should be reversed.
