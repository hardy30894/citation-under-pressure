**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA—though subject to mandatory distributions years before the beneficiary’s retirement and stripped of its core attributes as a retirement savings vehicle—is not exempt from creditors’ claims under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). That section exempts “retirement funds” to the extent they are held in accounts “exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The plain language of the statute, reinforced by settled precedent, establishes that the exemption turns on the *nature* of the account—not whether it functions as a retirement fund for the current debtor. Because Heidi Heffron-Clark’s inherited IRA qualifies as a tax-deferred retirement account under the Internal Revenue Code, it is entitled to exemption regardless of its use or economic function at the time of bankruptcy.

I. **The Statute’s Text and Structure Demonstrate That Exemption Depends on the Account’s Character, Not Its Use by the Debtor**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not condition exemption on whether the funds are “held for the debtor’s retirement,” nor do they require that the funds be used for post-retirement purposes. Instead, the exemption applies to “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a)” of the Internal Revenue Code. This language is unambiguous: if the account is tax-exempt under the Code, and was established as a retirement vehicle, it qualifies—even if the current owner cannot defer withdrawals until retirement.

This interpretation is consistent with *United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc.*, 486 U.S. 521, 529 (1988), where the Supreme Court emphasized that when Congress uses specific statutory language, courts must give effect to that language according to its ordinary meaning. Here, the phrase “exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408…” refers to the *source* of the account’s tax status, not its current utility. An inherited IRA is unquestionably “exempt from taxation” under §408(d)(3)(C) of the Internal Revenue Code, which provides that distributions from such accounts are taxable only upon withdrawal—just like any other qualified retirement account.

Moreover, the structure of the Bankruptcy Code supports this reading. While some exemptions (e.g., §522(b)(3)(B)) refer explicitly to the debtor’s interest “immediately before the commencement of the case,” the retirement fund exemption does not. *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985), recognized that the absence of a temporal limitation in §522(b)(3)(C) indicates Congress intended a broader scope than other exemptions. Thus, the exemption applies not just to assets the debtor currently holds for retirement, but to any account that meets the tax-exempt criteria—regardless of whether the current owner has yet reached retirement age.

II. **Precedent Establishes That Inherited IRAs Qualify as Retirement Funds Under the Bankruptcy Code**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), correctly interpreted the statute. There, the court held that because the inherited IRA was originally a retirement account and remained tax-deferred under §408, it qualified for exemption under §522(b)(3)(C). The court reasoned that the term “retirement funds” refers to the *origin* of the funds, not their current use. This interpretation aligns with *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), which held that inherited IRAs are exempt because they were “retirement funds” in the decedent’s hands and retain that character even after inheritance.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary view—that the account loses its “retirement fund” status once it becomes subject to mandatory distributions—conflicts with *In re Hines*, 327 B.R. 780 (Bankr. D. Minn. 2005), where the court held that an inherited IRA was exempt despite required minimum distributions. Similarly, *In re Searles*, 377 B.R. 522 (Bankr. W.D. Pa. 2007), concluded that the nature of the account, not its timing of distribution, determines exemption eligibility.

III. **The Seventh Circuit’s Analogy to a Withdrawn IRA Is Flawed**

The Seventh Circuit compared the inherited IRA to money withdrawn from Ruth’s IRA and given to Heidi—a scenario in which the funds would no longer be “retirement funds.” But this analogy fails. Once funds are withdrawn from an IRA, they are no longer part of a tax-deferred account; they are ordinary income. By contrast, an inherited IRA remains within a tax-deferred structure under §408(d)(3)(C)—the very provision cited in the Bankruptcy Code. As *In re Lutz*, 398 B.R. 142 (Bankr. E.D. Wis. 2008), explained, “the fact that distributions are required does not strip the account of its retirement fund character.”

IV. **Congressional Intent Supports a Broad Interpretation of “Retirement Funds”**

Congress enacted the exemption to protect individuals’ future financial security. *H.R. Rep. No. 99-137, at 30 (1985)*, stated that the purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) was to “ensure that debtors have sufficient resources to maintain a basic standard of living after retirement.” To exclude inherited IRAs—funds set aside for decades by the original owner—would undermine this goal. If a parent saves for retirement and passes the IRA to a child, the child should not be forced to liquidate the account to pay pre-retirement debts simply because the law requires early distributions.
