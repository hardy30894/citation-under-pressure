**ARGUMENT**

Section 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment when it uses a valid tribal-court conviction as a predicate offense. The statute punishes the new domestic assault offense, not the prior conviction; and the Constitution permits Congress to define recidivist liability by reference to earlier convictions even when those convictions arose in proceedings where the Sixth Amendment did not apply. *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 747, 114 S. Ct. 1921, 128 L. Ed. 2d 745 (1994); *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386, 128 S. Ct. 1783, 170 L. Ed. 2d 719 (2008). Bryant’s contrary rule wrongly transforms a permissible predicate-conviction statute into a collateral attack on a valid tribal judgment.

1. **The tribal convictions were constitutionally valid when entered.**  
The Sixth Amendment right to appointed counsel does not apply to tribal-court prosecutions. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384, 16 S. Ct. 986, 41 L. Ed. 196 (1896); *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S. Ct. 2709, 171 L. Ed. 2d 457 (2008). Tribal criminal procedure is governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act, not the Sixth Amendment. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 56-57, 98 S. Ct. 1670, 56 L. Ed. 2d 106 (1978). Because the prior convictions were valid when obtained, there is no Sixth Amendment defect in the convictions themselves.

2. **Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that punishes the latest offense, not the predicates.**  
The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that recidivist laws “penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747, 114 S. Ct. 1921; see also *Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. at 386. Section 117(a) follows that familiar model. It makes a defendant’s prior convictions an element of the federal offense, but the punishment is for the current domestic assault. That distinction is decisive. The Constitution does not prohibit Congress from defining a crime by reference to prior valid convictions, even when those convictions would not themselves satisfy the Sixth Amendment if entered in state or federal court. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748-49; *Parke v. Raley*, 506 U.S. 20, 27-34, 113 S. Ct. 517, 121 L. Ed. 2d 391 (1992).

3. **The Ninth Circuit’s rule improperly extends sentencing cases to the guilt phase.**  
*Nichols* approved use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction at sentencing under the Sentencing Guidelines. 511 U.S. at 748-49. The Court’s rationale turned on the limited role of sentencing and the longstanding permissibility of considering reliable prior convictions. Id. Nothing in *Nichols* suggests that a valid prior conviction becomes constitutionally unusable whenever Congress chooses to make it an element of a later offense. To the contrary, the Court has made clear that the Sixth Amendment inquiry concerns the validity of the prior conviction itself, not how Congress later uses that conviction in a separate prosecution. *Lewis v. United States*, 445 U.S. 55, 65-67, 100 S. Ct. 915, 63 L. Ed. 2d 198 (1980).

4. **Due process is satisfied because Congress may rely on valid tribal convictions.**  
Federal courts routinely recognize and rely on tribal judgments under principles of comity and congressional authorization. *Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15-16, 107 S. Ct. 971, 94 L. Ed. 2d 10 (1987); *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810-11 (9th Cir. 1997). Congress enacted Section 117(a) to address repeat domestic violence in Indian country, and it chose prior federal, state, and tribal convictions alike as predicates. 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). That legislative judgment is fully consistent with the Constitution because Congress may define criminal liability by reference to valid prior adjudications, and tribal convictions are valid adjudications under the governing sovereign. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200-05, 124 S. Ct. 1628, 158 L. Ed. 2d 420 (2004); *Wheeler v. United States*, 435 U.S. 313, 323-26, 98 S. Ct. 1079, 55 L. Ed. 2d 303 (1978).

5. **Bryant’s rule would create an unwarranted constitutional anomaly.**  
Under *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74, 99 S. Ct. 1158, 59 L. Ed. 2d 383 (1979), and *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47, an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction valid when entered may have legal consequences later. Nothing in the Sixth Amendment requires Congress to ignore valid tribal convictions when protecting domestic violence victims in Indian country. If the Constitution allowed the same conviction to be used at sentencing, *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748-49, it certainly permits Congress to use the conviction as a statutory predicate for a new recidivist offense. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule has no footing in Supreme Court precedent and should be reversed.
