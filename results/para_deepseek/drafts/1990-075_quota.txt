## ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. Congress provided an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s discretion, and the subdelegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration was fully authorized.

### I. The Delegation to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Test

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, so long as Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This standard has been applied with “wide latitude” and, with only two exceptions in 1935, every delegation challenged since *Hampton* has been upheld. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 373-74 (1989).

Section 811(h) provides multiple, specific constraints on the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling power. The Attorney General may act only upon a finding that temporary scheduling is “necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). He must consider three factors: the substance’s “history and current pattern of abuse”; the “scope, duration, and significance of abuse”; and the “risk to the public health.” § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)). He must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit the proposal to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be taken into account. § 811(h)(4). The scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. § 811(h)(2). These standards are far more precise than those previously upheld.

In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944), the Court upheld a delegation to set maximum commodity prices under the “generally fair and equitable” standard, even though the statute provided only “broad objectives” and “general directions.” In *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948), the Court sustained a delegation authorizing recovery of “excessive profits” under a “broad” and “general” standard. In *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), the Court upheld the FCC’s power to regulate broadcast licensing in the “public interest, convenience, or necessity.” In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to appoint conservators, noting that “well-known and generally acceptable standards” may suffice. And in *Sunshine Anthracite Coal Co. v. Adkins*, 310 U.S. 381, 399 (1940), the Court upheld a delegation to the Bituminous Coal Commission to fix minimum prices, citing the “elaborate administrative machinery” and “definite standards” provided.

The temporary scheduling provision is even more circumscribed. Unlike the delegations in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), which gave the President “unfettered discretion” to prohibit interstate transportation of oil or to approve codes of fair competition without any “policy” or “criterion,” here Congress defined the precise hazard to be avoided, the factors to be considered, and the duration of the order. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541-42. As this Court observed in *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373, those two cases “are not in point” because they involved “sweeping” delegations with “no standards” whatsoever.

### II. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the Controlled Substances Act to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Pursuant to that authority, the Attorney General promulgated 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns to the DEA Administrator “[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This regulation is a routine exercise of the Attorney General’s statutory authority to manage the Department’s law enforcement functions. *Cf. United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514-15 (1974) (recognizing that “the Attorney General has the power to delegate” functions under Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act). Nothing in § 811(h) prohibits subdelegation, and Congress’s silence does not bar it when the enabling statute—§ 871(a)—explicitly authorizes such delegation. *See United States v. Rock Royal Co-op., Inc.*, 307 U.S. 533, 577-78 (1939) (upholding subdelegation where the Secretary of Agriculture had general authority to delegate).

### III. The Temporary Scheduling Provision’s Standard Is Sufficient

The “intelligible principle” test is satisfied here. Congress defined the triggering condition—“imminent hazard to the public safety”—and specified the factors that must be considered. The requirement of a “determination” based on “all relevant data” provides a meaningful standard for judicial review of the Attorney General’s compliance with the statutory criteria. *Cf. Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892) (congressional delegation constitutional when “the legislative power is exercised, and the limits of the power granted are sufficiently defined”). The temporary scheduling provision thus embodies the “practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372.

For these reasons, the Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision is a constitutional delegation of legislative power, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA was authorized. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
