Respondent respectfully submits that the Ninth Circuit correctly construed 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) to reach only injuries that occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself, or at minimum to require a geographically shelf-based injury nexus. The statutory text, structure, and Supreme Court precedent all point that way.

Section 1333(b) extends LHWCA benefits for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress linked coverage to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” not merely to employment that has some causal relationship to shelf operations. That phrasing embeds a geographic limitation twice over: the operations must be “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” and the injury must “occu[r] as the result” of those operations. The better reading is that Congress extended coverage to shelf-based injuries caused by shelf operations, not to any injury anywhere that bears a causal connection to offshore work.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), confirms that OCSLA coverage is principally locale-driven. There, the Court explained that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. Although that statement arose in the context of § 1333(a)(2)(A), the Court’s reasoning reflects the Act’s basic design: OCSLA is a location-centered statute, tied to the shelf and structures thereon. Id. at 218-19. Tallentire’s footnote observing that § 1333(b) “makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act” does not support a no-situs rule. Id. at 219 n.2. The Court expressly said that provision “has no bearing on this case.” Id. The footnote is dictum, not a holding, and it should not be expanded beyond its limited context. See United States v. Montero-Camargo, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc).

The Fifth Circuit’s en banc decision in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is persuasive and should be followed. Mills held that § 1333(b) requires both that the injury result from shelf operations and that the injury occur on the shelf or over the waters above it. Id. at 362. That reading gives full effect to the statute’s geographic focus and avoids turning § 1333(b) into a nationwide compensation scheme for any worker whose employment has some upstream relationship to offshore production. See also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc., 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001); Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc., 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998).

Petitioner’s reliance on Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Curtis adopted a “but for” test, but it did so without confronting the statute’s geographic structure in a manner comparable to Mills, and its approach risks swallowing the situs limitation entirely. Id. at 809-11. If “resulting from” means only “caused in some remote sense by offshore operations,” then § 1333(b) would extend to a vast range of land-based accidents far removed from the shelf. Congress did not write such sweeping language.

This Court should therefore affirm the denial of OCSLA benefits. Valladolid’s fatal injury occurred onshore at La Conchita, not on the outer Continental Shelf. Under the proper reading of § 1333(b), that geographic fact is dispositive. See Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986); Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc).
