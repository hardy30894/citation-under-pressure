**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of statutory text and settled principles of statutory interpretation. The answer is no. Congress has unambiguously prescribed that multidistrict litigation (MDL) transferred under § 1407(a) “shall be remanded by the panel” to the original district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Ninth Circuit’s decision to uphold the District of Arizona’s exercise of § 1404(a) authority to retain jurisdiction for trial directly contravenes this clear statutory command and constitutes a fundamental misreading of the legislative framework governing MDL.

The text of § 1407(a) is not merely permissive; it is mandatory. The statute does not say “may” be remanded, but “shall” be remanded. This mandatory language reflects Congress’s deliberate policy choice: to limit the scope of transfer to pretrial proceedings only. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized, “where Congress uses a word with a settled meaning, we assume it intended that meaning.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 223, 231 (1989). The word “remand” carries a well-established legal meaning—“to send back” a case to the court from which it was taken. *Black’s Law Dictionary* (10th ed. 2014). Congress did not intend for the transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial; it intended for the case to return to the original district for trial, just as it was before transfer.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and cases like *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. These decisions, and the rule they cite, are not entitled to deference because they are inconsistent with the plain language of § 1407(a). *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984), teaches that administrative interpretations are entitled to deference only when the statute is ambiguous. Here, the statute is not ambiguous. The mandate to remand is clear, unqualified, and unambiguous. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) may not, by rule or practice, override a statutory command. *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001) (“[A]n agency’s interpretation of a statute is not entitled to deference when the statute itself is unambiguous.”).

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the district court’s order is not “clearly erroneous as a matter of law” fails to recognize that a violation of a mandatory statutory command is itself a legal error of the highest order. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 439 (1978) (“[W]hen Congress has spoken with clarity, courts must give effect to its intent.”). The district court’s decision to retain the case for trial under § 1404(a) is not merely a policy judgment—it is a jurisdictional overreach. Section 1404(a) permits transfer of a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only where the court has jurisdiction over the case. *42 U.S.C. § 1404(a)*. Once a case is transferred under § 1407(a), the transferee court’s jurisdiction is limited to pretrial proceedings. The authority to conduct trial is not conferred by § 1404(a); it is reserved for the original district under § 1407(a). To allow a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial is to permit it to circumvent the very statutory framework Congress designed.

The dissent’s invocation of “policy” and “convenience” as justification for this practice is particularly troubling. The judiciary may not rewrite statutes to accommodate administrative convenience. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 555 (1995) (“[T]he Constitution limits the power of Congress, and by extension, the power of the courts, to legislate in areas not within the scope of federal authority.”). The policy of judicial efficiency cannot override a clear statutory mandate. Indeed, the very purpose of § 1407 is to prevent the inefficiencies of duplicative litigation across multiple districts. But that purpose is undermined when the transferee court, having no stake in the original forum, retains the case for trial—thereby defeating the original plaintiff’s right to trial in the district where the action was originally filed.

The Ninth Circuit’s refusal to issue a writ of mandamus is equally flawed. The Bauman factors do not preclude mandamus relief here. Petitioners have no adequate remedy on appeal. The case is scheduled for trial in Arizona, and any error in venue will be irreparable once the trial concludes. *Bauman v. United States Dist. Court*, 557 F.2d 650, 653 (9th Cir. 1977). The prejudice is not merely “inconvenience” but a fundamental deprivation of a constitutional right to trial in the chosen forum. *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 427 (1962) (“The right to trial in the district where the action arose is a fundamental right.”). The district court’s order is not merely erroneous—it is a clear violation of a mandatory statutory command.
