ARGUMENT

The bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate the amount of post-petition interest or collection costs due on a nondischargeable student-loan debt that arose after the Chapter 13 estate closed and the debtor received a discharge. See 28 U.S.C. §§ 1334(a), 157(a); Valley Historic Ltd. P’ship v. Bank of N.Y., 486 F.3d 278, 287 (4th Cir. 2007) (“related to” jurisdiction exists only when the outcome “could conceivably have any effect on the estate being administered in bankruptcy”).

A bankruptcy court’s authority is confined to “core proceedings” arising under title 11 or arising in a case under title 11. 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2). Post-petition interest and collection costs on a nondischargeable obligation do not arise under title 11; they accrue by operation of non-bankruptcy law after the estate has been fully administered. See In re Kielisch, 258 F.3d 315, 321 (4th Cir. 2001) (post-petition interest on nondischargeable student loans “is not part of the bankruptcy estate”).

The proceeding below was commenced more than a year after the case closed. At that point the estate no longer existed, and any determination of post-petition amounts could have no conceivable effect on estate administration. Valley Historic, 486 F.3d at 287. The mere fact that the underlying loan had been listed in the bankruptcy does not convert subsequent, independent obligations into core matters. See Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 308 (1995) (related-to jurisdiction requires more than a “remote” connection to the bankruptcy case).

Nor does 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8) expand jurisdiction. That provision renders the debt nondischargeable absent undue hardship; it does not authorize a closed bankruptcy court to liquidate post-petition accruals. The bankruptcy court therefore exceeded the limits of its statutory authority when it fixed post-petition interest at $184.40 and denied collection costs.

The district court’s affirmance should be reversed.
