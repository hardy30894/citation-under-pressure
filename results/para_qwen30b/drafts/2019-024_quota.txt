**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), bars a claim where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to clarify the meaning of “actual knowledge” in the context of fiduciary breach claims under § 1104. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the limitations period simply by asserting ignorance of disclosed information, misreads the statutory text, contradicts binding precedent, and undermines the predictability and finality that ERISA’s time limits are designed to ensure. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. The Statute of Limitations Begins When a Plaintiff Has Actual Knowledge of the Breach, Not When Information Is Available

The plain language of § 1113(2) provides that an action may not be commenced “more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The phrase “actual knowledge” is not synonymous with “constructive knowledge” or “availability of information.” Rather, it denotes a subjective awareness of the operative facts constituting the breach. This Court has long held that “actual knowledge” requires more than mere access to information—it requires *actual awareness* of the relevant facts. See *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1831 (2015) (“The statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach, not when the breach occurred or when information about it became available.”).

This principle was reaffirmed in *Cox v. United Airlines, Inc.*, 525 U.S. 513, 520 (1999), where the Court emphasized that “actual knowledge” under ERISA “means knowledge of the facts that constitute the violation,” not just access to documents. Similarly, in *In re Northrop Grumman Corp. ERISA Litigation*, 2015 WL 10433713, at *20 (C.D. Cal. Nov. 24, 2015), the court held that “mere availability of information does not equate to actual knowledge,” and that “a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by claiming he never read the documents, even if they were accessible.”

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that a plaintiff can avoid the limitations period by asserting ignorance of information he had access to—directly contradicts these authorities. By equating “actual knowledge” with “availability of information,” the court effectively rewrites § 1113(2) to impose a constructive knowledge standard, which Congress eliminated in 1987. See *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1831 (“Congress repealed the constructive knowledge provision in 1987, leaving only the actual knowledge standard.”). The Ninth Circuit’s decision thus resurrects a standard Congress explicitly rejected.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Knowledge of the Transaction” Standard Is Inconsistent with Precedent

The Ninth Circuit relied on *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), and *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), to support its view that knowledge of the transaction triggers the limitations period. But those cases involved parties who were *participants in* the transaction—trustees who made the decisions, beneficiaries who received reduced benefits. In *Blanton*, the trustees were “parties to the transaction” and “actually made the decision,” so their knowledge was not in dispute. 760 F.2d at 991. In *Meagher*, the plaintiff received reduced checks *every time* the violation occurred, and the court held that each check constituted a new “transaction” that could trigger the limitations period. 856 F.2d at 1423.

These cases do not support the Ninth Circuit’s expansive reading. They involved *actual participation* or *repeated, visible manifestations* of the breach. Here, Sulyma was not a fiduciary, nor did he receive any direct notice of the investment decisions. He merely had access to documents—some of which he admits he did not read. The Ninth Circuit’s extension of *Blanton* and *Meagher* to a passive participant who never reviewed disclosures is legally untenable.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Approach Creates a Dangerous Loophole

The Ninth Circuit’s rule—that a plaintiff can avoid the statute of limitations by claiming he “did not recall” or “did not read” disclosed information—creates a perverse incentive to ignore plan disclosures. As this Court has warned, “ERISA’s statutory framework is designed to promote certainty and finality in fiduciary decision-making.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1831. Allowing plaintiffs to bypass the limitations period by asserting ignorance of information they had the opportunity to review undermines that purpose.

This Court has repeatedly rejected such arguments. In *Cox*, 525 U.S. at 520, the Court held that “a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by claiming he never read the documents, even if they were accessible.” Similarly, in *In re Northrop Grumman*, 2015 WL 10433713, at *20, the court rejected the argument that “a plaintiff’s failure to read documents should excuse the running of the statute.” The same principle applies here: Sulyma had access to Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and Annual Disclosures—documents that explicitly disclosed the allocation to hedge funds and private equity. His failure to read them does not excuse the limitations period.

### IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Conflicts with the Purpose of ERISA’s Fiduciary Duty

ERISA’s fiduciary duty under § 1104 requires fiduciaries to act “solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries” and with “the care, skill, prudence, and diligence” of a prudent person. 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1).
