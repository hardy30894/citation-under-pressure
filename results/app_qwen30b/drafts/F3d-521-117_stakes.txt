**ARGUMENT**

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. *U.S. Const. amend. IV*. A police officer may conduct a brief, investigatory stop—commonly known as a “Terry stop”—only when the officer has reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). Even then, the scope of the stop and any accompanying frisk must be strictly circumscribed by the exigencies of the situation. *Id.* at 20. Here, the officers lacked reasonable suspicion to justify the stop, and even if the stop were lawful, the subsequent pat-frisk of Michael Soares violated the Fourth Amendment because it was not supported by reasonable suspicion of criminal activity or a threat to officer safety.

**I. The Initial Stop Was Not Supported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The officers stopped the green Lexus based solely on the absence of headlights and malfunctioning brake lights. While the failure to illuminate headlights is a traffic violation under Massachusetts law, *see Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 85, § 11*, the mere absence of a light does not, standing alone, justify a stop absent some indication that the driver is aware of the violation or that the vehicle poses a danger. *See United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 231 (1985) (holding that a traffic violation must be “known” to the driver to justify a stop). The officers here did not observe the driver attempting to turn on the lights, nor did they see any evasive behavior. The absence of taillights was inferred from the lack of illumination, but this inference alone does not establish reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417–18 (1981) (reasonable suspicion must be based on “specific and articulable facts” and “rational inferences” drawn from those facts).

Moreover, the officers were in a high-crime area, but that alone does not justify a stop. *See *United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 422 U.S. 873, 884 (1975) (noting that “a high-crime area” is not a substitute for reasonable suspicion). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the mere fact that a person is in a high-crime area does not justify a stop.” *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989). The officers’ decision to stop the vehicle was based on a single, non-criminal traffic infraction—failure to illuminate headlights—without any corroborating evidence of wrongdoing. This falls short of the reasonable suspicion standard required by *Terry*.

**II. The Pat-Frisk Was Not Justified by Reasonable Suspicion or Officer Safety Concerns**

Even assuming the stop was lawful, the pat-frisk of Soares was unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has held that a frisk is permissible only when the officer has reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. This “reasonable suspicion” must be based on specific and articulable facts, not on generalized fears or subjective discomfort. *Id.* at 20.

The officers’ justification for the frisk rested entirely on Soares’s behavior after the stop: rocking back and forth, flailing his hands, using profanity, and expressing anger. While such conduct may be unsettling, it does not, by itself, constitute reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed. *See United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. at 231 (holding that “nervousness, evasiveness, or other conduct” must be “connected to criminal activity” to justify a frisk). The officers did not observe Soares reach for a weapon, make any threatening gestures, or attempt to conceal anything. The movements described—rocking, flailing, flipping hands—were not indicative of furtive conduct aimed at hiding a weapon. *See United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d 57, 62 (1st Cir. 2007) (holding that “mere nervousness or agitation” does not justify a frisk).

The officers’ subjective “uneasiness” is not a substitute for objective, articulable suspicion. *See *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 273 (2002) (noting that “subjective hunches” are insufficient). The fact that the officers were in a high-crime area and that Soares was Black does not justify a frisk. *See *United States v. Johnson*, 434 F.3d 1, 5 (1st Cir. 2006) (rejecting racial profiling as a basis for reasonable suspicion). The officers’ decision to remove Soares from the car and frisk him was not based on any objective threat but on a combination of subjective discomfort and racial stereotyping.

**III. The Frisk Was Not Justified by the “Protective Sweep” Exception**

The government argues that the frisk was justified under the “protective sweep” doctrine, which permits a limited search of a vehicle or premises for weapons when officers have a reasonable belief that someone may be present and pose a danger. *See *Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. 408, 411 (1997). But this exception applies only when officers are conducting a lawful arrest or search and have a reasonable belief that someone may be hiding in the vehicle. *Id.* at 412. Here, no arrest had yet occurred, and the officers had no reason to believe Soares was concealing a weapon. The protective sweep doctrine does not permit a frisk of a passenger who is not under arrest and who has not engaged in any conduct suggesting danger. *See *United States v. Johnson*, 434 F.3d at 5.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress. The stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion, and the pat-frisk was not justified by any articulable threat to officer safety. The evidence obtained as a result of the unlawful frisk must be suppressed under the exclusionary rule. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S.
