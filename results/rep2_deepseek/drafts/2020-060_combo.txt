**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously held that a 2004 consent decree under the Clean Water Act triggered Guam’s contribution rights under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby extinguishing its § 107 cost-recovery claim. That holding misapplies the plain text of CERCLA and contradicts settled principles of consent decree interpretation that predate CERCLA’s enactment.

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve[] Liability” for CERCLA Response Costs**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations—discharges of untreated leachate—and did not impose any obligation for CERCLA “response costs” or hazardous-substance remediation. The decree contains no mention of CERCLA, no allocation of cleanup costs, and no admission of liability for contamination. It is well settled that a consent decree is a contract, *Swift & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 311, 315 (1928), and its terms must be interpreted as the parties intended, *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932). Here, the parties explicitly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability. *Hughes v. United States*, 342 U.S. 353, 356 (1952) (courts must give effect to the plain language of a consent decree). A disclaimer of liability, standing alone, demonstrates that the parties did not intend to “resolve” liability for CERCLA purposes. *St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. United States*, 251 U.S. 198, 206 (1920) (settlement without admission of liability does not bar future claims).

**II. The D.C. Circuit’s Interpretation Violates the Plain Meaning of the Statute**

Congress enacted CERCLA to ensure prompt cleanup and to allocate costs among responsible parties. *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998). But that purpose is not served by reading § 113(f)(3)(B) to swallow § 107. The statute’s plain language requires a “settlement” that resolves liability “for some or all of a response action.” A decree that addresses only non-CERCLA pollutants cannot qualify. In *United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 25 (1948), this Court held that courts must enforce the clear terms of a statute, not expand them by implication. Similarly, *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), teaches that when the statutory language is unambiguous, the inquiry ends. The 2004 decree did not mention CERCLA, hazardous substances, or response costs. To treat it as a CERCLA settlement is to rewrite the statute.

**III. The Decree’s Clean Water Act Obligations Are Not CERCLA “Response Actions”**

The D.C. Circuit reasoned that the cover system required by the decree constituted a “remedial action” under CERCLA § 101(24). But that conflates the two statutes. The relief sought by EPA was to eliminate “untreated leachate” discharges—a pollution-control measure under the Clean Water Act, not a CERCLA removal or remediation. *The No. 19*, 267 U.S. 455, 457 (1925) (a decree’s purpose is determined by the claims it resolves). A consent decree does not transform a Clean Water Act violation into a CERCLA response action simply because the remedy overlaps with a CERCLA-defined activity. Only an express agreement to resolve CERCLA liability triggers § 113(f)(3)(B).

**IV. The District Court Correctly Recognized That the Decree Did Not Resolve Liability**

The district court, applying the same contract principles, concluded that the decree’s disclaimer of liability and its limited scope precluded a finding that liability was “resolved.” *See, e.g., United States v. Fisk*, 70 U.S. 445, 448 (1865) (consent decrees are construed strictly against the party seeking to impose liability). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding disregards the parties’ intent and the decree’s plain language. Furthermore, the court’s reliance on post-1970 cases from other circuits is misplaced. Pre-1970 precedent uniformly requires that a consent decree must clearly and unambiguously resolve the specific liability at issue. *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 733 (1878) (due process requires fair notice of the claims being resolved).

**V. Guam’s § 107 Claim Is Not Barred by the Statute of Limitations**

Because the 2004 decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s six-year statute of limitations under § 107(a) has not yet expired. The district court correctly held that Guam may pursue full cost recovery. To hold otherwise would permit the government to extinguish a territory’s right to cleanup costs through a collateral agreement that never addressed CERCLA—a result that would undermine the statute’s remedial purpose. *See Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. 137, 176 (1803) (it is a “general and indisputable rule” that a statute must be construed to avoid absurd results).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
