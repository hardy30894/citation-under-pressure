The reinstatement statute does not govern Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry because Congress did not clearly prescribe such a sweeping reach, and the Court has long required a clear statement before applying a statute to past conduct. See Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988); Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964). The presumption against retroactivity is especially strong where application would attach “new legal consequences” to completed conduct. See United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928); United States v. St. Louis, San Francisco & Texas Railway Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3-4 (1926).

Section 241(a)(5) is triggered only when the Government elects to reinstate a prior removal order after detecting an unlawful reentry. But the critical conduct here—Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry and his decades-long residence—occurred before the statute’s 1997 effective date. Applying the statute to him therefore does not regulate a merely ongoing status; it imposes a new disability on a completed act and strips away a form of relief that remained available when he reentered. Retroactive legislation is disfavored because “the first rule of construction is that legislation is to be considered as addressed to the future, not to the past.” Union Pacific Railway Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co., 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913); United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806). If Congress intends that result, it must speak plainly. See United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. at 162-63.

The Government may argue that Fernandez-Vargas’s later marriage and adjustment application do not matter because the statute merely governs the moment of enforcement. That misstates the retroactivity inquiry. A law is retroactive when it “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability” to prior conduct. Societe Francaise des Cafes & Restaurants v. Selig, 48 F.2d 203, 205 (2d Cir. 1931) (L. Hand, J.); see also Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. McGuire, 219 U.S. 549, 567 (1911). Fernandez-Vargas had at least the settled expectation that, absent a new statutory command, he could seek whatever relief the INA then permitted. Congress did not unmistakably extinguish that expectation in 1997.

The Court should also be guided by the longstanding rule that repeals by implication are not favored and that courts should not infer a harsh deprivation of existing rights absent clear text. See United States v. Tynen, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 88, 95-96 (1871); United States v. Heirs of Boisdoré, 49 U.S. (8 How.) 113, 122 (1850). Section 241(a)(5) contains no express instruction that it reaches aliens who reentered years before 1997 and had already accrued the practical ability to pursue adjustment. That silence is dispositive under the canon against retroactive operation.

Even if the statute were construed to reach pre-enactment reentries, that application would be impermissibly retroactive. The decisive question is whether the statute “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted.” Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 757 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (No. 13,156) (Story, J.). Fernandez-Vargas acted in 1982, not 1997. He remained in the United States for years under the legal regime then in force, and he later married a citizen and sought adjustment while relief remained open. To convert that past conduct into an automatic bar now is the very kind of “new disability” the retroactivity doctrine forbids. See Societe Francaise, 48 F.2d at 205; McGuire, 219 U.S. at 567.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse and hold that § 241(a)(5) does not apply to pre-1997 reentrants like Fernandez-Vargas. Alternatively, if the statute is construed to reach him, the Court should hold that application retroactive and invalid as applied.
