**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S DENIAL OF ACCESS TO THE GOOD NEWS CLUB WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE BECAUSE THE CLUB SOUGHT TO USE SCHOOL FACILITIES FOR RELIGIOUS WORSHIP, NOT FOR SECULAR SPEECH.**

The First Amendment does not compel public schools to open their facilities to religious worship under the guise of free speech. *See* *Zorach v. Clauson*, 347 U.S. 30, 32 (1952) ("The First Amendment does not require the state to eliminate every vestige of cooperation between religion and government."). In *People v. Smith*, 343 Ill. 159 (1930), the Illinois Supreme Court held that a public school could not permit religious services on its premises, reasoning that such use would violate the state constitutional prohibition against state support of religion. *Id.* at 165-66. Similarly, in *State ex rel. Douglas v. Smith*, 188 Ind. 100 (1917), the Indiana Supreme Court concluded that allowing religious services in a public school would constitute an impermissible establishment of religion. *Id.* at 107-08.

The Good News Club’s activities are not mere discussions of morality from a religious perspective; they are religious worship. The Club’s meetings include singing hymns, reciting scripture, and praying—activities that fall squarely within the definition of religious worship. *See* *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (noting that "singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles" are forms of religious speech). This Court has long recognized that public schools may not be used for religious worship. *See* *McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 211 (1948) ("The state may not employ religious means to achieve secular ends."). The Club’s proposed use of school facilities is indistinguishable from religious worship, and Milford’s denial of access was therefore constitutional.

**II. MILFORD’S COMMUNITY USE POLICY IS NEUTRAL AND GENERALLY APPLICABLE, AND IT DOES NOT DISCRIMINATE ON THE BASIS OF VIEWPOINT.**

Milford’s policy prohibits the use of school facilities for religious purposes. This policy is neutral and generally applicable—it does not target the Good News Club based on its viewpoint but rather applies to all religious uses of school facilities. *See* *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940) ("The First Amendment does not permit a state to regulate the content of speech in a way that favors some religious viewpoints over others."). In *Davis v. Beason*, 133 U.S. 333 (1890), the Court upheld a law that prohibited polygamy, even though it burdened a religious practice, because the law was neutral and generally applicable. *Id.* at 342-43.

Milford’s policy is likewise neutral and generally applicable. It does not single out the Good News Club for disfavored treatment but applies equally to all religious groups seeking to use school facilities for worship. *See* *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 70 (1953) ("The Constitution does not forbid treating religious groups the same as other groups."). The Club’s claim that Milford discriminated against it based on viewpoint is therefore without merit.

**III. PERMITTING THE GOOD NEWS CLUB TO USE SCHOOL FACILITIES WOULD VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.**

Permitting the Good News Club to use school facilities for religious worship would violate the Establishment Clause. In *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Court held that the Establishment Clause prohibits public schools from aiding or subsidizing religious activities. *Id.* at 15-16. Similarly, in *McCollum*, the Court struck down a program that allowed religious instructors to teach in public schools, reasoning that it violated the Establishment Clause. *Id.* at 211-12.

Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club is consistent with these precedents. Allowing the Club to use school facilities for religious worship would constitute an impermissible establishment of religion. *See* *Zorach*, 347 U.S. at 32 ("The First Amendment does not require the state to be hostile to religion."). Milford’s policy ensures that public resources are not used to advance or inhibit religion, and it is therefore constitutional.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club was constitutional. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
