# ARGUMENT

**OSHA LACKS JURISDICTION TO CITE UNINSPECTED VESSELS BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD ALREADY EXERCISES STATUTORY AUTHORITY OVER THE WORKING CONDITIONS OF SEAMEN**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Congress vested that authority in the Coast Guard, which by statute "shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States." 14 U.S.C. § 2. This Court should hold, as the Fifth Circuit correctly held below, that the Coast Guard's exercise of that authority is exclusive with respect to the working conditions of seamen, and that this exclusivity does not depend on whether the particular vessel happens to be "inspected" or "uninspected."

**A. This Court's Precedent Establishes an Industry-Wide Exemption for Seamen Aboard Vessels in Navigation**

The Fifth Circuit has twice held that "OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation." *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826-27 (5th Cir. 1983); *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980). In *Clary*, this Court considered a drilling barge materially indistinguishable from Rig 52 and held that the Coast Guard, not OSHA, is "the federal agency with statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen." 609 F.2d at 1122. *Donovan* reaffirmed that holding in the broadest terms, explaining that "[n]othing in OSHA shall apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels," and that "the Coast Guard's comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen's working conditions" creates "an industry-wide exemption [from OSHA] for seamen serving on vessels operating on navigable waters." 720 F.2d at 826-27.

That industry-wide approach reflects sound statutory interpretation. Section 4(b)(1) reflects "a general Congressional intent to forbid overlapping regulation of working conditions in any given industry by multiple federal agencies," because "[s]uch redundant control programs offer little except confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise." *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 827. A vessel-by-vessel or hazard-by-hazard inquiry into whether the Coast Guard has issued a regulation addressing the precise condition at issue would produce exactly the anomaly this Court warned against in *Donovan*—"steaming in and out of OSHA coverage" depending on which agency's regulations happen to cover a given hazard on a given day. *Id.* at 829. Congress intended instead that "a single, uniform set of rules should govern the maritime workplace," and because OSHA's own geographic and jurisdictional limitations preclude it from supplying that uniform rule, "it must be those of the Coast Guard." *Id.*

**B. The Distinction Between Inspected and Uninspected Vessels Does Not Defeat the Coast Guard's Exclusive Jurisdiction**

The Secretary's principal argument—that the Coast Guard's authority over uninspected vessels is too "minimal" to trigger the section 4(b)(1) exemption—cannot be squared with the statute or with the Coast Guard's actual practice. The Coast Guard is not a stranger to uninspected vessels. Congress expressly authorized it to issue safety regulations for such vessels covering fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors and backfire traps, ventilation of engine and fuel-tank compartments, and emergency alerting and locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. Pursuant to that authority, the Coast Guard has promulgated regulations addressing lifesaving equipment, fire-extinguishing equipment, backfire flame control, ventilation, cooking and heating systems, safety orientation, casualty reporting, and signaling lights for uninspected vessels. 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.*, 26.01 *et seq.* The Coast Guard's regulatory reach, in other words, is defined by category of vessel and category of seaman, not by whether a comprehensive inspection regime happens to attach.

Nothing in *Clary* suggests that its holding turns on inspected status. The vessel there, like Rig 52, was a drilling barge, and there is no indication in that opinion that it was an inspected vessel. As the Fifth Circuit correctly observed below, "the broad language of *Clary* does not turn on any such distinction," and the additional, narrower holding in *Clary*—that a particular OSHA regulation did not apply to a special-purpose vessel—does not displace *Clary*'s central holding that the Act "does not apply to the working conditions of seamen on vessels operating on the high seas." *Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1121-22.

**C. The Facts of This Case Confirm the Coast Guard's Primacy**

The Coast Guard, not OSHA, took "the lead role in investigating" the explosion aboard Rig 52, consistent with its jurisdiction under 46 U.S.C. § 6301 over marine casualties. OSHA's citation rested entirely on facts developed by the Coast Guard's own investigation—facts collected pursuant to the Coast Guard's exclusive statutory mandate to police the safety of seamen and vessels in navigable waters. To permit OSHA nonetheless to layer its own citation atop that investigation, based solely on the fortuity that Rig 52 was uninspected, would produce precisely the "confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise" that section 4(b)(1) was designed to avoid. *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 827.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be affirmed.
