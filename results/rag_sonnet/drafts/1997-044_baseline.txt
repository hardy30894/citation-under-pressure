# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge the Full Extent of Debt Traceable to Fraud, Including Treble Damages, Attorney's Fees, and Costs

### A. The Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts the Entire Debt "To the Extent Obtained By" Fraud

Section 523(a)(2)(A) provides that a discharge "does not discharge an individual debtor from any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." As this Court has instructed, "the starting point in every case involving construction of a statute" is its text. *Kelly v. Robinson*, 479 U.S. 36, 43 (1986). The words "any debt" are not limited to the value of the money or property originally received; they encompass every liability arising from the fraudulent conduct, including consequential damages such as the treble-damage award, attorney's fees, and costs assessed against Petitioner.

The Third Circuit correctly recognized that the phrase "to the extent obtained by" fraud modifies the *category* of debt Congress intended to except—debts for money, property, services, or credit procured through fraud—not the *quantum* of damages recoverable once fraud is established. As the court below explained, adopting the Eleventh Circuit's reasoning in *In re St. Laurent*, the term "debt" is broad enough to include "a judgement requiring the payment of both compensatory and punitive damages," and such a judgment is therefore "not dischargeable." Pet. App. (quoting *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 679 (11th Cir. 1993)). This textual reading finds direct support in *Pennsylvania Department of Public Welfare v. Davenport*, 495 U.S. 552 (1990), where this Court held that "[r]estitution obligations constitute debts within the meaning of § 101(11) of the Bankruptcy Code," id. at 552, confirming that the Code's definition of "debt" is expansive and encompasses liabilities flowing from a debtor's wrongful conduct, not merely the bare principal sum.

### B. This Court's Precedents Confirm That the Entire Fraud-Related Liability Is Nondischargeable

This Court's decision in *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), forecloses any contrary reading. There, the debtor "had defrauded [petitioners] in connection with the sale of certain corporate securities," and a jury "awarded them actual and punitive damages." Id. at 279. This Court held that the entire fraud judgment—including the punitive damages component—was subject to the nondischargeability provisions of § 523(a), reasoning that a defrauded creditor need only prove the elements of fraud "by a preponderance of the evidence." Id. Nothing in *Grogan* suggests that punitive or enhanced damages are severable from the "debt" arising from fraud; to the contrary, the decision treats the fraud judgment as a unitary nondischargeable obligation.

*Grogan*'s rationale rests on the Code's "fresh start" policy, which extends only to the "honest but unfortunate debtor." Id. at 286–87. A debtor who defrauds his victims is neither honest nor merely unfortunate. Allowing him to discharge the very damages the legal system imposes as a consequence of his fraud—damages that are, by definition, tied to and measured by the fraudulent conduct—would permit the fraudfeasor to use the bankruptcy laws as a shield rather than a sword for honest debtors. See *United States v. Sotelo*, 436 U.S. 268, 269 (1978) (rejecting attempts to use discharge provisions to escape liabilities Congress intended to survive bankruptcy).

This Court's decision in *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59 (1995), reinforces this reading by construing § 523(a)(2)(A) according to common-law fraud principles rather than technical limitations. The Court there examined the "level of reliance that § 523(a)(2)(A) requires," id. at 59, and looked to background common-law tort principles to interpret the statute's scope. Under common law, a defrauded plaintiff is entitled to recover the full measure of damages proximately caused by the fraud—not merely the value of money or property transferred. Reading § 523(a)(2)(A) consistently with these common-law antecedents, as *Field* commands, requires that the "debt" excepted from discharge include the complete panoply of damages the fraud victim is entitled to recover under applicable law, here the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act's treble-damages and fee-shifting provisions.

### C. Statutory Enhanced Damages Foreseeably Flow From the Fraud and Are Part of the Debt "Obtained By" That Fraud

The Third Circuit properly distinguished discretionary punitive damages from the mandatory treble-damages regime at issue here, observing that "the non-compensatory aspect of the award in this case was codified and therefore entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations to the plaintiffs." Pet. App. In this sense, the enhanced damages "may properly be seen as the debt he incurred through his conduct, rather than as punishment." Id. This reasoning tracks *Kelly*'s admonition that courts must look to the substance of a state-law remedy, not merely its label, when assessing its relationship to the underlying wrong. See 479 U.S. at 43 (rejecting reliance on isolated statutory language divorced from context). Because Cohen knew—or was recklessly indifferent to the fact—that violating the rent-control ordinance would trigger the Consumer Fraud Act's treble-damages provision, that liability is as much a product of his fraud as the underlying overcharges themselves.

### D. Permitting Discharge of Fraud-Enhanced Damages Would Undermine the Structure and Purpose of the Code

Finally, treating treble damages, fees, and costs as dischargeable would create an anomalous incentive: a debtor who commits fraud would face *less* total liability in bankruptcy than one who does not, since the very damages designed to punish and deter fraud would evaporate upon a Chapter 7 filing while compensatory damages for ordinary breach would not. This Court has repeatedly refused to construe the Code to produce such incongruous results. See *United States v. Energy Resources Co.*, 495 U.S. 545 (1990) (interpreting Code provisions to align with sensible congressional purposes rather than technical distinctions). The nondischargeability of the full $94,147.50 judgment against Cohen—including treble damages, fees, and costs—gives effect to Congress's clear intent that fraudfeasors, unlike honest debtors, may not use the Bankruptcy Code to escape the consequences of their wrongdoing.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
