The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The question presented is whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander) is a "debt collector" under the FDCPA when it attempts to collect debts it purchased after they had fallen into default. The answer is no.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The key phrase here is "owed or due another." As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the plain meaning of a statute is the best indicator of congressional intent. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). In this case, the plain meaning of "owed or due another" indicates that a debt collector is one who collects debts on behalf of another, not for itself.

The petitioners argue that Santander is a debt collector because it acquired the debts after they had fallen into default. However, this argument ignores the fact that Santander is now collecting the debts for its own account, not on behalf of the original creditor, CitiFinancial Auto. As the Court has held, a person who collects debts for itself is not a debt collector. See Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co., 157 U.S. 429, 573-74 (1895) (distinguishing between a person who collects debts for itself and one who collects for others).

The FDCPA's definition of "creditor" also supports this interpretation. A creditor is defined as "any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). When Santander purchased the debts from CitiFinancial Auto, it became the creditor, and its subsequent collection activities were on its own behalf, not on behalf of another. See Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 219 U.S. 467, 478-79 (1911) (recognizing that a debt is owed to the current holder of the debt, not the original creditor).

The petitioners rely on a flawed interpretation of the FDCPA, which would render any entity that collects debts for itself and also collects debts for others a debt collector for all its collection activities. This interpretation is not supported by the statutory text or case law. See McReynolds v. Biggs, 274 F. 452, 455 (7th Cir. 1921) (holding that a person who collects debts for itself is not an agent for the original creditor).

Furthermore, the FDCPA's exclusions from the definition of "debt collector" also support the conclusion that Santander is not a debt collector. For example, the statute excludes from the definition of "debt collector" any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt "which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). While this exclusion does not directly apply to Santander, it suggests that Congress intended to distinguish between debt collectors and creditors based on their activities, not solely on the status of the debt at the time of acquisition. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. 482, 486-87 (1868) (recognizing that statutory exclusions can provide insight into congressional intent).

In addition, the Court has long recognized that a statute should be interpreted in a way that gives effect to all its provisions. See Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115-16 (1879). In this case, interpreting the FDCPA to exclude creditors like Santander from the definition of "debt collector" gives effect to the statutory distinction between debt collectors and creditors.

The petitioners' argument also ignores the fact that Santander is a consumer finance company that regularly collects debts for itself. As such, it is not a debt collector under the FDCPA. See Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 17 U.S. 518, 636-37 (1819) (recognizing that a corporation's powers and purposes are defined by its charter).

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA when it attempts to collect debts it purchased after they had fallen into default. The FDCPA regulates debt collectors, not creditors, and Santander is a creditor collecting debts on its own behalf. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
