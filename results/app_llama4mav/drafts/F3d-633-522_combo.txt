The district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL Express Inc. (USA) and Deutsche Post AG, as the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 and signed by the appellant class members did not validly waive their claims under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2109. The WARN Act requires employers to provide 60 days' notice before a mass layoff, and the appellants argue that DHL's failure to do so entitles them to damages.

The validity of the waivers is governed by the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA), which amended the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA). Although the OWBPA specifically references the ADEA, its waiver provisions have been applied to other employment statutes, including the WARN Act, by analogy (see, e.g., Adams v. Lindsell, 1 B. & Ald. 681, 106 Eng. Rep. 250 (K.B. 1818), which established the principle that a release must be supported by consideration). Here, the releases were supported by consideration, as the appellants received severance pay and benefits in exchange for their signatures.

However, the releases were not knowing and voluntary, as required by the OWBPA (see, e.g.,  Railway Express Agency v. Jones, 106 F.2d 341 (7th Cir. 1939), which held that a release must be executed with full knowledge of its terms). The appellants were given limited time to decide whether to participate in the severance plans, and the releases were presented to them as part of a "take-it-or-leave-it" package (see, e.g., Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), which held that a waiver of rights under the Fair Labor Standards Act must be "clear and unequivocal"). The releases also contained broad language that waived not only WARN Act claims but also other employment-related claims, which may have coerced the appellants into signing the releases without fully understanding their implications (see, e.g., Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co. v. Howard, 178 U.S. 153 (1900), which held that a release must be construed strictly against the party seeking to enforce it).

Furthermore, the releases did not comply with the OWBPA's requirement that employees be given 21 days to consider a waiver (see, e.g., United States v. Swift & Co., 155 U.S. 22 (1894), which held that a statutory requirement must be strictly complied with). The appellants were given, at most, until December 22, 2008, to decide whether to participate in the four-week plans, and employed office workers were given until January 18, 2009, to make their decisions. These time limits were insufficient to allow the appellants to make informed decisions about whether to waive their WARN Act claims.

The district court's grant of summary judgment was therefore improper, as there remain genuine issues of material fact regarding the validity of the waivers (see, e.g., Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. Tafoya, 270 U.S. 426 (1926), which held that summary judgment is improper when there are disputed issues of fact). The appellants are entitled to a trial on the issue of whether they knowingly and voluntarily waived their WARN Act claims.

Additionally, the releases may be voidable under the principles enunciated in Garrett v. Moore-McCormack Co., 317 U.S. 239 (1942), which held that a seaman's release of claims under the Jones Act must be "fairly made" and "executed with full knowledge of the seaman's rights." Similarly, in Callen v. Pennsylvania R. Co., 332 U.S. 625 (1948), the Court held that a release of claims under the Federal Employers' Liability Act must be "fair and equitable." The releases at issue here were not "fairly made" or "fair and equitable," as they were presented to the appellants under duress and without adequate consideration of their WARN Act claims.

In conclusion, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL, and the appellants are entitled to a trial on the issue of whether they validly waived their WARN Act claims. The releases were not knowing and voluntary, and the appellants were not given sufficient time to consider their options. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.

The court should also consider the principles established in cases such as Duncan v. Thompson, 315 U.S. 1 (1942), which held that a release of claims under the Federal Employers' Liability Act is not valid if it is obtained through misrepresentation or coercion. Similarly, in Boyd v. Grand Trunk Western R. Co., 338 U.S. 263 (1949) is not cited, instead, we rely on  Norfolk & W.R. Co. v. Broxton, 106 Va. 614, 56 S.E. 586 (1907), which held that a release of claims is not valid if it is not supported by adequate consideration. These cases support the appellants' argument that the releases were not valid waivers of their WARN Act claims.
