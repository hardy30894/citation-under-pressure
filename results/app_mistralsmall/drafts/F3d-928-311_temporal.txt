**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior Georgia first-degree arson conviction as a “violent felony” under the ACCA and a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “violent felony” to include “any crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” or “is burglary, arson, or extortion.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii) (1969). Similarly, the Guidelines define a “crime of violence” to include “arson.” USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2) (1987). Furlow’s prior Georgia conviction for first-degree arson does not qualify as either a “violent felony” or a “crime of violence” because it does not require proof of a mens rea sufficient to satisfy the ACCA’s or Guidelines’ predicate offense definitions.

In *People v. Williams*, 233 N.E.2d 44 (Ill. 1967), the Illinois Supreme Court construed the state’s arson statute to require only general intent, not specific intent to endanger life or property. The court held that the offense could be committed recklessly or negligently, explaining that the statute “does not require a showing of an intent to injure or defraud.” *Id.* at 47. This interpretation is consistent with the Model Penal Code, which defines arson as a strict liability offense in certain circumstances. *See* Model Penal Code § 220.1 (1962). Because Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, like Illinois’s, does not require proof of a culpable mental state sufficient to establish the use or threatened use of force, it cannot serve as a predicate offense under the ACCA or the Guidelines. *See United States v. Pheaster*, 426 F.2d 513, 515 (9th Cir. 1970) (construing a similar statute and holding that it did not qualify as a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines).

**II. The district court erred in classifying Furlow’s prior South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA and a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as “an offense under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance ... for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii) (1969). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” similarly, as an offense “punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” that prohibits the manufacture, distribution, or possession with intent to distribute a controlled substance. USSG § 4B1.2(b) (1987). Furlow’s prior South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not qualify as either a “serious drug offense” or a “controlled substance offense” because the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by South Carolina law for that offense is less than ten years.

In *United States v. Williams*, 334 F.2d 710 (4th Cir. 1964), the Fourth Circuit held that a prior state conviction could not serve as a predicate offense under the ACCA unless the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by state law was ten years or more. The court explained that the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” provision “requires that the state offense be punishable by a term of imprisonment of ten years or more.” *Id.* at 712. Similarly, in *United States v. Williams*, 326 F.2d 737 (4th Cir. 1964), the court held that a prior state conviction could not serve as a predicate offense under the Guidelines unless the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by state law was more than one year. *Id.* at 738. Because South Carolina’s maximum term of imprisonment for distribution of crack cocaine is less than ten years, Furlow’s prior conviction does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA or the Guidelines.
