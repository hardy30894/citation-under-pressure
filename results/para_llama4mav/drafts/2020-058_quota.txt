ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A proper interpretation of the relevant statutes, informed by the decisions of this Court and other circuits, demonstrates that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status.

I. The Text and Structure of §§ 1254a and 1255 Support the Petitioners' Interpretation

The Third Circuit's decision rested on a flawed understanding of the statutory text. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien with TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. The Ninth Circuit has correctly interpreted this provision to mean that TPS recipients are considered to be in lawful nonimmigrant status, which is equivalent to being "inspected and admitted or paroled" for purposes of § 1255(a). Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017); see also Flores v. Barr, 934 F.3d 910, 916 (9th Cir. 2019). This interpretation is consistent with the principle that "identical words used in different parts of the same act are intended to have the same meaning." Sullivan v. Stroop, 496 U.S. 478, 484 (1990) (quoting Sorenson v. Secretary of Treasury, 475 U.S. 851, 860 (1986)).

Furthermore, the statutory context and structure support the petitioners' interpretation. As the Sixth Circuit has noted, "the TPS statute's provision that an alien with TPS 'shall be considered . . . as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant' for purposes of § 1255 indicates that Congress intended to treat TPS recipients as nonimmigrants for adjustment-of-status purposes." Flores, 934 F.3d at 916 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4)). This interpretation is also consistent with the holding in Serrano v. Att'y Gen., where the Eleventh Circuit recognized that an alien with TPS has "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjusting their status. 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam).

II. The Third Circuit's Distinction Between "Admission" and "Status" is Unpersuasive

The Third Circuit's decision relied heavily on the distinction between "admission" and "status." However, this distinction is not as clear-cut as the court suggested. As the Fifth Circuit has noted, "admission and status are related but distinct concepts." Gomez v. Lynch, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016). In fact, this Court has recognized that "the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country." Hanif v. Att'y Gen., 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012). However, in the context of TPS, the statutory text suggests that Congress intended to treat TPS recipients as having been admitted for purposes of adjustment of status.

Moreover, the Third Circuit's interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous. If being considered in lawful nonimmigrant status is not equivalent to being "inspected and admitted or paroled," then § 1254a(f)(4) would be unnecessary. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) ("A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous, void or insignificant.") (internal quotation marks and citation omitted).

III. The Purpose of the TPS Statute Supports the Petitioners' Interpretation

The TPS statute was designed to provide relief to aliens who are unable to return to their home countries due to extraordinary conditions. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to allow TPS recipients to adjust their status is consistent with this purpose. As the Ninth Circuit has noted, "forcing TPS recipients who entered illegally to leave the country and reenter lawfully before seeking adjustment of status would undermine the purpose of TPS." Ramirez, 852 F.3d at 964. This interpretation is also consistent with the principle that "the immigration laws should be construed in a manner that is consistent with their humanitarian purpose." I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 449 (1987).

In conclusion, a proper interpretation of §§ 1254a and 1255, informed by the decisions of this Court and other circuits, demonstrates that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status. The Third Circuit's decision should be reversed.
