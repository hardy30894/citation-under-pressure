**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing this class action as moot. The court’s rigid application of the general rule that a class action becomes moot when the named plaintiff’s individual claim is satisfied—without exception—ignores the fundamental nature of a class action as a representative suit. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a class action is not a mere aggregation of individual claims but a separate procedural entity that continues to exist even when the named plaintiff’s personal stake is extinguished. *See Hartford Life Insurance Co. v. Ibs*, 237 U.S. 662, 672 (1915); *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. 288, 302 (1853). Because the class retains a live controversy, the district court had jurisdiction to consider Lucero’s motion for class certification.

**A. The Class Action Survives Mootness of the Named Plaintiff’s Individual Claim**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that a class action does not become moot solely because the named plaintiff’s individual claim has been resolved. In *Hartford Life Insurance Co. v. Ibs*, the Court ruled that a suit brought on behalf of a class “does not abate by the death of the plaintiff” and that the “controversy is not ended” merely because the plaintiff’s personal interest is satisfied. 237 U.S. at 672. Similarly, *Moore v. New York Cotton Exchange* established that a class representative may continue to represent the class even after his individual claim becomes moot, because the “rights of the class” are independent. 270 U.S. 593, 609 (1926). These decisions rest on the principle that a class action is a “representative suit” in which the class itself has a legally cognizable interest. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. at 302; *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble*, 255 U.S. 356, 367 (1921) (a class action judgment binds all class members, confirming the class’s separate legal existence).

The district court’s reliance on the general mootness rule misapplies these precedents. That rule applies only to individual suits, not to class actions where the class’s controversy remains alive. *See Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Langer*, 168 F.2d 182, 186 (8th Cir. 1948) (holding that a defendant’s payment to the named plaintiff does not moot the class action because the class retains a “continuing interest”). Here, Lucero’s motion for class certification was pending when the district court dismissed the action. The class’s claims—alleging systematic violations of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act—were not resolved by the offer of judgment to Lucero alone. The class’s controversy is justiciable, and the district court erred in failing to recognize it.

**B. The Defendant’s Offer of Judgment Is a Voluntary Cessation That Does Not Moot the Case**

The Supreme Court has long held that a defendant cannot moot a case by voluntarily ceasing the challenged conduct—or, as here, by offering to satisfy the named plaintiff’s claim—unless the defendant shows that the conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). An offer of judgment that has not been accepted and is no longer outstanding is precisely the kind of “voluntary cessation” that does not automatically moot a case. *See Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911) (holding that a case is not moot if the challenged action is “capable of repetition, yet evading review”). Bureau of Collection Recovery’s tactic of “picking off” the named plaintiff before class certification is a paradigmatic example of conduct that evades review because it can be repeated in every future class action. The district court’s dismissal thus contravenes the voluntary cessation doctrine.

**C. The Named Plaintiff Retains a Personal Stake as a Class Representative**

Even if Lucero’s individual monetary claim were satisfied, he retains a personal stake in the class certification decision. The Supreme Court has recognized that the right to represent a class is a “procedural right” that is distinct from the underlying claim. *See Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555, 560 (2d Cir. 1968) (holding that the named plaintiff’s interest in class certification survives the defendant’s tender of full relief). Lucero’s motion for class certification was filed before the motion to dismiss and reflected his ongoing duty to represent the class. The district court’s refusal to consider that motion violated his right to seek certification on behalf of others similarly situated.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred by treating this class action as an ordinary individual suit. The class’s claims remain alive, and the defendant’s offer of judgment does not moot them. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to consider Lucero’s motion for class certification.
