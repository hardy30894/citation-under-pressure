**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment forbids a State from depriving any person of “life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has long recognized that clemency proceedings, though discretionary, implicate constitutionally protected interests when they are integral to the state’s punishment scheme. *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956) (due process applies to “any state procedure which is ‘an adjunct of the trial’ or ‘part of the machinery for seeing that [a] trial is fair’”). In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 18 (1979), the Court held that parole release proceedings, though not part of the adjudicatory process, nonetheless create a protected liberty interest where state law conditions release on specific findings. Similarly, Ohio’s clemency process, by providing a structured review mechanism culminating in a decision that may result in commutation or reprieve, creates a cognizable liberty interest. *See id.* at 12 (liberty interest arises where state law “places substantive limits on official discretion”); *see also* *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974) (prison disciplinary proceedings may implicate liberty interests where they affect “a prisoner’s liberty to be free from confinement conditions which affect the duration of his sentence”).

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that clemency lacks constitutional significance. *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), upon which the court relied, did not address whether clemency proceedings themselves create a protected interest; it merely held that Connecticut’s practice did not create a federally enforceable liberty interest. *Id.* at 465. The Court distinguished *Greenholtz* on the ground that parole release was governed by mandatory criteria, whereas clemency remained discretionary. *Id.* at 463. But Ohio’s clemency process, though discretionary, is structured and institutionalized—indeed, the state itself acknowledges that clemency is integral to the state’s punishment scheme. *See Griffin*, 351 U.S. at 18. Thus, Woodard possesses a protected liberty interest in the clemency process that demands due process protections.

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A “CHOICE” THAT COMPELS WAIVER OF THE RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long held that the government may not condition a benefit on the waiver of constitutional rights unless the waiver is voluntary and uncoerced. *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 404 (1963) (“[T]he Court must look to the coercive effect of the enactment as a whole, not merely to its bare language.”); *see also* *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972) (state may not condition public employment on waiver of First Amendment rights). The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that Woodard faces no compulsion because he may refuse to answer questions at the interview ignores the coercive reality: participation in the interview is presented as the only meaningful avenue to seek clemency. *See* Ohio Adult Parole Authority, Death Penalty Clemency Procedure § 4(C)(2) (1994) (interview is the primary means of presenting one’s case). This creates a classic “Hobson’s choice”: either participate and risk self-incrimination, or forgo participation and forfeit any meaningful opportunity to plead for mercy.

This Court has condemned such coercive conditions. In *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570 (1968), the Court struck down a federal statute that imposed the death penalty only upon defendants who waived their right to a jury trial, finding that the law “needlessly penalize[d] the assertion of constitutional rights.” *Id.* at 581. Similarly, in *Sipuel v. Board of Regents*, 332 U.S. 631 (1948), the Court invalidated Oklahoma’s refusal to admit a Black applicant to law school solely because she insisted on her constitutional right to equal protection. The principle is clear: the state may not condition a benefit on the relinquishment of constitutional rights. *See also* *Adler v. Board of Education*, 342 U.S. 485, 492 (1952) (state may not penalize public employees for invoking constitutional rights).

**III. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS, AS APPLIED TO WOODARD, VIOLATES THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.**

The Fifth Amendment’s self-incrimination clause applies not only to formal criminal proceedings but also to any proceeding where the state seeks to compel testimony that may later be used against the witness. *See* *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 6 (1964) (incorporating the Fifth Amendment against the states); *see also* *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616, 633 (1886) (the Fourth and Fifth Amendments protect against the compelled production of incriminating evidence). Ohio’s clemency process, by offering an interview as the primary means of presenting one’s case, effectively compels Woodard to choose between asserting his Fifth Amendment rights and seeking clemency. This is precisely the sort of coercion the Fifth Amendment forbids.

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Ex parte Hull*, 312 U.S. 546 (1941), is misplaced. *Ex parte Hull* involved access to courts and did not address whether the state could condition a benefit on participation in a proceeding that risked self-incrimination. Moreover, *Ex parte Hull* was decided long before the events at issue in this case, and its reasoning is inapplicable to the clemency context, where the stakes are far higher—life itself.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Woodard’s due process and Fifth Amendment rights were violated by Ohio’s clemency process. The judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be affirmed in part, reversed in part, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
