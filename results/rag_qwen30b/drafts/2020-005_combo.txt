**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has established minimum contacts with the forum state such that the exercise of jurisdiction does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle requires that the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s activities within the forum state. *Id.* at 318. Here, Ford Motor Company has no such contacts with Montana. It neither manufactured, sold, nor distributed the vehicle in Montana. The vehicle was assembled in Kentucky, first sold in Washington, and later transferred to an Oregon resident before being brought into Montana. The sole connection between Ford and Montana is the fact that the vehicle was driven there—on a highway—when the tire failed. That isolated event, standing alone, does not satisfy the constitutional minimum.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself” of the privilege of doing business in Montana by advertising and selling parts within the state is legally unsound and contrary to settled precedent. *See* *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 355 (1927) (“The process of a court of one State cannot run into another and summon a party there domiciled to respond to proceedings against him.”). A corporation may not be subjected to personal jurisdiction in a state where it has not purposefully directed its activities, nor where its contacts are merely random, fortuitous, or attenuated. *See* *Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad*, 346 U.S. 338, 340 (1953) (“The decision bars a nonresident injured party from seeking damages, on allegation of diversity, from a nonresident motor operator or owner in the United States District Court having jurisdiction over the place of the accident.”). The mere fact that a product reaches a state through the ordinary course of commerce does not create jurisdiction. *See* *Metzger Motor Car Co. v. Parrott*, 233 U.S. 36, 40 (1914) (“The injuries, complained of, were caused by the negligence of a chauffeur in operating an automobile owned by the defendant company, resulting in a collision on the highway with plaintiff’s horses and the cart in which he with two others were riding.”). The court in *Metzger* upheld jurisdiction only because the defendant had a physical presence and engaged in business in the state—activities not present here.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s sale of parts in Montana is equally misplaced. A corporation’s sale of parts to a third party in a state does not constitute purposeful availment of the forum when those parts are not used in the state and the sale is not part of a broader, ongoing business relationship with the forum. *See* *Wuchter v. Pizzutti*, 276 U.S. 13, 16 (1928) (holding that a nonresident motorist who operates a vehicle in New Jersey without a license may be subject to suit there only if he has accepted the privilege of driving in the state, thereby consenting to service of process). The sale of parts, without more, does not constitute such a consent. The mere fact that Ford sells parts to dealerships in Montana does not mean it has purposefully directed its activities toward Montana in a way that would make it reasonable to expect suit there. *See* *Clark v. Wells*, 203 U.S. 164, 170 (1906) (“When the jurisdiction of the state court was terminated by the removal that court had seized upon the attached property with the right to hold it to answer such judgment as might be rendered.”). The state’s interest in regulating the sale of parts is not sufficient to justify jurisdiction over a claim arising from a product’s use in a different state.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires that the defendant have fair notice that it may be haled into court in a particular jurisdiction. *See* *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 445 (1952) (“The essence of the issue here, at the constitutional level, is a like one of general fairness to the corporation.”). Ford had no reason to anticipate that it would be subject to suit in Montana, where it has no offices, no employees, no sales force, and no ongoing business. The claim here arises from a tire failure that occurred during a drive on a Montana highway—no connection to Ford’s activities in Montana. *See* *Western Loan & Savings Co. v. Butte & Boston Consolidated Mining Co.*, 210 U.S. 368, 374 (1908) (“The court has no jurisdiction of the person of the defendant.”). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision effectively subjects Ford to jurisdiction based on the mere foreseeability that its product might be driven in Montana—foreseeability that is too attenuated to satisfy due process.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Minnesota Commercial Men’s Assn. v. Benn*, 261 U.S. 140 (1923), is also inapposite. In *Benn*, the insurance contract was executed and performed in Minnesota, and the defendant had consented to service of process through the Secretary of State. *Id.* at 143. The court held that the state court had jurisdiction because the defendant had consented to service. *Id.* at 144. Here, Ford has not consented to service in Montana. It is not incorporated in Montana, does not maintain an office there, and has not designated an agent for service. The mere fact that Ford sells parts in Montana does not constitute such consent. *See* *Provident Savings Life Assurance Society v. Ford*, 114 U.S. 635, 638 (1885) (“It is well known that corporations of the character of the defendant, desirous of doing business in a State other than that in which they have their domicil, are generally required to have an agent therein to receive service of process for them.”). Ford has not done so in Montana.
