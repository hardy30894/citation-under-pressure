## ARGUMENT

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague—does not apply retroactively to convictions that were final before that decision. Under this Court’s settled retroactivity doctrine, a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies only to cases still on direct review, not to collateral challenges like Welch’s. *See Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965) (holding that *Mapp v. Ohio*’s exclusionary rule applied prospectively only, because the rule was not “an absolute prerequisite to a fair trial”). The same principle governs here.

Welch’s conviction became final in 2008, long before *Johnson* was decided in 2015. A conviction is final when the availability of direct appeal is exhausted. *See Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293, 299 (1967) (addressing retroactivity of *United States v. Wade*). For defendants whose convictions are already final, new constitutional rules “should not be applied retroactively” unless the rule is “fundamental to the integrity of the fact-finding process” or “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal lawmaking authority to proscribe.” *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244, 250 (1969) (internal quotation marks omitted). *Johnson* does not satisfy either exception.

First, *Johnson* did not announce a watershed rule that “implicates the fundamental fairness of the trial.” *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 639. The vagueness holding concerns the definition of “violent felony” for sentencing enhancement, not the accuracy of the guilt determination. The rule does not affect whether Welch committed the underlying offense of being a felon in possession; it merely narrows the range of prior convictions that can trigger a mandatory minimum sentence. This Court has consistently declined to give retroactive effect to rules that do not go to the reliability of the verdict. *See, e.g., Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719, 730 (1966) (holding *Miranda* warnings not retroactive because they are “not an absolute prerequisite to a fair trial”); *Desist*, 394 U.S. at 254 (holding *Katz* not retroactive because it does not “affect the integrity of the fact-finding process”).

Second, *Johnson* did not “place[] the conduct at issue beyond the reach of the criminal law.” *Stovall*, 388 U.S. at 297. The residual clause’s invalidity does not mean that Welch’s prior Florida robbery conviction is now immune from consideration. It simply means that the sentencing court must apply a different statutory provision—the elements clause or the enumerated-offenses clause—to determine whether the conviction qualifies as a violent felony. The conduct that *Johnson* addresses is definitional, not jurisdictional. *Cf. Desist*, 394 U.S. at 250 (distinguishing rules that “prohibit[] punishment of certain primary conduct” from those that “merely regulate the manner in which the state may proceed”).

Finally, the retroactivity analysis is the same regardless of whether the new rule is characterized as “substantive” or “procedural” under pre-1970 doctrine. This Court in *Linkletter* and *Desist* applied a balancing test that weighed the purpose of the new rule, the reliance of the states on the old rule, and the burden on the administration of justice. *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 636–37. All three factors favor prospective application here. The purpose of *Johnson*—to eliminate arbitrary sentencing—is not undermined by denying retroactive effect to final convictions; the reliance interest in the residual clause was substantial, as it had been applied for decades; and reopening thousands of ACCA sentences would impose an intolerable burden on the federal courts.

Because Welch’s conviction was final before *Johnson*, and because the *Johnson* rule does not meet the narrow exceptions for retroactive application under this Court’s pre-1970 precedents, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
