**ARGUMENT**

**THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A TRIAL COURT TO APPLY A HEIGHTENED “REASONED CHOICE” STANDARD TO DETERMINE THE COMPETENCY OF A DEFENDANT TO ENTER A PLEA OF GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL.**

**A. The Competency Standard for Pleading Guilty or Waiving Counsel Is the Same as the Standard for Standing Trial.**

The Constitution requires that a criminal defendant be competent before he may be tried, convicted, or sentenced. *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). The test for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Id.* This standard applies equally when a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 398 (1993). There is no constitutional basis for a higher, “reasoned choice” standard.

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that competency to waive counsel or plead guilty requires a “higher level of mental functioning” than competency to stand trial. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 266 (9th Cir. 1992). This Court has never distinguished between the competency required for trial and that required for a guilty plea or waiver of counsel. To the contrary, the “competence that is required of a defendant seeking to plead guilty or to waive counsel is the competence to waive a right, not the competence to participate in a trial.” *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399 n.10. The *Dusky* standard adequately ensures that a defendant’s waiver is knowing and voluntary.

**B. The “Reasoned Choice” Standard Imposes an Unwarranted Burden on Trial Courts.**

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard would require trial courts to assess not merely whether a defendant understands the proceedings and can assist counsel, but whether he can make a “reasoned choice” among alternatives. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 266. This standard is both vague and unworkable. It invites post-hoc litigation over the quality of a defendant’s decision-making, rather than focusing on whether the defendant understood the nature of the proceedings and the consequences of his choices.

This Court has long held that a valid waiver of constitutional rights need only be “knowing, intelligent, and voluntary.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The *Dusky* standard already incorporates these requirements. A defendant who understands the charges, the potential penalties, and the rights he is relinquishing is competent to waive those rights, even if his decision appears unwise or contrary to his interests. *See Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975) (defendant need not have “the skill and experience of a lawyer” to waive counsel). The “reasoned choice” standard improperly conflates competency with the wisdom of the defendant’s decision.

**C. The Trial Court Properly Found Moran Competent Under the Correct Standard.**

The record demonstrates that the trial court had no good faith doubt about Moran’s competency. Two court-appointed psychiatrists examined Moran and concluded he was competent to stand trial. Dr. O’Gorman found that Moran was “knowledgeable of the charges,” could “assist his attorney,” and was “fully cognizant of the penalties if convicted.” Dr. Jurasky similarly found Moran capable of aiding counsel. These evaluations satisfied the *Dusky* standard.

Moran’s monosyllabic responses and medication did not create a good faith doubt. The trial court inquired about Moran’s medication, and Moran acknowledged he was on medication but did not claim it impaired his understanding. The court was not required to conduct a further inquiry absent evidence of incompetence. *See Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 181 (1975) (trial court need not hold competency hearing unless there is “bona fide doubt” as to competency).

**D. The Post-Conviction Hearing Confirmed Moran’s Competency.**

Even if the trial court erred, the state post-conviction hearing cured any defect. The state court held an evidentiary hearing and found Moran competent to waive counsel and plead guilty. The Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that the state court applied the wrong standard. The state court’s order referenced the psychiatric reports, which applied the *Dusky* standard—the correct constitutional standard. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 398. The state court’s finding is entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *See Maggio v. Fulford*, 462 U.S. 111, 117 (1983) (per curiam) (state court competency findings are factual determinations entitled to deference).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
