**ARGUMENT**

I. Missouri’s mandatory retirement requirement for state judges does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA).

The ADEA expressly excludes from its definition of “employee” “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (1988). Appellants concede they were not elected, but contend they are not “appointees on the policymaking level.” This contention is meritless.

The policymaking-level exception was drawn from the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission’s (EEOC) prior regulations and reflects Congress’s intent to exclude high-level advisors and officials who exercise significant discretion in shaping government policy. *See, e.g., Stark v. Conn. Real Estate Comm’n*, 169 F. Supp. 712, 715 (D. Conn. 1959) (interpreting similar exclusionary language in federal employment statutes). The legislative history confirms that Congress sought to exempt “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments, such as cabinet officers, and persons with comparable responsibilities.” H.R. Rep. No. 92-805, at 12 (1972), reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180.

Judges, particularly those in appellate courts, routinely exercise policymaking authority by interpreting statutes, developing common law, and resolving ambiguities in legal principles. *See, e.g., Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (“There is no federal general common law.”); *Swift v. Tyson*, 41 U.S. 1, 18–19 (1842) (acknowledging judicial lawmaking in commercial law). As the Court of Appeals for the First Circuit recognized in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), judges “necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law.” This is policymaking in its most fundamental sense.

Moreover, Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan exercise significant administrative and supervisory duties. *See, e.g., In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc) (Supreme Court resolving internal circuit court disputes and adopting administrative rules). Such responsibilities are quintessentially policymaking. *See Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 629 (1935) (recognizing that officials with policymaking authority are subject to different treatment under federal employment laws).

Because Missouri judges are “appointees on the policymaking level,” they fall within the ADEA’s exclusion. *See, e.g., Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (holding that judges are not covered by the ADEA), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141 (1988) (same).

II. Missouri’s mandatory retirement requirement for state judges does not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

A state law does not violate the Equal Protection Clause if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425–26 (1961). Mandatory retirement ages for judges have consistently been upheld under this deferential standard.

The Court has long recognized that judges perform uniquely demanding roles that require “mental and physical stamina.” *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 315 (1976) (per curiam). Mandatory retirement at age 70 is a rational means of ensuring that judges possess the requisite faculties to perform their duties. *See, e.g., Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 629 (upholding mandatory retirement for judges under rational basis review); *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980) (same).

This Court has repeatedly deferred to state judgments in structuring their judiciaries. *See, e.g., Holt Civic Club v. Tuscaloosa*, 439 U.S. 60, 71 (1979) (“[S]tate legislatures are entitled to draw lines which may admittedly hurt some localities more than others.”); *Goesaert v. Cleary*, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948) (upholding state law regulating bartending by gender). Missouri’s decision to require retirement at 70 is a rational policy choice to maintain judicial competence and efficiency.

The Court has also recognized that mandatory retirement ages for judges serve important institutional interests, such as reducing delays in the administration of justice and avoiding the “unpleasantness of selectively removing aged or infirm judges.” *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 627; *Malmed*, 621 F.2d at 572. These interests are legitimate and rationally furthered by the challenged provision.

Finally, the Court has refused to recognize a fundamental right to governmental employment. *San Antonio Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973). Appellants’ claim that they have a constitutional right to serve as judges past age 70 is therefore foreclosed. *See Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement*, 427 U.S. at 313 (rejecting substantive due process challenge to mandatory retirement age for state employees).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
