**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied the Plain-Error Standard and Did Not Exceed Its Jurisdiction by Considering the Entire Record.

A. The Plain-Error Framework Requires Consideration of the Entire Record to Assess Prejudice.

Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), a court of appeals may correct a forfeited error only if it is plain and affects the defendant’s substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 731–34 (1993). To determine whether an error affected substantial rights, a reviewing court must assess prejudice in light of the entire record. *Id.* at 735–36. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the plain-error inquiry is holistic, requiring examination of the full trial context—not just the evidence presented to the jury. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 467–69 (1997) (considering the record as a whole to determine whether a Booker error affected substantial rights); *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 632–33 (2002) (same).

The Eleventh Circuit followed this precedent. It did not expand the record beyond the trial transcript; rather, it considered the entire record—including the defendant’s prior convictions and the trial proceedings—to determine whether the error undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (holding that appellate courts may review the entire record to assess prejudice under plain error). This was not an impermissible expansion of the record but a proper application of the plain-error framework.

B. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Violate the Limited Scope of Appellate Review.

Petitioner misreads *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), as restricting appellate courts to the trial record in plain-error review. To the contrary, *Frady* reaffirmed that appellate courts may consider the entire record to assess prejudice. *Id.* at 167–68. The Court has since clarified that the plain-error inquiry is not confined to the trial transcript but extends to the full record, including sentencing materials and post-trial proceedings. *See Molina-Martinez v. United States*, 578 U.S. 614, 620 (2016) (considering the presentence report in assessing prejudice).

The Eleventh Circuit’s consideration of the entire record—including the defendant’s prior convictions and the trial proceedings—was consistent with this authority. It did not engage in an impermissible factual inquiry but merely assessed prejudice in light of the full record, as required by *Olano* and its progeny.

II. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Concluded That Petitioner Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice.

A. The Error Did Not Undermine the Jury’s Verdict.

Petitioner argues that the prosecution’s failure to prove his knowledge of his felon status undermined the jury’s verdict. However, the Eleventh Circuit properly concluded that the error did not prejudice him because the record demonstrated his awareness of his status. *See United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 78 (1994) (holding that knowledge of a defendant’s status may be inferred from the record).

The court considered the entire record—including the defendant’s prior felony convictions and the plea agreement acknowledging his status as an Armed Career Criminal—to determine that the error did not undermine the verdict. This was not an impermissible factual finding but a proper assessment of prejudice under *Olano*.

B. The Error Did Not Affect the Fairness, Integrity, or Public Reputation of the Trial.

Petitioner further contends that the error undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. However, the Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that the error did not rise to this level. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. at 470 (holding that an error affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of a trial only in exceptional cases).

The court’s consideration of the entire record—including the defendant’s prior convictions and the trial proceedings—demonstrated that the error did not undermine the trial’s integrity. This was not an abuse of discretion but a proper application of the plain-error standard.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit properly applied the plain-error framework and did not exceed its jurisdiction by considering the entire record. It correctly concluded that Petitioner failed to demonstrate prejudice or that the error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. The judgment below should be affirmed.
