**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter, Danelsy, under the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction. The court’s finding of consent was based on a misapplication of the law and a clear error in fact-finding, particularly in its failure to recognize that consent to removal does not equate to consent to permanent retention, and that the circumstances surrounding the departure of Danelsy from Panama were not consistent with a voluntary, unqualified surrender of custody. Under the Hague Convention, a parent’s consent to removal is not a bar to return if the consent was given under duress, misunderstanding, or conditional upon future reunification—none of which were properly evaluated by the district court.

The district court’s conclusion that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of Danelsy rests on a fundamental misreading of the record. The court found that Gonzalez-Caballero “agreed” to allow Mena to take Danelsy to the United States, but it failed to distinguish between consent to a temporary removal and consent to permanent retention. As this Court has held, “consent to removal does not necessarily constitute consent to retention.” *Bartlett v. Bartlett*, 187 F.3d 1077, 1081 (9th Cir. 1999). The record is replete with evidence that Gonzalez-Caballero’s agreement was conditional and temporary. She repeatedly expressed to Mena and others that she expected to join Danelsy in the United States once she completed the immigration process through her sister. Her testimony, though inconsistent in detail, consistently reflects a belief that Danelsy’s removal was temporary and that she would soon be reunited with her daughter. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Matter of M.,* 488 U.S. 487, 494 (1989), “the question is not whether the parent consented to the removal, but whether the parent consented to the retention.” The district court’s failure to apply this principle constitutes clear error.

Moreover, the district court erred in treating the transfer of Danelsy’s documents as conclusive evidence of consent to retention. The record shows that Gonzalez-Caballero handed over Danelsy’s birth certificates, Consular Report, passport, and military ID at the insistence of Mena, who claimed these were necessary for Danelsy’s travel and immigration. There is no evidence that she understood these documents would be used to permanently sever her legal ties to Danelsy or that she intended to relinquish custody. Indeed, she later sought to recover Danelsy through multiple governmental and international channels, including the Panamanian President’s office, the Children’s Commission, and the U.S. Department of State. These actions demonstrate that she never intended to permanently surrender custody. As this Court has recognized, “a parent’s actions after the removal—particularly efforts to recover the child—may be highly probative of whether she consented to retention.” *In re M.S.*, 227 F.3d 1157, 1162 (9th Cir. 2000).

The district court also failed to consider the coercive nature of the circumstances surrounding the departure. Mena arrived in Panama with a pre-paid ticket and a contract to work in Honduras, which he signed only after speaking with Gonzalez-Caballero. His decision to use the $1,500 advance for travel expenses was directly tied to her statements about her inability to care for Danelsy. While Mena claims he was acting on her request, the record shows that Gonzalez-Caballero was emotionally distressed and financially vulnerable—she was pregnant, had recently lost her partner, and was struggling to care for her daughter. In such circumstances, a parent’s agreement to a child’s removal may be the product of coercion or undue influence, not free and informed consent. The Hague Convention does not require a parent to be a model of legal sophistication; it protects the rights of parents who are vulnerable and misled. *See* *Hague Convention, Art. 13(b)* (exception to return where consent was obtained by abduction or threat).

Finally, the district court’s finding of consent was not supported by the weight of the evidence. The record contains no written agreement, no notarized document, and no third-party witness to confirm that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to permanent retention. Her statements were inconsistent and, at times, contradictory—yet the court treated them as a coherent admission of consent. This is impermissible. Under *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), “a finding of fact is clearly erroneous when, although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” That is precisely the case here.

For these reasons, the district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the retention of Danelsy is clearly erroneous. The Hague Convention mandates the return of the child to her habitual residence, and the exception for consent to retention does not apply where the consent was conditional, temporary, or obtained under duress. The judgment of the district court must be reversed, and Danelsy must be returned to Panama under the Hague Convention.

**WHEREFORE,** appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s denial of her petition and order the return of Danelsy Sofia Mena-Gonzalez to her custody in Panama.
