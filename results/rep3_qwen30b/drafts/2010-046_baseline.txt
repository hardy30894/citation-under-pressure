**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s population reduction order, issued in the context of a long-standing, constitutionally mandated remedial effort, is not only consistent with the PLRA but is compelled by the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment. The Ninth Circuit correctly upheld the order as a necessary and narrowly tailored remedy to a pervasive, ongoing constitutional violation. This Court should affirm.

### I. THE PLRA DOES NOT PROHIBIT A COURT FROM ORDERING POPULATION REDUCTION TO REMEDY EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS

The PLRA’s central provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), prohibits federal courts from entering “any order that affects the operation of a prison” unless the order is “narrowly drawn,” “no more extensive than necessary,” and “the least intrusive means” to remedy a constitutional violation. The State contends that population reduction is categorically barred by the PLRA. But this argument misreads the statute’s text and purpose.

The PLRA does not create a per se bar on population reduction. Rather, it requires that any injunctive relief be “narrowly drawn” and “the least intrusive means” to remedy a constitutional violation. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1). The Supreme Court has made clear that the PLRA “does not prohibit courts from ordering prison population reductions when such reductions are necessary to correct a constitutional violation.” *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 515 (2011). In *Plata*, the Court held that the district court’s order reducing California’s prison population to 137.5% of design capacity was “narrowly drawn” and “the least intrusive means” to remedy the severe overcrowding that caused unconstitutional medical care conditions. *Id.* at 515–16.

The order here is precisely the kind of remedial measure the PLRA was designed to accommodate. The district court did not order arbitrary or wholesale release of inmates. Instead, it ordered a reduction to a level that would allow the state to provide constitutionally adequate medical care—specifically, to eliminate the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations: overcrowding. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 510. The court’s order was not a policy directive but a judicial response to a finding of constitutional failure. As the Court emphasized in *Plata*, “when a prison system is so overcrowded that it cannot provide constitutionally adequate medical care, the court may order a reduction in population to remedy the violation.” *Id.* at 516.

The State’s argument that the PLRA precludes any population reduction is inconsistent with *Plata* and the plain language of the statute. The PLRA does not prohibit remedies that are necessary to correct constitutional violations; it only requires that such remedies be narrowly tailored. The population reduction order here satisfies that standard. It is based on a detailed record of medical neglect, expert testimony, and a finding that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 510. The order is not a broad policy directive but a precise, fact-based remedy to a specific constitutional failure.

### II. THE ORDER IS NARROWLY DRAWN AND THE LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS TO REMEDY THE VIOLATION

The State argues that the order is overly broad because it requires the release of 38,000 to 46,000 inmates. But the order is not a blanket mandate for mass release. It is a targeted remedy to eliminate the conditions that render medical care unconstitutional. The court’s decision to set the population cap at 137.5% of design capacity was based on expert testimony and a comprehensive study of California’s prison system. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 511–12. That figure was determined to be the threshold at which the state could provide constitutionally adequate medical care without further compromising safety or security.

The order is also the least intrusive means available. The State had previously failed to comply with prior remedial orders, including those requiring the development of a medical care system. The district court found that California had “failed to comply with remedial orders” and admitted it was “unable to comply” with the stipulated plans. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 510. In such circumstances, the court may “take whatever steps are necessary to ensure compliance with the Constitution.” *Id.* at 516. The court’s order was not a punishment but a necessary step to enforce the Constitution.

The State’s alternative—maintaining the status quo—would perpetuate the constitutional violation. As the Court held in *Plata*, “the Constitution does not permit the state to maintain a prison system that is so overcrowded that it cannot provide adequate medical care.” *Id.* at 516. The court’s order is not an overreach; it is a necessary correction of a system that has failed to meet its constitutional obligations.

### III. THE PLRA’S “LESS INTRUSIVE” STANDARD DOES NOT REQUIRE THE COURT TO IGNORE THE CONSTITUTION

The State contends that the PLRA’s “least intrusive” standard requires courts to avoid population reduction even when necessary to remedy a constitutional violation. But this interpretation would render the PLRA’s remedial provisions meaningless. The Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and courts must enforce it even when doing so requires difficult choices. *Marbury v. Madison*, 1 Cranch 137, 177 (1803). The PLRA does not permit a court to ignore constitutional violations in favor of administrative convenience.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “the Constitution trumps the PLRA.” *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 516. When a prison system is so overcrowded that it cannot provide constitutionally adequate medical care, the court may order a reduction in population to remedy the violation. *Id.* at 516. The PLRA’s “least intrusive” standard does not mean “no intrusive means at all.” It means that the remedy must be tailored to the violation and not go beyond what is necessary. The order here meets that standard.
