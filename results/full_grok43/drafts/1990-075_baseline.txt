ARGUMENT

I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Supplies an Intelligible Principle.

Article I vests legislative power in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, yet “Congress may delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). Section 811(h) meets this standard. It authorizes temporary placement only upon a finding of “imminent hazard to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), after consideration of three enumerated statutory factors, id. § 811(h)(3), and after notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services whose comments must be considered, id. § 811(h)(4). These constraints are “far more specific” than those upheld in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420, 426 (1944), and are comparable to the goals, factors, and limits approved in Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 374-77.

The availability of judicial review for constitutional claims, see Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977), the rapidity of change in designer-drug manufacture, see Yakus, 321 U.S. at 432, and the technical complexity of the field, see Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372, further confirm that Congress has not “abdicate[d] its responsibility,” id. at 372.

II. Subdelegation to the DEA Administrator Is Lawful.

Congress expressly empowered the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), effectuates that grant by assigning CSA functions to the DEA Administrator. Such subdelegation is valid when the statute authorizes it and the delegatee remains subject to the same intelligible principle. See United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512-13 (1974). Nothing in § 811(h) suggests Congress intended to withhold this ordinary administrative mechanism.

III. The Nondelegation Doctrine Does Not Bar Criminal Sanctions.

That the delegated power may result in criminal liability does not alter the analysis. The Court has sustained delegations authorizing criminal penalties when an intelligible principle exists. See, e.g., Yakus, 321 U.S. at 426-27. The two 1935 cases striking down delegations involved statutes lacking any guiding standard. Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935); A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935). Section 811(h) supplies such a standard.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
