**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it improperly authorize the Attorney General to subdelegate that authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA). The non-delegation doctrine requires Congress to provide an "intelligible principle" to guide the delegatee. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that Congress enjoys "wide latitude" in defining the scope of delegated authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 369 (1989). Here, Congress provided specific, substantive standards. The Attorney General must consider eight distinct factors, including the substance's potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, and the history of its abuse. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). For temporary scheduling, Congress further requires a finding that the substance poses an "imminent hazard to public safety." 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). These standards are far more specific than those upheld in *J.W. Hampton* and are sufficient to satisfy the non-delegation doctrine. In contrast, the Court struck down a delegation in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 537 (1935), because Congress provided no standards to govern the President's course.

Moreover, the temporary scheduling provision is an emergency measure. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944), the Court upheld emergency delegations, noting that "the necessity of the situation" justifies a broader delegation. The factors listed in § 811(c) and the "imminent hazard" standard provide the necessary guidance to prevent arbitrary action. This is consistent with the Court's approach in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), which emphasized the importance of "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in regulated fields. The standards here are both well-known and specific.

Finally, the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is authorized. The Attorney General is a coordinate branch of government, and Congress may delegate to coordinate branches. *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). The Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the argument that delegations to coordinate branches are invalid. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935). The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely assigns the functions vested in him by Congress to the DEA Administrator. This is a valid exercise of the authority granted by the CSA. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 364-65 (upholding delegation to the Federal Sentencing Commission). The Court has also noted that the availability of judicial review is a factor in determining the validity of a delegation. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542 (1952). Here, the statute provides for notice and comment, ensuring that the Attorney General’s actions are subject to review. Therefore, the CSA’s delegation to the Attorney General and the subsequent delegation to the DEA are constitutionally sound.
