**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT PRECLUDE A FINDING THAT ERDMAN FAILED TO ACCUMULATE 1,250 HOURS OF SERVICE.**

*A. Standard of Review.*

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment plenarily, viewing all facts and drawing all inferences in the light most favorable to Erdman as the nonmoving party. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). Summary judgment is proper only where “there is no genuine issue for trial”—that is, only where the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmovant. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 249, 252 (1986). The movant bears the initial burden of demonstrating the absence of a genuine dispute. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). Nationwide cannot meet that burden here.

*B. The FMLA's 1,250-Hour Threshold Must Be Measured Under FLSA "Suffered or Permitted" Principles, Which Nationwide's Own Conduct Satisfies.*

An employee is eligible for FMLA leave only if she has worked 1,250 hours of service during the preceding twelve months. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The regulations implementing the Act direct that “hours of service” be determined “in accordance with the principles established under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) for determining compensable hours of work,” including the FLSA’s “suffered or permitted” standard. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). Under that standard, “[w]ork not requested but suffered or permitted is work time,” because “[t]he reason is immaterial” once “the employer knows or has reason to believe that [the employee] is continuing to work.” 29 C.F.R. § 785.11; *see also Reich v. Dep’t of Conservation & Nat. Res.*, 28 F.3d 1076, 1082 (11th Cir. 1994) (employer’s knowledge that work is being performed, not formal authorization, triggers compensability).

The record establishes precisely that scenario. Erdman’s supervisor, Patty Sarno, testified that Erdman regularly worked extra hours outside the office and that Sarno either paid her for them or permitted her to bank them as “comp” time. Nationwide therefore had actual knowledge—indeed, approval—of this work for years. That Getgen later disclaimed knowledge of “comp” time, only to concede in the January 2003 meeting that Erdman’s vacation calculations (which incorporated “comp” time) were correct, creates, at minimum, a triable dispute over whether Nationwide is bound by its own supervisors’ knowledge and acquiescence. A jury, not the District Court on summary judgment, must resolve whether these hours were “suffered or permitted” within the meaning of § 825.110(c) and § 785.11.

*C. Because Nationwide's Timekeeping Records Are Incomplete and Internally Inconsistent, the Burden-Shifting Framework of Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co. Applies.*

Where, as here, an employer’s own records of hours worked are inaccurate or inadequate, the employee is not held to a standard of mathematical precision. In *Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680 (1946), the Supreme Court held that an employee “has carried out his burden if he proves that he has in fact performed work for which he was improperly compensated and if he produces sufficient evidence to show the amount and extent of that work as a matter of just and reasonable inference.” *Id.* at 687. The burden then shifts to the employer “to come forward with evidence of the precise amount of work performed or with evidence to negative the reasonableness of the inference to be drawn from the employee’s evidence.” *Id.* at 687-88.

That framework applies with equal force to the FMLA’s hours-of-service inquiry, since § 825.110(c) expressly imports FLSA principles for calculating hours worked. Erdman’s own testimony, corroborated by Sarno’s, together with Getgen’s concession regarding the vacation-hours discrepancy, is sufficient evidence from which a jury could draw a “just and reasonable inference” that Erdman exceeded 1,250 hours. *Mt. Clemens Pottery*, 328 U.S. at 687. Nationwide has offered no reliable, contrary timekeeping records that would negate that inference as a matter of law; it has offered only Getgen’s post hoc, disputed denial. That is a jury question, not a basis for summary judgment.

*D. Nationwide's Own Representations Independently Preclude Summary Judgment on Eligibility.*

Even apart from the hours calculation, Nationwide’s human resources representative told Erdman that she saw “no problems” with the FMLA request—an unambiguous representation of eligibility made contemporaneously with Erdman’s leave request. Other courts have recognized that an employer’s designation of an employee as FMLA-eligible, or its representations regarding eligibility, bear on the eligibility determination and on principles of estoppel where the employee reasonably relies to her detriment. *See Woodford v. Cmty. Action of Greene Cnty., Inc.*, 268 F.3d 51, 56-57 (2d Cir. 2001); *see also Plumley v. S. Container, Inc.*, 303 F.3d 364, 372-73 (1st Cir. 2002) (employer representations regarding eligibility may estop later denial). Eligibility is assessed as of the date leave commences, *Brungart v. BellSouth Telecomms., Inc.*, 231 F.3d 791, 796 (11th Cir. 2000), and at that time Nationwide’s own agent confirmed eligibility. This, too, raises a genuine factual dispute inappropriate for resolution on summary judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the District Court on the FMLA claim should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
