ARGUMENT

The reinstatement provision cannot lawfully reach an alien whose reentry predated its enactment. Statutes operate prospectively unless Congress unmistakably directs otherwise. Chew Heong v. United States, 112 U.S. 536, 553 (1884). The text supplies no such direction. It therefore leaves untouched reentries effected before its effective date.

Even if the statute were read to embrace earlier reentries, that construction would render the provision impermissibly retroactive. A law that attaches new disabilities to completed acts “is retrospective and invalid.” Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). Reentry in 1982 was lawful conduct under the statutes then in force; applying the later reinstatement bar to that conduct imposes a new and severe penalty. See Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 390 (1798).

This Court has long refused to permit immigration statutes to operate retroactively when doing so would destroy vested rights or impose unforeseen sanctions. Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 738 (1893); Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86, 97 (1903). The same principle controls here. Petitioner’s marriage and application for adjustment rest on conduct completed years before the statute existed; the government may not now nullify those acts by reviving an ancient removal order.

Nor may the statute be saved by characterizing the new bar as merely procedural. When a change in law “affect[s] substantial rights,” it is substantive and cannot apply retroactively. Ex parte Medley, 134 U.S. 160, 171 (1890); see also Harisiades v. Shaughnessy, 342 U.S. 580, 593-94 (1952); Galvan v. Press, 347 U.S. 522, 531 (1954). The denial of all relief is a substantive disability, not a mere change in remedy.

Precedent confirms that deportation statutes receive prospective construction to avoid these constitutional difficulties. Marcello v. Bonds, 349 U.S. 302, 314 (1955); Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685, 688 (1957). The same rule applies to the reinstatement provision. Because petitioner reentered before the statute took effect, the order of removal cannot be reinstated.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
