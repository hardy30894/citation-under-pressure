**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist as to the Circumstances of the Waiver and the Signing of the Notice.**

Summary judgment is a device to be used cautiously, particularly where a party's intent, understanding, or state of mind is at issue. The Supreme Court has cautioned that summary procedures "should be used sparingly" where the evidence bears on questions of motive and understanding that are ordinarily left to the trier of fact. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). Likewise, where the papers submitted raise doubt about the good faith and comprehension of a witness, resolution by affidavit alone is improper, and the matter must proceed to trial. *Sartor v. Arkansas Nat. Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627–28 (1944).

Here, Manisy Moua's account of being told to "copy" an unexplained typed statement waiving the three-day HOEPA disclosure period, and the parties' dispute over whether the "Confirmation" section of the Notice of Right to Cancel was executed contemporaneously with the "Receipt" section, present precisely the kind of credibility and intent questions that *Sartor* and *Poller* hold are unsuitable for summary disposition.

**II. The Purported Waiver of the Statutory Disclosure and Rescission Periods Is Unenforceable Because It Contravenes the Public Interest the Statute Was Designed to Protect.**

TILA and HOEPA confer disclosure and rescission rights on borrowers not merely as a private contractual matter, but as protections "affecting the public interest." *Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O'Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 704 (1945). In *Brooklyn Savings Bank*, the Supreme Court held that statutory rights conferred on individuals "may not be waived or released" where doing so would defeat the remedial policy underlying the statute, because Congress intended such protections to extend beyond the individual to the public generally. *Id.* The same reasoning controls here. HOEPA's three-day advance-disclosure requirement and TILA's three-day rescission period exist to protect vulnerable borrowers — precisely the class to which the Mouas, facing imminent loss of their home through foreclosure, belonged — from being pressured into unfavorable loans without adequate time for reflection. A lender cannot extinguish that protection simply by having the borrower copy, in her own handwriting, a waiver she did not understand.

Moreover, remedial consumer-protection statutes must be construed liberally to accomplish their protective purpose. *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945) (remedial legislation "must not be interpreted or applied in a narrow, grudging manner"). Reading a waiver executed under these circumstances to bar the Mouas' three-year rescission right would defeat, rather than effectuate, that statutory purpose.

**III. The Notice of Right to Cancel Was Not "Clear and Conspicuous" Because It Was Internally Contradictory and Ambiguous.**

Statutes enacted for the protection of a particular class must be construed to further the legislative purpose, and ambiguity in the instrument furnished to satisfy such a statute must be resolved against the party who drafted it and who seeks to rely on it. *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967) (remedial statutes are "entitled to a broad construction" consistent with their protective purposes). Where, as here, two conflicting versions of the same Notice exist — one containing only an acknowledgment of receipt, the other containing both the receipt and a "Confirmation" that more than three days had already elapsed, though both are dated the same day — the document cannot be said to have given the Mouas clear and conspicuous notice of when their right to cancel actually expired. An ambiguous instrument furnished by the party who controls its drafting must be construed against that party. *Steven v. Fidelity & Cas. Co. of N.Y.*, 58 Cal. 2d 862, 878 (1962) (ambiguities in a form document are resolved against the drafter and in favor of the party the instrument was meant to protect). Rand, having hired Excel Title to conduct the closing and prepare the closing documents, cannot escape the consequence of the confusion those documents created.

**IV. The Prepayment Penalty Is Void and Unenforceable Because It Violates HOEPA's Express Prohibition.**

A contractual provision that violates a statutory prohibition is void and unenforceable, regardless of the parties' apparent assent. *McMullen v. Hoffman*, 174 U.S. 639, 654 (1899) (a contract made in violation of a statute enacted for the protection of the public "cannot be enforced" by the courts). HOEPA expressly prohibits prepayment penalties in loans of this character. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b). The promissory note's escalating interest and payment schedule, effectively penalizing early payoff, cannot be enforced consistent with *McMullen*'s teaching that courts will not lend their aid to give effect to contract terms Congress has forbidden. The presence of this unlawful term independently extends the Mouas' rescission period to three years under § 1635(a), and the district court erred in failing to so hold.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Mouas' extended right to rescind.
