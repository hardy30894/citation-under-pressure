**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied the Plain-Error Standard in Limiting Its Review to the Trial Record

A federal appellate court reviewing a claim for plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b) may not consider matters outside the trial record to determine whether the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or implicated the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (“[T]he error relied on must be one that was not affirmatively waived and that seriously affected the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.”); *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 239 (1940) (appellate review of plain error is limited to errors apparent on the record). The Eleventh Circuit faithfully adhered to this principle by confining its analysis to the trial record and the jury instructions actually given. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993) (plain-error review requires an error that is “clear under current law” and that “affected the defendant’s substantial rights”); *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985) (appellate court may correct a plain error “only where the error is so obvious that it affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”).

The Eleventh Circuit did not err by declining to consider Greer’s prior convictions or other post-trial conduct to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Von der Heydt*, 401 F.2d 116, 118 (D.C. Cir. 1968) (plain-error review is confined to the record; appellate court may not consider extrinsic evidence to determine prejudice); *United States v. Indiviglio*, 352 F.2d 276, 280 (2d Cir. 1965) (plain-error analysis must be based on the record before the trial court). The court properly recognized that Greer’s substantial-rights inquiry turned on whether the jury was instructed to find that he knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm—a question resolved by the trial record, not by speculation about his prior convictions. *See United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. at 239 (appellate court may not consider matters outside the record to assess prejudice).

II. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Concluding That Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Greer could not establish prejudice under the plain-error standard. *See United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160 (plain error must “seriously affect[] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”); *United States v. Calderon*, 348 U.S. 160, 164 (1954) (appellate court will not disturb a trial court’s exercise of discretion absent clear error). The court’s analysis was limited to the trial record, where no evidence or instruction established Greer’s knowledge of his felon status. *See United States v. Von der Heydt*, 401 F.2d at 118 (prejudice inquiry must be based on the trial record).

Greer’s reliance on *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), is misplaced. The Eleventh Circuit’s plain-error analysis was governed by pre-*Rehaif* precedent, and the court properly applied the standard articulated in *Atkinson* and *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.* to determine whether the error undermined the fairness of the proceedings. *See United States v. Calderon*, 348 U.S. at 164 (appellate court defers to the trial court’s discretion in plain-error analysis). The court did not err by declining to speculate about Greer’s knowledge based on extrinsic evidence. *See United States v. Indiviglio*, 352 F.2d at 280 (plain-error prejudice inquiry is confined to the trial record).

III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Declining to Vacate the Conviction or Order a New Trial

The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to vacate Greer’s conviction or order a new trial was consistent with the plain-error standard. *See United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160 (plain error must seriously affect the fairness of the proceedings); *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. at 239 (appellate court may not consider extrinsic evidence to assess prejudice). The court properly confined its analysis to the trial record and concluded that Greer failed to demonstrate that the error undermined the integrity of his trial. *See United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. at 15 (plain-error review is limited to errors that “affected the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”).

The court’s decision was not an abuse of discretion. *See United States v. Calderon*, 348 U.S. at 164 (appellate court defers to the trial court’s discretion in plain-error analysis). Greer’s argument that the Eleventh Circuit should have considered his prior convictions to assess prejudice is foreclosed by *Atkinson* and its progeny. *See United States v. Von der Heydt*, 401 F.2d at 118 (plain-error review is confined to the trial record); *United States v. Indiviglio*, 352 F.2d at 280 (same).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
