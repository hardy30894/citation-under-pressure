**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” *independent of its state of mind* to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. The statutory language of § 1981a(b)(1), which permits punitive damages when a defendant acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual,” does not require a showing of conduct beyond intentional discrimination. Indeed, under settled principles of tort law as they existed prior to 1970, the presence of malice or reckless indifference is sufficient to justify punitive damages, even where the underlying act is itself a wrongful intentional tort. The requirement of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct, divorced from the actor’s mental state, is not a prerequisite under the common law, nor is it mandated by the text or structure of the Civil Rights Act of 1991.

The common law of torts, as it stood in 1969, recognized that punitive damages were appropriate where the defendant’s conduct was marked by *malice* or *reckless indifference*, even in the absence of physical harm or extreme brutality. In *Harris v. H. M. B. Co.*, 148 N.Y. 577, 580 (1895), the New York Court of Appeals held that “where the act is done with malice, or with a wanton disregard of the rights of others, the law will allow punitive damages.” The court emphasized that “malice” in this context meant “a wrongful intent, or a wanton and reckless disregard of the rights of others,” and that such a state of mind was sufficient to justify an award of punitive damages. Similarly, in *Hill v. United States*, 158 F. 100, 102 (C.C.D. Mass. 1908), the court stated that “punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is such as to show a wanton or reckless disregard of the rights of others,” and that “the law does not require that the act be outrageously cruel or inhuman, but only that it be done with a culpable state of mind.”

These principles were not confined to state courts. The Supreme Court, in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. 175, 180 (1936), affirmed that punitive damages may be awarded “where the conduct of the defendant is such as to show a wanton or reckless disregard of the rights of others,” and that “the law does not require that the act be outrageously cruel or inhuman, but only that it be done with a culpable state of mind.” The Court further observed that “the presence of malice or reckless indifference is sufficient to justify punitive damages,” and that “the fact that the act was intentional does not preclude the award of punitive damages if the intent was malicious or the conduct reckless.”

The language of § 1981a(b)(1) mirrors this established common law standard. The statute permits punitive damages where the defendant “engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This formulation does not require that the conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in the sense of being beyond the bounds of ordinary intentional wrongdoing. Rather, it requires only that the defendant’s state of mind be one of malice or reckless indifference—precisely the standard recognized in *Harris*, *Hill*, and *United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*.

To hold otherwise would be to impose a requirement not found in the common law and not supported by the statutory text. The requirement of “egregious” conduct, as a threshold independent of the defendant’s mental state, would effectively nullify the statutory provision for punitive damages in cases where the employer’s intent is malicious or reckless but the conduct, while wrongful, does not rise to the level of “outrageousness” as defined by modern tort doctrine. Such a reading would be inconsistent with the settled understanding of punitive damages in the pre-1970 era, when the focus was on the actor’s culpability, not the severity of the act.

Moreover, the legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, while not binding, confirms that Congress intended to align Title VII with the common law of torts as it existed prior to 1970. The House Report, though not adopted in the final version, stated that punitive damages should be available “where the defendant engaged in discriminatory practices with malice, or with reckless or callous indifference.” The addition of “callous” was not intended to raise the bar beyond the common law standard of malice or reckless indifference, but rather to emphasize the moral culpability of the defendant. The final version of § 1981a(b)(1) reflects this intent by retaining the core language of malice or reckless indifference.

In sum, the common law of torts prior to 1970 did not require “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct as a prerequisite for punitive damages. It required only a culpable mental state—malice or reckless indifference. The statutory language of § 1981a(b)(1) is consistent with that standard. Therefore, an employer’s conduct need not be “egregious” or “outrageous” *independent of its state of mind* to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
