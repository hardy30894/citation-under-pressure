**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt for its own account, acted as a *creditor*, not a *debt collector*, and thus is not subject to the FDCPA’s regulatory regime. The statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion.

### I. The FDCPA’s Core Distinction Is Between Creditors and Debt Collectors, Not Between Defaulted and Non-Defaulted Debts

The FDCPA regulates only “debt collectors,” not creditors. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (“The Act’s definition of the term ‘debt collector’ includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed to another’” (quoting 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6))). This definition hinges on *who the debt is owed to* at the time of collection, not when it became delinquent. The Act’s structure confirms this: the definition of “debt collector” in § 1692a(6) includes only those who collect debts owed to *another*—not to themselves. See *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1315 (11th Cir. 2015) (“The statutory text is entirely transparent.... [A] person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition of the term”).

The FDCPA’s exclusions further confirm this interpretive framework. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was *not in default* when obtained. This exclusion exists to protect loan servicers who manage non-defaulted accounts. *S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977)*, as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. But the converse does not follow: the fact that a debt was in default at the time of purchase does not transform a purchaser into a “debt collector.” The Act does not define “debt collector” by default status. To hold otherwise would invert the statutory structure and render the exclusions meaningless.

### II. Santander Is a Creditor Collecting Its Own Debt, Not a Third-Party Collector

When Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, it acquired ownership of the debt. The complaint itself acknowledges that Santander “acquired defaulted consumer debt ... for a few cents on the dollar” and that it “began communicating with [the plaintiffs] ... in an attempt to collect on the alleged debts.” This is the quintessential conduct of a creditor collecting its own debt. *See* *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318 (“Our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection”).

The Fourth Circuit correctly rejected the plaintiffs’ argument that Santander’s prior role as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto rendered it a “debt collector” for all purposes. *See* *Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012) (“The terms ‘debt collector’ and ‘creditor’ are mutually exclusive under the FDCPA”); *Check Investors, Inc. v. FTC*, 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007) (same). A company may be both a creditor and a debt collector in different contexts, but its status at the time of collection determines FDCPA applicability. *See* *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another’”).

### III. The Statutory Text and Structure Do Not Support the Plaintiffs’ Interpretation

The plaintiffs’ argument rests on a flawed syllogism: because a person who receives a defaulted debt *solely for the purpose of facilitating collection for another* is excluded from the definition of “creditor,” such a person must be a “debt collector.” But this ignores the threshold requirement: a person must first satisfy one of the definitions in § 1692a(6) before the exclusions apply. *See* *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314 (“Where a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a ‘debt collector’ as a matter of course. [Instead], ... he must satisfy the Act’s substantive requirements”).

Santander does not meet any of the three definitions of “debt collector” in § 1692a(6). It does not use interstate commerce or the mails as its principal business purpose to collect debts. It does not regularly collect debts owed to others. And it does not use a name other than its own to suggest a third-party collection. *See* *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293. The fact that it purchased defaulted debts does not trigger the FDCPA. *See* *Ruth v. Triumph P’ships*, 577 F.3d 790, 796–97 (7th Cir. 2009) (rejecting argument that debt purchase transforms a creditor into a debt collector).
