The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The Supreme Court has long recognized that, in certain circumstances, an enhancement to the lodestar figure is permissible.

In Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Court established that a court may award a reasonable attorneys' fee to a prevailing party. The Court noted that the allowance should be made with reference to "the nature and the extent and the difficulty of the services rendered." Id. at 532-33. This principle has been reaffirmed in subsequent cases, including Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court held that a court may consider the skill and ability of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 124-25.

The principle that a court may enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results is also supported by other authorities. In City of New York v. Ransom, 217 N.Y. 163 (1916), the New York Court of Appeals held that a court may consider the results obtained and the skill and ability of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 168.

Furthermore, in G. & C. Merriam Co. v. Saalfield, 241 F. 682 (6th Cir. 1917), the Sixth Circuit held that a court may consider the results obtained and the skill and ability of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 685.

Additionally, the Court in Harrison v. Perea, 168 U.S. 311 (1897), recognized that a court has the authority to allow a reasonable fee to a party who has incurred expenses in litigating a matter. The Court emphasized the importance of considering the services rendered by counsel. Id. at 317-18.

In Anglo-American law predating the federal fee-shifting statutes, courts have also recognized the principle that a court may award a liberal allowance to a solicitor who has rendered exceptional service, as seen in Copp v. Cheshire, 1 Vern. 259 (1683). Id. at 260.

These authorities demonstrate that, under the principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts, a district court has the discretion to enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results. The lodestar figure is not always a sufficient measure of the value of the services rendered, and a court may consider other factors, including the quality of representation and the results obtained, when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar figure in this case was not an abuse of discretion. The court's detailed findings and specific evidence on the record supported the enhancement, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision. The longstanding principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts support the conclusion that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained.
