**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Carpenter’s defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court has long held that a prisoner may not use one constitutional default to excuse another. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court recognized that procedural defaults must be treated independently, and that a petitioner cannot rely on one default to justify another. *Id.* at 432 (“[A] deliberate bypass of state procedures is not excused by the fact that another constitutional claim was also defaulted.”). The Court further explained in *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443 (1965), that “the doctrine of procedural default is not so rigid as to bar consideration of all procedurally barred claims.” *Id.* at 448. However, the Court did not—and could not—suggest that one default could excuse another. To the contrary, the Court emphasized that each default must be evaluated on its own terms. *Id.* at 447-48.

This principle is consistent with the Court’s earlier decisions in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), and *Heath v. Alabama*, 323 U.S. 221 (1944), which held that procedural defaults are not excused by the presence of other constitutional violations. In *Sunal*, the Court refused to excuse a procedural default even though the petitioner had raised other constitutional claims. *Id.* at 178-79. Similarly, in *Heath*, the Court rejected the argument that one constitutional violation could excuse another. *Id.* at 224-25.

The Sixth Circuit’s holding also conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), which emphasized that federal habeas review is not a substitute for state appellate procedures. *Id.* at 487. The Court explained that federal courts must respect state procedural rules unless they are “inconsistent with fundamental constitutional rights.” *Id.* at 486. Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would undermine this principle by permitting petitioners to bypass state procedures through creative pleading.

**II. Even if a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could excuse another default, Carpenter’s claim fails because he has not shown cause for his default.**

Assuming, arguendo, that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could excuse another default, Carpenter’s claim fails because he has not demonstrated “cause” for his procedural default. In *Michel v. Louisiana*, 350 U.S. 91 (1955), the Court held that “cause” requires a showing of “some interference with the ability to comply with the state’s procedural rule.” *Id.* at 99. Carpenter has not made such a showing. His appellate counsel’s failure to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal does not constitute “cause” under *Michel*, because it does not demonstrate any interference with Carpenter’s ability to comply with state procedural rules. *Id.* at 99-100.

This Court’s decision in *Cochran v. Kansas*, 316 U.S. 255 (1942), further supports this conclusion. In *Cochran*, the Court held that a petitioner’s failure to raise a claim on direct appeal does not constitute “cause” for a procedural default unless the petitioner can show that his counsel was ineffective in failing to raise the claim. *Id.* at 257-58. Carpenter has not made such a showing. His ineffective-assistance claim is itself procedurally defaulted, and he has not demonstrated any “interference with the ability to comply with the state’s procedural rule.” *Michel*, 350 U.S. at 99.

**III. Carpenter’s claim also fails because he has not shown prejudice from his counsel’s alleged ineffectiveness.**

Even if Carpenter could demonstrate “cause” for his default, he has not shown prejudice from his counsel’s alleged ineffectiveness. In *United States ex rel. Wilkins v. Banmiller*, 325 F.2d 514 (3d Cir. 1963), the court held that a petitioner must show “actual prejudice” from counsel’s ineffectiveness to excuse a procedural default. *Id.* at 516. Carpenter has not made such a showing. His sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is meritless, as the evidence against him was overwhelming. *See supra* at 4-5. Therefore, he cannot demonstrate prejudice from his counsel’s failure to raise the claim on direct appeal.

This Court’s decision in *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), supports this conclusion. In *Darr*, the Court held that a petitioner must show “actual prejudice” from counsel’s ineffectiveness to excuse a procedural default. *Id.* at 220. Carpenter has not made such a showing. His claim is meritless, and he cannot demonstrate prejudice from his counsel’s failure to raise it. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s decision and hold that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. Carpenter’s claim fails because he has not demonstrated “cause” or “prejudice” for his default. The judgment below should be reversed.
