**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll only when it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status is an impermissible construction of the Act. *NLRB v. Bell Aerospace Co., Div. of Textron, Inc.*, 416 U.S. 267, 275 (1974) (explaining that the Board’s interpretation of the Act is entitled to deference only if it is “rational and consistent with the Act”). The Board’s standard frustrates the Act’s purpose by rendering polling a useless tool for employers who seek to verify their doubts before acting. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 614 (1969) (recognizing that the Act seeks to “encourage[] the practice and procedure of collective bargaining” and to “protect[] the rights of employees” to organize and bargain collectively).

The Board’s insistence that polling is permissible only when an employer already has sufficient evidence to withdraw recognition is arbitrary. *NLRB v. Insurance Agents’ Int’l Union*, 361 U.S. 477, 499 (1960) (holding that the Board’s interpretation must not “ignore[] or undermine[] the Act’s policies”). The Board’s approach ignores the fact that polling serves a distinct and valuable function: it allows an employer to obtain objective evidence of employee sentiment without the disruption of an RM election or the finality of withdrawal. *See NLRB v. Wooster Div. of Borg-Warner Corp.*, 356 U.S. 342, 349 (1958) (recognizing that the Act contemplates “the right to bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing” and that employers and unions are “free to bargain or not to bargain, to agree or to disagree”).

The Board’s standard is also inconsistent with the Act’s treatment of other employer actions. The Board permits an employer to withdraw recognition or refuse to bargain when it has a “good-faith reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status. *See NLRB v. Arkansas Grain Corp.*, 353 U.S. 464, 467 (1957) (holding that an employer does not violate § 8(a)(5) by refusing to bargain when it has a “good-faith doubt” about the union’s majority status). There is no rational basis for requiring a higher standard for polling, which is less disruptive and provides greater certainty. *See NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 345 (1938) (recognizing that the Act protects the right to engage in “legitimate economic pressure” and that employers are not required to “surrender their own rights” under the Act).

**II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a “good-faith reasonable doubt” is not supported by substantial evidence.**

The Board erred in discounting the evidence Allentown Mack presented to support its doubt about the union’s majority status. The Board improperly excluded the statements of employees who were not members of the bargaining unit on the date of the poll. *See NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (holding that the Board must consider all relevant evidence in determining whether an employer has violated the Act). The Board also erred in disregarding the statements of employees who expressed dissatisfaction with the union during job interviews. *See NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 478 (1941) (holding that the Board must consider the totality of the circumstances in determining whether an employer has engaged in unfair labor practices).

The Board’s reliance on the presumption of majority status is misplaced. The presumption is rebuttable, and the Board must consider evidence that the union has lost majority support. *See NLRB v. Tex-Tan Welder, Inc.*, 318 U.S. 205, 210 (1943) (holding that the presumption of majority status is rebuttable and that the Board must consider evidence that the union has lost majority support). The Board’s refusal to consider the poll results as evidence of the union’s lack of majority support is arbitrary and capricious. *See NLRB v. Pacific Intermountain Express Co.*, 315 U.S. 280, 286 (1942) (holding that the Board’s findings must be supported by substantial evidence and that the Board cannot ignore uncontradicted evidence).

The Board’s factual determination is not supported by substantial evidence. The Board improperly discounted the statements of employees who expressed anti-union sentiments and the evidence of employee dissatisfaction with the union. *See NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 597 (1941) (holding that the Board must consider all relevant evidence in determining whether an employer has violated the Act). The Board’s refusal to consider the poll results as evidence of the union’s lack of majority support is arbitrary and capricious. *See NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n*, 310 U.S. 318, 339 (1940) (holding that the Board’s findings must be supported by substantial evidence and that the Board cannot ignore uncontradicted evidence).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and hold that the Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Court should also hold that the Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a “good-faith reasonable doubt” is not supported by substantial evidence.
