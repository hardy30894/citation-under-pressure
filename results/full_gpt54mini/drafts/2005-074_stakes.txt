**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because the reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), does not apply to an alien who reentered the United States before the statute’s effective date, and, in any event, applying it to Petitioner would be impermissibly retroactive. The Supreme Court’s retroactivity cases require a court first to determine whether Congress clearly prescribed the statute’s temporal reach; if not, the court asks whether applying the statute to prior conduct would attach new legal consequences to completed acts. See **Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994)**; **Martin v. Hadix, 527 U.S. 343, 352-53 (1999)**; **INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001)**.

**I. Section 1231(a)(5) does not reach pre-enactment reentries absent a clear statement from Congress.**

Section 1231(a)(5) provides that if the Attorney General finds that an alien “has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed or having departed voluntarily,” “the prior order of removal is reinstated from its original date and is not subject to being reopened or reviewed.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Congress enacted that language in IIRIRA, effective April 1, 1997. **Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 309(a), 110 Stat. 3009-546, 3009-625**. Nothing in the text expressly states that the provision applies to illegal reentries completed before 1997. Under **Landgraf**, courts do not apply statutes to past conduct absent clear congressional intent. **Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994)**.

This Court’s analysis should follow the ordinary presumption against retroactivity. “The presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence.” **Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 265**. That presumption is especially strong where a statute strips away immigration relief that was previously available. See **INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 321-23 (2001)** (refusing to apply IIRIRA’s repeal of discretionary relief to aliens who pleaded guilty before enactment). Petitioner entered in 1982, fifteen years before § 1231(a)(5) took effect. Congress did not clearly say that the statute would reach such completed reentries.

**II. Applying § 1231(a)(5) to Petitioner would be impermissibly retroactive.**

Even if the statute reaches pre-enactment reentries, applying it to Petitioner would attach new legal consequences to his completed conduct. Under **Landgraf**, a statute is impermissibly retroactive when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” **Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994)**. The question is whether the statute “impair[s] rights a party possessed when he acted, increase[s] a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose[s] new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” **Id. at 280**.

Before 1997, Petitioner’s illegal reentry did not categorically foreclose him from pursuing adjustment of status through a qualifying marriage to a United States citizen. See **8 U.S.C. § 1255(i)**; **INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 325 (2001)**. Section 1231(a)(5), however, makes the prior removal order unreviewable and bars “any relief under this chapter.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). That is a profound change in legal consequences. The Supreme Court has recognized that retroactive immigration statutes are disfavored where they upset settled expectations and eliminate available relief. **St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321-25**.

The government may argue that Petitioner’s continued unlawful presence after 1997 defeats any retroactivity claim. That argument fails because the relevant predicate conduct here is the pre-enactment illegal reentry, not some new post-1997 offense. The Court in **Martin v. Hadix, 527 U.S. 343, 357-58 (1999)**, explained that a statute can be partially retroactive and partially prospective depending on what conduct triggers the legal consequence. Here, the decisive event was Petitioner’s 1982 reentry; the statute now uses that completed event to impose a new, harsher consequence. That is retroactivity.

Nor does the mere fact that Petitioner sought adjustment only after 1997 eliminate the problem. A statute may be retroactive even when the protected interest is one of eligibility for discretionary relief. In **St. Cyr**, the Court held that eliminating eligibility for relief can be impermissibly retroactive where the alien relied on prior law in entering or maintaining his position. **INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 325-26 (2001)**. Petitioner lived in the United States for years in the legal landscape that predated § 1231(a)(5); he should not be stripped of the possibility of relief by a statute enacted long after his reentry.

Finally, this Court should be guided by the Supreme Court’s treatment of the reinstatement provision itself. In **Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales, 548 U.S. 30, 44-46 (2006)**, the Court held that § 1231(a)(5) applies to an alien who remained unlawfully in the United States after the statute’s effective date and who had not taken action to obtain relief before then. But the Court also emphasized that retroactivity turns on whether the statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” **Id. at 44** (quoting **Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270**). On the facts of this case, Petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry and long-settled presence make the statute’s application impermissibly retroactive.

For these reasons, the petition should be granted and the reinstatement order vacated.
