**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text of Section 1407(a) Forecloses a Transferee Court from Retaining a Case for Trial Under Section 1404(a).**

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." This Court has repeatedly held that "shall" is the language of command. In *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482 (1947), the Court explained that when Congress uses "shall" as opposed to "may," it "normally creates an obligation impervious to judicial discretion." *Id.* at 485. So too here: Congress did not authorize the Panel to remand transferred cases *if convenient*; it commanded that remand occur once pretrial proceedings conclude. A district court sitting as a transferee court exercises only the authority granted to the Panel—coordination of *pretrial* proceedings—and cannot expand that grant by resort to the general venue-transfer provision of Section 1404(a).

This Court's settled approach to statutory construction begins and ends with the text Congress enacted. As Justice Thomas explained in *Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249 (1992), "courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there." *Id.* at 253–54. Section 1407(a) says that transferred actions "shall be remanded." No exception appears for cases a transferee judge deems ready, or convenient, for trial. Respondent's position requires reading into the statute words Congress never wrote—an approach this Court rejected in *Consumer Product Safety Commission v. GTE Sylvania, Inc.*, 447 U.S. 102 (1980), where it held that "the starting point for interpreting a statute is the language of the statute itself" and that absent ambiguity, "this language must ordinarily be regarded as conclusive." *Id.* at 108.

**II. Structural Canons of Construction Confirm That Section 1404(a) Cannot Be Used to Circumvent Section 1407(a)'s Remand Command.**

Congress knew how to authorize permanent transfer for trial when it wished to do so; it did so expressly in Section 1404(a), which permits transfer of a civil action "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice." No comparable authorization for permanent reassignment appears in Section 1407(a); instead, that provision speaks only of "coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings." Under the interpretive principle articulated in *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16 (1983), "where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion." *Id.* at 23. Congress's omission of any trial-transfer authority from Section 1407(a)—paired with its express, mandatory remand command—demonstrates that multidistrict transfer was designed to be temporary and limited to pretrial matters, leaving the parties' venue rights under Section 1404(a) to be exercised, if at all, by the court to which the case is ultimately returned, or before transfer occurs at all.

**III. Judicial Convenience Cannot Override an Unambiguous Statutory Mandate.**

The Ninth Circuit majority below did not dispute that Section 1407(a)'s remand command is unambiguous; it merely deferred to a "case law" practice built on policy preference rather than statutory text. That approach cannot survive this Court's repeated admonitions that courts may not rewrite statutes to reach outcomes they consider more efficient. In *TVA v. Hill*, 437 U.S. 153 (1978), the Court refused to graft an equitable exception onto the Endangered Species Act no matter how sympathetic the practical concerns, explaining that "it is not for us to speculate, much less act, on whether Congress would" have wanted a different result once the statute's plain terms were clear. *Id.* at 194. Likewise, in *Board of Governors, FRS v. Dimension Financial Corp.*, 474 U.S. 361 (1986), the Court rejected an agency's attempt to redefine "bank" based on modern banking realities, holding that "the definitions of course serve the statutory purposes, but the definitions, not the purposes, control." *Id.* at 373. And in *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*, 508 U.S. 248 (1993), the Court cautioned against using equitable considerations to expand remedies beyond what the statutory text authorizes. *Id.* at 253–54. The same discipline forecloses respondent's efficiency-based defense of self-transfer here.

**IV. The Practice Below Rests on Precedent That Even Its Progenitors Recognized Lacks Statutory Footing.**

The Second Circuit's decision in *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and the Third Circuit's decision in *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), which the courts below invoked to justify self-transfer, are candid that they depart from the statute's text on grounds of "policy or convenience," not law. Indeed, the very district court whose order is here reviewed acknowledged, quoting *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971), that "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests" self-transfer authority. As Judge Kozinski recognized in dissent below, this "egregious, oft-repeated error" has persisted only because litigants rarely obtain appellate review before trial intervenes. *Lexecon Inc. v. United States Dist. Court*, 61 F.3d 911 (9th Cir. 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). This Court should not perpetuate a quarter-century of unlawful practice building on itself; it should enforce the statute Congress wrote.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that a district court conducting pretrial proceedings under Section 1407(a) may not invoke Section 1404(a) to transfer a case to itself for trial, and should reverse the judgment below.
