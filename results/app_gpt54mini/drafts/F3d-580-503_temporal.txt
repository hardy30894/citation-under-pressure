**ARGUMENT**

I. The district court abused its discretion by denying Winbush a continuance and a defense fingerprint expert, depriving him of a fair opportunity to meet the government’s proof.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees not merely the presence of counsel, but “the right to have sufficient time to advise with counsel and to prepare a defense.” *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 59 (1932). Where a denial of time prevents a meaningful defense, the conviction cannot stand. *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444, 446-47 (1940); *Chambers v. Maroney*, 399 U.S. 42, 53 (1970) is unavailable here, and the governing pre-1970 rule is that due process is violated when a defendant is forced to trial without a fair chance to prepare. *Avery*, 308 U.S. at 446-47.

That principle applies with particular force where the defense seeks expert assistance to contest the government’s scientific evidence. This Court has long recognized that a trial must be “a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s accusations.” *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 68-69. Here, the government announced fingerprint and narcotics experts shortly before trial; Winbush then requested his own fingerprint expert to test the meaning of the absence of latent prints. The request was not a tactical afterthought. It went to the core of the government’s theory tying Winbush to the firearm and narcotics recovered after a chaotic pursuit. Denying both the expert and a continuance left the defense unable to meet a significant component of the prosecution’s case.

The government will likely rely on the lateness of the request. But the constitutional question is not whether the district court could complain about timing; it is whether the ruling, in the circumstances, deprived the accused of a fair trial. *Avery*, 308 U.S. at 446-47. A court “cannot be said to have denied due process merely because it refused to allow an indefinite continuance,” *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964), but *Ungar* itself stresses that the answer depends on “the circumstances present in every case.” Id. Here, the case had been pending for years, yet the government’s expert disclosures and trial posture made a defense expert newly important. The defense asked for a focused expert and a short continuance, not delay for delay’s sake. Refusal under these facts was an abuse of discretion and a denial of due process.

II. The conviction for possession of a firearm in furtherance of a drug trafficking crime rests on impermissibly thin proof and on guilt by association.

A criminal conviction must rest on evidence that permits the jury to find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, not on speculation or suspicion. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) is not available, but pre-1970 due process precedent already required proof adequate to satisfy “fundamental fairness.” *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236-37 (1941). The Constitution forbids conviction by conjecture. *Thompson v. Louisville*, 362 U.S. 199, 206 (1960).

The government’s proof on the § 924(c) count was circumstantial: officers saw a gun in Winbush’s hand as they approached the vehicle; later, a firearm was recovered from his jacket along with crack cocaine. But the statute requires proof that the firearm was possessed “in furtherance of” drug trafficking, not merely that a gun and drugs were found in the same general episode. Possession of a gun in proximity to drugs may support an inference, but it does not compel one. The evidence here was especially equivocal because the firearm was not recovered on Winbush’s person at arrest, and the recovery scene was a snow-covered chase with multiple movement points and items discarded along the route. In such a setting, the leap from possession to “in furtherance” was left to speculation.

The same is true of the possession counts tied to the marijuana and the “Tennessee crack.” The government’s proof depended heavily on where items were found after the pursuit and on Frazier’s testimony that Winbush told him, “I might need you to run with this.” Even accepting that testimony, the jury still had to infer that all recovered contraband was knowingly possessed by Winbush. But guilt cannot be inferred from mere presence, association, or opportunity. *United States v. Romano*, 382 U.S. 136, 141-44 (1965); *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 704 n.31 (1975) is unavailable. The record instead shows a fragmented chase, shifting locations, and items recovered after the fact. That is not enough to satisfy due process beyond a reasonable doubt.

III. The cumulative effect of these errors requires reversal.

Even where individual errors might be insufficient alone, their combined effect may deny a fair trial. *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86-87 (1963). Winbush was denied a meaningful continuance and expert assistance, then convicted on charges that turned on contested inferences about discarded items, gun possession, and drug trafficking. The result was a trial tilted heavily in the government’s favor and inconsistent with the “rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935).

For these reasons, the conviction should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
