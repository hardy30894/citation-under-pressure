ARGUMENT

Withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period requires the government to prove the accused’s membership during the relevant period beyond a reasonable doubt. In *Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp.*, 340 U.S. 558 (1951), this Court explained that a prior judgment is “prima facie evidence of the conspiracy and also of the performance of such acts in accomplishing it as the jury in the criminal case, in rendering a verdict of guilty, necessarily found to have occurred.” *Id.* at 568. The same principle governs here: once withdrawal is shown, the government must establish continued membership by evidence that the factfinder can rationally accept.

The government cannot shift that burden to the accused. *First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co.*, 391 U.S. 253 (1968), confirms that a party opposing summary disposition must produce evidence tending to show participation; otherwise the claim fails. *Id.* at 289. Likewise, when a defendant demonstrates withdrawal before the limitations period, the government bears the affirmative obligation to prove ongoing membership.

This Court has long required the prosecution to establish each element of conspiracy with competent proof. In *Haas v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 462 (1910), the Court held that certified copies of indictments supplied competent evidence upon which a commissioner could base a conclusion of probable cause. *Id.* at 480. The same evidentiary threshold applies at trial: the government must adduce proof that the defendant remained a member during the limitations window.

*Price v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 488 (1910), reinforces that the probative weight of charging documents is “necessarily limited to such counts as are identical.” *Id.* at 494. A general verdict cannot be presumed to rest on acts occurring after withdrawal; the government must identify and prove the specific acts within the period.

*McDaniel v. Traylor*, 212 U.S. 428 (1909), teaches that a fraudulent combination ties claims together so that they constitute “one claim.” *Id.* at 435. Once a defendant severs his connection, the government must prove a new, independent basis for liability within the limitations period rather than rely on earlier acts.

*Hyde v. Shine*, 199 U.S. 62 (1905), recognized that conspiracy jurisdiction turns on where the offense was committed. *Id.* at 76. A defendant who withdraws before the limitations period begins cannot be tried on the original agreement; the government must prove a fresh agreement or continued participation.

*Howard v. Fleming*, 191 U.S. 126 (1903), upheld a charge that “the guilt of the accused must be shown beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Id.* at 135. That standard remains constant; withdrawal does not relieve the government of its burden.

*Partmar Corp. v. Paramount Pictures Theatres Corp.*, 347 U.S. 89 (1954), affirmed that a prior judgment may supply prima facie evidence of conspiracy, yet the plaintiff still must connect the defendant to the unlawful conduct. *Id.* at 102. The government here must make that connection for the limitations period.

Finally, *In re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95 (1895), illustrates that jeopardy attaches only to offenses actually tried. *Id.* at 99. A defendant who withdraws is entitled to the same protection: the government must prove membership during the actionable period rather than rely on pre-withdrawal conduct.

The court of appeals erred in relieving the government of this burden. The judgment should be reversed.
