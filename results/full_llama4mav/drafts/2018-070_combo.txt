The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "applicable" in this context, but the Fifth Circuit has interpreted it to mean that state law applies only when necessary to fill a significant void or gap in federal law, as seen in Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

This interpretation is consistent with the principles of statutory construction and the legislative history of the OCSLA. The Act was passed to provide a body of law for the outer Continental Shelf, and Congress intended for federal law to be the primary source of law governing activities on the Shelf. See 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The incorporation of state law was meant to supplement federal law, not to duplicate or conflict with it. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 357-58 (1969) (explaining that the OCSLA was intended to provide a comprehensive body of law for the outer Continental Shelf).

The Ninth Circuit's holding that state law applies as long as it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law would lead to an expansive and potentially conflicting body of law on the outer Continental Shelf. This approach is not supported by the case law. In United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of assimilating state law into federal law, but this was in the context of a specific federal statute that explicitly adopted state law. The OCSLA's adoption of state law is more limited, and the "applicable and not inconsistent" language should be interpreted in light of this limitation.

Furthermore, the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, leaving no significant void or gap for state law to fill. See Opp Cotton Mills v. Administrator, 312 U.S. 126, 129-30 (1941) (describing the FLSA as a comprehensive statute). The existence of a savings clause in the FLSA, which allows for more protective state laws, does not necessarily mean that state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf. See Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 137-38 (1944) (explaining that the FLSA's savings clause is intended to allow for more stringent state regulations).

In light of these authorities, the Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated. The OCSLA does not permit the application of California's wage and hour laws to Newton's claims, as the FLSA provides a comprehensive framework for addressing wage and hour grievances on the outer Continental Shelf.
