ARGUMENT

I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD IS RATIONAL ONLY IF THE BOARD APPLIES IT AS AN OBJECTIVE, EVENHANDED TEST OF DOUBT RATHER THAN A DEMAND FOR PROOF OF ACTUAL MAJORITY LOSS

The National Labor Relations Act permits an employer to test a union's continuing majority support once the union's certification year has passed and the presumption of majority status becomes rebuttable. As Justice White explained in dissenting from denial of certiorari in a companion controversy, an employer may act "if it has a doubt, 'reasonably grounded' and 'based on objective considerations,' that the union no longer has the support of a majority of the employer's workers." Broadway v. Bogan, 459 U.S. 994, 994 (1982) (White, J., dissenting). That formulation—reasonable grounding in objective fact—is the touchstone the Board itself purports to apply. The standard is not irrational on its face; the vice lies in how the Board has administered it in this case.

An agency's chosen evidentiary standard survives judicial review only if the agency actually applies it, rather than using its label while covertly demanding a heavier showing. This Court's review of Board orders has never been a rubber stamp. Reviewing courts must examine "the preponderance of the testimony taken" and the whole record, not merely the evidence that supports the Board's conclusion. FCC v. Allentown Broadcasting Corp., 349 U.S. 358, 364 (1955) (quoting Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 492 (1951)). This Court there made clear that an examiner's or Board's factual conclusions are not owed uncritical acceptance; the "responsibility for decision" placed on the agency is "wholly inconsistent with the notion" that a reviewing body may treat findings as unassailable. Id. The same discipline applies here: calling a standard "reasonable doubt" does not immunize the Board's application of that standard from meaningful review if, in practice, the Board requires objective proof tantamount to an actual loss of majority support before it will credit any doubt as reasonable.

II. THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT PETITIONER LACKED A GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE ON THE RECORD AS A WHOLE

Even accepting the Board's own articulated standard, its finding that Allentown Mack lacked objective, reasonable doubt cannot stand because the Board disregarded probative, unrebutted evidence and imposed evidentiary exclusions inconsistent with a fair reading of the record.

First, the Board's treatment of individual employee statements—Ridgick's remark that "there was no need for a union," Marsh's complaint about dues, Bloch's report concerning the night shift, and Mohr's assessment as union steward that "the union would lose"—cannot be dismissed wholesale without a reasoned, record-based explanation tied to each individual's circumstances. Where an agency's ultimate conclusion rests on discounting specific individuals' statements, it must ground that discounting in particularized findings about those individuals, not categorical assumptions. This Court has required exactly that kind of individualized scrutiny before an agency's characterization of particular employees' conduct or status can be accepted as substantial evidence for a broader conclusion. See Florida v. Drake, 466 U.S. 978, 978–79 (1984) (describing the necessity of "new findings about each of the group leaders" before the Board's characterization of their status and influence could be sustained). The Board's blanket refusal to credit Ridgick's and Marsh's statements, and its refusal to consider what those statements, together with Bloch's and Mohr's reports, revealed about sentiment across the unit, was not the product of the individualized, record-grounded analysis that substantial-evidence review demands.

Second, the Board compounded this error by declining to weigh the aggregate, cumulative effect of the evidence Allentown Mack did present. A reasonable doubt need not rest on a statistical certainty of majority opposition; it need only be grounded in objective indicia sufficient to raise doubt. Broadway v. Bogan, 459 U.S. at 994 (White, J., dissenting). Seven direct repudiations in a unit of thirty-two employees, combined with secondhand reports from a union steward and a fellow employee describing broader dissatisfaction, are precisely the kind of objective considerations that a reasonable employer—not a court, not the Board, but an employer acting in good faith—could rely upon. The Board's insistence on treating each piece of evidence in isolation, and discounting each in turn until nothing remained, is the same error this Court has cautioned against when an agency's piecemeal treatment of a record produces a result at odds with the record's fair import. See FCC v. Allentown Broadcasting Corp., 349 U.S. at 364.

Finally, the ultimate poll results—19 to 13 against continued representation—confirm what the objective evidence indicated all along. While the Board correctly may not treat post-hoc poll results as validating an otherwise unsupported decision to poll, those results are not irrelevant background; they corroborate that Allentown Mack's doubt was reasonably grounded rather than fanciful. The Board's contrary finding, resting on selective exclusion of relevant evidence and an unexplained refusal to consider its cumulative weight, is not supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals enforcing the Board's order should be reversed.
