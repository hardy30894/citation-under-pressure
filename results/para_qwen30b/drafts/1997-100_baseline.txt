**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional guarantee is not a mere procedural formality—it is a fundamental protection against the oppressive power of the state to subject an individual to repeated prosecutions for the same criminal act. The question before this Court is whether that protection extends to a retrial on a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. The answer is yes. The California Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise.

### I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Applies to Sentencing Proceedings Where the Prior Conviction Allegation Is a “Same Offense” for Purposes of the Clause

The lead opinion in *Monge* attempts to distinguish noncapital sentencing proceedings from trials on guilt or innocence by asserting that the Double Jeopardy Clause “by its terms proscribes a second jeopardy ‘for the same offense’” and that sentencing determinations are not “offenses” within the meaning of the Clause. *Monge*, 19 Cal.4th at 836. This argument is legally unsound and contradicts the Supreme Court’s settled jurisprudence.

The Clause does not require that the second prosecution be for the same *statutory offense*; rather, it protects against being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the *same criminal act*. *U.S. Const., amend. V*. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the “same offense” inquiry is not limited to the formal elements of a crime but must consider the *nature and purpose* of the proceeding. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a sentencing enhancement after a prior sentencing hearing was reversed on appeal, even though the enhancement was not a separate crime. The Court emphasized that the sentencing hearing in a capital case “is not a mere formality” but “a critical stage of the criminal proceeding” that “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 439, 440.

The Court in *Bullington* did not limit its analysis to capital cases. It stated that the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” — including the presentation of evidence, the burden of proof, and the finality of the determination — are what trigger double jeopardy protection. *Id.* at 439. The Court explicitly rejected the idea that a sentencing hearing is “a mere administrative function.” *Id.* at 440. The same reasoning applies here: the trial on a prior conviction allegation is not a mere administrative step. It is a fact-intensive proceeding in which the state seeks to prove a prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, the defendant may invoke the privilege against self-incrimination, and the outcome directly affects the length of the sentence. These are the very hallmarks of a trial.

The lead opinion’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it involved a capital case is unpersuasive. The Court in *Bullington* did not say the Clause applies only in capital cases. It said the Clause applies when the proceeding “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 439. The California Supreme Court’s own decisions recognize that a prior conviction trial under Penal Code § 1025 includes many of these hallmarks: the prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, the defendant has the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, and the rules of evidence apply. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 228 (1996); *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993). These are not mere statutory niceties—they are constitutional protections that transform the proceeding into a trial-like event.

### II. The State’s Interest in Sentencing Does Not Justify Denying Double Jeopardy Protection

The lead opinion argues that because the state is not required to provide a trial on sentencing allegations, it may “circumscribe the procedural boundaries” of such a trial, including the exclusion of double jeopardy protection. *Monge*, 19 Cal.4th at 837. This reasoning is flawed. The fact that a state may choose not to provide a trial does not mean it may strip a defendant of constitutional protections once it chooses to provide one.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when a state affords a defendant a trial on a sentencing issue, it must afford the minimum protections of due process. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949). The Court stated that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* But that does not mean the Clause can be ignored. On the contrary, *Williams* itself recognized that the defendant’s right to due process includes the right to be heard and to have the facts determined fairly. The Court in *Bullington* reaffirmed that when a sentencing hearing becomes “a critical stage of the criminal proceeding,” the Double Jeopardy Clause applies. *451 U.S. at 439.*

The state’s interest in ensuring that dangerous offenders receive longer sentences is not a justification for violating constitutional rights. The purpose of the Double Jeopardy Clause is to protect the individual from the “oppressive power of the State” to harass and burden a defendant through repeated prosecutions. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957). The risk of such harassment is no less in a noncapital case than in a capital one. A defendant who is acquitted of a prior conviction allegation should not be forced to face the same charge again, especially when the state has already had a full opportunity to prove its case.
