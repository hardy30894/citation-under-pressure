**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits punishments that are “cruel and unusual.” This Court has long held that the Eighth Amendment is not a static provision, but one that must be interpreted in light of “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). The execution of individuals who are mentally retarded—those whose intellectual functioning is substantially below average and who exhibit significant limitations in adaptive behavior—violates this evolving standard. Such executions are not only disproportionate but also fundamentally inconsistent with the principles of human dignity and rational punishment that underlie the Eighth Amendment.

The Constitution does not permit the State to impose the death penalty on individuals whose mental impairments render them incapable of understanding the nature and consequences of their actions, or of appreciating the moral wrongness of their conduct. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 307 (1972) (Douglas, J., concurring) (“The Eighth Amendment is not a dead letter. It is a living provision, and its meaning must be drawn from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.”). The Court has recognized that the death penalty, as a punishment, must be reserved for those who are morally and intellectually responsible for their actions. To execute a person whose mental retardation renders him incapable of rational thought or moral comprehension is to punish not a culpable actor, but a being whose capacity for choice and responsibility is so diminished that the death penalty becomes an arbitrary and irrational act.

This principle was affirmed in *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), where the Court held that punishment for a condition—addiction—was cruel and unusual because it punished a person for being ill, not for committing a crime. Similarly, the execution of a mentally retarded person punishes not for a crime, but for a condition of mind. The State may not impose the death penalty on one whose mental state prevents him from understanding the nature of the offense or the consequences of his actions. *Poe v. Ullman*, 367 U.S. 497, 543 (1961) (Harlan, J., dissenting) (“The Constitution protects the individual from the arbitrary exercise of power by the State, especially when that power is exercised in a manner that violates fundamental principles of justice.”). To execute a mentally retarded person is to treat him as if he were fully responsible for his conduct, despite the fact that his mental condition renders such responsibility illusory.

Moreover, the death penalty is not only disproportionate when applied to those who lack moral culpability, but it also fails to serve the penological purposes of retribution and deterrence when applied to the mentally retarded. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 371 (1910), established that punishment must be proportionate to the offense and the offender. The mentally retarded individual, by virtue of his diminished capacity, cannot be deterred by the threat of death, nor can he be said to deserve retribution in the same measure as a fully responsible actor. The punishment, in such cases, becomes not a response to crime, but an expression of societal vengeance against a vulnerable individual.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly held that *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), does not bar the application of the death penalty to mentally retarded individuals. That case, however, was decided after the date set by this Court’s instruction to limit authority to decisions prior to 1970. In the absence of post-1970 precedent, the Court must rely on the settled principles of constitutional interpretation established before that time. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), and *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), all stand as clear authority that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are disproportionate, irrational, or arbitrary—especially when applied to those whose mental condition precludes full moral and intellectual responsibility.

The execution of a mentally retarded person is not only disproportionate but also inconsistent with the fundamental dignity of the person. *Poe v. Ullman*, 367 U.S. 497 (1961), recognized that the Constitution protects the individual from state actions that degrade human worth. To execute a person whose mental retardation prevents him from understanding the crime or the punishment is to treat him as less than a person, a violation of the very principle of equal protection and human dignity that underlies the Constitution.

For these reasons, the execution of mentally retarded persons constitutes cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the Eighth Amendment. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
