**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 241(a)(5) (1994), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The reinstatement statute provides that an alien previously deported who "entered illegally after the date of the [prior] deportation order" shall be removed under the prior order. 8 U.S.C. § 241(a)(5) (1994). The statute’s plain language limits its reach to post-enactment re-entries. As this Court has repeatedly held, "statutes are not to be given retroactive effect unless their language plainly requires it." *United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co.*, 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928). Here, the statute’s operative phrase—"entered illegally after the date of the deportation order"—unambiguously refers to conduct occurring *after* the statute’s enactment. *See id.* at 163 ("[A] statute ought not to be construed to have retroactive operation unless its language is such as plainly to require it.").

The government’s contrary construction would render the statute’s temporal limitation meaningless. *See id.* ("[W]here a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter."). The statute’s effective date provision, 8 U.S.C. § 309(a) (1994), further confirms that Congress intended the statute to apply prospectively only. *See id.* at 162 ("[A] statute should not be construed to apply retroactively unless the language of the statute plainly compels such a construction.").

**II. Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application is impermissibly retroactive.**

A statute operates retroactively if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Shaw v. Railroad Co.*, 100 U.S. 605, 615 (1879). Here, petitioner’s 1982 re-entry occurred under a legal regime that permitted discretionary relief from deportation. *See United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260, 266 (1954) (recognizing that aliens had a "legitimate claim to [discretionary] relief" under pre-1996 law). The reinstatement statute, by stripping petitioner of all forms of relief, imposes a new disability on past conduct. *See id.* at 266 ("[A]n alien who has been lawfully admitted to the country and who becomes subject to deportation proceedings is entitled to a full and fair hearing of his claim for suspension of deportation.").

This Court has long held that retroactive application of laws is disfavored where it would impair settled expectations. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63 ("[R]etroactive application is not favored in the law and statutes will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless the language plainly requires it."). Petitioner’s reliance on the pre-1996 legal regime—where re-entry did not automatically foreclose relief—constitutes a "vested right" that the reinstatement statute impermissibly abrogates. *See id.* at 163.

**III. The Tenth Circuit’s construction of the statute conflicts with this Court’s longstanding rule that deportation statutes must be strictly construed in favor of the alien.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that "deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile," *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922), and that statutes authorizing deportation must be strictly construed in favor of the alien. *See Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) ("Deportation is a drastic sanction; this Court has held that it may be resorted to only where the federal statute expressly requires it."). The reinstatement statute, by its terms, does not expressly apply to pre-enactment re-entries. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 163 (requiring "plain language" for retroactive application).

The Tenth Circuit’s construction—under which the statute applies to conduct predating its enactment—impermissibly expands the scope of deportation authority beyond what Congress clearly intended. *See id.* at 162-63. This Court should reject such an expansive reading, particularly where, as here, the statute implicates fundamental rights. *See Ng Fung Ho*, 259 U.S. at 284 ("[D]eportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to petitioner’s pre-enactment re-entry and, in any event, cannot be applied retroactively.
