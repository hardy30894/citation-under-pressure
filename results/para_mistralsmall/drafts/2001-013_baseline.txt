**ARGUMENT**

**I. OSHA HAS JURISDICTION OVER THE WORKING CONDITIONS ON MALLARD BAY DRILLING RIG 52.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (“Act”) applies to “employment performed in a workplace.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act defines “workplace” as “any place of employment at which employees are engaged in work.” *Id.* § 652(10). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that the Act’s definition of “workplace” is “broad and inclusive.” *Donovan v. Bel-Loc Diner, Inc.*, 780 F.2d 139, 142 (5th Cir. 1986) (quoting *Marshall v. Anaconda Co.*, 596 F.2d 370, 374 (10th Cir. 1979)). The Act’s coverage is not limited to traditional land-based workplaces; it extends to “any place where work is being performed by an employee of an employer.” *Id.* (quoting *Usery v. Godfrey Brake Serv., Inc.*, 545 F.2d 52, 55 (8th Cir. 1976)).

Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 is a “place of employment” where Mallard’s employees were engaged in work at the time of the explosion. The fact that Rig 52 is a vessel operating on navigable waters does not remove it from the Act’s coverage. The Act’s definition of “employer” includes “a person engaged in a business affecting commerce who has employees.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(5). The Supreme Court has held that the Act’s coverage extends to businesses engaged in interstate commerce, including those operating on navigable waters. *See Marshall v. Barlow’s, Inc.*, 436 U.S. 307, 313 (1978) (noting that the Act applies to “virtually all businesses affecting commerce”).

The Fifth Circuit has likewise recognized that the Act applies to workplaces on navigable waters. In *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), the court held that the Act applied to a drilling barge operating on the Outer Continental Shelf, rejecting the argument that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction preempted OSHA’s authority. *Id.* at 827. The court reasoned that the Act’s coverage was not limited to land-based workplaces and that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction did not preempt OSHA’s authority to regulate workplace safety. *Id.*

**II. SECTION 4(b)(1) OF THE ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to “working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction over workplaces merely because another federal agency has some regulatory authority over the vessel. *See Chao v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 278 F.3d 1266, 1270 (11th Cir. 2002) (holding that OSHA retains jurisdiction over a workplace even where the Coast Guard has some regulatory authority over the vessel).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), does not support Mallard’s preemption argument. In *Clary*, the court held that OSHA did not apply to the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, but the court’s reasoning was based on the specific statutory authority of the Coast Guard over seamen, not on the mere fact that the Coast Guard had some regulatory authority over the vessel. *Id.* at 1121-22. The court noted that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over seamen’s working conditions was “exclusive” because of the Coast Guard’s “comprehensive regulation and supervision” of such conditions. *Id.* at 1122.

Here, the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over uninspected vessels is limited and does not extend to occupational safety and health standards. The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is primarily focused on safety equipment and lifesaving devices, not on the broader occupational safety and health standards enforced by OSHA. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq., 26.01 et seq. The Coast Guard does not prescribe or enforce occupational safety and health standards for uninspected vessels, and its regulatory authority does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace safety on such vessels.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), further supports this conclusion. In *Donovan*, the court held that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over the Outer Continental Shelf did not preempt OSHA’s authority to regulate workplace safety on a drilling barge. *Id.* at 827. The court reasoned that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction was not exclusive and that OSHA’s authority to regulate workplace safety was not preempted. *Id.*

**III. THE COAST GUARD’S EXCLUSIVE JURISDICTION OVER SEAMEN’S WORKING CONDITIONS DOES NOT EXTEND TO UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

Mallard argues that the Coast Guard has exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen’s working conditions aboard all vessels, including uninspected vessels. This argument is incorrect. The Fifth Circuit has held that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over seamen’s working conditions is limited to vessels that are subject to the Coast Guard’s inspection and certification requirements. *See Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1121-22; *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826-27.

In *Clary*, the court held that OSHA did not apply to the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, but the court’s reasoning was based on the specific statutory authority of the Coast Guard over seamen, not on the mere fact that the Coast Guard had some regulatory authority over the vessel. *Id.* at 1121-22. The court noted that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over seamen’s working conditions was “exclusive” because of the Coast Guard’s “comprehensive regulation and supervision” of such conditions. *Id.* at 1122.

Here, Rig 52 is an uninspected vessel, and the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over such vessels is limited. The Coast Guard does not prescribe or enforce occupational safety and health standards for uninspected vessels, and its regulatory authority does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace safety on such vessels. Therefore, OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to Mallard for violations of the Act.
