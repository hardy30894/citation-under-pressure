**ARGUMENT**

I. THE PLAIN TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF § 1231(a)(6) DO NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION.

The statute provides that an alien “may be detained beyond the removal period.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). The word “may” grants discretion, but it does not confer unlimited power. Where a statute is susceptible to a construction that avoids serious constitutional questions, this Court adopts that construction. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Here, the purpose of post-removal detention is to ensure the alien’s presence for removal. Once removal becomes impossible or unforeseeable, detention ceases to serve that purpose and becomes indefinite. The statute should be read to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to effect removal. *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (courts must look beyond literal language to legislative intent). A contrary reading would transform a regulatory detention into a life sentence, which Congress did not plainly authorize.

II. EVEN IF THE STATUTE COULD BE READ BROADLY, THE CONSTITUTION FORBIDS INDEFINITE DETENTION OF RESIDENT ALIENS.

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas is a resident alien who entered the United States as a child and has lived here for decades. He is not an excludable alien stopped at the border. This Court has long recognized that resident aliens are entitled to due process of law. *Yamataya v. Fisher*, 189 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1903); *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953). The Fifth Amendment protects “any person” from deprivation of liberty without due process. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Indefinite civil detention—imprisonment that may last for life—is a severe deprivation that requires procedural and substantive justification.

A. Indefinite Detention Without a Meaningful Endpoint Violates Substantive Due Process.

Substantive due process forbids the government from imposing arbitrary restraints on liberty. *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923) (liberty includes freedom from bodily restraint). Detention that is not tied to a legitimate government purpose and has no fixed duration is arbitrary. In *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 302 (1944), the Court held that the government could not detain concededly loyal Japanese Americans. Just as detention of loyal citizens served no wartime purpose, indefinite detention of an alien whom no country will accept serves no removal purpose. The only conceivable justification—preventive detention based on dangerousness—is not a purpose of the immigration detention statute. Congress provided for detention pending removal, not for incapacitation.

B. This Case Is Controlled by *Wong Wing* and the Prohibition Against Punishment Without Trial.

*Wong Wing* held that Congress may not impose “imprisonment at hard labor” upon aliens without a criminal trial. 163 U.S. at 237. Indefinite civil detention that is punitive in effect likewise requires the protections afforded criminal defendants. Here, petitioner has been in custody for years with no prospect of release. That is punishment. The government cannot avoid constitutional constraints by labeling its action civil. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (due process requires clear standards to avoid arbitrary enforcement). When detention becomes permanent, it ceases to be regulatory and becomes penal.

C. *Shaughnessy v. Mezei* Does Not Control.

In *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 215 (1953), the Court upheld indefinite detention of an excludable alien who had never entered the United States. But the Court expressly distinguished resident aliens, noting that one who has “entered” the country enjoys constitutional protections. *Id.* at 212-13. Petitioner Zadvydas entered legally as a child. He is not a Mezei-like excludable alien. *See Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596 (resident alien entitled to full due process despite prior departure). Moreover, *Mezei* emphasized that the alien could be released if another country accepted him, but here no country will accept petitioner. The rationale of *Mezei* cannot justify detention that is permanent in practice.

D. The Government’s Interest in Detention Does Not Justify Permanent Imprisonment.

The only legitimate purpose of post-removal detention is to secure the alien’s presence for removal. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 538 (1952) (detention pending deportation is permissible to prevent flight). When removal is not reasonably foreseeable, that purpose evaporates. Continued detention then becomes arbitrary. *Endo*, 323 U.S. at 302. The government may impose reasonable supervision conditions, but indefinite incarceration violates due process. *Yamataya*, 189 U.S. at 101.

III. THE STATUTE MUST BE CONSTRUED TO AVOID THESE CONSTITUTIONAL PROBLEMS.

Consistent with *Crowell v. Benson*, this Court should construe § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit reasonable-time limitation. Detention beyond the 90-day removal period is authorized only while removal is reasonably foreseeable. When removal becomes unlikely, as here, continued detention is not permitted. The alternative—reading the statute to allow indefinite incarceration—raises serious constitutional doubts that Congress could not have intended. *Holy Trinity*, 143 U.S. at 472.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the district court ordering petitioner’s release should be affirmed.
