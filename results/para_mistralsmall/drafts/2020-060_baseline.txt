**ARGUMENT**

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam’s Liability for Response Costs Under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), Triggering the Three-Year Statute of Limitations for Contribution Claims and Precluding a § 107 Cost-Recovery Action.**

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the 2004 Consent Decree resolving Clean Water Act claims against Guam “resolved [its] liability . . . for some . . . of a response action” within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery suit against the United States. *Government of Guam v. United States*, 950 F.3d 1073, 1083 (D.C. Cir. 2020). CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) expressly permits a party that has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” to seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs). 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The statute’s plain text requires only that the settlement resolve liability for “some . . . of a response action,” not that it resolve all liability or include an express admission of fault. *Id.* The Consent Decree satisfied this standard because it compelled Guam to close the Ordot Dump, install a cover system, and pay a civil penalty—all measures constituting “remedial action” under CERCLA § 101(24). *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24) (defining “remedial action” to include “the cleanup or removal of released hazardous substances”).

Guam’s argument that the Consent Decree did not resolve its liability because it disclaimed any admission of fault is unavailing. *See* Appellee’s Br. 16–17. As the D.C. Circuit recognized, “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” *Guam*, 950 F.3d at 1083 (quoting *ASARCO LLC v. Union Pac. R.R. Co.*, 866 F.3d 1112, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017)). The Supreme Court has likewise held that CERCLA settlements need not include an express admission of liability to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 138–39 (2007) (noting that § 113(f)(3)(B) applies to settlements that “resolve liability” without requiring a judicial or administrative finding of fault). The Consent Decree’s substantive obligations—mandating cleanup and closure—demonstrate that Guam’s liability was resolved for purposes of § 113(f)(3)(B), regardless of the disclaimer.

Because the Consent Decree triggered § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s contribution claim accrued when the Decree was entered in 2004, and its three-year limitations period expired in 2007. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery action, filed in 2017, is therefore untimely. *See* *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004) (explaining that § 107 claims are barred if the plaintiff could have pursued contribution under § 113 but failed to do so within the limitations period).

**II. The D.C. Circuit Correctly Rejected Guam’s Policy Arguments and Constitutional Objections.**

Guam contends that applying § 113(f)(3)(B) here produces an absurd result because it would leave Guam without a remedy. *See* Appellee’s Br. 42–49. This argument ignores the statutory framework. CERCLA § 107(a) permits cost-recovery actions only by parties that have *incurred* response costs. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). Guam, as the owner and operator of the Ordot Dump, was itself a PRP under § 107(a) and thus could not sue the United States for costs it was legally obligated to pay. *See* *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 66–67 (1998) (holding that a PRP may not sue another PRP under § 107 for costs it was required to incur). The D.C. Circuit’s decision merely enforces the statute’s plain terms, ensuring that Guam’s remedy, if any, lies in contribution against other PRPs—not in a cost-recovery action against the United States. *Guam*, 950 F.3d at 1084.

Guam’s due process argument fares no better. *See* Appellee’s Br. 49. The limitations period in § 113(g)(3) is a procedural rule, not a substantive deprivation, and does not violate due process. *See* *Tex. v. New Jersey*, 379 U.S. 674, 683 (1965) (holding that statutes of limitation are “procedural in nature” and do not implicate due process concerns). Moreover, Guam’s argument fails because it conflates its own rights with those of hypothetical non-settling PRPs. *See* *Keepseagle v. Vilsack*, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016) (forfeiture doctrine bars raising claims not presented below).

**Conclusion**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision correctly applies CERCLA’s statutory scheme. The 2004 Consent Decree resolved Guam’s liability for response costs, triggering § 113(f)(3)(B) and barring Guam’s untimely § 107 claim. This Court should affirm.
