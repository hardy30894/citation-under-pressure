The Sixth Circuit's decision in this case is consistent with established Supreme Court precedent. A state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim if the ineffective-assistance claim is meritorious. See Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 753-54 (1991) (holding that a petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice to excuse a procedural default, and that ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause).

The Court has long recognized that ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause to excuse a procedural default. See Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) ("So long as a defendant is represented by counsel whose performance is not constitutionally ineffective under the standard established in Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), we discern no inequity in requiring him to bear the risk of attorney error that results in a procedural default."). The converse is also true: if counsel's performance is constitutionally ineffective, it can constitute cause to excuse a procedural default.

The Sixth Circuit correctly applied this principle in holding that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted. See Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446 (2000) (holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim if the ineffective-assistance claim is meritorious).

The District Court's determination that Carpenter's appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective in failing to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal is supported by the record. Under Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), counsel's performance is evaluated under a two-prong test: (1) whether counsel's performance was deficient, and (2) whether the deficient performance prejudiced the defendant. The District Court's finding of ineffective assistance is consistent with this standard.

In sum, the Sixth Circuit's decision is consistent with Supreme Court precedent, and the District Court's determination of ineffective assistance is supported by the record. The respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be affirmed.
