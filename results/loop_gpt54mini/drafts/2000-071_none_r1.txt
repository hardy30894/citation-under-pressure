Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club violated the First Amendment. Milford opened its facilities after hours for community use by district residents, including social, civic, recreational, and other community welfare activities. Once the government creates such a forum, it may not exclude speech because of disagreement with the speaker’s viewpoint. See Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951); De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937). Milford did exactly that. It allowed comparable youth and community groups to meet, but denied the Club because its moral instruction was explicitly Christian. That is viewpoint discrimination, which the First Amendment forbids. West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 400 (1923).

The school’s characterization of the Club’s activities as “religious worship” does not justify the exclusion. Religious speech is protected speech. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940), the Court held that the Constitution protects the “free exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship.” In Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943), the Court rejected burdens on the distribution of religious literature and solicitation tied to religious expression. In Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944), the Court again protected the dissemination of religious views. And in Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73, the Court held that a city could not deny a permit for religious meetings based on hostility to the religious character of the gathering. Milford’s policy is no different in principle: it excluded the Club because the Club’s speech was religious.

Milford cannot defend that exclusion by invoking the Establishment Clause. The Clause prohibits government sponsorship of religion, not equal access for private religious speech in an otherwise open forum. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), held that government must remain neutral between religion and nonreligion. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), made the same point in upholding an accommodation for religious instruction and explaining that the State may not show “callous indifference” to religion. Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 431-32 (1962), and School Dist. of Abington Twp. v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), barred state-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in the schools, but they did not authorize the State to suppress private religious speech simply because it is religious. Here, Milford did not face a prayer led by school officials or a curriculum imposed on students. It faced a private club seeking equal access after school hours under a facially open-use policy. Neutral access is not establishment. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314.

Milford’s own rationale confirms the constitutional error. It did not exclude the Club because of any neutral restriction on subject matter; it excluded the Club because the Club’s moral teaching came from a Christian perspective and included prayer, Bible stories, and scripture memorization. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected governmental efforts to suppress religious expression merely because it is religious. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 304; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 110-11; Follett, 321 U.S. at 577-78. The same constitutional principle applies here: when the State opens a limited forum to community speakers, it may not reserve the forum for secular viewpoints alone.

Nor is there merit to the claim that children might perceive endorsement. The proper constitutional response to any such concern is equal treatment and, if necessary, clarification that private speech is not school speech. The State may not cure a perceived Establishment Clause problem by engaging in viewpoint discrimination. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73. If the Constitution requires anything here, it is neutrality, not exclusion. Everson, 330 U.S. at 15-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 313-15.

Because Milford excluded the Good News Club based on its religious viewpoint, and because the Establishment Clause does not require that discrimination, the judgment should be reversed.
