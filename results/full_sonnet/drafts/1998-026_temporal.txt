# ARGUMENT

## I. A VIOLATION OF RULE 32(a)(2) DOES NOT WARRANT COLLATERAL RELIEF WHEN THE DEFENDANT HAD ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE OF HIS RIGHT TO APPEAL

### A. Collateral Relief Under Habeas Is Reserved for Errors Resulting in a Complete Miscarriage of Justice

This Court has long held that not every deviation from the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure entitles a prisoner to collateral relief. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), this Court confronted precisely the issue presented here—a sentencing court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal as required by Rule 32(a)(2)—and held that such an omission does not, standing alone, furnish grounds for relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. The Court explained that the failure to advise "is not of itself an error that can be raised by motion under § 2255," because the defect "is neither jurisdictional nor constitutional." *Id.* at 428. Rather, collateral relief is available only for errors that constitute "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Id.* at 428 (quoting *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205, 219 n.4 (1952)).

That standard reflects the settled understanding that collateral review is not a substitute for direct appeal, and that habeas relief does not lie for every technical noncompliance with procedural rules. See *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (holding that habeas corpus is not "the equivalent of an appeal" and ordinarily may not be used to correct errors that could have been, but were not, raised on direct review). Where, as here, the defendant already possessed the very knowledge the rule was designed to impart, no "fundamental defect" and no "miscarriage of justice" can be said to have occurred. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

### B. Harmless-Error Principles Confirm That Relief Is Unwarranted Where the Purpose of the Rule Has Been Fulfilled

This Court's harmless-error jurisprudence reinforces the conclusion that a court's oversight in failing to recite Rule 32(a)(2)'s advice does not automatically require relief. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court instructed that the inquiry is whether the error "had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining" the outcome, and that a court must ask "not were they [the jury or, by analogy, the defendant] right in their judgment, regardless of the error or its effect upon the verdict," but whether the error affected the substantial rights of the party. *Id.* at 764–65. The touchstone is prejudice, not the mere existence of a procedural misstep.

Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal and of the steps necessary to perfect that right. Where the District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero was in fact aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, the very purpose the Rule was designed to serve was accomplished notwithstanding the court's failure to recite the advice aloud. To grant relief in such circumstances would elevate form over substance and would extend collateral remedies to a class of errors that, under *Kotteakos*, caused no injury to any right the Rule was meant to protect.

### C. Petitioner's Position Would Convert a Procedural Advisement Requirement Into an Absolute Rule Foreclosing Any Inquiry Into Prejudice

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any violation of Rule 32(a)(2), without regard to the defendant's actual knowledge, automatically entitles him to relief—effectively treating the Rule as though it created a substantive right independent of its informational purpose. That approach cannot be reconciled with *Hill*, which expressly rejected the notion that Rule 32(a)2)'s directive is "jurisdictional" or of constitutional dimension such that any noncompliance is per se reversible on collateral attack. 368 U.S. at 428. Nor can it be reconciled with the general principle, reflected in *Kotteakos*, that federal courts do not set aside proceedings for errors that do not affect substantial rights. 328 U.S. at 764–65.

Indeed, if this Court were to hold that a Rule 32(a)(2) omission is fatal irrespective of the defendant's actual knowledge, it would create an anomaly: a defendant who was properly advised but who nonetheless elected, knowingly and voluntarily, not to appeal would have no claim for relief, while a defendant who possessed the identical knowledge—but whom the court forgot to formally advise—would receive an automatic remedy. Such a result would not vindicate any interest the Rule protects; it would simply reward the fortuity of a court reporter's transcript. The Rule protects a defendant's ability to make an informed decision about whether to appeal—nothing more. Where that decision was already informed, as the District Court found here, the rule's office has been served.

### D. The District Court's Factual Finding That Peguero Knew of His Right to Appeal Forecloses Relief

Because the District Court, after an evidentiary hearing, found as fact that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at sentencing, this case falls squarely within the rule of *Hill*. No fundamental defect occurred; no miscarriage of justice resulted. Respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
