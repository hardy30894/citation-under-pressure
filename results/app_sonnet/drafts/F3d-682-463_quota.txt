**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because a Reasonable Jury Could Find That Ottobre Harassed Wasek "Because of" His Sex.**

Title VII prohibits discrimination "because of ... sex," 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and the Supreme Court has confirmed that this prohibition reaches harassment between members of the same sex. *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc.*, 523 U.S. 75, 79 (1998). In *Oncale*, the Court rejected the notion that male-on-male harassment falls outside the statute, explaining that "statutory prohibitions often go beyond the principal evil to cover reasonably comparable evils, and it is ultimately the provisions of our laws rather than the principal concerns of our legislators by which we are governed." *Id.* at 79. The touchstone is not the harasser's or victim's orientation, but "whether members of one sex are exposed to disadvantageous terms or conditions of employment to which members of the other sex are not exposed." *Id.* at 80.

*Oncale* identifies several evidentiary routes for satisfying this standard, the first being proof that the harassment involved "credible evidence that the harasser was homosexual." *Id.* at 80. The record here supplies exactly that inference. Ottobre repeatedly grabbed Wasek's buttocks, poked him in the rear with a hammer handle and a sucker rod, and told him "you've got a pretty mouth," "boy you have pretty lips," and "you know you like it sweetheart." Wasek testified that he believed Ottobre engaged in this conduct because Ottobre was bisexual. This is not "merely offensive" horseplay; it is the kind of explicit, repeated sexual touching and remarks that the Sixth Circuit has held sufficient to create a jury question on the sexual-desire route of *Oncale*. See *Bowman v. Shawnee State Univ.*, 220 F.3d 456, 463 (6th Cir. 2000) (reversing summary judgment where a supervisor grabbed the plaintiff's buttocks and made sexually suggestive comments, because a jury could infer the conduct was motivated by sexual desire).

The Sixth Circuit's decision in *Vickers v. Fairfield Medical Center*, 453 F.3d 757 (6th Cir. 2006), does not compel a different result. *Vickers* denied relief because the harassment there was mockery of perceived homosexuality rather than an expression of desire. *Id.* at 763. Wasek's case is the opposite: Ottobre's conduct—groping, penetrative gestures with tools, and comments about Wasek's "pretty lips"—is conduct a jury could readily find was itself sexual in nature and motivated by desire, not by an effort to punish Wasek for failing to conform to gender norms.

At minimum, this is a disputed question of fact, and it was error to resolve it against Wasek at summary judgment. "Credibility determinations, the weighing of evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge" ruling on summary judgment. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Wasek's sworn testimony describing the touching, the comments, and his belief regarding Ottobre's motivation is competent evidence that must be credited and viewed in the light most favorable to him. *Id.* Whether that evidence proves the harassment was "because of sex" is a question for the jury, not for the court on paper.

**II. A Reasonable Jury Could Find the Harassment Was Severe or Pervasive.**

Hostile work environment liability requires conduct "sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim's employment and create an abusive working environment," assessed by looking at "all the circumstances." *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21, 23 (1993); *Meritor Sav. Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986) (recognizing that Title VII's prohibition "is not limited to 'economic' or 'tangible' discrimination" and reaches hostile-environment claims). Repeated physical touching of an intimate body part, penetrative gestures with tools, sexually explicit commentary, and the tacit encouragement of a supervisor who "laughed instead of intervening" easily satisfy this standard under the totality-of-the-circumstances test the Sixth Circuit applies. *Williams v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 187 F.3d 553, 562 (6th Cir. 1999) (listing frequency, severity, and physically threatening or humiliating nature of conduct as relevant factors).

**III. Arrow Energy Is Liable for Failing to Remedy Known Co-Worker Harassment.**

Once an employer knows or should know of co-worker harassment, it must take "prompt and appropriate corrective action," or it faces liability for its own negligence. *Blankenship v. Parke Care Ctrs., Inc.*, 123 F.3d 868, 872 (6th Cir. 1997). Here, Wasek reported Ottobre's touching and comments to Tripp, Smillie, and Strong—each of whom either laughed, told Wasek to "duke it out," or threatened him with a "starvation schedule" if he complained further. That is the antithesis of prompt remedial action, and a jury could find Arrow Energy negligent under *Blankenship* and *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 799 (1998).

**IV. The ELCRA Claim Rises and Falls with the Title VII Claim.**

Michigan courts look to Title VII precedent in construing ELCRA's parallel hostile-environment prohibition. *Radtke v. Everett*, 442 Mich. 368, 382, 501 N.W.2d 155 (1993). Because genuine issues of material fact preclude summary judgment on the Title VII claim, the same result follows under ELCRA.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
