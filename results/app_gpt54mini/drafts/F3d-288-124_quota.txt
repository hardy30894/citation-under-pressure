**Argument**

I. Bryant Has Standing To Seek Enforcement Of The Arbitration Award

The district court erred in holding that Bryant lacked standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award. An employee who is the beneficiary of an arbitral remedy may sue to enforce that award when the union does not adequately vindicate the employee’s interests. See *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 186 (1967) (recognizing that where a union breaches its duty of fair representation, an employee may pursue relief against the employer); *Hines v. Anchor Motor Freight, Inc.*, 424 U.S. 554, 562-63 (1976) (same). The Supreme Court has likewise held that individual employees may enforce contractual and arbitral rights secured for their benefit under a collective bargaining agreement. See *DelCostello v. International Brotherhood of Teamsters*, 462 U.S. 151, 164-65 (1983). Bryant, as the prevailing employee in the arbitration, seeks only the benefit of an award entered in his favor; he therefore has a concrete, particularized injury if Bell Atlantic refuses compliance. See *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1992).

II. The District Court Applied An Overly Demanding Standard At Summary Judgment

Summary judgment is proper only when no genuine issue of material fact exists and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56; *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248-52 (1986). The court must view the record in the light most favorable to the nonmovant and may not weigh the evidence or make credibility determinations. *Tolan v. Cotton*, 572 U.S. 650, 656-57 (2014); *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150-51 (2000). In employment discrimination cases, summary judgment is especially disfavored where motive and intent are disputed. *Henson v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 61 F.3d 270, 275-76 (4th Cir. 1995).

The district court also failed to account for the Supreme Court’s clarification that the McDonnell Douglas framework does not require a plaintiff to produce direct evidence of discrimination or disprove every articulated employer rationale at the prima facie stage. See *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 252-56 (1981); *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 507-08 (1993); *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 142-49. Under *Reeves*, a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with evidence that the employer’s explanation is false, may permit a factfinder to infer unlawful discrimination. 530 U.S. at 147-48. That is precisely the case here.

III. Bryant Presented Sufficient Evidence Of Disparate Treatment And Pretext

Bryant adduced evidence that he was disciplined repeatedly for failing to work Red Letter Days after Bell Atlantic adopted and then intensified a policy that fell especially harshly on him because of his race, color, and gender-related family obligations. A plaintiff may prove disparate treatment by showing that similarly situated employees outside the protected class were treated more favorably or that the employer’s explanation is a pretext for discrimination. *McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804; *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 253-54. Pretext may be shown through inconsistent explanations, deviation from normal practices, or evidence that the asserted reason lacks credibility. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 147; *Dennis v. Columbia Colleton Medical Center, Inc.*, 290 F.3d 639, 649-50 (4th Cir. 2002).

Bryant’s record contains evidence that Bell Atlantic refused available accommodations, imposed escalating discipline, and terminated him after repeated efforts to meet family obligations and with union support for alternative coverage. A jury could infer from these facts that Bell Atlantic’s repeated “insubordination” rationale was not the true reason for discharge. See *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 250-51 (1989) (plurality) (employment decisions motivated by protected characteristics violate Title VII); *Desert Palace, Inc. v. Costa*, 539 U.S. 90, 99-101 (2003) (circumstantial evidence may support discrimination claim).

IV. Bryant’s Retaliation Claim Also Survives Summary Judgment

Title VII prohibits retaliation against an employee for opposing unlawful employment practices or participating in protected activity. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a); *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 67-68 (2006). A retaliation plaintiff must show protected activity, materially adverse action, and causation. *Id.* at 68; *Strothers v. City of Laurel*, 895 F.3d 317, 327-28 (4th Cir. 2018). Bryant protested the Red Letter Day policy through grievances and union channels, and Bell Atlantic then escalated discipline and ultimately terminated him. Temporal proximity and a pattern of escalating punishment are sufficient to create a triable issue of causation. *Clark County School District v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 273-74 (2001) (per curiam); *Price v. Thompson*, 380 F.3d 209, 213-14 (4th Cir. 2004).

V. Section 1981 Independently Supports Bryant’s Race Discrimination Claim

Section 1981 guarantees all persons the same right “to make and enforce contracts” as white citizens. 42 U.S.C. § 1981(a); *Domino’s Pizza, Inc. v. McDonald*, 546 U.S. 470, 474-78 (2006). Race discrimination claims under § 1981 use the same basic proof structure as Title VII claims. *Gairola v. Virginia Department of General Services*, 753 F.2d 1281, 1285-86 (4th Cir. 1985); *Love-Lane v. Martin*, 355 F.3d 766, 786 (4th Cir. 2004). Because Bryant produced evidence from which a rational factfinder could conclude that Bell Atlantic’s stated reason was pretextual, his § 1981 claim should have proceeded to trial.

**Conclusion**

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for enforcement of the arbitration award and for trial on Bryant’s discrimination and retaliation claims.
