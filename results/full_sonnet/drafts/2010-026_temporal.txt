# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2's text limits its reach to matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency," and settled canons forbid enlarging that limit through judicial gloss.

Section 552(b)(2) exempts from mandatory disclosure only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." Congress chose that language, and when a statute's words are plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The Ninth Circuit did not enforce that text. It replaced "solely" with "predominantly" and grafted onto the statute a "risk of circumvention" requirement that appears nowhere in § 552(b)(2). That judicially invented two-part test cannot be reconciled with the statute Congress actually wrote.

This Court has repeatedly refused to let courts fashion exceptions to statutes that Congress did not enact. In Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222 (1957), the Court held that a specific venue statute meant what it said and declined to supplement it with an exception the text did not contain. The same discipline applies here: Congress described nine, and only nine, categories of exempt records, 5 U.S.C. § 552(b), and it limited Exemption 2 to matters related solely to internal personnel rules and practices. It did not authorize withholding of any record whose disclosure might carry some generalized "risk" to an unspecified agency interest. See Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282 (1929) (a statute's specification of the mode in which a thing is to be done excludes other modes).

Nor can the Ninth Circuit's expansion be justified as ordinary construction of a remedial statute. Because FOIA's exemptions operate against a background rule of disclosure, they should be treated the way this Court has treated other narrow exemptions carved out of broadly remedial legislation—strictly, and only where the case falls plainly within the exemption's terms. In A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945), this Court held that exemptions from remedial legislation are to be "narrowly construed," their application "limited to those plainly and unmistakably within their terms and spirit." Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc., 322 U.S. 607 (1944), applied the identical principle, refusing to extend a statutory exemption beyond situations falling squarely within its language. The same principle should govern FOIA's exemptions, which, like the exemptions in Phillips and Addison, carve narrow exceptions out of a broadly remedial command. The Ninth Circuit inverted that canon, construing Exemption 2 to reach any material an agency finds convenient to keep confidential, rather than narrowly, to reach only what Congress described.
