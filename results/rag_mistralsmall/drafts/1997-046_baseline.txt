**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere “act of grace,” but a constitutionally significant stage of the criminal justice system that implicates a prisoner’s liberty interest. *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) (recognizing that clemency decisions are “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review” but not foreclosing due process scrutiny where a state creates a structured process). The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that clemency is not a “wholly gratuitous” act, but rather an integral part of the state’s adjudicatory scheme. *See Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956) (due process applies to proceedings that are “an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant”).

This Court has long held that where a state establishes a formal process for clemency, due process protections attach. *See Board of Pardons v. Allen*, 482 U.S. 369, 378 (1987) (recognizing that prisoners may have a liberty interest in parole release derived from state procedures). Ohio’s clemency process, as outlined in Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07 and the Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s regulations, provides a structured review mechanism that creates a legitimate expectation of clemency consideration. *See Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979) (holding that a state-created expectation of parole release may constitute a protected liberty interest).

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A “HOBSON’S CHOICE” THAT COMPELS INMATES TO WAIVE THEIR RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION AS A CONDITION OF PARTICIPATING IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS.**

The Fifth Amendment’s Self-Incrimination Clause protects individuals from being compelled to incriminate themselves in any proceeding, including clemency interviews. *See United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. 181, 187 (1977) (holding that the Fifth Amendment privilege applies to all proceedings where testimony may lead to criminal prosecution). Ohio’s clemency process, as applied to Woodard, creates an unconstitutional dilemma: either participate in an interview and risk self-incrimination, or forego the opportunity to present mitigating evidence and remain silent.

This Court has condemned the use of unconstitutional conditions to compel waivers of constitutional rights. *See Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972) (holding that government may not condition benefits on the waiver of constitutional rights). The Sixth Circuit correctly identified this issue as a “Hobson’s choice,” where Woodard is forced to choose between asserting his Fifth Amendment rights and presenting his case for clemency. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976) (recognizing that a prisoner’s silence in a parole hearing may not be used against him, but declining to extend this rule to clemency proceedings).

**III. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THAT CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS, AS CONDUCTED HERE, VIOLATE DUE PROCESS.**

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that clemency proceedings are not subject to due process scrutiny is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. *See Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464 (declining to recognize a due process right in clemency but not foreclosing scrutiny where state procedures create a legitimate expectation). Ohio’s clemency process, as structured, creates a protected liberty interest that must be accompanied by minimal due process protections. *See Greenholtz*, 442 U.S. at 12 (holding that due process protections attach where a state creates a formalized process).

Moreover, the Fifth Amendment’s protections against self-incrimination are not limited to criminal trials but apply to any proceeding where testimony may lead to criminal liability. *See Washington*, 431 U.S. at 187. Ohio’s clemency process, by conditioning participation on the risk of self-incrimination, violates Woodard’s constitutional rights.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed. Ohio’s clemency process creates a protected liberty interest that triggers due process protections, and the state may not compel Woodard to waive his Fifth Amendment rights as a condition of participating in clemency proceedings.
