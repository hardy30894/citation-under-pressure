**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford Motor Company is subject to specific personal jurisdiction under the Due Process Clause. The Constitution requires only that a defendant’s forum contacts “give rise to the liabilities sued on.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 320 (1945). Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—marketing, selling, and servicing the same model of vehicle involved in this accident—are sufficiently related to the claims here that requiring Ford to defend in Montana does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Id.* at 316.

**I. Ford Purposefully Availed Itself of the Montana Market**

Ford deliberately targets Montana consumers. It markets the Ford Explorer nationally, maintains a network of dealers in Montana, sells parts and accessories to Montana residents, and provides repair services for Montana vehicles. These activities constitute “systematic and continuous” contacts with the forum. *Perkins v. Benguet Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 447 (1952). Ford’s conduct demonstrates a “purposeful availment” of the privilege of conducting business in Montana, thereby invoking the benefits and protections of Montana law. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). By placing its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased and used in Montana, Ford has “purposefully directed” its activities toward the forum. *Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 176 N.E.2d 761, 766 (Ill. 1961).

**II. The Claims “Arise From or Relate To” Ford’s Forum Contacts**

The specific-jurisdiction inquiry asks whether the plaintiff’s suit “arises out of or relates to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. This standard does not require that the defendant’s forum contacts **caused** the injury; a sufficient “connection” or “relationship” between the forum contacts and the litigation is enough. *Id.* at 320. Here, the claims center on an alleged design defect in a Ford Explorer—a vehicle that Ford marketed, sold, and serviced in Montana. Ford’s forum activities include selling the same model to Montana consumers, and the accident occurred in Montana. The relationship between Ford’s in-state sales and the litigation is direct: if Ford had not sold any Explorers in Montana, its contacts with the forum would be minimal; but because it did, the claim “relates to” those contacts. *See Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643, 648 (1950) (upholding jurisdiction where defendant’s forum solicitation gave rise to the obligation sued upon).

**III. A Single Contact Is Sufficient If the Cause of Action Has a Substantial Connection to the Forum**

Even if Ford’s Montana contacts were limited to advertising and parts sales, those contacts would still support jurisdiction. In *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957), this Court held that a single insurance contract mailed into the forum was enough to confer specific jurisdiction because the suit arose from that contract. Similarly, in *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927), the Court upheld a nonresident motorist statute under which a single act—driving into the state—gave rise to jurisdiction. Ford’s continuous marketing and sales in Montana are far more substantial than the single contacts in *McGee* and *Hess*. Thus, the connection between Ford’s Montana activities and the present litigation is “more than sufficient” to satisfy due process. *Travelers Health*, 339 U.S. at 648.

**IV. Personal Jurisdiction Is Consistent with Fair Play and Substantial Justice**

Ford is a global corporation with the resources to defend itself in any forum. It has invoked the protections of Montana law by selling and servicing vehicles there. Requiring Ford to answer in Montana for a defect that allegedly caused death in Montana imposes no unreasonable burden. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. The “minimum contacts” test is “flexible” and “depends on the quality and nature of the activity in relation to the fair and orderly administration of the laws.” *Id.* at 319. Here, Ford’s deliberate, ongoing, and profitable contacts with Montana make it “reasonable” to require Ford to defend a suit arising from the very product it markets in that state. *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940) (holding that jurisdiction is proper when the defendant has “certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice”).

**V. Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with the Due Process Clause as interpreted in *International Shoe*, *McGee*, *Hanson*, *Travelers Health*, *Perkins*, *Hess*, *Milliken*, and *Gray*. Ford’s purposeful availment of the Montana market, combined with the direct relationship between its forum contacts and the claims, provides the “minimum contacts” required for specific personal jurisdiction. The judgment below should be affirmed.
