# ARGUMENT

## I. Santander Is Not a “Debt Collector” Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) Because It Collects Debts Owed to Itself, Not to Another.

The FDCPA regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors collecting their own accounts. The statute defines a debt collector, in relevant part, as a person “who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This Court has already recognized that the statute’s coverage turns on this agency-like relationship: the debt collector acts on behalf of, or in relation to, debts owed to a third party. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (the Act “includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another’”). When Santander purchased the petitioners’ defaulted retail installment contracts outright, the debts ceased to be owed to “another”; they became debts owed to Santander itself. Santander therefore collected on its own account, precisely the conduct the statutory text excludes from the debt-collector definition.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309 (11th Cir. 2015), confirms this reading. There, as here, the plaintiffs argued that an assignee who once served as a third-party collector remained a “debt collector” merely because it later purchased the debt it had been servicing. The court rejected that argument, explaining that “our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection.” *Id.* at 1318. The relevant temporal frame is the moment of the challenged collection activity, not the debt’s origination or prior servicing history. Petitioners’ theory—that Santander’s prior status as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto locks in “debt collector” status even after outright purchase—cannot be squared with this textual command.

The Ninth Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204 (9th Cir. 2013), holding that “[t]he statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’” *Id.* at 1209. Petitioners ask this Court to adopt exactly the interpretation *Schlegel* rejected: that a debt’s pedigree, rather than its present ownership, controls whether the current holder is a debt collector. Nothing in the statutory text supports substituting an inquiry into original ownership for the plain command that the collector be gathering debts “due another” at the time it collects.

## II. Petitioners’ Default-Timing Theory Improperly Collapses the Statute’s Two-Step Structure.

Petitioners’ argument rests entirely on the exclusion found in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which removes from the debt-collector definition “any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt . . . owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another to the extent such activity . . . concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). But an exclusion, by definition, applies only to persons who already satisfy the threshold definition of “debt collector.” A person who collects for his own account never reaches the exclusion analysis because he never satisfies the “owed or due another” requirement in the first instance. Petitioners’ effort to work backward from an exclusion to establish threshold coverage inverts the statute’s structure and cannot substitute for the textual showing the Act requires.

This ordering principle finds support in ordinary interpretive canons. Where a modifying phrase—here, “another”—follows several coordinate terms, courts read it to apply uniformly rather than selectively. *See Paroline v. United States*, 134 S. Ct. 1710, 1721 (2014) (“When several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all” (quoting *Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920))). Applying that principle here, “another” modifies both “owed or due” and “asserted to be owed or due,” confirming that the debt-collector definition consistently requires a third-party relationship, not mere historical proximity to a prior collector’s conduct.

## III. The Circuit Split Petitioners Invoke Does Not Aid Their Position.

Petitioners rely on decisions such as *Bridge v. Ocwen Federal Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355 (6th Cir. 2012), *Ruth v. Triumph Partnerships*, 577 F.3d 790 (7th Cir. 2009), and *FTC v. Check Investors, Inc.*, 502 F.3d 159 (3d Cir. 2007), for the proposition that default status alone converts a debt purchaser into a debt collector. But those decisions do not analyze the statute’s threshold “owed or due another” requirement with the textual rigor this Court’s precedents demand; they instead assume that “creditor” and “debt collector” are mutually exclusive categories triggered solely by default timing. That assumption cannot overcome the statute’s plain text, which asks only whether the collector is gathering debts on behalf of another at the time of collection—not whether the debt happened to be delinquent when acquired.

## CONCLUSION

Because Santander purchased the petitioners’ loans outright and thereafter collected them for its own account, it was not collecting “debts owed or due . . . another” within the meaning of § 1692a(6). The judgment below should be affirmed.
