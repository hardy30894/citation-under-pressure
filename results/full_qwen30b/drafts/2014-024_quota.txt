**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—lies at the heart of the First Amendment’s protection against criminalizing speech absent a clear intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective “true threat” standard, which permits conviction based solely on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening, violates the constitutional requirement that speech be punished only when the speaker *intends* to threaten. This Court has long held that the First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a narrow category of unprotected expression, and the “true threat” exception is not an exception to the rule of subjective intent. Rather, it is *defined* by it. The Court must reverse the judgment below and remand for a new trial consistent with the constitutional standard established in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), and reaffirmed in *United States v. Davis*, 588 U.S. ___ (2019), *United States v. Alvarez*, 562 U.S. 596 (2011), and *Matal v. Tam*, 582 U.S. ___ (2017).

**I. The First Amendment Requires Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten**

The “true threat” exception to the First Amendment is not a license to punish speech based on its perceived danger to others. It is a narrow exception that applies only when the speaker *intends* to threaten. In *Virginia v. Black*, the Court held that “a true threat is a knowing and willful expression of intent to harm another person,” 538 U.S. at 359, and that “the First Amendment protects the expression of political views, even if they are offensive or threatening in tone, unless the speaker intends to threaten.” Id. at 360. The Court emphasized that “the threat must be made with the intent to threaten,” id., and that “a threat is not a threat unless the speaker intends it to be one.” Id. at 361. This is not a mere policy preference—it is a constitutional imperative. As the Court explained in *United States v. Alvarez*, 562 U.S. at 608, “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive, disagreeable, or even hateful, unless it falls within a narrow category of unprotected speech.” The “true threat” exception is one such category—but only when the speaker *subjectively* intends to threaten.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits conviction based on an objective standard, effectively criminalizes speech that a reasonable person might interpret as threatening, even if the speaker did not intend to threaten. This standard is constitutionally infirm. In *United States v. Davis*, 588 U.S. at ___, the Court reiterated that “a conviction under a statute that criminalizes speech must be supported by proof of the speaker’s intent to threaten.” Id. at ___ (slip op., at 12). The Court rejected the government’s argument that an objective standard sufficed, stating that “the First Amendment does not permit the government to punish speech based on its potential to cause fear, absent a showing that the speaker intended to cause that fear.” Id. at ___ (slip op., at 13). The Court further held that “a threat is not a threat unless the speaker intends it to be one,” id. at ___ (slip op., at 14), echoing *Virginia v. Black*.

**II. The Objective Standard Violates the Rule of Lenity and Due Process**

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also violates the rule of lenity and due process. The rule of lenity requires that ambiguous criminal statutes be construed in favor of the defendant. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 347 (1971). Section 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of “any threat to injure the person of another.” The term “threat” is not self-defining. Without a requirement of subjective intent, the statute becomes unconstitutionally vague, as it allows punishment for speech that a speaker may not even realize is threatening. In *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 292 (2008), the Court held that “a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits,” and that “a person of ordinary intelligence must be able to understand what is prohibited.” The Third Circuit’s standard fails this test: a person may write a violent rap lyric or post a dark joke on Facebook, believing it to be satire, yet be convicted under an objective standard that ignores intent.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including the defendant’s mental state. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The Court has repeatedly held that “the Constitution requires that a criminal defendant be found guilty only if the government proves every element of the offense, including the requisite mental state, beyond a reasonable doubt.” *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995). The Third Circuit’s instruction—requiring only that a “reasonable speaker” would foresee the statement as threatening—effectively eliminates the mental state requirement. This is impermissible. In *United States v. Alvarez*, 562 U.S. at 608, the Court emphasized that “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive, disagreeable, or even hateful, unless the speaker intends to threaten.” The Court cannot allow a conviction to stand when the speaker did not intend to threaten.
