The question presented in this case is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent, when the infringing component is part of a larger product. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, rather than limiting damages to the profits attributable to the infringing design elements.

The Patent Act provides that a design patent infringer "shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit" from the infringing article. 35 U.S.C. § 289. However, this provision must be read in conjunction with the requirement that damages be apportioned to reflect the value added to the infringing product by the patented design. As the Supreme Court has held, "the statutory measure of damages [for patent infringement] is 'damages adequate to compensate for the infringement.'" Dowling v. United States, 473 U.S. 207, 227 (1985) (quoting 35 U.S.C. § 284). In the context of design patents, this means that damages should be limited to the profits attributable to the infringing design.

The Federal Circuit's decision to affirm the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones is inconsistent with this principle. The court failed to require apportionment of damages to reflect the value added to the infringing product by the patented design. This failure is contrary to the holding in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), which established that damages for patent infringement must be apportioned to reflect the value of the patented invention.

In Garretson, the Court held that a patentee is entitled to recover damages "attributable to the use of the patented improvement" and that the patentee must "give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features" of the infringing product. Id. at 121. The Federal Circuit's decision in this case ignores this requirement and allows for an award of damages that is not apportioned to reflect the value of the patented design.

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the principles of statutory construction. The court failed to consider the plain meaning of the statute and the legislative history of § 289. As the Supreme Court has held, "the starting point for interpreting a statute is the language of the statute itself." Consumer Prod. Safety Comm'n v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980). A careful reading of § 289 reveals that it does not explicitly provide for damages based on the entire profits from the sale of a product that contains an infringing design element.

This Court should reverse the Federal Circuit's decision and hold that damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringing component. This approach is consistent with the principles of statutory construction and the requirement that damages be apportioned to reflect the value added to the infringing product by the patented design. By limiting damages to the profits attributable to the infringing design, this Court can ensure that design patent holders are fairly compensated for infringement, while also preventing windfalls to patent holders.
