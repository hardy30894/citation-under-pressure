**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). The Supreme Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 690-91. The Court held that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." *Id.* at 701.

The Fifth Circuit erred in upholding Zadvydas’s detention under § 1231(a)(6) because it failed to apply this reasonable-time limitation. The court’s prior standard—requiring Zadvydas to show that deportation was "impossible"—improperly shifted the burden and imposed an unworkable standard. *Id.* at 700. The Supreme Court rejected this approach, emphasizing that the habeas court must determine whether detention exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *Id.* at 704.

II. THE CONSTITUTION FORBIDS INDEFINITE DETENTION OF ADMITTED ALIENS

The Due Process Clause prohibits indefinite detention of aliens lawfully admitted to the United States. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 692. The Court distinguished *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), which involved an excludable alien at the border, noting that Mezei had "not effected entry into the United States." *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700 (citing *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 212). Unlike Mezei, Zadvydas was a resident alien with deep ties to this country, having lived here since childhood. *Id.* at 681.

The Court recognized that indefinite detention raises "serious constitutional concerns" because it risks "condemn[ing] the alien to near-permanent imprisonment." *Id.* at 691. The Government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention conflicts with the Constitution’s prohibition on punitive detention without clear congressional authorization. *Id.* at 692-93.

III. THE HABEAS COURT PROPERLY CONCLUDED THAT REMOVAL WAS NOT REASONABLY FORESEEABLE

The district court correctly determined that Zadvydas’s removal was not reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 681. The Government conceded that no country would accept Zadvydas, and his efforts to obtain Lithuanian citizenship—his apparent country of origin—had stalled. *Id.* at 681, 705. The Fifth Circuit’s prior conclusion that removal was merely "difficult" ignored the reality that Zadvydas faced permanent detention. *Id.* at 700.

The Supreme Court established a six-month presumptively reasonable period for post-removal detention, after which the Government must rebut a showing that removal is not likely in the reasonably foreseeable future. *Id.* at 705. Here, Zadvydas had been detained for years beyond this period, and the Government failed to meet its burden. *Id.* at 705-06.

IV. SUPERVISED RELEASE IS THE APPROPRIATE REMEDY

The Court made clear that the choice is not between imprisonment and "living at large," but between imprisonment and supervised release under conditions. *Id.* at 702 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(3)). The district court’s conditions—requiring Zadvydas to report monthly, maintain employment, and reside with his wife—adequately address any flight or safety risks. *Id.* at 706. The Government has not challenged these conditions, nor has Zadvydas violated them. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The Court should affirm the district court’s judgment that Zadvydas’s continued detention violates the Constitution and the statute. The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention, and the Due Process Clause forbids it. The habeas court properly concluded that removal was not reasonably foreseeable and ordered supervised release under appropriate conditions.
