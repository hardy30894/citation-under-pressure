**Argument**

The judgment should be reversed because the Constitution fixes the qualifications for Members of Congress and leaves no room for state supplementation. The qualifications Clauses are exclusive. Article I provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state when chosen; a Senator must be at least thirty, a citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the state when chosen. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. In **Powell v. McCormack**, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that these are the “exclusive qualifications” for membership in the House, and that Congress may not add to them. Id. at 522. The same logic applies to the States. If the Constitution itself fixes the qualifications, no state may alter them by indirection or by relabeling a disqualification as a ballot-access restriction.

That principle was confirmed in **U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton**, 514 U.S. 779 (1995). There, the Court invalidated Arkansas’s Amendment 73 and held that “the Framers intended the Constitution to be the exclusive source of qualifications for Members of Congress.” Id. at 800-01. The Court explained that the States “lack the power to impose qualifications for federal office, at least insofar as those qualifications are inconsistent with the Constitution.” Id. at 827. A state may not evade that rule by preventing otherwise qualified candidates from appearing on the ballot, because “[a] State, of course, may regulate the election process, but it may not, consistent with the Constitution, impose qualifications for service in the United States Congress.” Id. at 833-34.

This Court’s earlier decision in **Storer v. Brown**, 415 U.S. 724 (1974), does not save Amendment 73. Storer upheld certain ballot-access regulations as neutral election administration, but it also recognized that the Constitution forbids states from adopting measures that operate as substantive disqualifications. Id. at 729-30. Amendment 73 does not merely regulate ballot mechanics; it declares that persons who have served the specified number of terms “shall not be eligible” to appear on the ballot. That is a qualification, not a neutral election rule. See **Cook v. Gralike**, 531 U.S. 510, 523-24 (2001) (invalidating a state measure that “handicapped” congressional candidates and was not a permissible regulation of election procedure). The Constitution does not permit a state to do indirectly what it cannot do directly.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that congressional term limits are invalid under the Federal Constitution, but respondent cannot defend the remainder of Amendment 73. The State urges severance, yet the amendment was presented to voters as a single package tying state and federal term limits together. Severability depends on whether the valid portion would have been adopted without the invalid portion. **Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock**, 480 U.S. 678, 684-85 (1987); **Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Commission**, 286 U.S. 210, 234 (1932). Here, the amendment’s structure and campaign presentation make it impossible to know that the electorate would have approved state term limits standing alone. The provisions were marketed together as one reform. Where the unconstitutional provision is “so connected with the rest” that the measure would not have passed independently, the entire enactment falls. **Alaska Airlines**, 480 U.S. at 685.

Moreover, the Arkansas measure was drafted to operate as a unified constitutional revision. Its preamble, application clause, and ballot presentation repeatedly bundled state and federal offices together. That integration confirms the absence of a severable legislative bargain. See **Regan v. Time, Inc.**, 468 U.S. 641, 653 (1984) (plurality) (severability turns on whether remaining portions can function in a manner consistent with legislative intent). Because the federal and state term-limit provisions were politically and textually intertwined, the valid portions cannot be presumed independently enacted.

The Constitution leaves to the people of each state broad authority over their own officers, but that authority stops at the federal line. States may not alter the qualifications for Members of Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution. **Powell**, 395 U.S. at 522; **Thornton**, 514 U.S. at 800-01, 827, 833-34. Amendment 73 therefore violates Article I and cannot stand. The judgment should be reversed, and the amendment invalidated in full.
