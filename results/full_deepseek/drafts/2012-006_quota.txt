The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the applicable statute of limitations period. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 396 (1957). This is because the conspiracy statute requires the government to establish both the existence of an agreement and the defendant’s knowing participation in it during the limitations period. *United States v. Mardian*, 546 F.2d 973, 977 (D.C. Cir. 1976). The statute of limitations is an element of the offense that the government must prove, not an affirmative defense. *United States v. Persico*, 832 F.2d 705, 711 (2d Cir. 1987).

When a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal from the conspiracy before the limitations period, the government’s burden is not relieved. Instead, the government must then prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period—or, in other words, that the withdrawal was ineffective or the conspiracy continued. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1236 (7th Cir. 1981). As the Seventh Circuit explained, “once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was still a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period.” *Id.* at 1236. The Sixth Circuit agrees: “Once the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period.” *United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d 1077, 1083 (6th Cir. 1991). See also *United States v. Steele*, 685 F.2d 793, 803 (3d Cir. 1982) (withdrawal is an affirmative defense, but the government must still prove membership during the limitations period if the defendant raises the issue).

This allocation of the burden is consistent with the fundamental principle that the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The statute of limitations is a substantive element of the conspiracy offense, not a procedural bar. *Grunewald*, 353 U.S. at 396. Therefore, the government cannot rest on a presumption that the conspiracy continued unless it affirmatively proves that it did. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (noting that withdrawal severs the defendant’s liability for future acts of the conspiracy). The Supreme Court has long recognized that withdrawal is a “break in the chain” of the conspiracy, and after withdrawal, the government cannot rely on the acts of co-conspirators to hold the defendant liable. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464-65 (1978) (withdrawal is an affirmative defense, but the defendant must show “some affirmative act of withdrawal” that is “not merely a cessation of activity”).

In this case, the evidence showed that the leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the relevant statute of limitations period. The defendants presented this evidence in support of their motions for a new trial. The government offered no evidence to establish that the defendants remained members of a single, ongoing conspiracy during the limitations period. The district court denied the motions, and the court of appeals affirmed. Under the governing law, the government had the burden to prove—beyond a reasonable doubt—that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that each defendant was a member of it. *Read*, 658 F.2d at 1236; *Lash*, 937 F.2d at 1083. The government failed to meet that burden. The jury could not rationally infer the defendants’ membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period based solely on the evidence of the earlier split. *See United States v. Sweeney*, 688 F.2d 1131, 1139 (7th Cir. 1982) (the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, not just that it existed at some earlier time).

The court of appeals erred in affirming the denial of the new trial motions. The burden of persuasion on the question of whether the defendants were members of the conspiracy during the limitations period rests with the government. *Grunewald*, 353 U.S. at 396; *Mardian*, 546 F.2d at 977. The defendants’ evidence of withdrawal shifted the burden to the government to prove that the conspiracy continued and that the defendants remained members. *Read*, 658 F.2d at 1236; *Lash*, 937 F.2d at 1083. The government did not carry that burden. Accordingly, the convictions on the conspiracy counts must be reversed.
