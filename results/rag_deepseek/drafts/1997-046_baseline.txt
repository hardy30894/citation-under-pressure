**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

The clemency power is an act of grace, not a component of the adjudicatory process. This Court has consistently held that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). Ohio’s clemency process confers no substantive entitlement. The Governor enjoys “almost unfettered discretion,” and the Ohio Constitution imposes no mandatory standards. *See* *Jago v. Van Curen*, 454 U.S. 14, 20 (1981) (parole rescission not protected where early release is “a matter of grace”). Unlike parole-release statutes that create an expectancy of liberty, *see* *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979), clemency is purely discretionary. *Dumschat* rejected the argument that a high rate of favorable commutations creates a protected interest. 452 U.S. at 465. Because Woodard has no life or liberty interest in clemency, the Due Process Clause does not attach.

**II. The Option of a Voluntary Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits *compelled* self-incrimination. Ohio’s clemency procedure does not compel any statement. The interview is entirely voluntary; the inmate may decline to participate or may remain silent during the interview. “In the constitutional sense, compulsion is lacking” when a witness appears voluntarily and is free to refuse to answer. *United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. 181, 188 (1977). The regulation expressly provides that the interview occurs only “if the prisoner requests” it. No penalty attaches to declining the interview or to invoking the privilege. The clemency hearing proceeds regardless, and the parole board considers all other available information. Thus, there is no “Hobson’s choice” that forces a waiver of Fifth Amendment rights.

Any claim that participation might later lead to adverse use of statements is speculative and premature. *See* *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976) (holding it “premature” to adjudicate hypothetical self-incrimination risks). The Fifth Amendment does not require prophylactic judicial intervention before any concrete threat of compulsion arises. *Washington*, 431 U.S. at 188-89. Moreover, the State has a legitimate interest in obtaining information relevant to clemency, and the voluntary interview serves that interest without infringing constitutional rights.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Woodard has no protected interest in clemency, and Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
