The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited individual retirement account (IRA) is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the Bankruptcy Code. The plain language of Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts "retirement funds" from creditors' claims, but an inherited IRA does not qualify as "retirement funds" because it is not held for the debtor's retirement.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with the Bankruptcy Code's purpose of providing a fresh start for debtors by exempting certain assets that are necessary for their support and well-being. However, an inherited IRA is not necessary for the debtor's retirement and can be used for current consumption. As the Seventh Circuit noted, an inherited IRA is subject to different rules than a traditional IRA, including a requirement that the beneficiary withdraw a minimum amount from the account each year, and a prohibition on new contributions. 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A), incorporating 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B); 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C). These rules indicate that an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund, but rather a tax-deferral vehicle.

The Fifth Circuit's decision in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), is distinguishable because it focused on the fact that the funds in the inherited IRA were once "retirement funds" in the hands of the original owner. However, as the Seventh Circuit correctly noted, the Bankruptcy Code's exemption for "retirement funds" is not atemporal, and the fact that the funds were once someone else's retirement funds is not sufficient to qualify them for the exemption. Clark v. Rameker, Nos. 12-1241 & 12-1255, slip op. at 6 (7th Cir. Apr. 23, 2013).

Furthermore, exempting inherited IRAs from creditors' claims would not be consistent with the legislative intent behind Section 522. As the Supreme Court has noted, "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs." Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987). Exempting inherited IRAs would create an incentive for individuals to save more in IRAs and pass the funds to their heirs, potentially shielding large amounts of wealth from creditors. This is not the purpose of the Bankruptcy Code's exemptions, which are intended to provide a fresh start for debtors, not to shield inherited wealth.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is also consistent with the principle that exemptions should be narrowly construed. In re Barker, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985). By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from creditors' claims, the Seventh Circuit has ensured that debtors cannot shield assets that are not necessary for their support and well-being.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the Bankruptcy Code. The plain language of the statute, the legislative intent, and the principles of statutory construction all support this conclusion.
