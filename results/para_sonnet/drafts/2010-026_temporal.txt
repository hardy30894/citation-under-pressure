# ARGUMENT

## THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S "HIGH 2" EXEMPTION HAS NO BASIS IN THE TEXT OF FOIA AND CONTRAVENES THIS COURT'S COMMAND THAT EXEMPTIONS BE NARROWLY CONSTRUED

Exemption 2 of the Freedom of Information Act shields only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit's judgment-affirming holding that Exemption 2 also protects any document that is merely "predominantly" for internal use and whose release might "risk circumvention of agency regulation" cannot be squared with the statute Congress wrote, nor with this Court's repeated instruction that FOIA's exemptions are to be read narrowly against the withholding agency.

### A. The Statutory Text Forecloses the Ninth Circuit's Atextual Standard

This Court has long held that "the words of statutes—including revenue acts—should be interpreted where possible in their ordinary, everyday senses." *Crane v. Commissioner*, 331 U.S. 1, 6 (1947). The word "solely" in Exemption 2 admits no ambiguity: it means exclusively, and nothing else. Congress did not write that the exemption applies to matters "predominantly" related to internal personnel rules; it wrote "solely." Where Congress "has made its choice" in the language it enacted, courts are not free to substitute a looser standard of their own design. *United States v. Rutherford*, 442 U.S. 544 does not control here because it postdates 1970, but the principle it later articulated has deep roots in this Court's earlier decisions construing plain statutory text according to its terms. *See Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 227 (1957) (courts must not "distort the uncomplicated language" of a statute "in order to reach a result which is favored").

ESQD arc data are not rules or practices governing Navy personnel in any sense the statute contemplates. The maps are technical, physics-based calculations of blast radii—information that describes the external physical world, not internal agency housekeeping. That the Navy finds the information useful for its own planning does not transform it into a "personnel rule or practice" any more than a weather report used internally by an agency becomes an internal personnel matter. The Ninth Circuit's approach reads "solely" out of the statute altogether, replacing it with a balancing test nowhere authorized by Congress.

### B. This Court's Decision in *Rose* Confirms That Exemption 2 Is Narrow and Cannot Be Expanded to Swallow Sensitive Substantive Information

In *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352 (1976), this Court examined Exemption 2's legislative history and confirmed that FOIA reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." 425 U.S. at 360–61. The Court explained that the exemption's purpose was to relieve agencies of "the burden of assembling and maintaining for public inspection" matters "in which the public could not reasonably be expected to have an interest"—paradigmatically, mundane matters such as "file-keeping procedures" of no genuine public concern. *Id.* at 369–70. Nothing in *Rose* suggests that Congress intended Exemption 2 to reach information of profound public interest concerning the safety of a naval explosives depot situated near populated civilian communities. To the contrary, *Rose* teaches that the exemption is aimed at trivial, internal-housekeeping matters, not information the public urgently needs to evaluate risks to life and safety.

That the *Rose* Court left open, in dicta, whether some internal matter might be withheld if disclosure would risk "circumvention of agency regulation," *id.* at 369, provides no warrant for jettisoning the "solely" requirement altogether. A limited residual concern about circumvention of internal rules does not authorize courts to exempt from disclosure entire categories of externally-focused, scientific data merely because an agency also uses that data internally. The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" gloss, borrowed from the D.C. Circuit's freestanding invention rather than any statutory source, converts a narrow exception into a broad license for agencies to withhold whatever they find operationally convenient to conceal.

### C. Exemptions From Disclosure Must Be Narrowly Construed, and Doubts Resolved in Favor of Disclosure

This Court has consistently instructed that FOIA's statutory exemptions "are to be narrowly construed," *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, and that the general philosophy of the Act mandates disclosure "unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Id.* at 360–61. The agency bears the burden of justifying withholding, and that burden is not met by an amorphous showing that disclosure might theoretically be inconvenient to internal planning.

The presumption favoring disclosure that animates FOIA reflects a broader principle of statutory construction recognizing that when Congress enacts a general rule of disclosure with narrow, specified exceptions, courts should not construct additional exceptions from whole cloth. *Cf. Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Wisconsin*, 347 U.S. 672, 682 (1954) (courts should not extend statutory coverage beyond what Congress's chosen language supports). Just as courts may not enlarge exemptions from regulatory statutes beyond their text, they may not enlarge FOIA's exemptions beyond the "solely" limitation that Congress imposed.

Here, the ESQD arc data are unquestionably a matter of "genuine and significant public interest," *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369 (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965)) — precisely the touchstone by which this Court has distinguished exemptible from non-exemptible material. Petitioner and the community surrounding Naval Magazine Indian Island have an acute interest in understanding the blast radii of stored ordnance located across open water from their homes, hospitals, and schools. This is the antithesis of the trivial "parking, hours of work, lunch hour" material Congress meant to exclude from public scrutiny. *Id.* at 369.

### D. The Agency's Burden Was Not Met

Because the burden of establishing an exemption rests with the agency, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), and because exemptions are construed narrowly against the withholding party, the Navy's affidavits describing internal planning uses of the ESQD data cannot suffice to bring information admittedly directed at describing external, physical blast effects within a provision limited to matters related "solely" to internal personnel rules and practices. The Ninth Circuit's contrary holding should be reversed.
