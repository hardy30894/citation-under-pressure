A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Mr. Pearson’s first claim—that Congress lacked constitutional authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951—fails under settled Commerce Clause precedent. The Supreme Court long ago held that Congress may regulate not only the channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce, but also intrastate conduct that has a substantial relation to interstate commerce. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); Houston, East & West Texas Railway Co. v. United States, 234 U.S. 342 (1914). Congress may reach local conduct when necessary to protect the free flow of commerce among the States. Stafford v. Wallace, 258 U.S. 495 (1922); NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1 (1937).

Section 1951, the Hobbs Act, is a direct exercise of that authority. By its terms, it prohibits robbery and extortion that “in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce.” 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). The statute’s jurisdictional element limits its reach to conduct with an actual effect on commerce, and that limitation is precisely what renders it constitutional. See Stirone v. United States, 361 U.S. 212 (1960) (recognizing that the Hobbs Act protects interstate commerce from interference by robbery and extortion). Courts have repeatedly upheld federal regulation of activities affecting the movement of goods and money in interstate channels. Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942); Mandeville Island Farms, Inc. v. American Crystal Sugar Co., 334 U.S. 219 (1948).

The evidence here easily satisfied § 1951’s commerce nexus. Mr. Peterson testified that Mr. Goodeents is part of a national chain, purchases goods from both Kansas and out-of-state suppliers, and uses its receipts to buy goods produced outside Kansas. That proof shows the robbery depleted assets of a business engaged in interstate purchasing and thus affected commerce within the meaning of the statute. Congress may constitutionally punish robberies that diminish the assets of businesses engaged in interstate trade because such conduct, in the aggregate, burdens commerce among the States. Wickard, 317 U.S. at 127-28; Mandeville Island Farms, 334 U.S. at 236. Federal jurisdiction does not depend on a showing that this particular robbery halted a shipment or crossed state lines; a substantial effect on interstate commerce is enough. Jones & Laughlin Steel, 301 U.S. at 37.

Mr. Pearson’s broader constitutional attack therefore amounts to a disagreement with Congress’s policy judgment, not a valid challenge to its power. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that local conduct is immune from federal regulation simply because it occurs within one State. See Jones & Laughlin Steel, 301 U.S. at 36-37; United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100 (1941). Robbery of a business buying and selling interstate goods plainly falls within the class of conduct Congress may reach to safeguard commerce from obstruction. Stafford, 258 U.S. at 520.

Accordingly, the Hobbs Act is constitutional as applied here, and this Court should reject Mr. Pearson’s first assignment of error.
