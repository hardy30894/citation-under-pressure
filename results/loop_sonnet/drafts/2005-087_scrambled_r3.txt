**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884), the Court held that the Due Process Clause does not freeze criminal procedure into any single mode, but permits the States to experiment with and adopt varying procedural and substantive frameworks so long as the proceeding as a whole remains fundamentally fair. That same latitude extends to a State's choice of how mental-disease evidence may be used at trial, including whether such evidence is confined to an insanity defense or may also be deployed to negate an element of the offense.

This channeling approach is consistent with the historical understanding of insanity as an affirmative defense. Under the rule announced in *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), mental disease was treated as a matter to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a device for negating the prosecution's proof of intent element-by-element. Arizona's rule preserves that traditional separation.

This Court's decision governing federal insanity procedure, *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373 (1897), likewise treated the question of a defendant's sanity as a discrete inquiry once raised by the evidence, rather than as a freestanding avenue for defeating the mens rea element through unchanneled psychiatric testimony. Nothing in *Davis* suggests that the Constitution requires a State to permit mental-disease evidence to be used interchangeably for both purposes.

State courts confronting the proper scope and formulation of the insanity inquiry have long adopted differing standards without any suggestion that due process compels a single approach. See *Parsons v. State*, 81 Ala. 577, 2 So. 854 (1887) (adopting a test for criminal responsibility incorporating both cognitive incapacity and irresistible impulse). That jurisdictions have long differed in structuring the relationship between mental disease and criminal responsibility confirms that this is a matter of substantive criminal law entrusted to the several jurisdictions, not a uniform constitutional command.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules. In *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court explained that a state procedure violates due process only where it undermines the basic fairness of the trial as a whole—not merely because the State has declined to adopt the specific evidentiary approach a defendant would prefer. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), reinforced this understanding, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate every feature of criminal procedure that might be preferred by a defendant, but asks only whether the challenged practice denies a fundamental safeguard of liberty and justice.

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, recognizing that the adjudicative process necessarily requires the drawing of lines regarding the admissibility and use of evidence, and that such line-drawing by the States is entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

This Court's fundamental-fairness inquiry, articulated in *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), and *Palko*, asks whether a state procedure works a denial of the kind of basic fairness that due process guarantees, not whether the State has adopted the specific procedural framework a defendant would prefer. Arizona's channeling rule does not deny Clark a fair opportunity to present his mental-disease evidence; it was fully admitted and considered by the trial court on the question of insanity. The rule merely declines to allow that same evidence, freed from the burdens attending the insanity defense, to be redeployed against the mens rea element the State must independently prove.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's rule reflects the same latitude this Court recognized in *Hurtado*, respects the traditional separation between insanity and mens rea reflected in *M'Naghten's Case*, *Davis*, and *Parsons*, and comports with the fundamental-fairness principles of *Snyder*, *Palko*, *Spencer*, and *Betts*. Because Arizona's channeling rule does not deny Clark the basic fairness that due process demands, this Court should affirm the judgment below.
