# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or a park, the kind of property that has traditionally been used for purposes of public assembly and the exchange of ideas among citizens. See *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to social, civic, and recreational activities benefiting the community. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains substantial authority to control the use of its own property for the purposes to which it has been lawfully dedicated. See *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966) (state property may be preserved for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated, and government need not surrender control of its own property to every use citizens might wish to make of it). A government that opens its property for certain limited purposes is not thereby required to throw the property open to every other use, however incompatible with the purposes for which access was granted. *Id.*

This Court has likewise recognized that the government may impose reasonable, evenhanded conditions on the use of public property so long as those conditions are not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 398 (1953) (upholding a municipal licensing requirement governing the use of a public park for meetings, including religious meetings, as a reasonable regulation of the use of public property). Milford's Community Use Policy operates on the same principle: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. That line is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression.

The Good News Club's proposed meeting, as described in the stipulated record, opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" segment inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer. This is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open to all speakers.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. As the Court held in striking down state-composed classroom prayer, "it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). The Court reinforced that principle the following year in holding that devotional Bible reading conducted within the public schools, even on a voluntary basis, offends the neutrality the Religion Clauses require. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963).

The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms in which the school's educational mission is otherwise carried out, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. This Court has held that use of tax-supported public school buildings for religious instruction of children enrolled in the school offends the Establishment Clause even where attendance is not formally compelled. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 209–10 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time religious instruction precisely because it occurred away from school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored the religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause requires that state power be "no more . . . used so as to handicap religions[] than . . . to favor them"—a neutrality that cuts both ways. *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club immediately after school hours, in the school's own classrooms, would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
