## Argument

The district court's supplemental jury instruction on the meaning of "shielding" was erroneous and prejudicial to Xiang Hui Ye. The court's definition of "shielding" was overly broad and vague, and it failed to accurately convey the statutory meaning of the term.

The term "shielding" is not defined in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), and the district court was required to provide a clear and accurate definition to the jury. However, instead of relying on the plain meaning of the statute or relevant case law, the court provided a definition that was not grounded in the statute. This was error.

In United States v. Smith, 112 F.2d 83 (2d Cir. 1940), the Second Circuit held that a district court's jury instructions must be clear and accurate, and that ambiguous or vague instructions can be grounds for reversal. Here, the district court's definition of "shielding" was ambiguous and vague, and it failed to provide the jury with a clear understanding of the term.

Furthermore, the district court's definition of "shielding" was not supported by relevant case law. In United States v. Heitler, 274 F. 401 (N.D. Ill. 1921), the court held that the term "harboring" in a similar statute required proof of an intent to conceal or shield the individual from detection. The district court's instruction in this case failed to convey this intent requirement, and instead focused on the general concept of "shielding" without providing a clear definition.

The erroneous jury instruction was prejudicial to Ye because it allowed the jury to convict him based on a vague and overly broad understanding of the term "shielding." As a result, Ye's conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) should be reversed.

Moreover, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that Ye intended to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees. The government's evidence showed that Ye knew his employees were illegal aliens and that he took steps to conceal their employment, such as paying them in cash and not maintaining accurate records. However, the evidence did not demonstrate that Ye's actions were taken with the specific intent to shield the aliens from detection.

In Weiler v. United States, 323 U.S. 606 (1945), the Supreme Court held that a conviction under a similar statute required proof of a specific intent to commit the crime. Here, the government's evidence failed to demonstrate that Ye had the specific intent to shield the aliens from detection, and therefore, his conviction should be reversed.

In conclusion, the district court's supplemental jury instruction on the meaning of "shielding" was erroneous, and the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove Ye's intent to shield the aliens from detection. As a result, Ye's conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) should be reversed.
