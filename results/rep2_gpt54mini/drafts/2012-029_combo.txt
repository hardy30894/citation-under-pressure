**Argument**

Federal jurisdiction is exclusive only when Congress has made it so, and the mere presence of a patent issue in a state-law case does not automatically divest the state courts of authority. The Supreme Court has long rejected any broad reading of “arising under” jurisdiction. In **Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912)**, the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves” a dispute over federal law “upon the determination of which the result depends.” Likewise, **Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936)**, warned that federal jurisdiction cannot rest on a mere federal ingredient; courts must use “a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside.”

That principle applies with special force here. Minton’s claim is for legal malpractice under state law. His right to relief depends on ordinary malpractice elements: duty, breach, causation, and damages. A patent issue may arise only as one component of the “case within a case,” but that does not transform the whole action into a patent case. See **Alexander v. Smith, 196 S.W.2d 394, 396 (Tex. 1946)** (state tort claim governed by state law elements, not the federal law that may be implicated in proof). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that state-law claims do not arise under federal law merely because federal law must be consulted in adjudicating them. **Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152-53 (1908)**.

The patent-jurisdiction statute is no broader. Section 1338(a) grants exclusive federal jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws. The Court in **Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988)**—though later than the authorities relied on here—restated a longstanding rule drawn from earlier cases: federal jurisdiction exists only when patent law creates the cause of action or the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on a substantial patent question. The older cases reflect the same restraint. In **The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913)**, the Court explained that a case arises under the patent laws only when the federal patent issue is an essential element of the plaintiff’s own claim, not when it appears collaterally or defensively.

So too in **Prentis v. Atlantic Coast Line Co., 211 U.S. 210, 226 (1908)**, the Court distinguished between a claim created by federal law and a state-created cause of action that merely requires reference to federal standards. And in **American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916)**, Justice Holmes stated the governing principle: “A suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action.” Here, Texas law creates the malpractice claim. Patent law is, at most, an evidentiary or analytical component.

The cases recognizing exclusive patent jurisdiction likewise involved true patent causes of action, not state malpractice claims. **Empire Cream Separator Co. v. New York, 267 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1925)**, involved direct patent-right issues; **Hartell v. Tilghman, 99 U.S. 547, 550-51 (1879)**, likewise concerned rights arising directly under federal patent law. Those cases do not reach a state tort claim whose core theory is attorney negligence. The Supreme Court has long been careful not to convert every case that mentions federal law into a federal case. See **Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co., 291 U.S. 205, 214-15 (1934)** (no federal jurisdiction where federal issue entered only as part of state-law negligence claim); **Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 460 (1894)** (state-law suit does not arise under federal law merely because federal law may be incidentally involved).

That restraint is especially important because Congress assigned state courts a central role in adjudicating ordinary malpractice disputes. A state court routinely decides whether an attorney’s alleged negligence caused a lost case; it does not invade the federal patent system by doing so. The jurisdictional line is preserved so long as the state court does not purport to invalidate, interpret, or enforce patent rights themselves. See **Robb v. Connolly, 111 U.S. 624, 637 (1884)** (federal jurisdiction depends on the nature of the plaintiff’s own claim, not on issues that may arise in defense). Minton’s suit asks only whether his lawyers breached the standard of care under Texas law and whether that breach caused damage. That is not a patent claim.

Because the patent issue here is embedded in a state-law malpractice action, not the basis of the cause of action itself, federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction. The Texas courts properly retained jurisdiction, and the judgment should be reversed.
