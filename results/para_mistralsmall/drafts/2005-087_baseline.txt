**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Confer a Right to Present Expert Mental-Health Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Independent of an Insanity Defense**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause does not require states to permit criminal defendants to introduce expert evidence of mental illness to rebut the prosecution’s proof of mens rea unless the defendant is asserting an insanity defense. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (holding that due process does not mandate a particular insanity test or burden-shifting rule); *see also Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 752 (2006) (affirming that states may define the insanity defense and its relationship to mens rea). Arizona’s rule restricting expert mental-health testimony to the insanity defense does not violate due process because it does not eliminate the defendant’s ability to raise an insanity defense or otherwise challenge criminal responsibility. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752–53.

This Court has long recognized that states possess substantial latitude in defining the elements of criminal offenses and the defenses thereto. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–02 (1977) (upholding New York’s burden-shifting scheme for the affirmative defense of extreme emotional disturbance). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert mental-health evidence is a permissible exercise of this authority. *See id.* at 202 (noting that due process does not forbid states from allocating burdens of proof on affirmative defenses). The state’s rule does not preclude the defendant from presenting evidence of his mental condition; it merely channels that evidence into the statutorily defined insanity defense. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752–53.

**II. Arizona’s Rule Comports with the Due Process Requirement of Fair Notice and Rational Proof**

Due process requires that criminal statutes provide fair warning of prohibited conduct and that the prosecution’s burden of proof be rational and not arbitrary. *See United States v. Bailey*, 444 U.S. 394, 403 (1980) (discussing the requirement of fair notice). Arizona’s rule does not offend these principles. The state’s insanity statute, Ariz. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 13-502, provides clear notice that mental illness may negate criminal responsibility if it satisfies the statutory test. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752 (describing Arizona’s insanity defense). The rule does not arbitrarily exclude relevant evidence; rather, it ensures that evidence of mental illness is considered in the context of the insanity defense, where the state has allocated the burden of proof. *See Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 202–03 (upholding a state’s allocation of the burden of proof for an affirmative defense).

Moreover, the rule does not undermine the prosecution’s burden to prove mens rea beyond a reasonable doubt. *See In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (holding that the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt). Arizona’s rule merely prevents the defendant from using expert mental-health evidence to negate mens rea directly, instead requiring that such evidence be used to support an insanity defense. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752–53. This distinction is rational because it prevents the jury from conflating the concepts of insanity and mens rea, which are distinct under Arizona law. *Id.* at 753.

**III. The Arizona Courts’ Application of the Rule Did Not Violate Due Process**

The Arizona courts’ application of the state’s rule did not deprive Clark of due process. The trial court permitted Clark to present expert testimony about his mental condition, but only in support of his insanity defense. *See id.* at 752. The court did not exclude the evidence entirely; it merely confined its use to the statutorily defined insanity defense. *Id.* This limitation is consistent with due process because it does not prevent Clark from presenting his defense or from introducing relevant evidence in support of that defense. *See id.* at 752–53.

The Arizona Supreme Court’s affirmance of Clark’s conviction did not contravene clearly established federal law. *See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1)* (setting the standard for federal habeas review). The state court’s decision was a reasonable application of *Leland* and *Patterson*, which permit states to define the scope of insanity defenses and the use of mental-health evidence. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752–53. Accordingly, Clark’s due process claim fails.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona courts should be affirmed.
