# ARGUMENT

## I. The Trustee's Discretion to Apply Income Under a Support Standard Does Not Defeat Mrs. Davis's Qualifying Income Interest for Life.

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) requires that the surviving spouse be "entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals." I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I). The Amendment satisfies this requirement because it commands the trustee, in mandatory terms, to "pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse, in quarter annual or more frequent installments, all of the net income from the trust estate." The word "shall," not "may," governs the trustee's obligation to distribute the entire income stream; the phrase "in the trustee's reasonable discretion" that follows modifies only the manner in which that mandatory distribution is to be applied for Mrs. Davis's health, support, and accustomed manner of living—not whether the income will be paid at all.

This distinction is precisely the one drawn by the Supreme Court in Northeastern Pennsylvania National Bank & Trust Co. v. United States, 387 U.S. 213 (1967). There, the trust instrument permitted the trustee either to distribute income currently or to accumulate it, leaving the spouse without any guaranteed right to the income itself. The Court held that a spouse is not "entitled for life to all the income" where the trustee retains the power to withhold or accumulate income rather than pay it out. 387 U.S. at 220. Northeastern Pennsylvania Bank thus disqualified the trust because the beneficiary's entitlement to income was itself contingent on the trustee's unfettered discretion to pay or not to pay. The Davis Amendment presents the opposite structure: the trustee has no power to accumulate or withhold income for the benefit of the remaindermen. Every dollar of net income must be paid to or applied for Mrs. Davis; the trustee's discretion operates only within that mandatory distribution, to determine the "convenient installments" and purposes—health, education, support, maintenance, comfort, and welfare—consistent with her accustomed manner of living. Because no income can ever be diverted from her or accumulated for the remainder beneficiaries, the defect that doomed the trust in Northeastern Pennsylvania Bank is absent here.

## II. Any Ambiguity in the Amendment Must Be Resolved Under State Law in Favor of the Decedent's Manifest Intent to Provide His Wife a Life Income Interest.

Federal estate tax consequences turn on the property interests created under governing state law. Commissioner v. Estate of Bosch, 387 U.S. 456 (1967). In Bosch, the Supreme Court instructed that while state-court determinations of property interests are not automatically binding on the federal courts for tax purposes, the substantive question of what interest the instrument creates is nonetheless a question of state law, to be answered as the state's highest court would answer it. 387 U.S. at 465. This Court applied that principle in the marital-deduction context in Estate of Rapp v. Commissioner, 140 F.3d 1211 (9th Cir. 1998), where the trust instrument's income and support provisions were construed according to state-law principles of trust interpretation to determine whether the surviving spouse's interest satisfied the QTIP requirements.

Under California law, a trust instrument must be construed to give effect to the intention of the trustor, and where an instrument is ambiguous, extrinsic evidence of surrounding circumstances is admissible to resolve that ambiguity. Applying that principle, the decedent's intent in executing the Amendment is unmistakable: paragraph 2 is captioned "Life Estate to Surviving Spouse," and it directs the trustee to pay "all of the net income" to Mrs. Davis for her life. The reference to "health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare" was plainly intended to guide the trustee's judgment as to how—not whether—income should be paid, mirroring the kind of support language routinely used to describe an income beneficiary's standard of living without converting a mandatory income trust into a discretionary one. Construing the Amendment consistent with the decedent's evident purpose of providing for his wife's care while preserving principal for his daughters, as Bosch and Rapp require, confirms that Mrs. Davis's entitlement to all trust income is unconditional.

## III. The Remaining Statutory Requirements of Section 2056(b)(7) Are Satisfied.

No provision of the Amendment permits the trustee to appoint any part of the trust property to any person other than Mrs. Davis during her lifetime, as section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II) requires. The successor beneficiaries, Pencke and Bennett, take only upon Mrs. Davis's death, and the "narrow standard" invasion-of-principal provision in paragraph 5 operates solely for Mrs. Davis's own benefit, after exhaustion of her separate trust assets—again for her health, support, and maintenance. Such a provision, far from disqualifying the interest, reinforces that every power exercisable during Mrs. Davis's lifetime runs to her advantage alone.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Amendment mandates distribution of all net trust income to Mrs. Davis for life, subject only to the trustee's discretion as to the manner of application, her interest is a "qualifying income interest for life" under section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Tax Court's contrary construction should be reversed, and the marital deduction claimed by the estate should be allowed.
