**ARGUMENT**

**I. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because a Reasonable Jury Could Find That Appellants' Protected Safety Reports Were a Contributing Factor in BNSF's Discipline.**

The district court resolved a quintessential jury question—BNSF's motive—on a paper record. That was error. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases where "motive and intent play leading roles" and "the proof is largely in the hands of the alleged" wrongdoer. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). Here, BNSF's motive for disciplining Foster, Kline, and Snyder is precisely what is disputed, and precisely what BNSF controls: the investigating officers, the hearing record, and the ultimate disciplinary decision were all creatures of BNSF itself.

The causation standard governing railroad-safety retaliation is not demanding. Courts have long held, in the analogous railroad-injury context, that a plaintiff need only show that the forbidden consideration "played any part, even the slightest," in the challenged action. *Rogers v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506 (1957). Appellants' evidence easily meets that threshold. Kline and Snyder identified the bridge's lack of side rails and lighting in their very first written statements to Trainmaster Dixon on the night of the accident. Foster gave a recorded statement to Claims Representative Murphy on April 5 criticizing BNSF's decision to route crew changes across a dangerous, unlit bridge that employees had previously flagged as hazardous. The very next day—April 6—BNSF issued notice that it was investigating the same employees who had just reported the unsafe conditions. That sequence is not innocuous. "The fact that the discharges followed closely on the heels of" protected conduct "is a potent circumstance" supporting an inference of retaliatory purpose. *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 602 (1941). A jury, not a court on summary judgment, should decide whether that timing reflects coincidence or retaliation.

**II. The Escalating Scrutiny and Irregularities Surrounding the Disciplinary Hearing Independently Support an Inference of Pretext.**

Appellants' evidence of increased, undisclosed operations testing during the pendency of the investigation—testing so significant that Kline and Snyder altered their work assignments, sometimes at a financial cost, to avoid being blamed for co-workers' errors—is itself probative of retaliatory intent. Conduct need not be a formal discharge to support liability; discriminatory treatment that is "inherently destructive" of protected rights, or that has more than a "comparatively slight" adverse effect, permits an inference of unlawful motive without independent direct proof, and where such effect is more than slight, the surrounding circumstances must be weighed by the trier of fact. *NLRB v. Great Dane Trailers, Inc.*, 388 U.S. 26, 34 (1967). Subjecting the very employees who reported unsafe conditions to heightened, secret surveillance—while the disciplinary charges against them remained pending for nine months—is the sort of conduct a jury could find "inherently destructive" of the right to report safety hazards.

The hearing itself compounds the problem. Dixon and Knutstrom were not neutral witnesses; Dixon had received the initial safety complaints, and both men were the architects of the theory that the crew-change location was "east" of the bridge, directly contradicting the sworn testimony of every plaintiff and crew member who said they were told to cross west. A reviewing tribunal may not simply accept the employer's account by isolating favorable testimony; it must consider "whatever in the record fairly detracts" from the weight of that evidence. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Here, that includes the uniform, contrary testimony of Foster, Kline, Snyder, and their fellow crew member, as well as the undisputed absence of any written crew-change instruction. A jury could reasonably conclude that General Manager Thompson's post-hearing decision to discipline Foster, Snyder, and Kline—but not Dixon or Knutstrom—reflected pretext rather than an evenhanded application of operating rules.

**III. Credibility Determinations of This Kind Cannot Be Resolved on Summary Judgment.**

Where, as here, the outcome turns on whose account of events—Appellants' or BNSF's supervisors'—is to be believed, summary judgment is foreclosed. It is well established that a court's task on such a motion "is not himself to weigh the evidence and determine the truth of the matter," particularly where competing testimony bears on intent or credibility. *Sartor v. Arkansas Nat. Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Nor may an employer insulate itself from liability merely by reciting facially neutral rule violations after the fact; the ultimate question is whether the proffered reasons are pretext for a forbidden purpose, and that inquiry likewise depends on the whole record and the fact-finder's assessment of the parties' credibility. *NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 289 (1965).

**Conclusion**

Because genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding causation, pretext, and BNSF's motive, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for trial.
