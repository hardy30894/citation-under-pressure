**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right—distinct from the insanity defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state in order to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent. Arizona’s rule, which limits the use of such evidence to the affirmative defense of insanity, is consistent with constitutional due process and comports with long-standing precedent recognizing that states retain broad authority to define the scope and procedural contours of criminal defenses.

The Due Process Clause protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial, including the right to present a defense. See *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998). However, this right is not absolute, and the Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert testimony in any manner he chooses. *Id.* at 315. Rather, the Constitution permits states to impose reasonable limits on the admissibility of evidence so long as those limits do not render the trial fundamentally unfair. *See, e.g., Idaho v. Wright*, 497 U.S. 805, 818 (1990) (“The Due Process Clause does not require a State to admit evidence that is so unreliable as to have no tendency to prove the matter at issue.”).

Arizona law properly confines the use of expert testimony on mental state to the insanity defense. Under Arizona law, a defendant asserting insanity bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that, at the time of the offense, he was unable to appreciate the nature and quality of his conduct or to distinguish right from wrong. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501. This is a well-established, affirmative defense that requires a specific burden of proof and a distinct evidentiary standard. The state’s decision to limit the use of expert testimony to this defense is not arbitrary or irrational; it reflects a legitimate policy choice to prevent the confusion of issues and to ensure that the jury is not misled into believing that a defendant’s mental condition negates criminal intent without meeting the higher burden required for an insanity acquittal.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld state rules that restrict the use of expert testimony in criminal cases when such rules serve important state interests. In *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 410 (1988), the Court held that a state may exclude evidence under a rule of evidence that is “reasonably designed to further an important interest in the administration of justice.” The Arizona rule serves such an interest by preserving the integrity of the insanity defense and preventing the dilution of the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt. If a defendant could introduce expert testimony on mental state to undermine intent without meeting the burden of proving insanity, the state’s interest in maintaining a clear distinction between mental incapacity and criminal culpability would be undermined.

Moreover, the defendant in this case was not denied the opportunity to present evidence of his mental state. He introduced expert testimony on his schizophrenia and delusional beliefs, which the jury considered in evaluating his insanity defense. The trial court did not bar such evidence; it merely ruled that it could not be used to negate criminal intent unless the defendant first established insanity. This is not a denial of due process. *See* *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 654 (1984) (a defendant is not denied due process when the state “does not provide a particular procedural safeguard” unless the absence of that safeguard “renders the trial fundamentally unfair”).

The Supreme Court has never held that a defendant has a constitutional right to use expert testimony on mental state to rebut criminal intent outside the framework of an insanity defense. To the contrary, in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), the court upheld a rule that limited the use of psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense, noting that “the insanity defense is a creature of statute and the state may define its scope.” The Court has similarly recognized that states may “define the elements of crimes and the defenses to them,” including the conditions under which mental illness may be considered. *See* *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 697 (1975) (“The State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.”).

Clark’s argument that he was denied due process because he could not use expert testimony to show he lacked intent is foreclosed by *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), and *People v. Mendoza*, 13 Cal. 4th 1007 (1996), where courts upheld similar state rules. The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision affirming the trial court’s ruling was consistent with these precedents and did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

In sum, the Due Process Clause does not require Arizona to allow a defendant to use expert mental health testimony to rebut criminal intent unless he first satisfies the burden of proving insanity. The state’s rule is reasonable, serves important interests in the administration of justice, and does not render the trial fundamentally unfair. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
