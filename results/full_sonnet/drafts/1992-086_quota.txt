# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL CONSTITUTES IMPERMISSIBLE VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION IN A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM

This Court has long recognized that "public property or facilities dedicated to public expressive activities" may fall into different categories, each triggering distinct First Amendment scrutiny. *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44 (1983). Even where a State designates a limited public forum—open only to certain speakers or subjects—the State "must respect the lawful boundaries it has itself set" and may not discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint within the boundaries it has drawn. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). While the State retains discretion to preserve the forum "for the discussion of certain subjects," *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46, once it permits discussion of a subject, it cannot then exclude speakers because it disagrees with the perspective from which they approach that subject.

The Second Circuit correctly identified the Center Moriches facilities as a limited public forum open to civic, social, and recreational uses, including discussions of family values and child-rearing—precisely the subject matter of Lamb's Chapel's film series. Pet. App. Once a forum is opened to a particular subject, exclusion of a speaker addressing that same subject from a religious perspective is not a permissible subject-matter limitation but forbidden viewpoint discrimination. This Court's decision in *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), later confirmed this principle, holding that denial of funding to a student publication solely because it expressed views "from a religious standpoint" constituted viewpoint discrimination violating the Speech Clause. *Id.* at 831. The same logic applies here: the District permitted secular discussions of family and childrearing but excluded Lamb's Chapel's discussion of the identical subject solely because its viewpoint was religious.

The District's Rule No. 7—barring use "for religious purposes"—singles out religious viewpoints for exclusion while permitting other perspectives on family values, humanism, and psychology, as evidenced by the District's approval of Jerry Huck's lecture series on "Psychology and the Unknown," which addressed parapsychology and metaphysics. Pet. App. This selective exclusion mirrors the discrimination condemned in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), where this Court held that a university could not deny student religious groups access to facilities generally available to other student organizations. This Court explained that "the University's exclusionary policy violates the fundamental principle that a state regulation of speech should be content-neutral." *Id.* at 277. Although the Second Circuit attempted to distinguish *Widmar* on the ground that it involved university students rather than outside community groups, nothing in *Widmar*'s reasoning turns on the forum's higher-education context; rather, its core holding—that access cannot be conditioned on suppression of religious viewpoint—applies with equal force to community use of public school facilities after hours.

## II. THE DISTRICT'S JUSTIFICATION—AVOIDING AN ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE VIOLATION—CANNOT SUSTAIN THE EXCLUSION

The District has suggested that permitting Lamb's Chapel's film series would violate the Establishment Clause. This argument fails under this Court's settled Establishment Clause jurisprudence. Applying the tripartite test of *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602 (1971), the District's facilities are neutrally available to a broad range of community organizations for a broad range of purposes; permitting Lamb's Chapel equal access would have a plainly secular legislative purpose—facilitating community use of otherwise vacant facilities—and would not have the primary effect of advancing religion, since the District itself, not Lamb's Chapel, would remain the offeror of access, extended on a religion-neutral basis to all applicants. *See id.* at 612-613.

This Court's decision in *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), forecloses the District's Establishment Clause defense. Although *Mergens* arose under the Equal Access Act rather than the First Amendment directly, this Court's Establishment Clause analysis is instructive: allowing student religious groups equal access to a school-created limited forum does not offend the Establishment Clause because "secondary school students are mature enough and are likely to understand that a school does not endorse or support student speech that it merely permits on a nondiscriminatory basis." *Id.* at 250. The same reasoning applies to adult community members using school facilities after hours: no reasonable observer would conclude that the District endorses Lamb's Chapel's religious views merely because it permits the Chapel equal access to facilities available to secular civic groups.

Indeed, refusing access because the speech is religious—while permitting comparable secular speech—raises its own Establishment Clause difficulties, insofar as it demonstrates hostility toward religion rather than neutrality. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that the Establishment Clause does not license discrimination against religious viewpoints; neutrality, not exclusion, is the constitutional touchstone. *See Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947) (First Amendment requires government to be "neutral" toward religion, "neither favoring nor disfavoring" it).

## III. THE SECOND CIRCUIT'S RELIANCE ON *DEEPER LIFE* CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH THIS COURT'S FORUM DOCTRINE

The court below relied on its prior *Deeper Life* precedents to conclude that outside religious organizations occupy a different constitutional posture than student groups. That distinction has no grounding in this Court's forum-analysis cases. The touchstone under *Perry* and *Cornelius* is the nature of the forum the government has created, not the identity of the excluded speaker. Once the District opened its facilities to outside community organizations addressing family and social issues, it created a limited public forum in which viewpoint neutrality is constitutionally compelled regardless of whether the speaker is a student group or a community organization. *See Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
