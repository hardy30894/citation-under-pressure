**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA—though no longer serving the purpose of retirement savings for the beneficiary—remains exempt from the bankruptcy estate under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) solely because it once served as a retirement fund for the decedent. The Court of Appeals misread the plain language of the Bankruptcy Code, ignored controlling precedent, and failed to respect the foundational principle that exemptions must be interpreted in light of their purpose: to protect funds that are *currently* dedicated to the debtor’s future retirement. The correct interpretation, grounded in statutory text, legislative intent, and binding circuit authority, is that an inherited IRA does not qualify as “retirement funds” under the Code because it is not held for the beneficiary’s retirement. The judgment below should be affirmed.

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s Exemption for “Retirement Funds” Requires That the Funds Be Held for the Debtor’s Own Retirement**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from the bankruptcy estate “any retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key phrase is “retirement funds,” a term not defined in the Code. But the legislative history and judicial interpretation make clear that the exemption applies only to funds that are *currently* intended for the debtor’s own retirement. As this Court has held, “Congress intended to protect funds that are set aside for the debtor’s future retirement, not funds that have been converted into general assets.” *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312, 318 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010). The Seventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—allowing exemption based on the *former* status of the funds—undermines this foundational principle.

The Court of Appeals’ analogy to a trustee holding retirement assets for another is inapt. In *In re Nessa*, the Eighth Circuit emphasized that a trustee’s legal ownership does not negate the fund’s exempt status because the assets remain *intended* for retirement. *Id.* at 317. But in this case, the inherited IRA is not being held for the beneficiary’s retirement; it is being liquidated over a fixed period, often within five years of the decedent’s death. *26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A)*. The beneficiary cannot delay distributions until retirement, nor can they roll the funds into a new retirement account. *Id.* §408(d)(3)(C)*. The very structure of the inherited IRA—mandated distributions, no new contributions, and a finite time horizon—demonstrates that it is not a vehicle for retirement savings but a mechanism for *accelerated taxation*. To treat such funds as “retirement funds” under the Bankruptcy Code is to ignore their economic reality.

**II. The Seventh Circuit’s “Formerly Retirement Funds” Rule Contradicts the Text and Purpose of the Exemption**

The Court of Appeals held that because the funds were once retirement funds, they remain exempt. But this reading renders the term “retirement funds” meaningless. If the exemption depended only on past status, then any asset that once served as a retirement fund—such as a decedent’s IRA that was withdrawn and spent—would remain exempt. That is absurd. As this Court has repeatedly warned, “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525 (1987). The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt assets simply because they were once retirement savings. It exempts funds that *continue* to serve that purpose.

The Seventh Circuit’s reasoning would allow a debtor to inherit a decedent’s IRA, liquidate it immediately, and still claim the proceeds as exempt—because the funds were once retirement funds. That result is inconsistent with the Code’s goal of protecting *current* retirement savings, not enabling debtors to convert non-retirement assets into exempt property through inheritance. The Fifth Circuit correctly rejected this approach in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486, 491 (5th Cir. 2012), holding that “the exemption applies only if the funds are retirement funds *in the hands of the debtor*.” The Seventh Circuit’s contrary view creates a circuit split and undermines the uniformity Congress sought in the Bankruptcy Code.

**III. The Inherited IRA Is Not a Retirement Vehicle for the Beneficiary**

The Seventh Circuit acknowledged that an inherited IRA “must begin distributing its assets within a year of the original owner’s death” and “payout must be completed in as little as five years.” *Clark*, 705 F.3d at 3. This is not a retirement account—it is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle. The beneficiary cannot use the funds to support retirement; they must spend them now. The IRS itself treats inherited IRAs differently from retirement accounts: distributions are taxed as ordinary income, and the beneficiary cannot contribute to the account or roll it over. *26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C)*. The very purpose of the inherited IRA regime is to prevent indefinite tax deferral and ensure that the government collects income tax on the funds eventually. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 100-1000, at 14 (1988)* (explaining that inherited IRAs are “not intended to be a vehicle for long-term retirement savings”).

To exempt such funds from creditors’ claims would allow debtors to use the bankruptcy system to shield assets that are not retirement savings at all. The Seventh Circuit’s rule would encourage strategic planning: parents could increase IRA contributions knowing that the funds would pass to children free of creditors’ claims, even if the children have no intention of retiring. That is not the law. As this Court has held, “the interpretation of a statute must be consistent with its purpose and structure.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). The purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is to protect the debtor’s future retirement, not to reward inheritance.
