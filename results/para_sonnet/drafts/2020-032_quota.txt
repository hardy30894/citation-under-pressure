# ARGUMENT

## A Claim for Nominal Damages Alone Does Not Preserve a Live Case or Controversy Once the Challenged Policy Has Been Repealed

Article III confines federal courts to deciding "Cases" and "Controversies," and this limitation "subsists through all stages of federal judicial proceedings." *Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 477 (1990). A case becomes moot when "the issues presented are no longer 'live'" or the parties "lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome." *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Once GGC repealed the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged Student Code of Conduct provision, Petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief became moot—a conclusion Petitioners did not even contest below. The only question is whether a bare request for nominal damages, untethered to any actual injury, can artificially resurrect a controversy that has otherwise evaporated. It cannot.

### 1. Nominal Damages Divorced from Compensable Injury Do Not Satisfy Redressability

To satisfy Article III, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision will "redress" a concrete injury. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992). Redress must flow to an actual, particularized harm—not to the "abstract value of a constitutional right." *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986) (holding that damages in § 1983 actions may not be based on "the abstract 'value' or 'importance' of constitutional rights"). This Court has been emphatic that constitutional torts, like their common-law counterparts, "may support the award of nominal damages when a plaintiff proves a violation but cannot prove actual injury sufficient to entitle him to compensatory damages." *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978). Nominal damages under *Carey* exist precisely as a substitute for—not a supplement to—unproven compensatory injury. They are not an independent, freestanding injury capable of sustaining a live controversy once the predicate constitutional violation has ceased and no compensable harm was ever pled.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision below, following *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), correctly applied this principle. As the panel explained, Petitioners' amended complaint never alleged the kind of "actual injury" — monetary loss, emotional distress, reputational harm — that this Court in *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230–31 (11th Cir. 2000), recognized as the necessary predicate for compensatory relief. Petitioners' repeated invocation of "damages" and "irreparable harm," without specifying any injury, was nothing more than a placeholder for the abstract value of the right itself — precisely what *Stachura* forbids using as a damages measure. Where, as here, the plaintiff's only remaining request is for a token award untethered to any pleaded injury, and the policy that occasioned the alleged violation no longer exists, the case no longer presents the kind of "adversary" relationship necessary to sustain jurisdiction. See *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 107 (1998) (redressability requires that the relief sought will remedy the alleged injury, not merely vindicate the constitutional principle at stake).

### 2. Voluntary Cessation Does Not Revive a Controversy Where, As Here, There Is No Reasonable Expectation of Recurrence

Petitioners cannot escape mootness by invoking the voluntary-cessation doctrine. That doctrine exists to prevent defendants from manipulating the judicial process by "temporarily halt[ing]" unlawful conduct only to resume it after dismissal. *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). But even under voluntary cessation, the case remains moot if the defendant demonstrates that "there is no reasonable expectation" that the alleged violation will recur. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953). Here, GGC's repeal was not a litigation tactic — it was a permanent, formal amendment to institution-wide policy that "superseded" the challenged provisions and has remained in force since February 2017. The district court found, and the Eleventh Circuit did not disturb the finding, that GGC "unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies" with "no reasonable basis to expect" their return. That finding satisfies the heavy burden of demonstrating that subsequent events have made it "absolutely clear" the challenged conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur. *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013).

### 3. Permitting Nominal Damages to Save Otherwise Moot Claims Would Eviscerate Article III's Case-or-Controversy Requirement

Adopting Petitioners' rule — that a bare prayer for nominal damages keeps any constitutional claim perpetually alive — would allow plaintiffs to convert every declaratory judgment action into a permanent damages vehicle simply by appending a one-dollar request to the complaint, regardless of whether the underlying policy has been repealed and regardless of whether any actual injury exists. Such a rule would collapse the distinction this Court has carefully maintained between suits that vindicate constitutional principles in the abstract and suits that redress concrete, particularized injury. See *Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins*, 578 U.S. 330, 340 (2016) ("Article III standing requires a concrete injury even in the context of a statutory violation."). It would also undermine the "prudential" and constitutional value of mootness doctrine in allowing government defendants to reform unconstitutional policies without perpetual exposure to litigation over claims that no longer serve any remedial purpose. See *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312, 316 (1974) (per curiam).

## Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
