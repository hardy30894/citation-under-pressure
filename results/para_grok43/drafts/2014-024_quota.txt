I. Section 875(c) Requires Subjective Intent to Threaten.

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) demands proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), holds that the First Amendment permits prohibition of true threats only when the speaker “means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an unlawful act.” This language requires the government to establish the defendant’s purpose to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person would perceive a threat.

Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), reinforces the distinction, protecting “political hyperbole” absent evidence the speaker intended to threaten. Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), similarly confines unprotected incitement to speech “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action.” R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 388 (1992), confirms that content-based restrictions on threats must target the speaker’s intent rather than listener reaction alone.

Criminal statutes are presumed to incorporate mens rea. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952), requires “some mental element” for offenses “constituting an injury to the public.” Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994), and United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994), apply this presumption to avoid criminalizing innocent conduct. An objective standard under § 875(c) would criminalize speech lacking any culpable mental state, contrary to these holdings.

Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition, 535 U.S. 234, 253 (2002), and United States v. Alvarez, 567 U.S. 709, 717-18 (2012), further caution against expanding unprotected categories without precise mens rea limits. The Third Circuit’s objective test in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), cannot survive Black’s requirement of subjective intent. Circuits adopting that requirement correctly align § 875(c) with constitutional precedent. See United States v. Bagdasarian, 652 F.3d 1113, 1122 (9th Cir. 2011).

The district court’s instruction therefore permitted conviction without the requisite proof of subjective intent.
