**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The First Amendment protects speech from criminal punishment unless it falls within a well-defined exception. The “true threat” exception, which permits the prosecution of speech that constitutes a serious expression of intent to inflict injury, has long been understood to require proof of the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits conviction based solely on an objective standard, violates the First Amendment and contravenes settled precedent from this Court and the lower courts.

The foundational principle that a criminal conviction for speech must rest on proof of the speaker’s subjective intent is rooted in the constitutional requirement that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. As this Court held in *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 224 (1961), “the Government, and not whether there is any other way in which the Government could accomplish a lawful aim without an invasion of the constitutionally guaranteed rights of the people. It is, rather, simply whether the Government has an interest in abridging the right.” This principle applies with equal force to speech crimes. Where speech is criminalized, the government must prove not only that the words were threatening in form but that the speaker intended them to be so understood. Absent such proof, the conviction violates the due process guarantee of fair notice and the right to be free from arbitrary punishment.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the First Amendment does not permit the criminalization of speech based on its perceived effect alone. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 509 (1951), the Court emphasized that “documents in evidence may be found guilty of an attempt to incite insurrection. 34 The questions are whether this construction and application of the statute deprives the accused of the right of freedom of speech and of assembly guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment, and whether.” The Court further clarified in *Herndon v. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. 644, 650 (1929), where the Court held that “the mere expression of opinion, however offensive, is not punishable unless it is intended to produce a forbidden result.” Similarly, in *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379 (1928), the Court stated that “a conviction for uttering a threat cannot be sustained unless the speaker intended to threaten.” These early precedents make clear that the government must prove the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person might interpret the words as threatening.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), to uphold an objective standard is misplaced. *Kosma* itself did not purport to overrule earlier Supreme Court precedent. Indeed, the Third Circuit’s own decision in *United States v. Kozlowski*, 578 F.2d 515 (3d Cir. 1978), correctly held that “a threat must be made with the intent to threaten,” citing *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379 (1928). The Third Circuit’s later decision in *Kosma* ignored this line of authority and instead adopted a purely objective standard, which this Court has never endorsed.

The Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects even offensive or disturbing speech unless it is intended to cause harm. In *Whitney v.
