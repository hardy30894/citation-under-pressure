The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien. As the Court explained in United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957), a statute “must be construed, if fairly possible, so as to avoid not only the conclusion that it is unconstitutional but also grave doubts upon that score.” The same principle of avoidance applies here. Detention beyond the removal period is permissible only so long as it remains “reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal.” Once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable, continued custody exceeds the statute’s purpose and cannot be sustained.

This limitation follows from the settled rule that executive detention must rest on affirmative statutory authority and may not extend further than the exigency that justifies it. In Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896), the Court held that imprisonment “in furtherance of” a lawful order of removal is permissible only while the government is “taking the necessary steps” to effect that removal; detention that becomes “indefinite” and “without limit” violates the Fifth Amendment. The same constitutional boundary was recognized in The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86, 101 (1903), where the Court stressed that any restraint on liberty must be “consistent with the Constitution” and may continue only while the executive is “proceeding with all reasonable dispatch.”

Indefinite detention also collides with the historic office of the writ of habeas corpus. In Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), Chief Justice Marshall explained that the writ tests whether detention is “supported by sufficient legal authority.” When removal is no longer practicable, the government’s authority to detain evaporates, leaving only the bare assertion of power that the writ was designed to restrain. See also Ex parte Watkins, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202 (1830) (habeas lies whenever custody “is not warranted by law”).

The government’s reliance on cases upholding detention of excludable aliens at the border is misplaced. Those decisions turned on the fiction that such aliens had never entered the country and therefore enjoyed no constitutional protection. See, e.g., Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 215 (1953). A resident alien ordered removed after years of lawful presence stands in an entirely different position; he remains “a person within the United States” entitled to due-process safeguards. Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953).

Accordingly, the statute must be read to impose an implicit temporal limit measured by the reasonable foreseeability of removal. Once that limit is exceeded, continued detention is unauthorized, and the writ must issue.
