Milford violated the Good News Club’s First Amendment rights by excluding it from an otherwise open after-hours community forum. The district had opened its facilities to residents for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” Once the State creates such a forum, it may not exclude speech because officials disapprove of the speaker’s viewpoint. Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951). Milford allowed other youth and community groups to meet, but denied the Club because its instruction was Christian. That is viewpoint discrimination, which the First Amendment forbids. West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 400 (1923).

The Club’s meetings were also protected religious speech. The Constitution does not permit government to treat religious expression as outside the protection of free speech. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940), held that the First Amendment safeguards the “free exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship.” Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943), protected the dissemination of religious literature and solicitation in support of religious expression. Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944), likewise rejected burdens on the distribution of religious views. And Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73, held that a municipality could not deny a permit for religious meetings because of hostility to the religious character of the gathering. Milford’s denial rests on the same forbidden premise: that speech including prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitation to faith may be excluded because it is religious.

The Establishment Clause does not justify that exclusion. The Clause bars government sponsorship of religion; it does not authorize the State to suppress private religious speech in a forum opened for community use. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), held that government must remain neutral between religion and nonreligion. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), reaffirmed that the Constitution does not require “callous indifference” to religion. Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 431-32 (1962), and School Dist. of Abington Twp. v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), prohibited state-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools, but they did not hold that a school may exclude private religious speech from an otherwise open forum. Milford did not face a prayer led by school officials or a religious exercise imposed on students. It faced a private club seeking equal access under a neutral-use policy. Neutral access is not establishment. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314.

Milford’s effort to characterize the Club’s meetings as “religious worship” does not change the analysis. The Court has repeatedly recognized that religious expression is protected expression even when it is devotional or evangelistic. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 304; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 110-11; Follett, 321 U.S. at 577-78. The school did not exclude all speech on a subject it had left outside the forum; it excluded one perspective on morals and character because that perspective was religious. That is the very discrimination the First Amendment forbids. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73.

Nor does any concern that children might perceive endorsement support Milford’s position. The proper constitutional response to any such concern is equal treatment and clear disclaimer, not suppression of private speech. The State may not avoid an asserted Establishment Clause problem by engaging in viewpoint discrimination. Everson, 330 U.S. at 15-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 313-15. The Constitution requires neutrality, not hostility. Because Milford excluded the Good News Club based on its religious viewpoint, and because the Establishment Clause does not require that discrimination, the judgment should be reversed.
