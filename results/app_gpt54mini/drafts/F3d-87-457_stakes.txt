### ARGUMENT

#### I. Chapter 93-121’s disclosure scheme is facially overbroad because it burdens protected advocacy far beyond what the State may constitutionally require.

The First Amendment protects “the right of the people…to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.” U.S. Const. amend. I. Lobbying lies close to that core guarantee. See NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. 415, 429–30 (1963) (treating collective efforts to influence public action as protected association and petitioning); Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 422–23 (1988) (laws burdening the ability to engage speakers to disseminate political views implicate “core political speech”); Riley v. National Federation of the Blind of North Carolina, Inc., 487 U.S. 781, 797–98 (1988) (compelled disclosure of fundraising-related information burdens First Amendment rights and is subject to exacting scrutiny).

Under well-established doctrine, a statute is facially invalid when it “prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech” judged in relation to its plainly legitimate sweep. United States v. Stevens, 559 U.S. 460, 473 (2010); Virginia v. Hicks, 539 U.S. 113, 118–19 (2003). Florida’s law fails that test because it compels disclosure not merely of payments for direct lobbying, but of “all lobbying expenditures, whether made by the lobbyist or by the principal, and the source of funds for all such expenditures,” including broad categories such as “food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging.” Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). That regime sweeps well beyond any narrowly tailored reporting necessary to identify who is attempting to influence government.

Compelled disclosure is constitutional only if the State demonstrates a sufficiently important interest and a substantial relation between the disclosure requirement and that interest. Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64, 66–68 (1976) (per curiam). But Buckley also cautioned that disclosure burdens must be tied to a legitimate informational interest and not extend farther than needed. Id. at 68–74. Here, Florida has not limited its demand to direct payments for lobbying contacts, but requires itemized reporting of ancillary expenditures that may reflect ordinary business activity, advocacy preparation, or generalized public outreach. That kind of compelled accounting burdens speech and association beyond the narrow disclosure upheld in Buckley. See id. at 66–68; see also Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, 594 U.S. 595, 607–10 (2021) (exacting scrutiny requires narrow tailoring and an “exacting” fit between means and ends).

The statute’s breadth is especially problematic because it reaches both lobbyists and principals, effectively compelling disclosure of internal financial arrangements and advocacy expenses even where the connection to any specific lobbying contact is attenuated. Such compelled reporting chills participation in political advocacy. See Bates v. City of Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516, 523–24 (1960) (recognizing that compelled disclosure can deter membership and participation); Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60, 64–65 (1960) (invalidating compelled identification requirements that burden anonymous expression). Because the statute’s burdens fall on a substantial amount of protected petitioning activity, its disclosure provisions are facially invalid. See Stevens, 559 U.S. at 473; Hicks, 539 U.S. at 118–19.

#### II. The contingency-fee ban is unconstitutional because it prohibits a lawful compensation arrangement for petitioning activity and is not supported by any modern First Amendment rationale.

Section 11.047 flatly bars lobbyists from being compensated on a contingency basis for successful influence on legislative or executive outcomes. That prohibition directly restricts the market for petitioning activity and therefore burdens speech and association. See Meyer, 486 U.S. at 422–23; NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. at 428–30.

Although Florida may invoke old common-law suspicions of contingent compensation, the Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that restrictions on expression or advocacy must be justified by more than generalized concerns. See United States v. National Treasury Employees Union, 513 U.S. 454, 468 (1995) (government must show the challenged restriction advances its interests in a direct and material way); Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, 558 U.S. 310, 340–41 (2010) (independent political advocacy may not be suppressed absent adequate constitutional justification). A categorical ban is especially suspect where the government identifies no corruption rationale unique to this compensation structure and no evidence that ordinary ethics rules or disclosure requirements would be inadequate. See Buckley, 424 U.S. at 25–28, 66–68; McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission, 572 U.S. 185, 204–06 (2014) (anti-corruption interests must be specific and cannot justify broad prophylactic limits untethered to actual risk).

The contingency-fee ban also targets a method of retaining advocates to petition government. The First Amendment does not permit Florida to foreclose a compensation arrangement simply because the State disfavors the advocate’s incentive structure. See Button, 371 U.S. at 429–30; Meyer, 486 U.S. at 424–25. Because the statute suppresses protected petitioning by forbidding a common and lawful means of securing representation, it cannot stand under exacting scrutiny. See Riley, 487 U.S. at 798; Americans for Prosperity Foundation, 594 U.S. at 607–10.

#### III. At minimum, the Court should invalidate the challenged provisions facially because the statute’s overbreadth and chilling effect are substantial.

A litigant need not await prosecution where a statute’s existence chills protected expression and imposes a credible choice between compliance and sanction. See Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 152–53 (1967). And facial invalidation is appropriate when a law’s unconstitutional applications are substantial in relation to its legitimate sweep. Stevens, 559 U.S. at 473; Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). Florida’s statute regulates far more than direct lobbying contacts, compels expansive financial disclosure, and prohibits a compensation model that facilitates petitioning. That combination creates precisely the kind of overbroad burden on First Amendment activity that facial review exists to prevent. See Button, 371 U.S. at 433; Riley, 487 U.S. at 798; Stevens, 559 U.S. at 473.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
