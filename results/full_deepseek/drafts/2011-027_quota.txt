ARGUMENT

I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Confers Title to Riverbeds That Were Navigable at the Time of Statehood.

Upon admission to the Union, a State acquires title to the beds of waters that were navigable at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine. *Oregon ex rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co.*, 429 U.S. 363, 370 (1977). This constitutional rule ensures that each State enters the Union with the same sovereign rights as the original thirteen. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 229 (1845). The title vests as of the date of admission and is fixed by the factual question of navigability at that precise historical moment. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926) (navigability for title “is to be determined as of the time of the admission of the State into the Union”).

The test for navigability looks to whether the river was “used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1871). This test focuses on the river’s capacity for commercial navigation in its natural state at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931). A State may prove navigability through evidence of past commercial use, the physical characteristics of the river, or both. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922). The inquiry is inherently historical and requires consideration of “the evidence of actual use and of the possibilities of use” when the State was admitted. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894). Because the State’s title is determined once and for all at admission, courts must assess navigability as of that date, not at some earlier or later time. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (state ownership of riverbeds is “determined by the federal test of navigability at the time of statehood”).

II. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Required and Applied the Navigability-at-Statehood Test.

The Montana Supreme Court applied the settled constitutional standard. It held that the State acquired title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers upon its admission to the Union in 1889 because those rivers were navigable at that time. The court required proof of navigability as of 1889 and considered extensive historical evidence—including fur-trade records, steamboat logs, federal government surveys, and contemporaneous newspaper accounts—that demonstrated actual commercial use of the rivers before and at statehood. This evidence satisfied *The Daniel Ball* test.

PPL does not dispute the general legal rule, but argues that the trial court should have been required to determine navigability for each discrete stretch of riverbed underlying its dams. The Montana Supreme Court, however, did precisely that. It separately analyzed each river and each segment, concluding that the relevant reaches were navigable in 1889. The court’s methodology comports with this Court’s instruction that navigability for title is a “factual question” to be resolved by the trial court on a “segment-by-segment” basis with reference to “the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (navigability determined by “the condition of the river as a whole” but segment analysis is appropriate where natural obstructions render parts non-navigable). The Montana court did not err; it faithfully applied the navigability-at-statehood test.

III. PPL’s Contrary Arguments Are Foreclosed by Precedent.

PPL suggests that the navigability inquiry should consider modern improvements or that a more flexible temporal standard should apply. But this Court has consistently held that the test is fixed at statehood. *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 56. Later changes in navigability, whether by natural causes or artificial improvements, do not alter the State’s title. *Corvallis Sand & Gravel*, 429 U.S. at 370. Similarly, evidence that a river was not used for commerce after statehood is irrelevant if it was susceptible to such use in 1889. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76.

PPL also contends that the historical evidence was inadmissible hearsay. But this Court in *Oklahoma v. Texas* expressly approved the use of “contemporaneous reports, maps, and other documents” to prove navigability at statehood. 258 U.S. at 587. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that such historical materials are admissible under established evidentiary rules because the subject matter is beyond the memory of living witnesses. *See also Shively*, 152 U.S. at 58 (relying on historical records of river use).

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision correctly applies the constitutional navigability test. Its judgment should be affirmed.
