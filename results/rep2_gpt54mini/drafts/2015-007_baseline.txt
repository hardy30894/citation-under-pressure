Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, as applied here, does not violate the Sixth Amendment or the Eighth Amendment. Under *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Constitution forbids executing a mentally retarded offender, but *Atkins* expressly left to the States the task of implementing that categorical rule. Id. at 317. Florida did so by allowing defendants to litigate mental retardation as a bar to execution under section 921.137 and Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.203. The Florida Supreme Court correctly held that Hurst had already received a full evidentiary hearing on mental retardation, and that he was not entitled to relitigate that issue before a jury as a constitutional matter.

The Sixth Amendment argument fails because *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), requires a jury to find only those facts that increase the maximum punishment authorized by the jury’s verdict. Id. at 602. Florida’s scheme, as applied to Hurst, complied with that command because the jury convicted him of first-degree murder and necessarily found the elements of the capital offense. The jury then heard the aggravation and mitigation evidence relevant to sentencing. Unlike the scheme invalidated in *Ring*, Florida did not assign to a judge the exclusive role of finding the existence of a new capital-eligibility fact in the first instance. See *Hurst v. Florida*, 777 So. 2d 225 (Fla. 2000); *Bottoson v. Moore*, 833 So. 2d 693 (Fla. 2002). At minimum, Florida reasonably relied on *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), which upheld Florida’s capital sentencing structure, before *Ring* changed the constitutional landscape. Id. at 464.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment require jury unanimity for a death recommendation in Florida. This Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not invariably require unanimity in state criminal jury verdicts. See *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404 (1972). And in the capital context, the Court has repeatedly upheld guided-sentencing schemes that do not mirror the historic unanimity rules of the common law. See *McKoy v. North Carolina*, 494 U.S. 433, 444 (1990) (distinguishing the unanimity requirements at the penalty phase from guilt-phase unanimity); *Buchanan v. Angelone*, 522 U.S. 269, 275 (1998) (capital sentencer need not be instructed to find specific mitigating circumstances). Florida’s recommendation procedure does not itself impose death; it is advisory, and the judge independently enters the sentence after considering the record. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465.

The Eighth Amendment claim fails for the same reason. The Court has held that “death is different,” but it has not constitutionalized any particular jury-voting threshold for capital sentencing. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 605; *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991). Florida’s procedure permits individualized sentencing, consideration of mitigation, and judicial review of aggravation. That is exactly the kind of reliability the Court has required. See *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 357 (1977); *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). The fact that Florida does not require unanimity in the advisory recommendation does not make its system cruel and unusual. The Eighth Amendment forbids arbitrary imposition of death, not every sentencing structure that differs from other states. See *Pulley v. Harris*, 465 U.S. 37, 45 (1984).

As to mental retardation, *Atkins* left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” 536 U.S. at 317. Florida’s decision to require a judicial determination on the bar to execution before submission of mitigation to the jury falls squarely within that discretion. The Constitution does not require the jury to decide every threshold issue bearing on eligibility for punishment, especially where the State provides a separate mechanism for adjudicating the exemption. See *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 358 (2004) (Ring announced a procedural rule). Hurst received the process Florida law afforded, and the state courts found he was not mentally retarded.

In short, neither *Ring* nor *Atkins* invalidates Florida’s capital sentencing system here. The judgment below should be affirmed.
