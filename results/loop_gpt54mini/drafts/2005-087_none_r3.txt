Arizona’s rule is consistent with due process. The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a fair opportunity to defend, but it does not require a State to recognize a freestanding right to use psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea apart from insanity. The State must prove the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but it may define the legal effect of mental-health evidence and confine that evidence to an insanity defense. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

The pre-1970 decisions on insanity and mental capacity confirm that conclusion. Davis held that, in federal prosecutions, the government must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt once the issue is raised, 160 U.S. at 484-85, but that was a federal rule of criminal procedure and did not constitutionalize a diminished-capacity defense. Leland squarely rejected the claim that due process forbids requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, emphasizing the State’s authority to allocate the burden on criminal responsibility. 343 U.S. at 798-99. If due process permits a State to place the entire burden of insanity on the accused, it plainly permits Arizona’s more limited rule: psychiatric evidence may be used to support insanity, but not as an independent means of disproving intent. See Salinger v. United States, 272 U.S. 542, 548-49 (1926).

Petitioner’s reliance on the right to present a defense is misplaced. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), invalidated a rule that categorically barred the defendant from calling an accomplice witness, because it denied him “the testimony of witnesses to establish a defense.” Id. at 19. Arizona did nothing comparable. Clark was not barred from calling witnesses, cross-examining the State’s witnesses, or contesting intent through ordinary evidence. The State simply limited the legal use of one category of expert testimony. That is a substantive choice about criminal responsibility, not a denial of compulsory process or due process.

Nor do the cases involving mental illness and competency support petitioner. Blackburn v. Alabama, 361 U.S. 199 (1960), addressed the voluntariness of a confession extracted under coercive circumstances. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), concerned competence to stand trial. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), required a competency hearing when the record created a bona fide doubt about competence. None of those cases recognized a constitutional right to introduce psychiatric evidence to negate intent independent of insanity. They protect the fairness of the process and the defendant’s ability to participate in it; they do not require States to adopt diminished-capacity doctrines.

General due process doctrine points the same way. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-06 (1934), held that due process is violated only by procedures that offend “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” No such principle requires States to permit psychiatric proof to serve as a separate route to acquittal on mens rea. Indeed, the Court has long recognized the States’ authority to organize the law of criminal responsibility. Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910), recognized the distinct treatment of mental evidence in criminal cases, and Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), reflects the Court’s continued deference to the States’ administration of justice.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would constitutionalize a defense this Court has never required and would drastically limit state power to define insanity. Before 1970, this Court had approved substantial state control over the burden and use of mental-responsibility evidence, while insisting only that the trial remain fundamentally fair. Arizona satisfied that standard here. The judgment should be affirmed.
