**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer, firmly rooted in this Court’s precedents, is yes—in rare and exceptional cases supported by specific evidence.

**I. This Court Has Consistently Recognized That the Lodestar May Be Enhanced for Superior Performance and Exceptional Results**

In *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), this Court held that although the lodestar is presumptively reasonable, an upward adjustment may be justified “in the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional.” *Id.* at 899. The Court explicitly rejected the argument that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” *Id.* at 901. Instead, it quoted *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), for the proposition that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901 (quoting *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 435).

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) (*Delaware Valley I*). There, the Court stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Although the Court cautioned that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” it did not foreclose such adjustments. *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). Rather, it left open the possibility of enhancement in an extraordinary case. *Id.*

The language of *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* is clear: enhancements for quality of performance and results obtained are not categorically prohibited. They are permitted in rare cases where the applicant provides specific evidence. The district court’s award here falls squarely within that narrow category.

**II. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Supported by Specific Evidence and Detailed Findings**

The district court did not enhance the lodestar based on vague or generic observations. Instead, the court set forth detailed findings explaining why the lodestar figure did not fully reflect the quality of representation and the results achieved. The court relied on “its own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case,” including the court’s “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006).

This Court has recognized that a district judge’s firsthand observation of counsel’s performance is a proper basis for assessing the quality of representation. In *Blum*, the Court required “specific evidence” but did not limit the source of that evidence to extrinsic testimony or exhibits. 465 U.S. at 899. The district judge—who presided over the entire litigation—is uniquely positioned to evaluate counsel’s skill, commitment, and professionalism. His detailed factual findings, grounded in his own observation and experience, satisfy the “specific evidence” requirement.

Petitioners’ argument that the district court improperly “testified” is unfounded. A judge’s reasoned explanation of his observations, set forth in a published order, does not violate due process. To the contrary, the judge provided transparency and accountability, enabling meaningful appellate review. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion.

**III. The Lower Courts Are Uniform in Allowing Such Enhancements**

Every circuit to have considered the issue agrees that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* permit enhancements for exceptional performance and results in rare cases. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). There is no circuit split. The uniform understanding of the courts of appeals confirms that this Court’s precedents allow such enhancements.

**IV. The Facts of This Case Present the Rare Circumstances Warranting Enhancement**

This case is exceptional. Plaintiffs, foster children in a state-run system, obtained sweeping injunctive relief that fundamentally reformed Georgia’s foster care system. The district court found that counsel’s performance “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Kenny A.*, 454 F. Supp. 2d at 1286. The record demonstrates that counsel achieved extraordinary results through extraordinary effort, dedication, and skill. The enhancement was modest—a multiplier of 1.75—and was supported by specific, detailed findings. This is precisely the “rare” and “exceptional” case that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* contemplated.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
