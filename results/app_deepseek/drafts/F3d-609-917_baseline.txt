**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Properly Granted Summary Judgment Because Ray’s State Tort Claims Are Preempted by Federal Law**

The district court correctly held that Ray’s false imprisonment and negligence claims are preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 (ADA), 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). The ADA provides that “a State . . . may not enact or enforce a law, regulation, or other provision having the force and effect of law related to a price, route, or service of an air carrier.” *Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 374, 383 (1992). The Supreme Court has interpreted the phrase “related to” broadly, holding that state laws are preempted if they have “a connection with or reference to” airline rates, routes, or services. *Id.* at 384.

Ray’s claims directly implicate American’s “service” as an air carrier. The tarmac delay, the decision not to allow deplaning, and the provision of food and lavatories are all integral components of airline operations. In *Hodges v. Delta Airlines, Inc.*, 44 F.3d 334, 336 (5th Cir. 1995) (en banc), the court held that “services” under the ADA include “items such as ticketing, boarding, food, and beverage service, and baggage handling.” Similarly, the Ninth Circuit has held that “the boarding process, the provision of food and drink, and the handling of passengers’ luggage” are services subject to preemption. *Charas v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 160 F.3d 1259, 1261 (9th Cir. 1998) (en banc).

Ray’s false imprisonment claim challenges American’s decision to keep passengers on the plane during the delay. This decision is a quintessential operational service—the management of passengers during irregular operations. *See Branche v. Airtran Airways, Inc.*, 342 F.3d 1248, 1257 (11th Cir. 2003) (holding that claims challenging “the manner in which the airline boarded passengers” were preempted). Likewise, her negligence claim, which alleges inadequate food, water, and lavatory facilities, directly relates to the provision of in-flight services. *See Smith v. Comair, Inc.*, 134 F.3d 254, 259 (4th Cir. 1998) (holding that claims regarding “the handling of passengers and their luggage” are preempted).

Ray’s attempt to characterize her claims as “garden-variety torts” does not avoid preemption. The ADA preempts state law claims that “relate to” airline services, even if they are based on general tort principles. *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 386. As the Supreme Court explained, “the ADA’s preemption provision is not limited to state laws that directly regulate prices, routes, or services; it also preempts state laws that have a ‘significant impact’ on those matters.” *Id.* at 390. Ray’s claims would impose state-law duties on American’s decisions about when to deplane passengers and how to provide in-flight amenities—decisions that are central to airline operations and subject to federal regulation.

**II. Alternatively, the District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Ray’s Rule 56(f) Motion**

Even if Ray’s claims were not preempted, the district court properly denied her motion for a continuance under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(f). A party seeking a continuance must “show by affidavit or declaration that, for specified reasons, it cannot present facts essential to justify its opposition.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(d). The district court has broad discretion to deny such motions. *See Humphreys v. Roche Biomedical Labs., Inc.*, 990 F.2d 1078, 1081 (8th Cir. 1993).

Ray’s motion failed to identify any specific facts she would discover through additional discovery. She merely speculated that further discovery might reveal evidence of American’s “intentional or negligent” conduct. This is insufficient. *See United States v. $148,840.00 in U.S. Currency*, 521 F.3d 1268, 1276 (10th Cir. 2008) (holding that a Rule 56(f) motion must “state with specificity” the facts sought and why they are essential). Moreover, the key facts—the duration of the delay, the pilot’s statements, and the conditions on the plane—were already known to Ray through her own deposition testimony. The district court did not abuse its discretion.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of American Airlines.
