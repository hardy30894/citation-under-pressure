# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of ANILCA Confirms That NPS Retains Regulatory Authority Over Activities Occurring Within Conservation System Unit Boundaries.

Statutory interpretation begins, and often ends, with the words Congress chose. "It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 103(c) exempts land "conveyed to the State" from laws "applicable solely to public lands within" a conservation system unit—it says nothing about laws of general applicability that Congress intended to govern the unit as a whole, such as the hovercraft ban here. Sturgeon's reading would insert a limitation Congress did not write, contrary to the settled principle that courts must give effect to the text Congress actually enacted rather than the text a litigant wishes it had enacted. Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 485.

## II. The United States' Dominant Navigational Servitude Independently Authorizes NPS to Regulate Hovercraft Use on the Nation River Regardless of Title to the Riverbed.

Even assuming Alaska holds title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River, that title is qualified by the federal government's authority over navigable waters. The Commerce Clause vests in Congress a power over navigation that "is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations, other than are prescribed in the constitution." Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 196 (1824). That power attaches to any waterway that is, in the words of this Court, "navigable in fact," and such waters "are navigable in law" for purposes of federal regulation. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). The scope of federal authority over navigable waters is broad, extending to control of the full "flow, use, or development" of a waterway wherever it may affect interstate commerce. United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 426 (1940).

Crucially, this authority does not depend on federal ownership of the riverbed. As this Court held in United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967), "the interest of the United States in the flow of a navigable stream originates in the Commerce Clause," and that interest constitutes "a dominant one which can be asserted to preclude or condition" a State's exercise of authority over the same waters, notwithstanding the State's ownership of the submerged lands under the equal-footing doctrine. Rands, 389 U.S. at 123. The same principle was reaffirmed in United States v. Grand River Dam Authority, 363 U.S. 229, 231 (1960), where the Court explained that the federal servitude over navigable waters exists "whether the waters flow over public, private, or state-owned lands." Alaska's ownership of the bed of the Nation River—even if established—therefore does not divest the United States, acting through NPS, of authority to regulate hovercraft operation on the water's surface.

## III. Longstanding Precedent Confirms the Federal Government's Authority to Regulate Activity Occurring Within the Boundaries of a Federal Reservation for Purposes Consistent with the Reservation's Function.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the federal government's power to protect lands and resources it administers is not confined by the formal boundaries of title. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525–26 (1897), the Court upheld a federal statute reaching activity on privately owned land because that activity threatened to defeat the purposes of adjoining public land, reasoning that the government's proprietary interest carries with it authority "to protect [its] lands from trespass and injury." The same logic informed Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 536–37 (1911), where the Court held that the government, "considered as a proprietor," possesses "power to control the use of its public lands" and to prescribe rules necessary to their protection, in the same manner "as an ordinary individual" would regarding his own property. And in Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States, 243 U.S. 389, 404–05 (1917), the Court confirmed that private or state-derived rights within or adjoining federal reservations remain subject to the government's regulatory authority unless Congress has clearly relinquished it.

These decisions establish a consistent rule: authority to regulate for the protection of a federal reservation's purposes travels with the reservation's boundaries, not merely with the government's bare title to particular parcels within them. NPS's hovercraft ban—designed to protect wildlife, visitor safety, and the wilderness character of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve—is precisely the sort of protective regulation these cases contemplate. Nothing in ANILCA § 103(c) purports to override this settled framework; to the contrary, Congress legislated against the backdrop of this authority and gave no indication that it intended to dismantle it for non-federal inholdings and waters physically situated within conservation system units.

## CONCLUSION

The plain text of § 103(c), the federal navigational servitude, and this Court's longstanding recognition of the government's authority to protect federal reservations all confirm that NPS lawfully regulated Sturgeon's hovercraft use within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. The judgment below should be affirmed.
