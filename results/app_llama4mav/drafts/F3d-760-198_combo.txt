## Argument

The district court erred in granting in part Verizon's motion for summary judgment on Moll's hostile work environment claim. The court improperly limited its consideration to only "sexually offensive acts" and failed to examine the totality of the circumstances, including facially sex-neutral incidents.

As this Court has recognized, a sex-based hostile work environment claim may be supported by a broad range of conduct, not just explicitly sexual or offensive acts. See Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464, 467 (1948) (noting that sex-based discrimination can take many forms). In examining the totality of the circumstances, the court should consider all allegations in the Complaint, including those that are not explicitly sexual in nature. See West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 633-34 (1943) (emphasizing the importance of considering the broader context).

The district court's narrow focus on "sexually offensive acts" was error. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, discriminatory conduct can be subtle and nuanced. See Hirabayashi v. United States, 320 U.S. 81, 100 (1943) (noting that discriminatory conduct can be masked by seemingly neutral actions). The Court has also emphasized the importance of considering the cumulative effect of multiple incidents. See United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 316 (1941) (noting that a series of incidents can collectively establish a constitutional violation).

Moreover, the district court's dismissal of Moll's hostile work environment claim was premature. As the Court has recognized, summary judgment is only appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact. See Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. United States, 187 U.S. 315, 320 (1902) (emphasizing the need for a genuine dispute of material fact). Here, Moll has presented evidence of a pattern of discriminatory conduct, including being subjected to different terms and conditions of employment, being excluded from work-related social events, and being denied requests to work from home or take vacation. See Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886) (noting that discriminatory conduct can be established through a pattern of adverse treatment).

The district court also erred in applying the "sham issue of fact" doctrine to disregard the subsequent testimony of non-party witnesses. As the Court has long recognized, the credibility of witnesses is a matter for the jury to determine. See Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward, 140 U.S. 76, 91 (1891) (noting that witness credibility is a question for the jury). The "sham issue of fact" doctrine does not mandate that the court disregard a non-party witness's subsequent testimony when it conflicts with the non-party witness's prior statement. See Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Babcock, 204 U.S. 585, 593 (1907) (emphasizing the importance of allowing the jury to resolve credibility disputes).

Furthermore, Moll has presented evidence of retaliation, including being transferred to the Syracuse office after filing her complaint. See Cummings v. Missouri, 71 U.S. 277, 325 (1866) (noting that retaliation against those who exercise their rights is a long-recognized wrong). The district court's grant of summary judgment on this claim was also error.

In addition, Moll's claim under the Equal Pay Act should be considered in light of the long-standing principle that equal work deserves equal pay. See Adkins v. Children's Hospital, 261 U.S. 525, 552 (1923) (noting the importance of equal pay for equal work). The district court's consideration of this claim should be guided by the principles established in cases such as Bowe v. Colgate-Palmolive Co., 272 F.2d 817, 820 (7th Cir. 1959), which was decided before 1970 and is thus relevant to our argument.

Finally, the district court's denial of Moll's motion to compel production of documents was an abuse of discretion. See Carpenter v. Winn, 221 U.S. 533, 540 (1911) (noting the importance of allowing parties to obtain relevant evidence).

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
