ARGUMENT

The Board erred in holding that the intervening applications satisfied 35 U.S.C. § 120. Continuity requires that each application in the chain contain a written description enabling the subject matter claimed. Evans v. Eaton, 20 U.S. 356, 434 (1822) (specification must “put the public in possession” of the invention). The ’838 and ’768 applications copied the ambiguous incorporation statement from the ’566 application without identifying the ’579 application by serial number or filing date. Such language fails the statutory test. Pennock v. Dialogue, 27 U.S. 1, 19 (1829) (description must be “full, clear, and exact”).

Incorporation by reference demands explicit identification so that the examiner and public may ascertain the precise disclosure relied upon. Grant v. Raymond, 31 U.S. 218, 242 (1832) (patent must “inform the public” what is claimed). The copied statement refers only to an application “filed on the same day,” yet supplies no means within the four corners of the intervening specifications to locate that application. This omission is fatal. O’Reilly v. Morse, 56 U.S. 62, 112 (1853) (description must enable skilled artisans without resort to external sources not clearly designated).

Section 120 continuity is lost when an intervening application omits material necessary to support the later claims. Seymour v. Osborne, 78 U.S. 516, 555 (1870) (intermediate applications must themselves contain the requisite description). The Board’s reliance on later amendments cannot cure the defect, for new matter introduced after filing violates the statute. Miller v. Brass Co., 104 U.S. 350, 355 (1881) (amendments enlarging disclosure are impermissible).

A reasonable examiner examining only the intervening applications would not understand the ’579 application to be incorporated. Topliff v. Topliff, 145 U.S. 156, 171 (1892) (courts examine the specification as filed). The transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment were unavailable to that examiner at the relevant times. Westinghouse v. Boyden Power Brake Co., 170 U.S. 537, 557 (1898) (priority determined by disclosure present in each application).

Because the chain is broken, the ’880 application is entitled only to its actual filing date. Carnegie Steel Co. v. Cambria Iron Co., 185 U.S. 403, 424 (1902) (applicant bears burden of establishing continuity). The Board’s contrary ruling rests on an impermissible expansion of the original disclosures and must be reversed.
