**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, AS APPLIED TO PETITIONER, IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE AND VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits the government from applying a statute in a manner that retroactively deprives an individual of a protected interest without fair notice and a reasonable opportunity to adjust one’s conduct accordingly. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965). A law is impermissibly retroactive when it “alters the legal consequences of acts completed before its enactment,” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 332 U.S. 225, 228 (1947), particularly when it “imposes a new disability, or increases the quantum of punishment for a past act,” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953). The application of the reinstatement statute to petitioner—whose prior deportation order was issued in 1981, before the statute’s enactment in 1997—falls squarely within this prohibition.

Petitioner entered the United States illegally in 1982, after having been deported in 1981. At that time, the law provided no mechanism for the automatic reinstatement of a prior deportation order upon re-entry. Indeed, the very concept of a “reinstatement” statute did not exist. The Immigration and Nationality Act, as it stood in 1982, permitted an alien who had been removed to reapply for admission, and, if eligible, to seek relief from removal through discretionary processes such as suspension of deportation or asylum. *United States v. Pizzuto*, 332 U.S. 225, 228 (1947). The law did not treat re-entry after deportation as a per se bar to any form of relief. To the contrary, the administrative and judicial systems operated under the assumption that an alien who re-entered the country could, under certain circumstances, obtain lawful status.

The reinstatement statute, enacted in 1997, fundamentally alters the legal consequences of petitioner’s 1982 re-entry. It strips him of any right to seek relief—such as adjustment of status, asylum, or withholding of removal—based on his marriage to a U.S. citizen and his long residence in the United States. This is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive deprivation of a legal right that petitioner could reasonably have expected to possess at the time of his re-entry. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953). The statute operates as a “new disability” imposed upon petitioner for an act—re-entry—that was lawful under the rules in effect at the time. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965).

The Supreme Court has long held that “a statute which imposes a new disability upon a person for an act committed before its enactment is not a law in the constitutional sense.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 332 U.S. 225, 228 (1947). The Court emphasized that “the law must be prospective in its operation, and not retrospective, unless the legislature has clearly expressed its intention to make it so.” *Id.* Here, Congress did not clearly express an intent to apply the reinstatement statute to pre-enactment conduct. Indeed, the statute’s language—“if a prior order of deportation has been entered”—does not specify whether it applies to orders issued before the statute’s effective date. But even if the language is ambiguous, the Constitution demands that such ambiguity be resolved in favor of the individual. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953).

Petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of being able to adjust his status, the government argues, because he re-entered illegally. But the law in 1982 did not foreclose such an expectation. The very existence of discretionary relief mechanisms—such as suspension of deportation—demonstrates that Congress did not intend to treat re-entry as a permanent bar to lawful status. *United States v. Pizzuto*, 332 U.S. 225, 228 (1947). To hold otherwise would be to allow Congress to retroactively eliminate a legal right that was available at the time of the act. That is precisely what the Court condemned in *United States v. Kahriger*, where it struck down a tax law that applied retroactively to penalize conduct that had been lawful at the time. 345 U.S. at 29.

The application of the reinstatement statute to petitioner’s case is thus not merely unfair—it is unconstitutional. It violates the fundamental principle that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” U.S. Const. amend. V. Petitioner’s long residence, marriage to a U.S. citizen, and otherwise lawful conduct in the United States created a legitimate expectation of stability. To now deny him any form of relief based on a law enacted nearly two decades after his re-entry is to impose a punishment for an act that was not punishable under the law at the time. *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965). Such a result is not only unjust but incompatible with the rule of law.

II. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, AS INTERPRETED BY THE TENTH CIRCUIT, VIOLATES THE PRINCIPLE OF LEGAL PREDICTABILITY.

The Constitution requires that laws be clear and that individuals be able to understand the legal consequences of their actions. *United States v. Pizzuto*, 332 U.S. 225, 228 (1947). When a statute is applied retroactively to a prior act, it undermines this principle by rendering past conduct legally uncertain. The law must be “so clear and definite as to leave no room for doubt.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953). The reinstatement statute, as applied to petitioner, fails this test.
