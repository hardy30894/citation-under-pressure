**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board Erred by Extending Harari I's Narrow "Initial-Filing" Exception to the Intervening '838 and '768 Applications**

**A. Harari I Created a Narrow, Fact-Bound Exception That Does Not Apply Here**

In *Harari v. Hollmer*, 602 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2010) ("*Harari I*"), this Court did not adopt a general rule that ambiguous incorporation language may always be cured by reference to an applicant's subjective intent. Rather, the Court limited its holding to a specific procedural posture: an original filing accompanied by a transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment that, together, clarified an ambiguity present in the copied disclosure. The Court held that "[t]he proper standard by which to evaluate the sufficiency of incorporation by reference language, at this stage of the proceedings, is whether the identity of the incorporated reference is clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented." *Id.* at 1352-53 (emphasis added). The italicized qualifier was not surplusage. It confined the holding to the unique evidentiary record before the examiner reviewing the '880 application's initial filing—a record that included curative papers filed contemporaneously with the ambiguous language.

The Board's judgment on remand erased this limitation by applying the same "reasonable examiner" inquiry to the '838 and '768 applications, neither of which was accompanied by any transmittal sheet, preliminary amendment, or other curative filing identifying the '579 application by serial number. The Board thus transformed a narrow exception into a general license to look past the four corners of an application whenever convenient to the patentee. That is not what *Harari I* held, and this Court should not permit the Board to disregard the qualifying language this Court deliberately included in its opinion.

**B. The Ordinary Incorporation-by-Reference Standard Governs the '838 and '768 Applications, and That Standard Is Not Satisfied**

Outside the narrow circumstance addressed in *Harari I*, this Court has long required that a host application "identify with detailed particularity what specific material it incorporates and clearly indicate where that material is found in the various documents." *Advanced Display Sys., Inc. v. Kent State Univ.*, 212 F.3d 1272, 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2000). Similarly, in *Zenon Environmental, Inc. v. U.S. Filter Corp.*, 506 F.3d 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2007), the Court reaffirmed that incorporation by reference is only effective where "the referenced document is described in the host document with sufficient particularity to identify it." *Id.* at 1379. And as the predecessor court to this Circuit explained in *In re de Seversky*, 474 F.2d 671 (C.C.P.A. 1973), incorporation must be clear and unequivocal on the face of the application, without resort to extrinsic clarification supplied years after filing.

The '838 and '768 applications flunk this standard. They copy verbatim the '566 application's statement referring to an application "filed on the same day as the present application"—a statement that was true when made in the '566 application, but which became facially false the moment it was carried forward into applications filed on entirely different dates. No transmittal sheet, no preliminary amendment, and no other contemporaneous document accompanies either the '838 or the '768 application to alert an examiner that the statement should be understood to refer to the '579 application rather than to some application filed on the actual filing date of the '838 or '768 application. A reasonable examiner reviewing either of these applications in isolation—the only proper frame of reference under *Advanced Display Systems* and *Zenon*—would have no basis to identify the '579 application as the incorporated reference. The ambiguity that *Harari I* found curable by extrinsic documents at the initial filing stage was never cured in these later applications; it was simply repeated.

**II. Because the '838 and '768 Applications Fail to Incorporate the '579 Application, the '880 Application Is Not Entitled to the Priority Benefit of the '566 Application's Filing Date**

Section 120 of the Patent Act requires an unbroken chain of applications, each of which satisfies the written description requirement of 35 U.S.C. § 112, first paragraph, with respect to the claimed subject matter. *See In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d 527, 537 (C.C.P.A. 1981) (priority under § 120 requires that "each application in the chain of applications" leading to the claimed application satisfy the written description requirement for the subject matter at issue). This Court has likewise held that a patentee seeking the benefit of an earlier filing date must demonstrate written description support "in each of the intervening applications, not just the earliest." *PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.*, 522 F.3d 1299, 1306 (Fed. Cir. 2008).

Because the '838 and '768 applications do not properly incorporate the '579 application, they lack written description support for any claim limitation dependent on that material. The chain of priority is therefore broken at the '838 application, and the '880 application cannot reach back to the '566 application's April 13, 1989 filing date. At most, the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the '708 application's December 20, 1996 filing date—the earliest application in the chain that unambiguously and correctly identifies the '579 application by serial number and filing date.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Board's judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions that the '880 application is entitled only to the priority benefit of the '708 application's filing date.
