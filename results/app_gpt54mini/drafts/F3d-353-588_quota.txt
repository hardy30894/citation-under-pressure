### Argument

#### I. The district court erred in holding that Epps failed to create a triable issue that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled under the ADA and the MHRA.

Summary judgment is inappropriate when the record permits a reasonable jury to find that the employer acted on a prohibited perception of disability. Under the ADA, an individual is “regarded as” disabled if the employer mistakenly believes that the employee has an impairment that substantially limits a major life activity, or believes an actual impairment is substantially limiting when it is not. *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999); *School Bd. of Nassau Cnty. v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 282 (1987). The Eighth Circuit has repeatedly held that an employer’s reliance on medical restrictions, inability-to-perform assumptions, or generalized judgments about an employee’s physical condition can support a “regarded as” claim. *Breitkreutz v. Cambrex Charles City, Inc.*, 450 F.3d 780, 783-84 (8th Cir. 2006); *Conant v. City of Hibbing*, 271 F.3d 782, 784-85 (8th Cir. 2001).

The record here is enough to create that inference. Hardy reviewed Epps’s workers’ compensation history, noted that he “had filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims,” and relied on Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps had degenerative disk disease and would likely need a different occupation. Hardy’s memoranda did not merely describe temporary medical limitations; they concluded that Epps could not perform the duties of a regular officer because officers must bend, squat, and run, and because no light-duty work was available. A jury could infer that Pine Lawn acted on the belief that Epps was broadly unable to function as a police officer, which is precisely the sort of stereotyping the ADA forbids. *Murphy v. United Parcel Serv., Inc.*, 527 U.S. 516, 524-25 (1999); *Albertson’s, Inc. v. Kirkingburg*, 527 U.S. 555, 566-67 (1999).

The district court’s analysis also overlooked that the MHRA is construed to provide broad protection against disability discrimination. Missouri courts look to federal disability-discrimination principles in interpreting the MHRA, but the statute is not limited to express admissions of disability; it reaches adverse actions taken because of an employer’s perception of impairment. *Cannady v. St. Louis Cnty.*, 530 S.W.3d 878, 884-85 (Mo. Ct. App. 2017); *Lane v. Amherst Residential Care, LLC*, 544 S.W.3d 370, 374-75 (Mo. 2018). At minimum, Hardy’s memoranda and Pine Lawn’s subsequent termination decision create a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Epps was terminated because the City regarded him as unable to perform the essential functions of his job. See *Knutson v. AG Processing, Inc.*, 394 F.3d 1047, 1050-51 (8th Cir. 2005).

#### II. The district court also erred in dismissing Epps’s Missouri workers’ compensation retaliation claim on sovereign-immunity grounds.

Missouri’s workers’ compensation retaliation statute, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780, creates a cause of action when an employee is discharged “because he or she has exercised any of the rights granted” by the workers’ compensation law. The Missouri Supreme Court has long recognized that retaliation claims under § 287.780 serve an important remedial purpose and should be adjudicated on the merits when the employer’s motive is disputed. *Greer v. Sys. Dynamics Int’l, Inc.*, 475 S.W.2d 513, 516-17 (Mo. 1972); *Jud v. Mo. Baptist Med. Ctr.*, 863 S.W.2d 319, 321-22 (Mo. Ct. App. 1993). Epps presented evidence that Hardy repeatedly relied on the number of compensation claims Epps had filed and his extended work absences in recommending termination. That is sufficient to create a jury question on retaliatory motive. *Smith v. Amequity, Inc.*, 997 S.W.2d 111, 113-14 (Mo. Ct. App. 1999).

The sovereign-immunity ruling was also erroneous because Missouri has waived immunity for torts within the scope of its public-entity liability statute. Under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 537.600, sovereign immunity is waived in specified circumstances, and public entities cannot invoke immunity where the legislature has authorized suit. *State ex rel. Bd. of Trs. of City of N. Kansas City Mem’l Hosp. v. Russell*, 843 S.W.2d 353, 355-56 (Mo. banc 1992); *Southers v. City of Farmington*, 263 S.W.3d 603, 609-10 (Mo. banc 2008). At a minimum, the City was not entitled to summary judgment on an immunity theory without a careful analysis of whether the asserted claim fell within the statutory waiver and whether the alleged conduct was governmental or proprietary in character. *Daly v. U.S. Dep’t of Interior*, 745 F.3d 867, 870-71 (8th Cir. 2014) (summary judgment inappropriate where immunity issue depends on disputed factual predicates).

#### III. The intentional-infliction claim was likewise improperly dismissed on sovereign-immunity grounds.

Missouri recognizes intentional infliction of emotional distress where a defendant’s conduct is “so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency.” *Nazeri v. Mo. Valley Coll.*, 860 S.W.2d 303, 316 (Mo. banc 1993). While such claims are difficult, a municipality may not obtain summary judgment simply by asserting immunity where the legislature has not clearly barred the action or where the record permits a finding of intentional, discriminatory termination. *Doyle v. Lambert*, 110 S.W.3d 575, 580-81 (Mo. Ct. App. 2003); *Allen v. Mo. Dep’t of Corr.*, 436 S.W.3d 840, 842-43 (Mo. 2014). Hardy’s use of Epps’s compensation history and medical condition as grounds for dismissal, if believed, could support a finding of outrageous conduct when combined with the alleged retaliatory motive. See *Chubb Group of Ins. Cos. v. C.F. Murphy & Assocs., Inc.*, 656 S.W.2d 766, 778 (Mo. banc 1983).

Because the record contains ample evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled and terminated him in retaliation for pursuing workers’ compensation benefits, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
