**ARGUMENT**

The decision below must be reversed because the District Court certified a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This Court has long held that a plaintiff must prove each element of his claim by competent evidence, and that a class action cannot be maintained where individualized proof of damages predominates.

**I. The District Court Erroneously Failed to Require Admissible Evidence That Damages Are Capable of Class-Wide Proof**

The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) requires a district court to determine whether "questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This determination demands a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence, not mere acceptance of a plaintiff's expert's say-so. *See* *American Pipe & Construction Co. v. Utah*, 414 U.S. 538, 547 (1974) (explaining that "although the prerequisites to a class action contained in Rule 23(a)(2) through (4) had been met, the requirement of Rule 23(a)(1) that 'the class (be) so numerous that joinder of all members is impracticable' was not satisfied").

Here, the District Court accepted Plaintiffs' expert Dr. McClave's damages model even though that model was not tied to the sole theory of antitrust impact the court had permitted—deterrence of overbuilder entry. The model calculated damages based on a benchmark of cable prices from other markets, but it did not isolate the effect of overbuilder deterrence from other potential causes of higher prices. This is not a model capable of proving class-wide damages; it is a model that assumes the answer.

This Court has consistently required that damages be proven with reasonable certainty, not speculation. *See* *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931) (holding that "the evidence show the extent of the damages as a matter of just and reasonable inference, although the result be only approximate"). A model that fails to account for the specific theory of liability cannot provide such an inference. *See* *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (holding that "the jury could conclude as a matter of just and reasonable inference from the" evidence).

**II. The District Court's Reliance on an Unsupported Damages Model Was an Abuse of Discretion**

The District Court's finding that Dr. McClave's model could measure class-wide damages was clearly erroneous. The model did not measure the impact of the alleged anticompetitive conduct—deterrence of overbuilders—but instead measured the difference between Comcast's prices and prices in other markets. This approach conflates correlation with causation. *See* *Standard Oil Co. v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1, 62 (1911) (rejecting a damages theory that failed to isolate the effect of the challenged conduct).

Moreover, the District Court failed to require that the model be capable of application to each class member's individual circumstances. In *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974), this Court explained that "there is nothing in either the language or history of Rule 23 that gives a court any authority to conduct" a particular inquiry. That analysis must include an examination of whether the proposed method of proving damages is capable of common proof.

**III. The Court of Appeals Erred in Deferring to the District Court's Clearly Erroneous Findings**

The Court of Appeals applied an overly deferential "clearly erroneous" standard to the District Court's factual findings. But a finding is clearly erroneous when, as here, the reviewing court is "left with a" definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972) (explaining that "our responsibility is not to substitute findings we could have made had we been the fact-finding tribunal; our sole function is to review the record to determine whether the findings of the District Court were clearly erroneous"). The District Court's acceptance of Dr. McClave's model was devoid of such support because the model did not measure the impact of the only theory of liability the court had permitted.

This Court has long recognized that a district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class without ensuring that common questions predominate. *See* *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 45 (1940) (holding that a class action cannot be maintained where a key issue is not common to the class); *see also* *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332, 336 (1969) (discussing the requirement of a common question of law or fact under Rule 23).

**IV. The District Court's Certification Violated the Requirement That Antitrust Impact Be Proven by Common Evidence**

Antitrust impact—the fact of injury—must be proven by common evidence in a class action. *See* *Klor's, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 207, 213 (1959) (requiring that antitrust plaintiffs prove "actual injury" to competition). Here, the District Court permitted Plaintiffs to proceed on a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders—but the evidence of impact was not common to all class members. Some class members may have been affected by overbuilder deterrence; others may not have been. The District Court's finding that impact could be proven by common evidence was therefore clearly erroneous. *See* *United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, 395 (1956) (discussing the relevant market for determining antitrust injury).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
