**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255 BECAUSE IT IS A LAWFUL ENTRY FOLLOWING INSPECTION**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that Temporary Protected Status (TPS) does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The plain text of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A grant of TPS is precisely such a lawful entry. As the Ninth Circuit recognized in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), TPS recipients are “deemed to have been inspected and to have entered the country lawfully.” This interpretation is consistent with the statutory framework, which treats TPS as a form of lawful nonimmigrant status. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) (stating that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant”).

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion misreads the statute. The court relied on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), for the proposition that “admission” requires physical entry. *Id.* at 485. But *Hanif* did not address TPS, nor did it hold that lawful status cannot constitute an admission. Rather, it distinguished between *entry* and *status*, a distinction that does not apply here because TPS recipients are lawfully present in the United States following inspection. *See also Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016) (recognizing that “admission” is a factual occurrence, but noting that “status” describes the type of permission to remain in the U.S.).

II. **CONGRESS INTENDED TPS TO FUNCTION AS A MECHANISM FOR ADJUSTMENT OF STATUS**

The Third Circuit’s interpretation renders § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless. That provision explicitly states that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The court’s holding that TPS does not constitute an admission would effectively nullify this provision, as it would prevent TPS recipients from satisfying § 1255’s admission requirement. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions.”).

This interpretation also conflicts with the statutory purpose of TPS. As the Ninth Circuit explained in *Ramirez*, Congress created TPS to provide temporary relief to nationals of countries facing extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). It would be illogical to interpret the statute in a way that forces TPS recipients to leave the United States and reenter lawfully—a process that could take years and expose them to danger—simply to adjust their status. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964.

III. **THE BIA AND OTHER CIRCUITS HAVE RECOGNIZED THAT TPS CAN CONSTITUTE AN ADMISSION**

The Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) has held that an adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission. *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). While the Third Circuit distinguished this case, it did not address the broader principle that lawful status can equate to an admission. *See Sanchez v. Johnson*, 2018 WL 6427894, at *4 (D.N.J. 2018) (citing *Espinosa-Guillot*).

Other circuits have reached the same conclusion. The Ninth Circuit in *Ramirez* held that TPS recipients are “deemed to have been inspected and to have entered the country lawfully.” 852 F.3d at 960. The Sixth Circuit in *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), likewise recognized that TPS can satisfy the admission requirement for adjustment of status. *See also Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam) (holding that TPS does not exempt recipients from § 1255(a)’s admission requirement but acknowledging that TPS provides lawful status).

IV. **THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD CREATE AN UNINTENDED BAR TO ADJUSTMENT OF STATUS**

The Third Circuit’s holding creates an absurd result: TPS recipients who entered the United States unlawfully are barred from adjusting their status, while those who entered lawfully may do so. This interpretation ignores the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), which treats all TPS recipients as being in lawful nonimmigrant status. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4).

Moreover, the court’s reliance on *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), is misplaced. *Taveras* addressed whether an alien in removal proceedings could be considered “admitted” for purposes of cancellation of removal. *Id.* at 290. It did not address TPS or the specific statutory framework at issue here. The court’s attempt to distinguish *Taveras* on factual grounds does not justify its erroneous interpretation of § 1255.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that the Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The plain text of the INA, the statutory framework, and the decisions of other circuits and the BIA all support the conclusion that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status. The Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
