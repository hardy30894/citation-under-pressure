**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a critical conflict in the interpretation of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), the statutory provision that defines “mixed cases” for purposes of judicial review. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, which holds that such jurisdictional dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit, is contrary to the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L.Ed.2d 433 (2012), and to the text, structure, and purpose of the Civil Service Reform Act. The Court should reverse.

### I. The D.C. Circuit’s Distinction Between “Procedural” and “Jurisdictional” Dismissals Is Contrary to *Kloeckner* and the Statutory Text

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that *Kloeckner* did not “eviscerate” its precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), rests on a distinction the Supreme Court never recognized: that procedural dismissals (e.g., untimeliness) are reviewable in district court, while jurisdictional dismissals (e.g., voluntariness of retirement) are not. But *Kloeckner* explicitly rejected such a distinction as inconsistent with the statute. There, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a *procedural* ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court did so not because the case was “procedurally defective,” but because the employee had been “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” and had alleged discrimination. Id. at 604.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* by asserting that the case involved a “procedural” dismissal while Perry’s case involved a “jurisdictional” one is legally untenable. The Supreme Court did not define “procedural” in *Kloeckner* as a category distinct from jurisdictional. Rather, it defined the scope of review based on whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). In *Kloeckner*, the Court emphasized that the employee had been “affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB,” and that the Board’s dismissal was based on a procedural defect, not lack of jurisdiction. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court did not ask whether the Board had jurisdiction; it asked whether the action was appealable. The same is true here.

Perry was affected by an action—his mandatory retirement and thirty-day suspension—that is appealable to the MSPB under 5 U.S.C. § 7512. His claim that the action was involuntary due to discrimination is precisely the kind of claim that triggers the mixed-case exception. The Board’s dismissal was not because the action was not appealable, but because it concluded that Perry had voluntarily accepted it. That is not a jurisdictional bar; it is a factual determination about the voluntariness of the action. As the Federal Circuit has recognized, “the Board’s jurisdiction depends on whether the action was voluntary,” and “the Board may not hear a case over which it lacks jurisdiction.” *Conforto v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 713 F.3d 1113, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). But the Board’s jurisdictional determination is not a “procedural” one; it is a threshold question of whether the case falls within the Board’s authority. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to reclassify this as a “jurisdictional” dismissal, while treating *Kloeckner*’s untimeliness dismissal as “procedural,” is a distinction without a difference under the statute.

### II. *Kloeckner* Overruled *Powell* by Necessity

The D.C. Circuit claims that *Powell* remains binding because *Kloeckner* did not address jurisdictional dismissals. But *Kloeckner* did not merely “address” procedural dismissals—it held that *all* mixed cases, regardless of the ground for dismissal, are reviewable in district court if the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). The Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner* makes clear that the forum for review depends not on the *reason* the Board dismissed the case, but on whether the case was a mixed case *at the time of the action*. The Court stated: “The question is not whether the Board reached the merits, but whether the employee was affected by an action appealable to the MSPB and alleged discrimination.” 133 S. Ct. at 604.

This reasoning necessarily undermines *Powell*, which held that when the Board dismisses a case for lack of jurisdiction—because the action was voluntary—review lies in the Federal Circuit. *Powell* treated the voluntariness of the action as a jurisdictional bar, but *Kloeckner* treats the same issue as a factual question that does not strip the case of its mixed-case status. The D.C. Circuit’s insistence that *Powell* survives because the Board “lacked jurisdiction” is inconsistent with *Kloeckner*’s focus on the *action*, not the Board’s jurisdiction. If the action is appealable and the employee alleges discrimination, the case is a mixed case—regardless of whether the Board later finds the action voluntary.
