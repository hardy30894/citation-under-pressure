**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Grant of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an “Admission” into the United States Under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.**

The text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the conclusion that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a satisfies § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.”

**A. Section 1254a(f)(4) Equates TPS with Lawful Nonimmigrant Status, Which Necessarily Includes Admission.**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The INA defines “admission” and “admitted” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). By the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). The Third Circuit erred by drawing an artificial distinction between “status” and “admission,” ignoring that the two concepts are inextricably linked for nonimmigrants. An alien cannot obtain lawful nonimmigrant status without first being inspected and admitted. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1201(a) (requiring consular officer to issue visa only after determining alien is entitled to nonimmigrant status); 8 U.S.C. § 1225(a) (requiring inspection of all aliens seeking admission). Congress’s decision to treat TPS recipients as being in lawful nonimmigrant status therefore necessarily carries with it the attendant requirement of admission.

**B. The Statutory Context Confirms That TPS Recipients Are Deemed Admitted.**

Congress knows how to distinguish between “admission” and “status” when it wishes. In § 1255(k), for example, Congress separately requires both “lawful admission” and continuous “lawful status.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(2)(A). But in § 1254a(f)(4), Congress did not merely place TPS recipients in “lawful status”; it placed them in “lawful status *as a nonimmigrant*.” That phrase is significant. The INA uses the term “nonimmigrant” to describe an alien who has been “admitted” into the United States for a temporary purpose. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) (defining “nonimmigrant” as an alien “having a residence in a foreign country which he has no intention of abandoning and who is visiting the United States temporarily”). By equating TPS with nonimmigrant status, Congress signaled that TPS recipients are to be treated as though they were admitted.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the canon against superfluity is misplaced. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). If TPS recipients were already “admitted” under § 1255(a), the court reasoned, then § 1255(c)(2)’s bar for failure to maintain lawful status would be redundant. But that ignores that § 1255(a) and § 1255(c)(2) serve different functions: the former is a threshold eligibility requirement, while the latter is a bar that can be waived. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(c)(2) (providing exception for failure “through no fault of his own or for technical reasons”). Treating TPS as an admission does not render § 1255(c)(2) superfluous; it simply means that TPS recipients satisfy the threshold requirement and must still overcome any applicable bars.

**C. The Purpose of TPS Supports Petitioners’ Interpretation.**

TPS was designed to provide humanitarian relief to nationals of countries suffering from armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s interpretation undermines that purpose by forcing TPS recipients who entered illegally to choose between remaining in the United States with temporary protection and leaving to seek lawful admission abroad—often to countries that remain unsafe. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964. Congress could not have intended such an anomalous result. *See Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Jong Ha Wang*, 450 U.S. 139, 144 (1981) (recognizing that immigration statutes should be construed to effectuate their ameliorative purpose).

**D. The Third Circuit’s Reliance on Agency Interpretation Is Unpersuasive.**

The Third Circuit cited an INS General Counsel opinion issued shortly after TPS was enacted. But that opinion is not entitled to deference. *See Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Miranda*, 459 U.S. 14, 19 (1982) (per curiam) (requiring “appropriate deference” to agency decisions, but only when the agency’s interpretation is reasonable). Here, the agency’s interpretation conflicts with the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), which equates TPS with nonimmigrant status. Moreover, the agency has not consistently adhered to this view. *See* 7 USCIS Policy Manual B.2(A)(5) (acknowledging that TPS recipients are considered in lawful status). The agency’s interpretation is therefore entitled to little weight.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
