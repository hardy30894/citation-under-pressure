# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) Exempt Only Funds Actually Set Aside for a Debtor's Own Retirement

The Bankruptcy Code exempts from the estate "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation" under specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C); id. §522(d)(12). The operative word is "retirement." As the Seventh Circuit correctly recognized, that word does not describe merely the historical origin of an asset, but its present function: money "held for the owner's retirement." *In re Clark*, Nos. 12-1241 & 12-1255, slip op. at 3 (7th Cir. Apr. 23, 2013). An inherited IRA, by contrast, "must begin distributing its assets within a year of the original owner's death," and payout "must be completed in as little as five years." *Id.* Far from being a vehicle for retirement savings, "an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." *Id.*

This Court has likewise construed retirement-related exemptions by asking whether the asset performs a retirement function, not merely whether it once bore that label. In *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320 (2005), the Court held that traditional IRAs qualify for the "similar plan or contract" exemption in §522(d)(10)(E) because they "provide substitutes for wages lost upon retirement" and impose penalties designed to discourage pre-retirement withdrawal. *Id.* at 325. The inquiry turned on the account's functional characteristics—penalty structures and payout timing tied to the *debtor's* retirement—not on the mere fact that the funds derived from a retirement source. The inherited IRA at issue here fails that functional test: Heidi Heffron-Clark faces no penalty for withdrawing the entire balance the day after filing for bankruptcy, and indeed the Internal Revenue Code compels distribution regardless of her age or proximity to retirement. 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B).

## II. An Inherited IRA Lacks Every Attribute That Makes Retirement Funds Deserving of Protection

Congress structured the retirement exemption to protect savings that are unavailable for current consumption and dedicated to the debtor's future security. Three features of ordinary IRAs serve that purpose: contributions may continue, withdrawals before age 59½ trigger a penalty tax, and the account need not be liquidated until the owner nears retirement age. An inherited IRA has none of these features. "[N]o new contributions can be made, and the balance cannot be rolled over or merged with any other account." *Clark*, slip op. at 2 (citing 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C)). There is no early-withdrawal penalty; the beneficiary may cash out the entire account immediately with no tax consequence beyond ordinary income tax. And distribution is mandatory on a schedule keyed to the *decedent's* death, not the beneficiary's retirement. *Id.*

The Seventh Circuit's hypothetical illustrates why this matters: had Ruth Heffron withdrawn the $300,000 from her own IRA before death and simply handed it to her daughter, no one would suggest the cash retained its character as "retirement funds" once it left the tax-favored account. *Id.* at 4–5. "Either way, the money used to be 'retirement funds' but isn't now." *Id.* at 5. The mode of transfer—death versus gift—cannot logically alter that conclusion, because in both cases the funds have become "an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings." *Id.* at 8.

## III. Petitioners' Reading Would Convert a Targeted Exemption Into an Unbounded Shelter for Inherited Wealth, Contrary to the Statute's Design

Petitioners, following *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), argue that because the statute exempts "retirement funds" without expressly limiting the phrase to "the debtor's" retirement funds—unlike neighboring provisions such as §522(b)(3)(B)—Congress meant the exemption to travel with the funds regardless of whose retirement they serve. But the absence of the word "debtor's" reflects only that Congress declined to impose the *temporal* restrictions found elsewhere in §522, such as the two-year domicile requirement for the homestead exemption, §522(b)(3)(A), so that ordinary employer contributions made during a pending bankruptcy remain protected. *Clark*, slip op. at 6. It does not license transforming any asset that once touched a retirement account into permanently exempt property, no matter how many hands it passes through or how divorced it becomes from any retirement purpose.

Accepting petitioners' rule would allow tax-deferred savings to "pass down through the generations without ever being subject to income tax" or creditor process, *id.* at 3, effectively creating a dynastic wealth-shelter that Congress never authorized. This Court has cautioned against exactly this brand of purposivism: "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and it "frustrates rather than effectuates legislative intent simplistically to assume that whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law." *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (emphasis omitted). Congress balanced the goal of protecting retirement savings against the interests of creditors; it did not extend that protection to windfalls that function as ordinary inheritances merely because the underlying dollars once resided in someone else's retirement account.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
