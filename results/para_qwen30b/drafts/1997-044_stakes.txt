**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DEBT IS NONDISCHARGEABLE UNDER 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) BECAUSE COHEN COMMITTED ACTUAL FRAUD THROUGH IMPLIED MISREPRESENTATIONS AND RECKLESS DISREGARD FOR THE TRUTH.

The Bankruptcy Court correctly determined that Edward S. Cohen’s debt to the tenants is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. That provision excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The Supreme Court has long held that “actual fraud” includes not only affirmative misrepresentations but also “fraud by silence” where a party fails to disclose material facts when under a duty to speak. *See, e.g.,* *United States v. D’Amato*, 422 U.S. 534, 540 (1975) (fraud may be committed by omission where there is a duty to disclose); *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1992) (citing *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir. 1995)).

Here, Cohen knowingly charged rents in excess of the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance, which he had a legal duty to observe. He admitted he never consulted the Rent Leveling and Stabilization Board, never read the ordinance, and never sought legal advice despite owning multiple rental properties. *See* Transcript of Nov. 12, 1993 Trial, at 53–54, 72–73. His silence in the face of this legal obligation constituted an implied representation that the rents he charged were lawful. *See* *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991) (debt non-dischargeable where debtor “impliedly represented” that a business venture was legitimate despite knowing it was not). The fact that Cohen did not make an affirmative statement is immaterial. As the Third Circuit has recognized, “a failure to disclose a material fact may constitute fraud when the party has a duty to disclose.” *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d at 1118.

Moreover, Cohen’s conduct demonstrates reckless indifference to the truth, satisfying both the knowledge and intent prongs of § 523(a)(2)(A). The Supreme Court has held that “intent to deceive” may be inferred from “reckless disregard for the truth.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). Recklessness requires more than negligence—it involves “unreasonable conduct in disregard of a known or obvious risk from which it is highly probable that harm would follow.” *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. at 834 (quoting *Prosser & Keeton on Torts*, § 34, at 213 (5th ed. 1984)). Cohen’s pattern of conduct—charging excessive rents across multiple properties, failing to seek guidance from the Board or counsel, and ignoring a formal notice of overcharging—constitutes precisely such reckless disregard. *See* *In re Van Horne*, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir. 1987) (finding reckless indifference where debtor ignored repeated warnings and failed to investigate legal obligations).

Cohen’s claim that he “did not know” the rents were unlawful is irrelevant. The Bankruptcy Code does not require actual knowledge of the falsity of a representation; it requires only that the debtor acted with reckless disregard. *See* *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d at 1118–19. His failure to investigate a legal obligation that directly affected his financial conduct—charging rent in a regulated market—was not mere negligence but a conscious indifference to the consequences. The Bankruptcy Court’s finding of fraud was not clearly erroneous and must be upheld.

II. THE TREBLE DAMAGES AWARDED UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE NONDISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE THEY ARE NOT PUNITIVE AND ARE FORESEEABLE CONSEQUENCES OF FRAUDULENT CONDUCT.

Cohen argues that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act (N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.) are punitive and thus dischargeable. This argument fails. The award here is not punitive in nature but is instead a statutory remedy designed to compensate victims and deter future violations. *See* *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir. 1993) (treble damages under state consumer protection law are not “punitive” for purposes of § 523(a)(2)(A) when they are mandatory and not discretionary).

The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages for violations of its provisions. *N.J.S.A. 56:8-19*. Unlike discretionary punitive awards, which are imposed at the court’s discretion and serve primarily to punish, statutory treble damages are automatic and directly tied to the harm caused. *See* *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not “obtained by” fraud). But the Ninth Circuit’s reasoning in *Levy* is inapposite here. There, the court considered a jury’s discretionary award of punitive damages. In contrast, the treble damages in this case were not discretionary but were statutorily mandated and fully foreseeable at the time Cohen charged the excessive rents.
