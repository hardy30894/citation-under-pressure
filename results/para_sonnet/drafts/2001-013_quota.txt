# ARGUMENT

**Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction over uninspected vessels because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over the working conditions at issue.**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts from the Act only "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . *exercise* statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1) (emphasis added). The statute's operative verb is not "possess" or "may exercise," but "exercise." Congress thus made actual regulatory action, not latent jurisdictional potential, the touchstone of preemption. Because the Coast Guard has never exercised authority over the occupational safety and health of workers aboard uninspected vessels such as Rig 52, OSHA's jurisdiction remains intact.

This Court and others have consistently construed parallel language in the Act to require an agency's demonstrated exercise of authority over the specific hazard, not merely a general grant of jurisdiction over the industry. In *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976), the Fifth Circuit itself held that the Federal Railroad Administration's general statutory authority over railroad safety did not oust OSHA jurisdiction until the FRA actually promulgated standards addressing the particular working condition. The D.C. Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Commission*, 548 F.2d 1052 (D.C. Cir. 1976), holding that § 4(b)(1) preemption tracks the scope of the sister agency's actual regulatory coverage, hazard by hazard. Both decisions confirm that Congress designed § 4(b)(1) to prevent duplicative regulation of the *same* working condition, not to create industry-wide exemptions wherever another agency has any conceivable authority to act.

The Coast Guard's regulatory regime for uninspected vessels bears out this hazard-specific approach. Congress and the Coast Guard have built an elaborate, graduated inspection scheme; only vessels meeting statutory criteria—size, cargo, passenger capacity—are subject to comprehensive Coast Guard inspection and certification. 46 U.S.C. § 3301. Uninspected vessels, including drilling barges like Rig 52, receive only the narrow safety regulations enumerated in 46 U.S.C. § 4102—fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation equipment, and signaling devices. Nothing in that limited catalog addresses the occupational hazards implicated here: blowout-prevention procedures, well-control training, or the workplace conditions that caused the explosion on Rig 52. The Coast Guard's own investigation confirmed as much, declining to fault Mallard under any Coast Guard standard precisely because none existed for the hazard in question.

This Court's decision in *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89 (2000), illustrates the proper method of analysis. There the Court examined, field by field, whether the Coast Guard had actually regulated a specific aspect of tank-vessel operation before displacing state law; where the Coast Guard had regulated comprehensively, state law fell, but where it had not, state authority survived. The same discipline governs here: the question is not whether the Coast Guard theoretically *could* regulate occupational safety aboard uninspected vessels, but whether it *has*. It has not.

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), cannot bear the weight the panel placed on it. Those decisions announced the broad proposition that "OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826–27, but neither opinion confronted, nor had occasion to confront, the distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels, or whether the Coast Guard had actually exercised authority over the specific hazard at issue. Categorical seaman-status rules of that kind conflate the § 4(a) "workplace" inquiry with the § 4(b)(1) preemption inquiry, which this Court's precedents treat as analytically distinct. See *Colomb v. Texaco, Inc.*, 736 F.2d 218 (5th Cir. 1984) (addressing seaman status without reaching preemption's scope).

Deference principles reinforce this reading. The Secretary's regulation, 29 C.F.R. § 1910.1(b), interprets § 4(b)(1) to exempt only working conditions actually covered by another agency's standards—a construction fully consistent with the statutory text and entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984) ("if the statute is silent or ambiguous with respect to the specific issue, the question for the court is whether the agency's answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute"). Even under the less deferential standard of *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134 (1944), the Secretary's longstanding, reasoned interpretation—grounded in the Act's remedial purpose of protecting workers "so far as possible," *Donovan v. Dewey*, 452 U.S. 594, 599 (1981)—merits substantial weight.

A categorical exemption for all vessels, inspected or not, would leave thousands of workers on drilling barges, tenders, and similar craft without any effective occupational-safety oversight whatsoever—precisely the regulatory vacuum Congress sought to avoid. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
