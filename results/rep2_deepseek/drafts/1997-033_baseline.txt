## ARGUMENT

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) unambiguously forecloses a transferee district court from invoking § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial after the conclusion of coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings. The statute provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” transfer actions “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings to the district from which it was transferred.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word “shall” imposes a mandatory duty. *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 35 (1998) (“The Panel ‘shall’ remand. That is the instruction.”).

Nothing in § 1407 authorizes a transferee court to circumvent this mandatory remand by using § 1404(a), the general change-of-venue statute. Section 1404(a) permits a district court to transfer a civil action “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). But when a case is before a court solely by virtue of a § 1407 transfer for pretrial proceedings, that court’s authority is limited to the purposes for which the transfer was ordered. The remand requirement is the “congressionally prescribed endpoint” of multidistrict litigation. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40. A transferee court cannot expand its own jurisdiction by invoking a general venue provision to override a specific statutory command.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision below, and the precedent it followed, erroneously conflated two distinct statutory schemes. In *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124–25 (2d Cir. 1971), the Second Circuit held, without any analysis of § 1407’s text, that a transferee court could consider § 1404(a) motions during pretrial proceedings because such motions are “pretrial” in nature. But that reasoning ignores the structure of § 1407: the Panel, not the transferee court, retains the power to transfer venues for trial. The Multidistrict Panel’s own Rule 14(b), which purports to authorize transferee courts to invoke § 1404(a), cannot override a statute. *See In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971) (acknowledging “nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial”).

This Court’s decision in *Lexecon* resolved the conflict. The Court held that a transferee court may not use § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40. The Court emphasized that § 1407’s “detailed mechanism” for remand would be “destroyed” if a transferee court could “short-circuit the Panel’s remands by keeping cases for itself.” *Id.* at 38, 40. Because the Panel had already concluded the transferred case would not benefit from further coordination, the District of Arizona had no authority to refuse remand.

Here, the District of Arizona took precisely the action *Lexecon* forbade. After the Panel under § 1407 transferred Lexecon’s claim for pretrial proceedings, the district court denied Lexecon’s motion for remand and instead granted Milberg’s countermotion to transfer the case “to itself for trial” under § 1404(a). That order directly contravened § 1407(a)’s remand mandate. The court of appeals’ decision to deny mandamus, based on the view that the issue could be resolved on appeal, does not change the legal error. Because the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction to retain the case for trial—or, at minimum, acted in excess of its statutory authority—the judgment must be reversed.

The policy considerations favoring convenience or efficiency cannot override Congress’s clear choice. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40 (rejecting argument that “the policy of § 1407 is best served by allowing transferee courts to retain jurisdiction over transferred cases when further proceedings would be more convenient”). Plaintiffs have a statutory right to have their case returned to the original forum for trial, absent a proper § 1404(a) transfer by the original district court after remand. The transferee court’s self-assignment usurped that right.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit must be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to order the District of Arizona to remand the action to the Northern District of Illinois.
