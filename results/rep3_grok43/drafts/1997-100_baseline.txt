**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), bars retrial of a prior-conviction allegation once a reviewing court has found the evidence insufficient. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), this Court held that the Clause precludes retrial when a sentencing proceeding “was itself a trial on the issue of punishment” possessing “the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 439. The same conclusion follows here.

California Penal Code § 1025 grants a statutory right to jury trial on prior-conviction allegations. The prosecution must prove the allegation “beyond a reasonable doubt.” *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 862 (1974). The rules of evidence apply, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224 (1996), and the defendant enjoys the rights of confrontation and cross-examination, *id.* at 228 n.6. These procedures are materially indistinguishable from those that triggered double-jeopardy protection in *Bullington* and *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1984).

The State’s contrary position rests on the observation that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* involved capital sentencing. Yet nothing in those opinions limits their reasoning to capital cases. The dispositive inquiry is whether the proceeding “resemble[s] a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause,” *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209, not the label affixed to the proceeding. *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), expressly left the question open, noting only that “a reasonable jurist” could have viewed the issue as unresolved. *Id.* at 393.

Because the California proceeding at issue carries the same procedural protections and imposes the same risk of increased punishment that *Bullington* found decisive, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial after a finding of evidentiary insufficiency. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
