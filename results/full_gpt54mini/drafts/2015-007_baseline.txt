**Argument**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, as applied here, does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The governing question after *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), is whether any fact necessary to increase a defendant’s punishment beyond the statutory maximum is found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. The Supreme Court in *Ring* held that Arizona’s judge-only finding of aggravating circumstances was unconstitutional because “aggravating factors operate as ‘the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense.’” 536 U.S. at 609, quoting *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000). But *Ring* did not hold that the jury must unanimously recommend death, nor did it require the jury to make every sentencing determination itself. Instead, the Sixth Amendment is satisfied so long as the jury finds the facts that expose the defendant to death. See *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609; *Apprendi*, 530 U.S. at 490.

Florida’s scheme, as described by the Florida Supreme Court, requires the jury to hear the evidence in the penalty phase and render an advisory recommendation, after which the judge imposes sentence and makes written findings regarding aggravation and mitigation. The Florida Supreme Court had previously upheld that structure, but *Ring* called into question whether the aggravating circumstances must be found by the jury. The proper constitutional issue is therefore narrower than petitioner suggests: whether Florida may require the jury to advise rather than formally adjudicate sentencing facts. Under *Ring*, once a jury has already found the defendant guilty of first-degree murder, the Constitution requires jury factfinding only as to the aggravators that make death legally available. 536 U.S. at 609; see also *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296, 303-04 (2004).

This Court’s later cases confirm that the Sixth Amendment turns on jury determination of the aggravating facts, not on unanimity in the ultimate sentencing recommendation. In *Kansas v. Carr*, 577 U.S. 108 (2016), the Court reaffirmed that capital sentencing procedures need not be regimented into a single constitutional formula so long as the jury’s role protects the defendant’s rights. And in *McKinney v. Arizona*, 589 U.S. 119 (2020), the Court emphasized that the Sixth Amendment concerns the jury’s role in finding aggravating circumstances, not every downstream weighing process. Florida’s advisory recommendation is therefore not itself the constitutional defect petitioner claims.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require a unanimous death recommendation. The Court has long recognized that the Constitution affords states substantial discretion in capital sentencing procedures. See *Pulley v. Harris*, 465 U.S. 37, 50-51 (1984). While the Eighth Amendment demands heightened reliability in capital cases, see *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976); *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604-05 (1978), it has never been held to mandate unanimity in a jury’s death recommendation. To the contrary, the Court has rejected efforts to constitutionalize a particular form of capital-sentencing process absent a clear command from precedent. See *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994-95 (1991); *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 195 (1976).

Petitioner’s mental-retardation argument fails for the same reason. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of the intellectually disabled, but it expressly left to the states “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon their execution of sentences.” 536 U.S. at 317, quoting *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 416-17 (1986). That means Florida may assign the threshold determination to the court, apply its own procedures, and require the defendant to prove the claim under state law. See *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6, 7-8 (2005) (per curiam). The Constitution requires only that a defendant not be executed if intellectually disabled; it does not prescribe a jury-trial mechanism for that question. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317.

Accordingly, Florida’s sentencing process does not offend the Sixth or Eighth Amendments as petitioner frames the issue. The Constitution requires jury findings on the facts that make death legally possible; it does not require a unanimous jury recommendation or jury adjudication of intellectual-disability claims. See *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609; *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. The judgment should be affirmed.
