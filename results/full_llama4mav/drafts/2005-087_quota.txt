The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Constitution permits states to allocate the burden of proof on insanity to the defendant, as Arizona has done. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952). In Leland, the Court held that due process is not offended when a state requires a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. at 799. Similarly, in Clark's case, Arizona law allows the defendant to present expert evidence on insanity, but limits its use to that defense.

The Court has long recognized that states have flexibility in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses to them. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977) (holding that a state may require a defendant to prove extreme emotional disturbance by a preponderance of the evidence). In Patterson, the Court emphasized that the Due Process Clause does not require a state to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact that might mitigate the degree of culpability. Id. at 207. Here, Arizona has chosen to limit the use of expert evidence on mental state to the insanity defense, and the Constitution does not prohibit this approach.

The Court has also acknowledged that the insanity defense is a matter of state law and policy. See Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 536-37 (1968) (plurality opinion) (noting that the insanity defense is a "complex and controversial matter" that is "peculiarly a matter for legislative judgment"). Arizona's decision to restrict the use of expert evidence on mental state to the insanity defense is a legitimate exercise of its authority to define the elements of crimes and defenses.

Furthermore, the Court has held that a state's rule limiting the admissibility of certain evidence does not necessarily violate due process. See Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 43 (1996) (plurality opinion) (holding that a state rule excluding evidence of voluntary intoxication did not violate due process). In Egelhoff, the Court emphasized that the Constitution does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all relevant evidence. Id. at 42. Similarly, Arizona's rule limiting the use of expert evidence on mental state to the insanity defense is a valid exercise of its authority to regulate the admissibility of evidence.

The lower court's decision in this case is consistent with these precedents. By limiting the use of expert evidence on mental state to the insanity defense, Arizona has not relieved the prosecution of its burden to prove the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (holding that the prosecution must prove all elements of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt). The prosecution still must prove that Clark had the requisite criminal intent, and the jury is free to consider all relevant evidence, including Clark's behavior and statements, in determining whether the prosecution has met its burden.
