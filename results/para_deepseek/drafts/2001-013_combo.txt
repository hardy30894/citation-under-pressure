**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction over Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that Coast Guard authority preempts OSHA under Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act. Pre-1970 precedent establishes that the Coast Guard does not exercise comprehensive statutory authority over the occupational safety and health of employees on uninspected vessels, and that the crew of a drilling barge in territorial waters are not “seamen” for purposes of preemption.

**I. The Coast Guard’s Authority Over Uninspected Vessels Is Limited and Does Not Occupy the Field of Occupational Safety and Health.**

Section 4(b)(1) preempts OSHA only when another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is confined to specific equipment and safety features, not general working conditions. *United States v. Moran*, 210 U.S. 324, 339 (1908), recognized that the Coast Guard’s power to “promote safety of life and property” is not plenary; it extends only to matters “not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department.” The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels, codified at 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01, address fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation—not the comprehensive occupational safety standards that OSHA enforces. This limited regulation does not constitute the “exercise of statutory authority” that triggers preemption. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947), instructs that preemption requires a “clear and manifest purpose” by Congress. No such purpose exists for uninspected vessels, where the Coast Guard’s regulatory footprint is minimal.

**II. The Crew of a Drilling Barge in Territorial Waters Are Not “Seamen” Under Pre-1970 Law.**

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the crew were “seamen” relies on post-1970 cases. Under pre-1970 precedent, employees on a drilling barge moored in Louisiana’s territorial waters are not “seamen” because their work is not traditionally maritime in nature. In *Senko v. La Crosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 370, 372 (1957), the Supreme Court held that a worker on a dredge was a seaman only if his duties “contribute[d] to the function of the vessel or to the accomplishment of its mission.” A drilling barge’s mission is mineral extraction, not navigation. *Wilkes v. Mississippi River Sand & Gravel Co.*, 202 F.2d 383, 384 (6th Cir. 1953), held that employees on a dredge who “did not perform any duties which were essential to the navigation of the vessel” were not seamen. The Rig 52 crew performed drilling operations, not navigational tasks. Moreover, *Standard Dredging Co. v. Murphy*, 319 U.S. 306, 309 (1943), upheld state workers’ compensation jurisdiction over a dredge in territorial waters, rejecting the argument that maritime employment precluded state regulation. Employees on a stationary barge in state waters are not “seamen” exempt from OSHA.

**III. The Barge Is a “Workplace” Within a State Under Section 4(a).**

Section 4(a) applies the OSH Act to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” Rig 52 was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, which are part of the State. *The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17, 33 (1903), held that state jurisdiction extends to “the waters of the State within its territorial limits.” *Standard Dredging Co.*, 319 U.S. at 309, applied state workers’ compensation to a dredge in Louisiana waters, recognizing that the “workplace” was within the State. The barge was a fixed workplace, not a vessel in navigation. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 569 (1944), similarly held that the Longshoremen’s Act did not cover workers on a pier extension because the “maritime employment” exception required a closer connection to navigation. The drilling barge’s permanent location and function as a drilling platform place it squarely within the state workplace.

**IV. Overlapping Federal Regulation Would Create Confusion, but Congress Did Not Intend to Exclude Uninspected Vessels from OSHA.**

The Fifth Circuit worried about “overlapping regulation” between OSHA and the Coast Guard. But pre-1970 cases hold that overlapping federal jurisdiction is permissible when Congress has not clearly preempted. *Davis v. Department of Labor*, 317 U.S. 249, 255 (1942), sustained concurrent state and federal jurisdiction over maritime workers, noting that “the mere fact that the Federal Government has also acted in the field does not exclude state action.” *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 6 (1946), reaffirmed that state remedies for maritime injuries are not preempted absent “clear and unambiguous” congressional intent. For uninspected vessels, the Coast Guard’s limited authority does not bar OSHA from enforcing safety standards that fill gaps in protection.

**V. The Coast Guard’s Investigation Does Not Demonstrate Exclusive Jurisdiction.**

The Coast Guard investigated the explosion under 46 U.S.C. § 6301, but that statute authorizes investigations—not regulation of working conditions. *Maryland Casualty Co. v. Lawson*, 101 F.2d 732, 734 (5th Cir. 1939), distinguished between the Coast Guard’s “investigative” and “regulatory” functions, holding that an investigation does not preempt state compensation laws. The Coast Guard’s post-accident inquiry does not establish that it exercises statutory authority over occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels.

**CONCLUSION**

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited, the crew of a drilling barge in territorial waters are not seamen, and the barge is a workplace within a State. The OSH Act therefore applies. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal should itself be reversed, and the citations reinstated.
