# ARGUMENT

## I. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Recognize a "Class of One" Cause of Action Where a Plaintiff Fails to Allege Membership in Any Class or Group

The Equal Protection Clause guarantees that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, §1. This Court's precedents make clear that the touchstone of an equal protection claim is differential treatment based on membership in an identifiable class. *See* City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985) (explaining that the Equal Protection Clause "is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike," and analyzing claims through the lens of classifications affecting groups). The Seventh Circuit's novel "class of one" theory—permitting a plaintiff to proceed without alleging membership in any class—cannot be reconciled with this framework.

### A. The Text and History of the Equal Protection Clause Presuppose Classification

The very phrase "equal protection" implies a comparison between groups, not merely between an individual and an undifferentiated mass of others. This Court has consistently analyzed equal protection claims by asking whether government action creates a "classification" that treats similarly situated groups differently. *See* Romer v. Evans, 517 U.S. 620, 631 (1996) (equal protection "central purpose" is to prevent government from "impos[ing] burdens on any group by reason of *animus* toward that group as a class"). The Seventh Circuit's decision, *see Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386 (7th Cir. 1998), dispenses with this class-based inquiry entirely, holding that Olech could state a claim by alleging she was targeted "solely" out of a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." That formulation—borrowed from *Esmail v. Macrane*, 53 F.3d 176, 180 (7th Cir. 1995)—transforms the Equal Protection Clause into a general tort remedy for arbitrary or ill-motivated government action, a role this Court has never assigned to it.

### B. This Court's Precedents Confirm That Equal Protection Analysis Requires a Comparator Class

In *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979), this Court held that equal protection liability requires proof that the challenged action was taken "because of," not merely "in spite of," its adverse effects upon an identifiable group. The "because of" inquiry is meaningless absent a class against which the plaintiff's treatment can be measured. Similarly, in *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976), this Court explained that equal protection doctrine turns on whether official action reflects a discriminatory purpose directed at a class of persons. No such purpose can be alleged, as a matter of logic, when the plaintiff claims to be a "class" unto herself.

This Court's decision in *Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 416 U.S. 1, 8 (1974), likewise presupposes that equal protection review addresses classifications—there, a zoning ordinance affecting groups of unrelated persons—rather than individualized grievances about the application of otherwise valid, generally applicable requirements to a single property owner.

### C. The "Class of One" Theory Threatens to Federalize Ordinary Municipal Decisionmaking

Permitting equal protection suits absent any class allegation would convert every disagreement over local land-use, permitting, or licensing decisions into a potential federal constitutional claim. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against reading the Equal Protection Clause to displace the traditional deference owed to local governments in matters of economic and regulatory policy. In *Federal Communications Commission v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993), this Court reaffirmed that legislation "is accorded a strong presumption of validity" and that those attacking rationality "have the burden to negative every conceivable basis which might support it." That deferential framework is designed for review of classifications—line-drawing between groups—not case-by-case litigation over whether a single permit condition was "arbitrary" as applied to one applicant.

The Seventh Circuit's rule would require federal courts to police the ordinary discretionary judgments of local zoning boards, water departments, and licensing officials whenever a disappointed applicant claims that but for personal animus, a different result would have obtained. This Court has never sanctioned such intrusive federal oversight of local governance under the Equal Protection Clause. *See Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440 (noting that "the general rule is that legislation is presumed to be valid" absent a suspect classification or fundamental right).

### D. Respondent's Claim, Properly Understood, Sounds in Substantive Due Process, Not Equal Protection

To the extent Olech contends that Willowbrook's 33-foot easement demand was arbitrary or lacked any rational relationship to a legitimate governmental purpose, her remedy—if any—lies in substantive due process, not equal protection. This Court's substantive due process jurisprudence already provides a vehicle for testing the rationality of individualized government action without requiring the artificial construct of a "class of one." *See* Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365, 395 (1926) (assessing whether a zoning ordinance bears a "substantial relation to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare"). Collapsing due process rationality review into the Equal Protection Clause—simply by relabeling the claim—eliminates the distinct doctrinal boundaries this Court has carefully maintained between the two clauses.

### E. Conclusion

Because Olech's complaint alleged no membership in any class or group, and because the Equal Protection Clause by its terms and through this Court's precedents requires such an allegation, the Seventh Circuit erred in recognizing a "class of one" equal protection claim. The judgment below should be reversed.
