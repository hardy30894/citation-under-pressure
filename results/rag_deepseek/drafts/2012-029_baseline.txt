**ARGUMENT**

The decision below rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the “substantial federal issue” standard. The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim that requires proof of a patent-law “case within a case” necessarily triggers exclusive federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. The federal patent issue here is neither substantial nor central to the state-law claim, and exercising federal jurisdiction would disturb the congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.

**I. The Federal Patent Issue Is Not “Substantial” Under *Grable*.**

In *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005), this Court held that federal question jurisdiction exists over a state-law claim only when the claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” The “substantial” requirement demands more than mere relevance; the federal issue must be “significant to the federal system as a whole.” *Id.* at 313.

Here, the federal patent issue—whether the experimental use exception would have saved Minton’s patent from the on-sale bar—is not substantial in the *Grable* sense. The issue is case-specific and fact-bound, turning on the primary purpose of a single lease between Minton and Stark. It does not implicate the validity or scope of any patent right beyond the parties’ dispute. As this Court recognized in *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 810 (1988), a federal issue is not substantial when it is “merely incidental” to the state-law claim. The Texas Supreme Court’s own analysis confirms that the patent issue is but one element of Minton’s malpractice claim, which also requires proof of duty, breach, and damages under state law. *See* Tex. Sup. Ct. Op. at 5 (citing *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004)). A federal issue that is “only one of several elements” does not satisfy *Grable*’s substantiality requirement. *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 390 (5th Cir. 2008).

Moreover, the federal issue is not “actually disputed” in any meaningful sense. Minton’s claim fails on causation regardless of the experimental use exception’s merits. The trial court granted summary judgment because Minton could not show that the lease’s primary purpose was experimental—a factual question that does not require resolution of any contested patent-law principle. *See* Tex. Sup. Ct. Op. at 4. A federal issue that is “hypothetical” or “unnecessary” to the state court’s decision cannot confer federal jurisdiction. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 315.

**II. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of State and Federal Judicial Responsibilities.**

The fourth *Grable* factor—whether federal jurisdiction would “disturb any congressionally approved balance”—is dispositive. *Id.* at 314. Congress has not indicated that every state malpractice claim touching on patent law must be litigated in federal court. To the contrary, state courts have long adjudicated legal malpractice claims that require application of federal law, including patent law, without undermining uniformity. *See, e.g., New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 343 (Neb. 2005) (rejecting federal jurisdiction over patent-law malpractice claim); *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516, 525 (Cal. Ct. App. 2010) (same). The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), improperly expands federal jurisdiction by ignoring *Grable*’s federalism analysis. As the dissent below observed, those decisions “fail[] to follow the test set forth by the Supreme Court.” Tex. Sup. Ct. Op. at 10 (Guzman, J., dissenting).

Allowing federal jurisdiction here would transform every state malpractice suit arising from patent litigation into a federal case, overwhelming the federal docket with routine negligence claims. This Court has cautioned against such “a gigantic shift of traditionally state cases to federal courts.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319. The balance Congress struck in § 1338(a) reserves federal jurisdiction for cases where patent law is the “central” issue, not a collateral element of a state tort claim. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 809.

**III. The Texas Supreme Court Erred by Relying on Federal Circuit Precedent That Misapplies *Grable*.**

The Texas Supreme Court adopted the Federal Circuit’s reasoning in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept*, which held that any state malpractice claim requiring proof of patent infringement “necessarily” raises a substantial federal issue. *See* Tex. Sup. Ct. Op. at 6–7. But those decisions conflate “necessary” with “substantial.” A federal issue may be necessary to a state claim without being substantial in the *Grable* sense. *See Singh*, 538 F.3d at 390. The Federal Circuit’s approach also ignores *Grable*’s command to consider federalism. *See* Tex. Sup. Ct. Op. at 10 (Guzman, J., dissenting) (noting that *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* “conduct[] no federalism analysis”). This Court should reject that approach and reaffirm that state courts retain jurisdiction over legal malpractice claims that incidentally involve patent law.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case remanded for consideration on the merits.
