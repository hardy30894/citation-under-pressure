**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF OF THE DEFENDANT’S SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) on the ground that the First Amendment’s “true threat” exception is satisfied by an objective standard. This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a historically grounded exception, such as incitement, obscenity, or fighting words. *See Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969). The “true threat” doctrine is no exception. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (plurality opinion) (holding that “true threats” are unprotected speech but declining to resolve whether a subjective intent standard applies).

This Court’s precedents make clear that the First Amendment requires a culpable mental state when speech is criminalized. *See United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994) (holding that the government must prove knowledge or recklessness as to the age of performers in child pornography prosecutions). The same principle applies here. A defendant cannot be convicted of a “true threat” without proof that he *intended* his words to be taken as a threat. *See id.* at 73 (“[W]here a statute regulates expressive conduct, the government must prove some mental state as to each element of the offense.”).

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—adopted in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991)—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. *Kosma* held that a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. *Id.* But this objective standard conflicts with the First Amendment’s requirement that speech restrictions be narrowly tailored to serve compelling interests. *See Ashcroft v. ACLU*, 542 U.S. 656, 666 (2004). An objective standard risks criminalizing protected speech merely because a listener *subjectively* feels threatened, regardless of the speaker’s intent. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 365 (O’Connor, J., concurring) (“[T]he speaker’s intent is the crucial element in determining whether a statement is a true threat.”).

This Court should clarify that the First Amendment requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten. *See id.* at 360 (plurality opinion) (“[T]he speaker must have intended the statement as a threat.”). The government’s contrary position—that an objective standard suffices—would chill constitutionally protected speech by allowing speakers to be punished for ambiguous statements that a reasonable person *might* interpret as threats, even if the speaker did not intend them as such. *See id.* at 365 (O’Connor, J., concurring) (“[A] speaker’s words may be ‘threatening’ in a colloquial sense, but that does not necessarily mean they are ‘true threats’ unprotected by the First Amendment.”).

II. **THE JURY INSTRUCTIONS IN THIS CASE VIOLATED THE FIRST AMENDMENT BY OMITTING THE SUBJECTIVE INTENT REQUIREMENT.**

The district court instructed the jury that a “true threat” is one that a reasonable person would interpret as a threat. *See* App. 26a. This instruction omitted the critical requirement that Elonis *subjectively intended* his statements to be threats. The omission was constitutional error.

This Court has repeatedly held that jury instructions must faithfully reflect the law. *See Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 72 (1991) (“[J]ury instructions must be evaluated in the context of the instructions as a whole and the trial record.”). Here, the instruction failed to require proof of Elonis’s subjective intent, thereby allowing the jury to convict him based solely on an objective assessment of his statements. This error was not harmless. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 23 (1967) (holding that constitutional errors are harmless only if they did not contribute to the verdict).

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that an objective standard suffices—is unsupported by this Court’s precedents. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 360 (plurality opinion) (“[T]he speaker must have intended the statement as a threat.”). The government’s reliance on *Kosma* is misplaced, as that decision predates this Court’s clarification in *Black* that the First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent. *See id.* at 359-60.

III. **THE GOVERNMENT’S POSITION WOULD UNCONSTITUTIONALLY CHILL PROTECTED SPEECH.**

The government’s objective standard would criminalize a vast amount of protected speech. For example, a frustrated employee venting on social media—even in hyperbolic terms—could be prosecuted if a reasonable listener felt threatened, regardless of the speaker’s intent. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 365 (O’Connor, J., concurring) (“[T]he First Amendment does not permit the criminalization of speech based solely on the listener’s reaction.”).

This Court has long recognized the chilling effect of vague or overbroad speech restrictions. *See Reno v. ACLU*, 521 U.S. 844, 874 (1997) (“[T]he danger of censorship and self-censorship in the area of political and social discourse is too great.”). The government’s position here would have precisely that effect, deterring individuals from engaging in robust debate on matters of public concern for fear of prosecution. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with the First Amendment.
