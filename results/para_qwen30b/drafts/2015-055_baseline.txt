**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to cases like Welch’s—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court’s holding in *Johnson* that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, is a substantive rule of criminal law that governs the scope of federal punishment. As such, it applies retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including Welch’s, under the settled principle that “a decision of this Court that a statute is unconstitutionally vague is a rule that is retroactive in the sense that it applies to all cases, even those already final, that are still subject to collateral attack.”

The Court has long recognized that when a decision invalidates a statute as unconstitutionally vague, it announces a rule that “clarifies the law” and “does not merely announce a new rule of procedure.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989). In *Johnson*, the Court held that the ACCA’s residual clause failed to provide “fair notice” of the conduct it punished, rendering it “so vague that it fails to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punishes.” 576 U.S. at 604. This is not a procedural rule; it is a substantive limitation on the government’s power to punish. The Court emphasized that the clause’s “hypothetical risk” standard “makes the application of the clause unconstitutionally arbitrary and unpredictable,” and thus violates the Due Process Clause. Id. at 605. Such a holding necessarily invalidates any prior conviction that relied on the residual clause for ACCA enhancement—precisely the situation here.

Welch’s 1996 Florida “strong arm robbery” conviction was used to trigger the ACCA’s mandatory minimum sentence. At the time of his conviction, the Florida statute criminalized robbery by “force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” But the Eleventh Circuit, relying on *McCloud v. State*, 335 So.2d 257 (Fla. 1976), correctly interpreted Florida law to require more than mere snatching. The Florida Supreme Court had held that “robbery must be accomplished with more than the force necessary to remove the property from the person.” *Robinson v. State*, 692 So.2d 883, 886 (Fla. 1997). Thus, the Florida statute, as interpreted by its highest court, required a level of force that could “present a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—the very standard the residual clause was meant to capture. But *Johnson* invalidated that clause as unconstitutionally vague, rendering any reliance on it for sentencing impermissible.

The Court has made clear that when a rule “clarifies the law” by invalidating a statute as unconstitutionally vague, it applies retroactively to all cases still open for collateral review. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311. In *Chapman v. United States*, 500 U.S. 453 (1991), the Court held that a decision invalidating a sentencing enhancement under the ACCA was retroactive because it “clarified the meaning of the statute.” Similarly, in *Johnson*, the Court did not create a new procedural rule; it declared that the residual clause, as applied, violated due process. That holding is not limited to future cases. It is a substantive rule that “announces a new principle of law” that “has been recognized as a constitutional right.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 301.

Welch’s case is still subject to collateral review. He filed a motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 after *Johnson* was decided, and the district court denied a certificate of appealability. But the Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson* created a new basis for relief: the ACCA’s residual clause is unconstitutional. The Court has held that “a decision that a statute is unconstitutionally vague is retroactive in the sense that it applies to all cases, even those already final, that are still subject to collateral attack.” *United States v. Johnson*, 576 U.S. 591, 605 (2015). Welch’s case was not final when *Johnson* issued; it was pending on collateral review. Therefore, *Johnson* applies to his case.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the law as it stood at the time of the decision. But the law has changed. The Court’s holding in *Johnson* is not a mere procedural refinement—it is a constitutional limitation on the government’s power to punish. It is retroactive by principle and precedent. To hold otherwise would allow the government to impose mandatory minimums based on a clause the Court has now declared unconstitutionally vague. That would violate the fundamental guarantee of due process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. The rule announced in *Johnson* applies retroactively to Welch’s case, and his sentence must be vacated and remanded for resentencing without reliance on the residual clause.
