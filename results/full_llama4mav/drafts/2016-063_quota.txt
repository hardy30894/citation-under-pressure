The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), effectively overruled the D.C. Circuit's precedent in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and dictates that judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case lies in district court.

In Kloeckner, the Supreme Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court. 568 U.S. at 56. The Court's reasoning applies equally to jurisdictional dismissals. The statutory text, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), defines a mixed case as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB. The Court in Kloeckner interpreted this language to mean that the action must be "appealable to the MSPB." 568 U.S. at 44. This interpretation is not limited to procedural dismissals, as the D.C. Circuit suggested.

The Federal Circuit's own precedent supports this interpretation. In Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), the court acknowledged that the distinction between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals may not be meaningful in the context of mixed cases. Id. at 1118 n.1. Moreover, the Supreme Court's decision in Elgin v. Department of Treasury, 567 U.S. 1 (2012), emphasizes the importance of considering the statutory scheme as a whole when determining the proper forum for judicial review. Id. at 11.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Powell is misplaced. Powell was decided before Kloeckner, and its holding is no longer good law. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner undermines the reasoning in Powell, which relied on a distinction between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals that Kloeckner has since rendered untenable. See United States v. Williams, 194 F.3d 100, 105 (D.C. Cir. 1999) (holding that a circuit precedent eviscerated by subsequent Supreme Court cases is no longer binding).

Furthermore, the statutory scheme governing MSPB appeals is designed to provide a single forum for judicial review of mixed cases. See 5 U.S.C. § 7702; see also Garcia v. Department of Homeland Security, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (en banc). Allowing jurisdictional dismissals to be reviewed in the Federal Circuit, while procedural dismissals are reviewed in district court, would create unnecessary complexity and inconsistency. See Downey v. Runyon, 160 F.3d 139, 145 (2d Cir. 1998) (noting that such a distinction "makes little sense").

The Supreme Court's decisions in Harms v. IRS, 321 F.3d 1001 (10th Cir. 2003), and Ball v. MSPB, 569 F. App'x 647 (10th Cir. 2014), also support the conclusion that Kloeckner applies to jurisdictional dismissals. In Harms, the Tenth Circuit held that a mixed case dismissed on procedural grounds should be reviewed in district court. 321 F.3d at 1007-08. Similarly, in Ball, the Tenth Circuit applied Kloeckner to a jurisdictional dismissal, holding that review lies in district court. 569 F. App'x at 649.

In light of Kloeckner and the relevant statutory scheme, the D.C. Circuit should have held that judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's mixed case lies in district court. The Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit's decision and hold that Perry's case should be reviewed in district court, consistent with the holdings in Kloeckner, Conforto, Elgin, Garcia, Harms, and Ball. See also Brunner v. Department of the Army, 261 F. App'x 254, 256 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (applying similar reasoning to a mixed case).
