**ARGUMENT**

The District Court’s admission of testimonial evidence from Assistant District Attorneys (ADAs) and a police lieutenant violated fundamental principles of evidentiary fairness and deprived Appellants of a fair trial. The testimony—offered by government agents in their official capacities—was not merely cumulative or harmless; it was prejudicial, improper, and legally impermissible under settled rules governing witness credibility, legal conclusions, and the interpretation of evidence. These errors collectively undermined the jury’s ability to assess the facts impartially and must require reversal.

First, the admission of opinion testimony from ADAs and a police lieutenant that vouched for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera constitutes a clear violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 701, which prohibits lay witnesses from testifying in the form of opinions that “are not rationally based on the witness’s perception.” *See United States v. Brawner*, 352 F.2d 846, 850 (D.C. Cir. 1965). Here, the ADAs and the lieutenant testified directly about whether Ramos and Rivera were truthful, whether they acted reasonably, and whether their accounts were consistent with the evidence. Such testimony is precisely what Rule 701 was designed to exclude: “a layman’s conclusion about another person’s honesty or truthfulness,” *United States v. D’Amato*, 342 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1965), because it usurps the jury’s role as factfinder. As this Court held in *United States v. Cianci*, 332 F.2d 522, 525 (2d Cir. 1964), “the jury must be left free to weigh the credibility of witnesses without being told by government officials whether those witnesses are telling the truth.”

Second, the testimony constituted impermissible legal conclusions. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 704, expert testimony may not state an opinion on the ultimate issue of guilt or innocence, but even lay testimony may not offer legal conclusions. *See United States v. Sarno*, 334 F.2d 502, 505 (2d Cir. 1964). Yet here, the ADAs and the lieutenant repeatedly stated whether probable cause existed to arrest Cameron and Higgenbottom, whether the officers had authority to act, and whether the evidence supported the charges. These are not matters of perception—they are legal determinations reserved for the court and jury. To allow government agents to testify as to whether probable cause existed is to invite the jury to defer to the very law enforcement actors whose conduct is under scrutiny. *See United States v. Mancuso*, 323 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1963); *cf.* *United States v. Galloway*, 318 F.2d 144, 147 (2d Cir. 1963) (holding that “a prosecutor’s opinion on the sufficiency of evidence” is inadmissible).

Third, the testimony improperly interpreted evidence that the jury could equally well analyze. The ADAs and the lieutenant offered interpretations of photographs, statements, and physical movements—such as whether Cameron reached into the squad car or whether her gestures indicated obstruction. This violates the principle that “a witness may not interpret evidence for the jury when the jury can draw its own inferences.” *United States v. Pascucci*, 320 F.2d 504, 507 (2d Cir. 1963). In *United States v. Ruggiero*, 319 F.2d 124, 127 (2d Cir. 1963), this Court emphasized that “the jury must be permitted to decide for itself whether the evidence supports a particular inference,” and that “to permit a government agent to tell the jury what to believe is to undermine the jury’s independence.” The same holds true when such agents are prosecutors or supervisors who have a stake in the outcome.

These errors were not harmless. The District Court’s failure to intervene when the ADAs and lieutenant repeatedly testified about credibility, probable cause, and the weight of evidence created a pervasive atmosphere of governmental endorsement of the officers’ version of events. *See United States v. Lopinto*, 325 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1963) (“When the prosecution’s own agents testify as to the truthfulness of other witnesses, the danger of prejudice is especially acute”). The jury was thus presented with a narrative shaped not by neutral evidence, but by the very institutions charged with enforcing the law. This is incompatible with due process. *See United States v. DeCavalcante*, 322 F.2d 500, 504 (2d Cir. 1963) (“A defendant is entitled to a trial before a jury unswayed by the appearance of official bias”).

Appellants also contend that the District Court erred in denying judgment as a matter of law on the basis of the security camera footage. While the photographs do not conclusively prove Appellants’ version, they *do* present a material conflict in the evidence that precludes any reasonable jury from accepting Appellees’ account. *See United States v. Kline*, 327 F.2d 509, 511 (2d Cir. 1964) (“Where there is a direct contradiction between eyewitness testimony and photographic evidence, the jury must be allowed to resolve the conflict”). The images show Cameron standing outside the vehicle, gesturing with her right hand while holding bags behind her back—consistent with her testimony and inconsistent with the claim that she reached into the car. The absence of any visible contact between her hand and the interior of the vehicle renders Appellees’ story implausible as a matter of law. *See United States v. Tocco*, 324 F.2d 501, 503 (2d Cir. 1963) (“If the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable jury could find for the defendant, judgment as a matter of law is proper”).
