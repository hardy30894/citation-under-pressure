The judgment should be affirmed because Missouri’s retirement rule for judges falls outside the ADEA and is fully consistent with equal protection.

First, the ADEA does not clearly reach state judges. When Congress legislates in an area of traditional state authority, it must speak with unmistakable clarity before federal law will be read to displace state policy. See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947); see also Penn Dairies, Inc. v. Milk Control Comm’n of Pennsylvania, 318 U.S. 261, 270-71 (1943); Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). The qualifications, tenure, and retirement of state judges are matters at the core of state sovereignty and the structure of state government. See Collector v. Day, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 113, 127-28 (1871); Coyle v. Smith, 221 U.S. 559, 567-68 (1911). Missouri therefore retains authority to define the tenure of its judiciary unless Congress unmistakably removed that power.

Nothing in the ADEA does so. The statute’s exclusions for elected officials, their personal staff, and appointees on the policymaking level reflect a deliberate choice to keep certain state and local officers outside federal age-discrimination rules. See Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 468-70 (1991); although decided later, it correctly applies the clear-statement principle grounded in earlier cases. Under the pre-1970 authorities, courts should not read a federal statute to upset the internal structure of state government absent direct congressional command. See Rice, 331 U.S. at 230; Hines, 312 U.S. at 67.

Even apart from statutory construction, Missouri’s rule does not violate equal protection. Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement rules are tested for rational basis, not strict scrutiny. See Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464, 466-67 (1948); Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm’rs, 330 U.S. 552, 556-57 (1947). The State need only show a rational relationship between the age line and legitimate governmental interests. See Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co., 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911); McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961); Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc., 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955). Missouri easily meets that standard. It may reasonably conclude that age seventy is a workable proxy for the declining physical and mental stamina that the judicial office demands, that a fixed rule avoids the difficulties of individualized competency hearings, and that it promotes orderly administration of the courts. See Tigner v. Texas, 310 U.S. 141, 147-48 (1940); Lincoln Union v. Northwestern Co., 335 U.S. 525, 536-37 (1949).

The Equal Protection Clause does not require Missouri to adopt the least restrictive or most precise means. A classification is valid if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it. See Lindsley, 220 U.S. at 78-79; McGowan, 366 U.S. at 425-26. Nor must the State wait until individual judges demonstrably fail before acting. Legislatures may draw lines to meet practical administrative needs, even if the line is imperfect in some cases. See Goesaert, 335 U.S. at 466-67; Kotch, 330 U.S. at 556-57.

Here, Missouri acted within that traditional leeway. A fixed retirement age for judges serves legitimate interests in judicial efficiency, public confidence, and regular turnover in a branch of government that depends on the perception of competence and vigor. That policy judgment belongs to the State unless the Constitution or a valid federal statute plainly forbids it. No such prohibition exists. The district court correctly dismissed the ADEA and equal protection claims, and its judgment should be affirmed.
