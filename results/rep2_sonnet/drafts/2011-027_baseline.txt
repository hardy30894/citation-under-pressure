# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EQUAL-FOOTING DOCTRINE REQUIRES A STATEHOOD-TIME NAVIGABILITY DETERMINATION, AND THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THAT TEST.

This Court has long held that title to the beds of navigable waters passes to a State upon its admission to the Union under the equal-footing doctrine, and that the relevant inquiry is whether the waterway was navigable *at the time of statehood*. See *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845); *Martin v. Waddell's Lessee*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 367 (1842). In *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), this Court confirmed that "the question of navigability" for purposes of title "is determined as of the time of statehood," and that "a determination of navigability in fact at the date of statehood" governs title, regardless of the river's present or subsequent characteristics. *Id.* at 10.

The Montana Supreme Court's decision faithfully applied this settled framework. Consistent with *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the court recognized that navigability for title purposes turns on whether the river, "in its natural and ordinary condition," was used or susceptible of use "as highways of commerce" at the time the State entered the Union. *Id.* at 76. This is precisely the historical inquiry Montana's courts undertook: examining evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers' capacity for commercial navigation as of 1889, the date of Montana's statehood.

PPL's contrary suggestion—that navigability should be assessed segment-by-segment based on present-day conditions or without regard to the historical record at statehood—cannot be reconciled with this Court's precedent. In *United States v. Utah*, this Court expressly approved a segment-by-segment analysis of navigability, holding that "different portions of the same river" may be navigable or non-navigable depending on their individual characteristics, but crucially, this segment analysis remains anchored to the statehood-era condition of each stretch. 283 U.S. at 77. The Montana courts did exactly this: they assessed each relevant stretch of river based on evidence of its navigability in 1889, not by reference to modern conditions altered by nearly a century of subsequent development, including PPL's own dams.

## II. THE HISTORICAL EVIDENCE OF STATEHOOD-ERA NAVIGABILITY WAS PROPERLY ADMITTED AND AMPLY SUPPORTS THE DISTRICT COURT'S FINDINGS.

Because navigability for title purposes must be assessed as of 1889, courts necessarily rely on historical records, since, as the Montana Supreme Court correctly observed, "there is no one alive today who personally witnessed the condition of Montana's rivers in 1889." This reality does not render such evidence inadmissible; rather, it triggers well-established hearsay exceptions designed for precisely this circumstance.

This Court has recognized that historical documents bearing on navigability determinations are appropriately considered through recognized evidentiary channels. In *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), this Court relied extensively on historical accounts, government surveys, and testimony regarding river conditions at the relevant statehood period to resolve navigability disputes. *Id.* at 11-20. The reliance on such records is not merely permissible but necessary, given that navigability-for-title determinations by definition require reconstruction of conditions predating the lives of any current witness.

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied Montana's evidentiary rules—analogous to the Federal Rules of Evidence—governing self-authenticating documents, ancient documents, and public records exceptions to hearsay. The historical evidence at issue, including government reports, newspaper accounts, and other public records more than twenty years old and bearing no indicia of unreliability, falls squarely within these traditional exceptions. PPL's objection that no living witness can attest to the *accuracy* of century-old historical records misconstrues the nature of the inquiry: the very premise of navigability-for-title litigation is that courts must reconstruct historical fact through the best available contemporaneous evidence, precisely as this Court did in *Utah v. United States*.

## III. PPL'S PROPOSED FEDERAL-PREEMPTION AND SERVITUDE DEFENSES DO NOT DISPLACE THE STATE'S TITLE TO THE RIVERBEDS.

PPL's attempt to escape compensation obligations by invoking the Federal Power Act and the federal navigational servitude fails for reasons independent of, but reinforcing, the navigability-for-title analysis. This Court held in *Federal Power Commission v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), that the Federal Power Act does not by itself extinguish state property rights absent clear congressional authorization. *Id.* at 249-51. The FPA's dual-regulation structure, described in *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission*, 328 U.S. 152 (1946), preserves state authority over "the beds and banks" of waterways even as it grants federal licensing authority over dam construction and operation. *Id.* at 161 n.6, 175-76.

Nothing in the FPA purports to alter the equal-footing doctrine's allocation of title at statehood. PPL's predecessors built dams atop state-owned riverbeds without acquiring title or paying compensation; the federal license authorizing construction of a hydroeletric facility "neither transfers nor diminishes any right of possession or enjoyment possessed by" the underlying landowner. *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983). The State's ownership of the riverbeds, vested at statehood under the equal-footing doctrine, remains undisturbed by subsequent federal licensing, and Montana's assertion of compensation rights under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is fully consistent with federal law.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
