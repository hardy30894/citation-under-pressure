## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to those who commit fraud. The question presented is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The plain language of this provision indicates that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable. The Court should interpret this provision to bar discharge of debts incurred through fraudulent conduct.

In Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), this Court held that a creditor must prove the elements of § 523(a)(2)(A) by a preponderance of the evidence. The elements of a claim under § 523(a)(2)(A) are: (1) the debtor obtained money, property, or services through a material misrepresentation; (2) the debtor knew the representation was false or made with gross recklessness as to its truth; (3) the debtor intended to deceive the creditor; (4) the creditor reasonably relied on the debtor's false representations; and (5) the creditor sustained a loss and damages as a proximate result of the debtor's materially false representations. See, e.g., In re Poskanzer, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992).

The debt at issue here meets these elements. Cohen's failure to disclose the true rental amounts and his implicit representation that the rents charged were lawful constitute a material misrepresentation. Cohen's reckless disregard for the truth, demonstrated by his failure to investigate the rent control ordinance and his surcharging tenants for tax and water charges, satisfies the knowledge and intent elements. See In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir.1995) (holding that intent to deceive can be inferred from a debtor's reckless disregard for the truth).

The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable. The Eleventh Circuit's decision in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir.1993), is persuasive on this point. The court held that punitive damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are part of the "debt" incurred by the debtor through their fraudulent conduct. Similarly, the treble damages awarded here are a direct result of Cohen's fraudulent conduct and are therefore nondischargeable.

In contrast, the Ninth Circuit's decision in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir.1992), is distinguishable. The court held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not directly related to the debt obtained by fraud. However, this reasoning is not applicable here, where the treble damages are a statutory remedy for Cohen's fraudulent conduct.

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that debts resulting from fraud, including treble damages awarded under state law, are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code.
