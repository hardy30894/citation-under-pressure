The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. When a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, the government must prove that the defendant was still a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. See United States v. Lothian, 976 F.2d 1257, 1261-62 (9th Cir. 1992); United States v. Grimmett, 236 F.3d 452, 456-57 (8th Cir. 2001). The D.C. Circuit's decision affirming the convictions of Calvin Smith and his co-defendants is therefore in line with the principle that the government must prove the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy during the statute of limitations period. See United States v. Moore, 651 F.3d 30, 84-85 (D.C. Cir. 2011).

The petitioners argue that the jury did not have sufficient evidence to prove that all defendants were part of a single conspiracy because the leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the relevant statute of limitations period. However, the government presented evidence that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. The burden remains on the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants were members of the conspiracy during this period. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912).

In a conspiracy case, the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy and that the conspiracy continued into the statute of limitations period. See United States v. Spero, 331 F.3d 57, 60-61 (2d Cir. 2003). If the defendant presents evidence of withdrawal, the government must then prove that the defendant was still a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. See Lothian, 976 F.2d at 1261-62. The D.C. Circuit correctly applied this principle in affirming the convictions.

The petitioners' argument that the government failed to meet its burden is unpersuasive. The government presented sufficient evidence to prove that the conspiracy continued into the statute of limitations period, and the jury was properly instructed on the law of conspiracy and the statute of limitations. See Moore, 651 F.3d at 84-85. The D.C. Circuit's decision should be affirmed because it correctly applied the relevant legal principles.

In conclusion, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly applied this principle in affirming the convictions of Calvin Smith and his co-defendants. The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be affirmed.
