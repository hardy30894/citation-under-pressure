# ARGUMENT

## I. Congress Used "Actual Knowledge" to Reject a Subjective-Recollection Test That Would Defeat the Statute's Purpose

Section 1113(2) commands that suit be barred "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." This Court has long held that "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The Ninth Circuit's construction—requiring proof that a plaintiff subjectively remembers reading and absorbing disclosed information—reads a recollection requirement into the statute that its plain terms do not contain. "Actual" modifies "knowledge" to distinguish it from *constructive* knowledge that Congress deliberately repealed in 1987, not to superimpose a memory test divorced from what a plaintiff was in fact told. Words in a statute take their meaning from the office they perform in context, *Fox v. Standard Oil Co. of N.J.*, 294 U.S. 87, 95 (1935), and here the office of "actual" is to eliminate constructive-notice liability from filed reports—not to immunize a plaintiff who was handed the very documents describing the challenged investments.

## II. Longstanding Equitable Principles Treat Available Means of Knowledge as Knowledge Itself

Long before ERISA, this Court repeatedly held that a plaintiff cannot escape a limitations bar merely by failing to avail himself of information placed before him. In *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 143 (1879), the Court explained that where a plaintiff had access to the facts, "the means of knowledge are the same thing in effect as knowledge itself." The Court applied the same principle in *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874), holding that a limitations period runs once a plaintiff has been afforded a fair opportunity to discover the relevant facts, regardless of whether he in fact appreciated their significance. And in *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435 (1918), this Court again refused to toll a limitations period for a plaintiff who could, through ordinary diligence, have discovered the facts underlying his claim.

This equitable tradition did not disappear with modern statutes of limitations; it informs them. As the Court observed in *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946), equitable doctrines governing accrual and discovery are "read into every federal statute of limitation." That principle counsels against interpreting "actual knowledge" in a manner that rewards a plaintiff's failure to read materials Intel affirmatively placed in his hands—materials disclosing, in plain terms, the very asset allocations he now challenges as imprudent. Sulyma's approach would resurrect, under a different label, the constructive-knowledge standard Congress deliberately excised from § 1113, since virtually no disclosure could ever satisfy it if a plaintiff need only assert he does not "recall" reading it.

## III. A Plaintiff Cannot Manufacture a Limitations Defense Through Willful Avoidance of Disclosed Information

This Court's decisions addressing knowledge requirements elsewhere in federal law confirm that a party may not deliberately shield himself from knowledge and then claim ignorance to his advantage. In *Spurr v. United States*, 174 U.S. 728 (1899), the Court held that a bank officer who certified checks without inquiring into the state of the drawer's account could not escape liability by professing ignorance of facts he had every opportunity to learn; reckless indifference to available information was treated as tantamount to knowledge. Similarly, in construing the knowledge element of "willfulness," this Court in *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), recognized that culpable mental states are properly assessed by reference to the actor's conduct in the face of information available to him, not by his post-hoc protestations of subjective unawareness.

Sulyma's position—that a plaintiff who received Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and website disclosures describing the alternative-investment strategy nonetheless lacks "actual knowledge" because he does not recall reading them—asks this Court to credit precisely the kind of self-serving denial these cases refused to countenance. Under the Ninth Circuit's rule, a fiduciary defendant could disclose every relevant fact with complete transparency, and a plaintiff could still indefinitely postpone the three-year clock simply by asserting a failure of memory. That result cannot be squared with the equitable principles this Court has applied for over a century, nor with the statutory text Congress chose.

## IV. The Statute's Structure Confirms That Disclosure Satisfies the Knowledge Requirement

Congress's 1987 repeal of the constructive-knowledge provision, leaving only "actual knowledge," was designed to shorten—not effectively eliminate—the three-year bar for fiduciaries who make full disclosure. Reading "actual knowledge" to require proof of subjective recollection would convert the three-year period into a nullity in every disclosure-based ERISA case, contrary to the statute's evident design and to this Court's admonition that statutory text be enforced "according to its terms," *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, without engrafting a judge-made recollection requirement that eviscerates its function.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
