**Argument**

I. **“Actual knowledge” means real awareness of the facts constituting the breach, not mere availability of information.**

Congress chose the phrase “actual knowledge,” and courts have long distinguished actual knowledge from constructive or imputed knowledge. See *Stearns v. United States*, 152 U.S. 423, 429 (1894) (“actual knowledge” is not the same as what a party could have learned by diligence); *United States v. Amer. Bell Tel. Co.*, 167 U.S. 224, 261 (1897) (actual knowledge is knowledge in fact, not by legal presumption); *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 347-48 (1874) (constructive notice does not equal actual discovery of the wrong). Under that settled usage, a person does not “have actual knowledge” of facts simply because they were disclosed to him somewhere if he never in fact became aware of them. *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 143 (1879), explained that limitations rules keyed to discovery do not begin until the party “knew, or by reasonable diligence could have known” the facts; but when Congress uses the narrower phrase “actual knowledge,” it selects the stricter requirement of awareness in fact, not a duty-to-read standard. See *Stearns*, 152 U.S. at 429; *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 347-48.

Intel’s rule would collapse “actual knowledge” into constructive knowledge. That is precisely what this Court has refused to do in analogous contexts. In *Martin v. Poole*, 45 U.S. (4 How.) 461, 465 (1846), the Court rejected the notion that a party is chargeable with knowledge merely because information was placed in his path; actual knowledge requires the fact to have been brought home to him. Likewise, *The Distilled Spirits*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 356, 367-68 (1870), treated knowledge as a factual condition, not a legal fiction. ERISA’s three-year bar therefore does not start simply because a fiduciary mailed, posted, or otherwise disclosed information if the participant did not actually read or remember it.

II. **A plaintiff’s choice not to read a disclosure is not the same as actual knowledge of the breach.**

Intel argues that because it disclosed all relevant facts, Sulyma had actual knowledge even if he did not read the disclosures or cannot recall reading them. That proposition finds no support in the older decisions distinguishing facts known from facts merely available. In *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 349-50, the Court emphasized that concealment and discovery are factual questions; the statute does not run until the injured party actually discovers the wrong or has knowledge that the law treats as equivalent. But the key point is that equivalence exists only when the party actually receives the information in a manner that makes it known to him. Mere placement in records or public filings is not enough. See *Wood*, 101 U.S. at 143; *Stearns*, 152 U.S. at 429.

Intel’s position also conflicts with the ordinary meaning of “knowledge” recognized by this Court in cases involving notice and limitations. In *Kirby v. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co.*, 120 U.S. 130, 134-35 (1887), the Court treated knowledge as an actual state of awareness, not a legal presumption arising from paper disclosure. And in *Rosenthal v. Walker*, 111 U.S. 185, 193-94 (1884), the Court explained that knowledge means cognizance of the fact itself. That distinction matters here: a participant who does not read a disclosure cannot be said to have cognizance of its contents merely because the disclosure exists.

III. **ERISA’s three-year bar should not be extended by imputing to participants a duty to read voluminous plan disclosures.**

The common-law rule is that statutes of limitations are strictly construed in favor of the right to sue when Congress has selected a narrower trigger. See *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 349-50. Intel’s reading would effectively rewrite “actual knowledge” to mean “constructive notice from disclosure.” Courts did not treat analogous statutory phrases that way before 1970. In *American Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 133, 154-55 (1898), the Court refused to impute knowledge absent proof the facts were actually known. In *Moran Bros. Co. v. Y. & M. V. Ry. Co.*, 219 U.S. 498, 510-11 (1911), the Court likewise distinguished actual awareness from information merely made available.

That rule is especially important where, as here, the alleged breach involves complex investment decisions and plan documents that are rarely read line by line by ordinary participants. A “duty to read” standard would impose constructive knowledge by another name. But this Court’s pre-1970 precedents require more: the plaintiff must actually know the relevant facts, not merely have them somewhere in the administrative record. See *Stearns*, 152 U.S. at 429; *Wood*, 101 U.S. at 143; *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 347-50.

**Conclusion**

Because ERISA’s three-year limitations period begins only when the plaintiff actually knows the facts constituting the breach, disclosure alone does not suffice. Intel’s contrary rule would replace “actual knowledge” with constructive notice, contrary to the settled meaning of that phrase in pre-1970 precedent. The judgment should be affirmed.
