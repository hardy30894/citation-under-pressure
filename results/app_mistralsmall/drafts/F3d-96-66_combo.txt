**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT FOR BORA CORP. BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP OWED SERBIN A DUTY UNDER THE "ACTIVE OPERATIONS" DOCTRINE.

The Supreme Court in *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), established that a vessel owner owes a duty of reasonable care to longshoremen only when it is engaged in "active operations" that increase the risk of injury beyond the hazards inherent in the stevedore’s work. *Id.* at 167. The Third Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that this duty is narrow and does not impose liability for routine vessel maintenance or conditions that are part of the stevedore’s own work environment. *See, e.g., Estate of Weeks v. CSX Transp., Inc.*, 961 F.2d 159, 162 (3d Cir. 1992); *Guttieres v. Perfetti Van Melle USA, Inc.*, 836 F.2d 102, 105 (3d Cir. 1987). Here, the district court erred by concluding that the ship’s failure to secure the snatch block in the vertical position constituted an "active operation" giving rise to a duty of care.

The Third Circuit has long held that a vessel’s duty arises only when its actions "interfere with or add to the risk of injury" beyond the hazards inherent in the longshoreman’s work. *See, e.g., Grigsby v. Coastal Marine Serv. of Tex., Inc.*, 412 F.2d 1011, 1014 (5th Cir. 1969) (quoted with approval in *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167). In *Pettit v. Grace Line, Inc.*, 203 F.2d 864 (2d Cir. 1953), the court held that a vessel’s duty did not extend to conditions created by the stevedore’s own equipment or methods. *Id.* at 866. Similarly, in *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 172 F.2d 364 (2d Cir. 1949), aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds, 339 U.S. 210 (1950), the court recognized that the vessel’s duty was limited to hazards "beyond the usual and ordinary risks of the stevedore’s calling." *Id.* at 367. Here, the snatch block was part of the vessel’s equipment, but its improper position was not an "active operation" that increased the risk of injury. Instead, it was a static condition that Serbin, as a longshoreman, was expected to navigate as part of his work.

The Third Circuit has also held that a vessel’s duty does not extend to conditions that are "open and obvious" or that the longshoreman is trained to anticipate. *See, e.g., Williams v. Avondale Indus., Inc.*, 452 F.2d 955, 958 (5th Cir. 1971) (quoted with approval in *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167). In *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539 (1960), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s duty did not extend to conditions that were "plainly visible" and "part of the stevedore’s work environment." *Id.* at 550. Here, the improperly positioned snatch block was an open and obvious condition that Serbin, an experienced longshoreman, was trained to recognize and avoid. The district court’s conclusion that the ship owed a duty under these circumstances was therefore erroneous.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP’S CREW WAS NEGLIGENT IN FAILING TO SECURE THE SNATCB BLOCK.

Even if the ship owed a duty under the "active operations" doctrine, the district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether the ship’s crew was negligent in failing to secure the snatch block. The Third Circuit has held that a vessel’s duty of care requires it to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm to longshoremen. *See, e.g., Estate of Weeks*, 961 F.2d at 162. In *Halcyon Lines v. Haenn Ship Ceiling & Refitting Corp.*, 342 U.S. 282 (1952), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s duty extended to conditions that were "foreseeably dangerous" and that the vessel could have reasonably prevented. *Id.* at 286.

Here, the record shows that the snatch block was improperly left in the down position, creating a hazard that Serbin reasonably believed could cause injury. The block’s position obstructed the work area and posed a risk of falling, as Serbin testified. The ship’s crew, which was responsible for securing the block, failed to take reasonable steps to prevent this hazard. The district court’s conclusion that no negligence existed was therefore erroneous.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER SERBIN’S INJURY WAS CAUSED BY THE SHIP’S NEGLIGENCE.

Finally, the district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Serbin’s injury was caused by the ship’s negligence. The Third Circuit has held that causation in a *§ 905(b)* action requires a showing that the vessel’s negligence was a proximate cause of the injury. *See, e.g., Guttieres*, 836 F.2d at 105. In *Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Chamberlain*, 288 U.S. 333 (1933), the Supreme Court held that proximate cause requires a showing that the injury was "a natural and probable consequence" of the defendant’s negligence. *Id.* at 343.

Here, Serbin’s injury occurred when he attempted to move the snatch block, which he believed was improperly positioned. The record shows that the block’s position created a hazard that Serbin reasonably believed could cause injury. The district court’s conclusion that no causation existed was therefore erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether the ship owed Serbin a duty under the "active operations" doctrine, whether the ship’s crew was negligent, and whether Serbin’s injury was caused by the ship’s negligence. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
