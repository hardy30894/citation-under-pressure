**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal Fee-Shifting Statutes Are Designed to Secure Fees That Reflect the True Value of Counsel's Work, Not Merely to Reimburse Billed Hours.**

Congress enacted fee-shifting provisions such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988 to ensure that private litigants could vindicate important federal rights. As this Court explained of an analogous fee provision, a prevailing plaintiff acts "not for himself alone but also as a 'private attorney general,' vindicating a policy that Congress considered of the highest priority," and "one who succeeds in obtaining an injunction under that Title should ordinarily recover an attorney's fee unless special circumstances would render such an award unjust." *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968). Congress's purpose "to encourage individuals injured" to bring suit, *id.*, is served, not undermined, when a court may recognize—through a modest enhancement—that counsel's performance and the results achieved exceeded what the hourly rate alone was designed to capture. A rule that forecloses any recognition of extraordinary service would blunt the very incentive Congress created.

**II. Courts Have Long Possessed Discretion to Award Fees Commensurate with the Value and Quality of the Services Actually Rendered.**

The determination of a reasonable fee has never been a mechanical exercise divorced from the quality of the work performed. This Court has repeatedly confirmed that the allowance of counsel fees is "a matter within the sound discretion of the court before whom the cause was tried." *United States v. Waters*, 133 U.S. 208, 213 (1890). That discretion is not limited to arithmetic; it extends to judging the worth of what was actually delivered to the client and to the public. Congress itself has directed courts, in fee-shifting contexts, to award "such reasonable counsel and attorney fees … as the court shall determine is just and reasonable, as compensation for the services rendered." *Bachman v. Lawson*, 109 U.S. 659, 660 (1884). The touchstone is the *service rendered*, not a bare hourly figure. Similarly, in reviewing discretionary fee allowances, this Court has treated the propriety of an award as bound up with the particular circumstances of the litigation and the effort expended, rather than any rigid formula. *Hilborn v. United States*, 163 U.S. 342, 343–45 (1896). And where a statute directs that a fee be taxed as part of a judgment, this Court has cautioned against reducing that statutory entitlement to a mere formalism; the "mere declaration of the state statute could not alter the true nature of the obligation" to compensate for what was actually earned. *Missouri State Life Insurance Co. v. Jones*, 290 U.S. 199, 202 (1933). These decisions reflect a consistent principle: reasonableness is a substantive inquiry into the value of representation, of which quality and results are integral components—not a categorical exclusion of those factors.

**III. A Standard Rate Calculation Is a Floor, Not a Ceiling, on Fair Compensation for Extraordinary Performance.**

This Court's compensation jurisprudence confirms that a fixed rate formula does not preclude additional payment reflecting value beyond the ordinary expectation. In construing wage agreements that calculated compensation through a standard formula, the Court recognized that a bonus could properly function as "an appropriate increase in the guaranteed overtime compensation, as well as an increase in the guaranteed, regular hourly rate of pay." *Walling v. Harnischfeger Corp.*, 325 U.S. 427, 430 (1945). Likewise, in *149 Madison Avenue Corp. v. Asselta*, 331 U.S. 199, 204 (1947), the Court accepted that parties could adopt "a formula whereby such a fixed hourly rate could be calculated," while still permitting additional payments layered atop that baseline. The lodestar is no different in kind: it establishes a presumptively reasonable baseline, but nothing in that methodology forbids a modest enhancement when the record demonstrates that the hourly rate, however carefully calculated, did not capture the true value of what was performed.

**IV. Just Compensation Principles Confirm That an Award Must Reflect the Actual Value of What Was Provided.**

This Court's just-compensation cases likewise instruct that compensation must track real value rather than an artificial ceiling. In *United States v. Cors*, 337 U.S. 325, 332–34 (1949), the Court rejected an approach that would have deducted value attributable to the very circumstances that made the property valuable, insisting instead that compensation correspond to true market value. So too here: an hourly rate untethered from the demonstrated, exceptional value of counsel's work is not a "reasonable" fee in any meaningful sense. Because attorneys are already bound to "exercise reasonable care and skill in the performance of the … undertaking," *Savings Bank v. Ward*, 100 U.S. 195, 198 (1880), a fee award that never permits recognition of skill markedly exceeding that baseline effectively treats exceptional and merely adequate representation identically—a result inconsistent with the statute's purpose of enabling effective representation. Indeed, this Court has recognized that the presence or absence of "effective representation" bears directly on what a fee award should reflect. *Hopkins v. Cohen*, 390 U.S. 530, 535 (1968).

**CONCLUSION**

The district court, exercising the discretion this Court's precedents confirm it possesses, found on a developed record that counsel's performance and the results obtained were extraordinary and not captured by the lodestar. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed. This Court should do the same.
