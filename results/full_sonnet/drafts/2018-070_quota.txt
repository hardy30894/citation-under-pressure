# ARGUMENT

## I. OCSLA's Text and History Confirm That State Law Applies on the Shelf Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law

Congress enacted OCSLA to ensure that a complete body of law would govern the outer Continental Shelf, an area lying beyond the territorial jurisdiction of any State. *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep't of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988). Congress considered but rejected extending admiralty law wholesale to the Shelf, and instead declared federal law to apply "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law was borrowed only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with" federal law, and only *as* federal law. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355–57 (1969). This structure mirrors the treatment of federal enclaves, where state law is adopted derivatively, to fill interstices Congress left open, not as an independent source of regulation. *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293–94 (1958).

*Rodrigue* confirms this gap-filling function. There, state wrongful-death claims were permitted only because the Death on the High Seas Act supplied "no remedy" for deaths on fixed platforms, since such platforms are treated as "islands," not vessels, and the accidents did not involve "traditional maritime activity." *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 360, 366. The Court incorporated Louisiana law precisely because federal law left a gap. *Id.* at 355. Had a federal remedy existed, OCSLA's borrowing provision would have had no work to do.

*Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), reinforces this understanding, explaining that OCSLA's purpose was to define a body of law for Shelf installations by borrowing state law only where federal law does not already govern. *Id.* at 480–81. Likewise, in *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court treated Louisiana's limitations statute as federal law under § 1333(a)(2) only because no analogous federal limitations period existed for the claim at issue. *Id.* at 99–101. Nowhere has this Court held that state law displaces, or operates alongside, a complete federal statutory scheme merely because it is not technically inconsistent with that scheme.

## II. The "Significant Void or Gap" Test, Not the Ninth Circuit's "Applicable and Not Inconsistent" Gloss, Properly Implements OCSLA

The Fifth Circuit—which decides the great majority of OCSLA cases—has long held that state law is adopted only to fill "a significant void or gap" in federal law's coverage. *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). Under that test, courts ask first whether federal law already speaks comprehensively to the subject; state law is borrowed only if it does not. *Id.* Although *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), addressed a different context—maritime contract claims where the threshold question was whether maritime law applied "of its own force"—it did not abandon the gap-filling premise; where maritime law occupies the field, there is no gap for state law to fill, and where it does not, state law may be borrowed. *Id.* at 1047. Both decisions proceed from the same principle: OCSLA's borrowing clause presupposes an *absence* of governing federal law, not a mere absence of conflict.

## III. Treating the FLSA as Leaving Room for State Law Whenever the Two Are Not Inconsistent Contradicts the Comprehensive Character of the Federal Wage Scheme

Congress designed the FLSA as a comprehensive charter for minimum wage and overtime obligations, including its own savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), identifying precisely when more protective state law may operate concurrently. Where Congress has legislated so comprehensively, courts do not layer additional state regulation onto the scheme merely because it is not technically inconsistent. Cf. *Gade v. Nat'l Solid Wastes Mgmt. Ass'n*, 505 U.S. 88, 98 (1992) (state law is displaced when it stands as an obstacle to a comprehensive federal scheme, even absent direct conflict). Because the FLSA already contains its own mechanism for accommodating state protections, treating OCSLA as an independent gateway for California's separate meal-and-rest-break regime—which the FLSA's savings clause does not itself recognize in this context—would circumvent Congress's calibrated design. This Court has cautioned against permitting state law to operate as a freestanding regulatory overlay on subjects Congress has already occupied through detailed federal legislation and agency regulation. *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 295–96 (1979) (agency regulations promulgated under comprehensive federal statutes carry "the force and effect of law" sufficient to displace state law); see also *Wyeth v. Levine*, 555 U.S. 555, 576 (2009).

The Ninth Circuit's "applicable and not inconsistent" test collapses OCSLA's gap-filling design into an ordinary preemption inquiry, converting every fixed Shelf platform into a satellite of the adjacent State's entire regulatory code so long as no direct conflict exists. That approach cannot be squared with *Rodrigue*, *Sharpnack*, *Continental Oil*, or the structure of the FLSA. This Court should hold that OCSLA borrows state law only to fill genuine gaps in federal coverage, and should reverse the judgment below.
