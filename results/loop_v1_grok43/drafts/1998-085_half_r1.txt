**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct that is egregious or outrageous, independent of the employer's state of mind. This standard derives from the common-law rule that exemplary awards punish only aggravated wrongdoing beyond the mental element of the underlying tort.

In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 558 (1818), the Court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant's conduct has been characterized by aggravating circumstances. The same principle appears in Wilkes v. Dinsman, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 89, 130 (1849), which required a degree of negligence or misconduct evincing reckless disregard amounting to an aggravated wrong. Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), reiterated that the jury may award exemplary damages only upon proof of gross negligence or willful misconduct that is morally culpable.

Later decisions confirmed that malice or reckless indifference alone does not suffice. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), stated that punitive damages are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 563 (1886), likewise required willful misconduct or gross negligence indicating a conscious indifference to consequences. The requirement of independent outrageousness also appears in The Schooner Palmyra, 25 U.S. (12 Wheat.) 1, 15 (1827), where the Court explained that punitive damages serve to punish the wrongdoer for an aggravated wrong. Similarly, in The Marianna Flora, 24 U.S. (11 Wheat.) 1, 54 (1826), the Court emphasized that the conduct must be oppressive or outrageous beyond the elements establishing liability. Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163, 176 (1873), likewise confirms that exemplary awards rest on proof of aggravated misconduct.

Because the 1991 amendments to Title VII incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
