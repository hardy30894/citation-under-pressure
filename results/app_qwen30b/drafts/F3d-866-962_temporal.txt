**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the retaliation claims of Rita Foster, Brian Kline, and Michael Snyder under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The plaintiffs’ protected activity—reporting unsafe working conditions at the Creston bridge—was a substantial factor in BNSF’s adverse employment actions, including disciplinary measures and increased scrutiny. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the plaintiffs, establishes a triable issue of fact as to whether BNSF retaliated against them for engaging in protected activity, in violation of the FRSA.

Under the FRSA, it is unlawful for a railroad employer to “discharge, demote, or in any other manner discriminate against any employee” who “has reported or is about to report a violation of a safety regulation or a condition that presents a substantial and immediate danger to the public or to employees.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The statute’s purpose is to “encourage employees to report safety hazards without fear of reprisal,” and courts have long recognized that retaliation may take forms beyond outright discharge. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 372 (1948) (noting that “the purpose of the statute is to protect the employee who reports violations from employer retaliation”).

Here, the plaintiffs engaged in protected activity when they reported the dangerous conditions at the bridge—specifically, the absence of handrails, lighting, and a walkway—during their statements to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy. Kline and Snyder explicitly noted the unsafe conditions in their written statements. Foster, in her recorded statement to Murphy, referenced prior employee reports to BNSF about the bridge’s danger. These statements were made in the context of a safety incident involving a fellow employee’s fall and were directly related to the operation of the train and the safety of crewmembers. The fact that the reports were made to company officials in the course of an internal investigation does not diminish their protected status. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372.

The timing of BNSF’s actions following these reports is highly suggestive of retaliation. Within days of the plaintiffs’ disclosures, BNSF initiated an investigation into possible rule infractions by the crew. While the investigation was ostensibly neutral, the record reveals that the plaintiffs were subjected to increased operations testing—defined by the district court as foremen observing employees’ work without their knowledge—while the hearing was pending. This surveillance, which created a climate of fear and compelled employees to avoid working with less experienced crewmates, constitutes a form of adverse employment action. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372 (holding that “any action which tends to discourage employees from reporting violations” is actionable under the statute).

Moreover, the disciplinary outcomes were not uniformly applied. While all crewmembers were subject to the same investigation, the plaintiffs—those who reported the unsafe conditions—received more severe discipline. Foster, who was already on a three-year review period, was terminated. Kline received a thirty-day record suspension and a one-year review period, while Snyder received a formal reprimand and the same probationary period. The Public Law Board later reversed these decisions, reinstating Foster and removing the reprimand and suspension from Snyder and Kline’s records. This reversal by an independent arbitration panel under the Railway Labor Act, 45 U.S.C. § 153, strongly suggests that the original discipline was not based on objective rule violations but on the plaintiffs’ reporting conduct. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372 (noting that “the employer’s motive in taking adverse action is central to the inquiry”).

BNSF’s argument that the discipline was based on legitimate safety violations is undermined by the fact that the same conduct—failing to blow the whistle, turning off the headlight, and fouling the tracks—was not uniformly punished. The fact that the investigation was conducted by individuals—Dixon and Knutstrom—who had previously testified that the crew was instructed to change crews east of the bridge, while the plaintiffs testified they were instructed to do so west of the bridge, raises serious questions about the impartiality of the process. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372 (holding that “the employer’s motive in taking adverse action is central to the inquiry”).

The district court erred in concluding that no reasonable jury could find that the plaintiffs’ protected activity was a substantial factor in BNSF’s adverse actions. The evidence of timing, differential treatment, and the reversal of discipline by an independent panel creates a genuine issue of material fact. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372 (holding that “the purpose of the statute is to protect the employee who reports violations from employer retaliation”).

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court must be reversed, and the case remanded for trial on the retaliation claims under the FRSA.
