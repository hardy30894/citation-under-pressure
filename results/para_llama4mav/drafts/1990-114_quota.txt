ARGUMENT

I. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement for State Court Judges Does Not Violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)

The ADEA prohibits employers from discriminating against employees on the basis of age, but it exempts certain individuals, including "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question presented is whether Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption.

The Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" and therefore are excluded from the ADEA's protection. As the First Circuit Court of Appeals observed in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), "policymaking" is a part of the function of judging, as judges create law by filling the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. See also EEOC v. Massachusetts, 680 F.Supp. 455, 462 (D.Mass. 1988). The Missouri Supreme Court's decisions, for example, establish common law for the entire state, Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and sometimes involve close and difficult questions of law with far-reaching implications, Cruzan v. Harmon, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc).

Moreover, Missouri's appointed judges exercise considerable policymaking responsibility in the supervision of the Missouri court system. The Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, the Chief Judges of the various divisions of the court of appeals, and the Presiding Judges in the circuit courts have general supervisory powers, Mo. Const. art. V, § 4, and the Missouri Supreme Court is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar.

The legislative history of the ADEA's exemptions supports the conclusion that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." The Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180, indicates that the exemption is intended to apply to individuals with policymaking responsibilities at the highest levels of state or local government. See also United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (requiring clear statement of congressional intent to preempt state law in traditionally sensitive areas).

II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement for State Court Judges Does Not Violate the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause

The Equal Protection Clause requires that state laws be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is rationally related to several legitimate government interests, including the need to ensure that judges are physically and mentally capable of performing their duties, Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), and the desire to promote a representative judiciary by allowing for the appointment of younger, more diverse judges, Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The Supreme Court has consistently held that age is not a suspect classification, Murgia, 427 U.S. at 313-314, and that mandatory retirement provisions are constitutional as long as they are rationally related to a legitimate government interest, Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979). See also San Antonio School Dist. v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973) (holding that the right to an education is not a fundamental right guaranteed by the Constitution). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges meets this standard, as it is designed to ensure the integrity and effectiveness of the judiciary.

The rational basis for Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement is further supported by the fact that it eliminates the need for individualized determinations of a judge's fitness to serve, Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), and reduces the risk of delays in the administration of justice caused by the death or disabling illness of sitting judges, Trafelet, 594 F.2d at 627, 629.

In conclusion, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges does not violate the ADEA or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. The Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" and therefore are excluded from the ADEA's protection, and that the mandatory retirement requirement is rationally related to legitimate government interests. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals.
