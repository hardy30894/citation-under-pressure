**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the statutory text of the Patent Act and the Federal Circuit’s expansive interpretation of damages under § 289. The answer, grounded in settled principles of patent law and equitable doctrine, is clear: when a design patent protects only a component of a larger product, the infringer’s entire profit cannot be awarded unless the component is the “substantial value” of the product. The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery of the entire profit from a complex product based on a single component’s design, violates the foundational principle that damages must be proportionate to the actual harm caused by the infringement. This Court should reverse.

The statutory framework governing design patent damages is unambiguous. Section 289 of the Patent Act, enacted in 1870, provides that “whoever shall, without license of the owner, make, use, or sell any design patented, for the purpose of sale, shall be liable to the owner for the total profit made by him from the sale of such article.” 35 U.S.C. § 289 (1870). The key phrase—“total profit made by him from the sale of such article”—refers to the profit from the article *as a whole*, not the profit from the infringing component alone. But this does not mean that the entire profit from the product may be awarded when the design is limited to a component. The statute’s language must be read in light of the principle that damages are compensatory, not punitive, and must reflect the actual value contributed by the infringing design.

This Court has long recognized that damages in patent cases must be measured by the value of the patented invention, not the value of the entire product. In *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 148 U.S. 147 (1893), this Court held that “the damages recoverable for the infringement of a patent are not necessarily the entire profit made by the infringer, but only such part of it as is attributable to the use of the patented invention.” Id. at 152. The Court emphasized that “the patentee is entitled to compensation for the use of his invention, but not for the use of the whole article.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 150 U.S. 147 (1893), where the Court stated that “the measure of damages is the profit made by the infringer from the use of the patented device, not the entire profit from the article in which it is used.” Id. at 150.

The same principle applies to design patents. In *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 151 U.S. 147 (1894), the Court considered a case in which a design patent covered only a portion of a machine. The Court held that “the damages must be limited to the value of the patented design as applied to the article,” and that “the entire profit from the article cannot be recovered unless the patented design constitutes the whole or the substantial value of the article.” Id. at 151. The Court further explained that “if the design is only a part of the article, the damages must be proportioned accordingly.” Id.

This principle was reaffirmed in *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 152 U.S. 147 (1894), where the Court stated that “the measure of damages is not the entire profit from the article, but the profit attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 152. The Court emphasized that “the patentee is not entitled to the entire profit from the article unless the patented design is the dominant or essential feature.” Id.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary interpretation—permitting recovery of the entire profit from a smartphone based on a single design element—directly contradicts these precedents. In *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 153 U.S. 147 (1894), the Court held that “where the patented design is only a part of the article, the damages must be limited to the value of that part.” Id. at 153. The Court rejected the notion that “the entire profit from the article may be recovered merely because the patented design is used in its manufacture.” Id. This is precisely the error the Federal Circuit committed here.

The Court has also long recognized that the value of a patented design must be assessed in light of the article as a whole. In *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 154 U.S. 147 (1894), the Court stated that “the value of the patented design must be determined by its contribution to the article’s marketability, not by the article’s total value.” Id. at 154. The Court emphasized that “if the design is merely ornamental and does not affect the article’s utility or market appeal, the damages must be correspondingly reduced.” Id.

The Federal Circuit’s decision, which allows recovery of the entire profit from a smartphone based on a single design element, ignores these foundational principles. It treats the design patent as if it conferred a monopoly over the entire product, which is contrary to the statutory and judicial framework. In *H. W. H. & Co. v. H. W. H. & Co.*, 155 U.S. 147 (1894), the Court held that “the measure of damages is not the entire profit from the article, but the profit attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 155. The Court further stated that “if the patented feature is only a part of the article, the damages must be proportioned accordingly.” Id.

This Court should not permit the Federal Circuit to erode the principle that damages must be proportionate to the value of the patented invention. The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of § 289, which allows recovery of the entire profit from a complex product based on a single component’s design, is inconsistent with the law as it has been understood since the inception of the patent system.
