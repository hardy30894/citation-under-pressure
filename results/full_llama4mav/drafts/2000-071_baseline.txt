The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a constitutional restriction on speech in a limited public forum. The policy is reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and is viewpoint-neutral.

The Court of Appeals correctly held that the subject matter of the Club's activities was "quintessentially religious" and thus fell outside the scope of the limited public forum created by Milford. The Club's activities, which included singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, were not merely the teaching of morals from a religious perspective, but rather were the equivalent of religious instruction and worship. See Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000).

The Supreme Court has recognized that a religious perspective can constitute a separate viewpoint on a wide variety of seemingly secular subject matter. See Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 831 (1995). However, this does not mean that speech that is "quintessentially religious" is entitled to the same protection as speech that merely expresses a religious viewpoint on a secular subject. The Court has distinguished between speech that is "religious in nature" and speech that is "directed at a religious audience" or "conducts a religious worship service." See Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981).

In this case, the Club's activities were not merely the expression of a religious viewpoint on a secular subject, but rather were a form of religious instruction and worship. The school's exclusion of the Club from its facilities was therefore not viewpoint discrimination, but rather subject matter discrimination, which is permissible in a limited public forum. See Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 49 (1983).

Furthermore, permitting the Club to use the school's facilities would have raised Establishment Clause concerns. The school's community use policy is designed to avoid the appearance of endorsing or promoting religion, and excluding the Club from its facilities is a reasonable means of achieving this goal. See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993).

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a constitutional restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the exclusion of the Club was a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral application of that policy.
