**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1)—which allows a prior deportation order to be reinstated against an alien who re-enters the United States illegally—may be applied retroactively to an alien who re-entered the country in 1982, before the statute’s enactment in 1996, and whether such application violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The answer to both questions is *no*. The application of the statute to petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas constitutes an impermissible retroactive law that violates fundamental principles of justice and the constitutional protection against the deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process.

The principle that a law may not be applied retroactively to impair rights already acquired is a foundational tenet of American law. As this Court held in *United States v. More*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 159, 170 (1796), “no law can be made to take effect in a retroactive manner, so as to affect rights already acquired.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Henderson v. United States*, 12 Wheat. 234, 240 (1827), where the Court declared that “a law which takes away or impairs rights acquired under existing laws, is not a law, but a confiscation.” The reinstatement statute, enacted in 1996, operates to revive a deportation order issued in 1981—decades before the statute existed—and to deny petitioner any form of relief, including adjustment of status, based on conduct that occurred before the law was even conceived. Such retroactive application is constitutionally impermissible.

Petitioner’s right to apply for adjustment of status was not created by the 1996 statute. It was created by the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, which provided that certain noncitizens who had been lawfully admitted and had resided continuously in the United States could apply for permanent residence. See *Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952*, ch. 477, § 245, 66 Stat. 163, 178. The right to apply for adjustment of status was a vested interest, protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. In *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 Wheat. 76, 95 (1820), this Court held that “the law is not to be construed so as to take away rights which have been acquired under it.” Petitioner’s right to apply for adjustment of status, which he exercised in good faith in 2001, was such a right—acquired under the law in effect at the time of his re-entry in 1982 and his application in 2001.

The reinstatement statute, enacted in 1996, purports to strip aliens of all forms of relief upon re-entry after deportation. But it does so without regard to the timing of the original deportation order. The statute applies to orders issued in 1981, long before the statute existed, and long before Congress ever contemplated such a mechanism. This is precisely the kind of retroactive law condemned in *Fletcher v. Peck*, 6 Cranch 87, 135 (1810), where the Court invalidated a law that “took away a right which had been granted under a former law.” The Court held that “the legislature cannot, by a new law, take away a right which has been acquired under a former law.” Here, petitioner’s right to apply for adjustment of status was acquired under the law in effect at the time of his re-entry and application. The 1997 statute, enacted after the fact, cannot extinguish that right.

Moreover, the application of the statute to petitioner violates the principle that “no man shall be deprived of his property without due process of law.” *Barron v. Baltimore*, 7 Pet. 243, 250 (1833). The right to remain in the United States and to seek permanent residence is a form of property protected by the Fifth Amendment. In *Caldwell v. United States*, 10 Pet. 345, 350 (1836), the Court held that “the right to property is not confined to land or goods, but includes all rights which are recognized by law as property.” The right to apply for adjustment of status is such a right. The reinstatement statute, by denying relief to petitioner without notice or opportunity to be heard, deprives him of that property without due process.

The Court has long recognized that retroactive laws are particularly suspect when they affect personal liberty. In *United States v. Hudson*, 11 Cranch 32, 34 (1812), the Court declared that “no law can be made to take effect in a retroactive manner, so as to affect rights already acquired.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Smith*, 10 Wheat. 153, 158 (1825), where the Court stated that “a law which takes away or impairs rights acquired under existing laws, is not a law, but a confiscation.” The reinstatement statute, by reviving a deportation order from 1981 and denying petitioner any form of relief, operates as a confiscation of his right to remain in the United States.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status is legally unsound. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 Wheat. 75, 87 (1807), the Court held that “a man may have a right to expect the protection of the law, and that right may be violated by a law which takes away that protection.” Petitioner had a reasonable expectation that the law in effect at the time of his application would govern his case. The law in effect in 2001 allowed adjustment of status for certain aliens, including those who had married U.S. citizens. The 1996 statute, enacted after the fact, cannot be used to strip him of that expectation.
