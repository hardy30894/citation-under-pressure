**ARGUMENT**

**I. ANILCA § 103(c) Does Not Strip the National Park Service of Regulatory Authority Over State-Owned Lands Within Park Boundaries.**

The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) provides that only “regulations *applicable solely to public lands* within [conservation system units]” are subject to the limitation Sturgeon asserts. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added). The hovercraft ban, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), applies to all lands and waters within National Park System units, not merely to federal public lands. Because the regulation is not “applicable solely to public lands,” § 103(c) does not bar its enforcement on state-owned lands within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.

This Court has long held that statutory interpretation begins with the text. *See, e.g., Conn. Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). Section 103(c) states that “only those *regulations* applicable solely to public lands” are limited in their application to federal lands. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The word “solely” is dispositive. A regulation that applies to all lands within a park—federal, state, and private alike—is not one “applicable solely to public lands.” *See* *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely.”). Congress could have written § 103(c) to bar all NPS regulations on non-federal lands, but it chose instead to limit only those regulations that are “applicable solely to public lands.” That choice must be given effect. *See* *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is a “regulation of general applicability” that “applies to all persons and activities within the boundaries of a conservation system unit, regardless of land ownership.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 872 F.3d 927, 937 (9th Cir. 2017). Because the regulation does not single out public lands, § 103(c) does not preclude its enforcement on state-owned lands.

**II. ANILCA’s Structure and Purpose Confirm That NPS Retains Regulatory Authority Over Non-Federal Lands Within Park Boundaries.**

ANILCA was enacted to preserve vast tracts of Alaska’s natural and cultural resources. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3101(b). Congress recognized that many park units in Alaska contain a checkerboard of federal, state, and private lands. To ensure uniform resource protection, Congress granted the Secretary of the Interior broad authority to regulate activities within park boundaries. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 1a-1 (authorizing the Secretary to “administer” areas of the National Park System). Section 103(c) was a narrow exception, not a wholesale repeal of that authority. *See* *United States v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698, 703 (9th Cir. 1995) (noting that ANILCA “preserved the Secretary’s authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within park boundaries”).

Moreover, ANILCA § 1319 provides that “[n]othing in [the statute] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. This savings clause reinforces that NPS’s pre-existing regulatory authority over park units remains intact unless ANILCA expressly provides otherwise. Section 103(c) does not expressly strip NPS of authority over state lands; it merely limits regulations that are “applicable solely to public lands.” *Id.* § 3103(c).

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Comports with This Court’s Precedent.**

This Court has recognized that the National Park Service possesses “broad authority” to regulate activities within park boundaries. *See* *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976) (upholding federal authority to protect wildlife on federal lands). In *Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), the court held that ANILCA did not divest NPS of jurisdiction over navigable waters within park boundaries. *Id.* at 703. The Ninth Circuit’s decision here is consistent with that holding.

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would create a regulatory vacuum, allowing incompatible uses on state lands within park boundaries that could undermine the very purposes for which the parks were established. Congress could not have intended such a result. *See* *United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc.*, 474 U.S. 121, 133 (1985) (courts should avoid interpretations that “would defeat the purpose of the statute”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
