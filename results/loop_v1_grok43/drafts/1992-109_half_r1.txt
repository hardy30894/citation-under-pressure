The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when determining a defendant's competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. Instead, the same standard that governs competency to stand trial suffices. See Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam) (defendant must have "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him).

This Court has long treated the competency inquiry as unitary. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that due process requires a hearing whenever the evidence raises a bona fide doubt about the defendant's capacity to participate in the proceedings, without distinguishing among the stages of trial, plea, or waiver. Id. at 385. The same principle appears in Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), where the Court remanded for a competency determination in the context of self-representation, again applying the Dusky formulation without elaboration or elevation.

Waiver of counsel has always been measured by the same baseline. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), required that any relinquishment be "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," id. at 464, but anchored that inquiry in the defendant's overall capacity to understand the proceedings. Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), and Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), reaffirmed that the validity of a waiver turns on whether the defendant possesses "the mental capacity to make an understanding and intelligent choice," Adams, 317 U.S. at 275, without imposing a separate, more demanding test. Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise examined the defendant's comprehension of the charges and consequences under the ordinary standard of rational understanding. Id. at 724.

Guilty pleas receive identical treatment. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show the plea was voluntary and intelligent, yet the Court derived that requirement from the same due-process foundation that protects the right to trial itself. Id. at 242. No opinion of this Court has ever superimposed a distinct "reasoned choice" gloss on these precedents.

The Ninth Circuit's contrary rule cannot be reconciled with the uniform standard established in these decisions. Because the record here contains psychiatric evaluations finding Moran competent under Dusky, the state courts properly accepted his plea and waiver. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
