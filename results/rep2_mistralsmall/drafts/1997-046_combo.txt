**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN WHICH WOODARD HAS A DUE PROCESS RIGHT.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Woodard lacks a constitutionally protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings. This Court has long recognized that clemency is an integral part of the criminal justice system, and states may not deprive individuals of liberty without due process. *See Ex parte Grossman*, 277 U.S. 87, 100 (1928) ("The power of the President under the Constitution to pardon offenses against the United States is unlimited except in cases of impeachment."); *cf. United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833) ("A pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws.").

The Court has also held that where a state creates a right to a hearing or review, due process protections attach. *See Londoner v. City & County of Denver*, 210 U.S. 373, 385 (1908) ("Where the legislature has authorized a hearing, it is not an empty form."). Ohio’s clemency process, as outlined in its regulations, provides a structured review mechanism that directly impacts a prisoner’s liberty interest. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 118 F.3d 1198, 1203 (6th Cir. 1997) (discussing Ohio’s clemency procedures). Thus, Woodard has a protected liberty interest in the clemency process, and due process requires that the process not be arbitrary or fundamentally unfair. *See Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 570 (1972) ("The Fourteenth Amendment’s procedural protection of liberty and property is a safeguard against arbitrary or wrongful government deprivation.").

**II. WOODARD’S FIFTH AMENDMENT RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION IS VIOLATED BY OHIO’S CLEMENCY INTERVIEW REQUIREMENT.**

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that Woodard’s Fifth Amendment rights are not implicated is erroneous. The Fifth Amendment provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. This right applies not only in formal criminal proceedings but also in any context where a person’s statements may later be used against him. *See Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) ("The object of the constitutional protection is to prevent the extortion from the witness of evidence which may be used against him in a criminal prosecution.").

Ohio’s clemency process, by requiring an interview as part of the review, creates a direct compulsion to speak. The regulation states that any statement made during the interview "shall be considered at the clemency hearing." Ohio Death Penalty Clemency Procedure § IV.C.2. This compels Woodard to either waive his Fifth Amendment rights or forego the opportunity to present his case for clemency. Such a "Hobson’s choice" violates the Fifth Amendment’s prohibition against compelled self-incrimination. *See Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493, 497 (1967) ("The choice given to the defendant was either to forfeit his job or to incriminate himself.").

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), is misplaced. *Baxter* did not address clemency proceedings, nor did it involve a situation where participation in a state-created process was compelled. *Id.* at 317-18. Here, Ohio’s clemency process is structured such that Woodard must either participate in an interview—risking self-incrimination—or forgo the opportunity to present mitigating evidence. This is precisely the type of compulsion the Fifth Amendment forbids. *See Lefkowitz v. Turley*, 414 U.S. 70, 78 (1973) ("The Fifth Amendment’s privilege against self-incrimination protects witnesses in state as well as federal proceedings.").

**III. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS MUST COMPLY WITH DUE PROCESS REQUIREMENTS.**

Due process requires that clemency proceedings not be arbitrary or fundamentally unfair. *See United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 447 (1971) ("The sentencing judge [must] consider all relevant mitigating evidence."). Ohio’s process, by compelling Woodard to choose between self-incrimination and forgoing clemency, is fundamentally unfair. The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that Woodard’s risk of self-incrimination is "purely academic" ignores the coercive nature of the process. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 118 F.3d at 1206 (Nelson, J., dissenting).

This Court has long held that due process requires fair procedures in contexts where liberty interests are at stake. *See Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath*, 341 U.S. 123, 162 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) ("The requirements of due process in any situation vary with the circumstances."). Ohio’s clemency process, by conditioning participation on waiver of Fifth Amendment rights, fails this standard.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s decision and hold that Woodard has a protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings and that Ohio’s clemency process violates his Fifth Amendment rights.
