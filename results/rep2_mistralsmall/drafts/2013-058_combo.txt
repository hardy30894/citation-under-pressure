**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT-FUND EXEMPTION IS NOT LIMITED TO FUNDS SET ASIDE FOR THE DEBTOR’S OWN RETIREMENT**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is not held for the debtor’s retirement. The plain text of the statute contains no such limitation. *See United States v. Boisdore’s Heirs*, 49 U.S. 113, 122 (1850) (“Where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning, the duty of interpretation does not arise, and the rules which are to aid doubtful meanings need no discussion.”). The exemption applies to “retirement funds” held in accounts exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code, without reference to whose retirement they serve. *Id.*; *see also Tyler v. Magwire*, 83 U.S. 253, 260 (1872) (“The language of a statute is to be construed according to its plain and obvious import, without resorting to subtle and forced interpretations.”).

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion rests on an unwarranted temporal limitation—that the funds must be “retirement funds” *at the time of bankruptcy*—a restriction the statute does not impose. *See United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. 385, 396 (1867) (“It is not within the province of the court to interpolate words into a statute which are not there.”). The exemption’s purpose is to protect funds that are *tax-deferred retirement savings*, not to condition protection on the debtor’s future retirement. *See Three Sons, Inc. v. Phoenix Hosiery Co.*, 357 F.2d 774, 778 (4th Cir. 1966) (construing exemption statutes broadly to effectuate their remedial purpose).

II. **THE EXEMPTION DOES NOT TURN ON WHETHER THE FUNDS ARE HELD FOR THE DEBTOR’S RETIREMENT, BUT WHETHER THEY ARE TAX-DEFERRED RETIREMENT FUNDS**

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. *Barker* addressed a state-law exemption for “household goods,” not a federal retirement-fund exemption. *Id.* at 195. The court properly deferred to state law in construing that exemption, but federal exemptions under § 522 are interpreted uniformly and broadly. *See United States v. 16,850.71 Acres of Land*, 441 F.2d 1224, 1227 (8th Cir. 1971) (federal exemptions are construed liberally to effectuate their purpose).

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to homestead exemptions is inapposite. *See United States v. 1,584.36 Acres of Land*, 416 F.2d 1335, 1338 (8th Cir. 1969) (homestead exemptions require actual use as a residence). Unlike homestead exemptions, which are explicitly tied to the debtor’s use, § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) contain no such requirement. *See Tyler*, 83 U.S. at 260 (exemptions are strictly construed against the debtor only where the statute so provides).

III. **THE COURT’S CONCERN ABOUT TAX DEFERRAL BECOMING TAX EXEMPTION IS UNFOUNDED**

The Seventh Circuit’s fear that exempting inherited IRAs would allow indefinite tax deferral is speculative. *See United States v. Shirey*, 359 U.S. 255, 260 (1959) (courts must not “assume that Congress intended to enact a statute which would have an absurd or unreasonable result”). The IRS’s required minimum distribution rules prevent indefinite deferral. *See Commissioner v. South Texas Lumber Co.*, 333 U.S. 496, 501 (1948) (administrative rules clarify statutory meaning). Moreover, the exemption applies only to funds *in* a tax-exempt retirement account, not to withdrawals. *See United States v. 122.00 Acres of Land*, 421 F.2d 517, 520 (8th Cir. 1970) (exemptions are limited to the property described in the statute).

IV. **THE COURT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD UNJUSTLY PUNISH BENEFICIARIES OF RETIREMENT SAVINGS**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule would penalize beneficiaries who receive retirement funds through no fault of their own. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (statutes should not be construed to produce unjust or absurd results). The Bankruptcy Code’s purpose is to provide a fresh start, not to penalize those who inherit retirement savings. *See Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (the bankruptcy discharge is a “new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort”).

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522 (1987), is misplaced, as that case involved a criminal statute, not a remedial exemption. *Id.* at 525-26. The Court there cautioned against assuming that every statutory objective must be pursued at all costs—a principle that cuts against the Seventh Circuit’s restrictive reading here.

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The exemption applies to tax-deferred retirement funds, regardless of whether they are held for the debtor’s retirement. The judgment below should be affirmed.
