# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2's Plain Text Limits Its Scope to "Internal Personnel Rules and Practices," Not All Documents an Agency Deems Sensitive

FOIA embodies "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Consistent with that philosophy, the statute's nine exemptions are "explicitly exclusive," *U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989), and "are to be interpreted narrowly," *id.* Exemption 2 exempts only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The ESQD data at issue—technical calculations about blast radii used in facility design—has nothing to do with personnel rules. It does not govern how Navy employees are hired, trained, disciplined, or managed. It is safety and engineering data, not an internal personnel practice in any ordinary sense of that phrase.

The Ninth Circuit's "High 2" doctrine, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), abandons the statute's text. *Crooker* substituted "predominantly internal" for the statutory word "solely," reasoning that Congress meant something broader than the text says. 670 F.2d at 1056-57. But this Court has never sanctioned rewriting an exemption's operative language to accommodate an agency's litigating position. Indeed, this Court has cautioned that FOIA exemptions "must be narrowly construed," *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, precisely because Congress carefully balanced disclosure against secrecy, *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). The "predominantly internal" gloss inverts that balance: rather than asking whether a document is *solely* about internal personnel matters, the Ninth Circuit's test asks only whether disclosure might be inconvenient for the agency.

## II. The Ninth Circuit's "Risk of Circumvention" Inquiry Has No Textual Basis and Swallows the Statute

Even accepting, arguendo, that some personnel materials whose disclosure might undermine agency regulations fall within Exemption 2's "Low 2" core, the Ninth Circuit went further, holding that any document—regardless of subject matter—is exempt if it is "predominantly internal" and its disclosure "would present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation." Pet. App. at [citing panel op.]. This standard, drawn from *Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1073-75, and adopted wholesale below, has no textual anchor in § 552(b)(2). The word "circumvention" appears nowhere in the statute. This Court's decision in *Rose* mentioned circumvention only in dicta discussing why certain low-level personnel rules were *not* exempt, not as an independent basis for exempting substantive agency data. 425 U.S. at 369.

By converting Exemption 2 into an open-ended "risk of circumvention" test, the Ninth Circuit created a standard that could swallow any information whose release might complicate an agency's regulatory mission—precisely the sort of unbounded exemption this Court rejected in *Tax Analysts*, where it insisted that exemptions be given their "explicitly exclusive" scope rather than be extended by judicial gloss to serve perceived policy needs. 492 U.S. at 151 (quoting *Adm'r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

## III. The Ninth Circuit's Own Precedent Confirms Exemption 2 Was Never Meant to Reach Data Like the ESQD Maps

The panel majority itself acknowledged that its prior decisions—*Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), and *Maricopa Audubon Society v. U.S. Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997)—had limited Exemption 2 to genuine "law enforcement materials." Pet. App. at [citing panel op.]. In *Maricopa*, the court held that goshawk nesting-site data did not "relate 'solely,' or even predominantly, 'to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency,'" 108 F.3d at 1085 (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2)), and rejected the government's attempt to invoke Exemption 2 for scientific data having no connection to personnel administration. The ESQD arc maps are indistinguishable from the Maricopa nesting data: both are technical, scientific information compiled for reasons unrelated to internal personnel management, yet sensitive because of what they reveal about vulnerable locations. If nesting sites are not "personnel rules and practices," neither are blast-radius calculations.

The majority below attempted to distinguish this line of authority by insisting that *Hardy*'s reliance on "law enforcement" materials was merely "a sufficient, but not necessary, condition" for Exemption 2, Pet. App. at [citing panel op.], but that reading cannot be squared with the statute's plain text or with *Maricopa*'s holding that the nesting data failed Exemption 2 review on independent grounds having nothing to do with law enforcement.

## IV. Any Legitimate Security Concerns Are Addressed by Exemptions 1 and 7, Not by Distorting Exemption 2

As Judge Fletcher recognized in dissent below, if the Navy genuinely believes the ESQD maps pose a national-security risk, "[e]xemption 1 of FOIA specifically exempts from disclosure classified matters 'kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy.'" Pet. App. at [dissent] (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1)). The Navy's failure to classify the very documents it claims are dangerous undercuts its litigating position and confirms that Exemption 2 is being used as an ill-fitting substitute for the exemptions Congress actually crafted for security-sensitive information. Distorting Exemption 2's "internal personnel rules and practices" language to fill that gap contravenes this Court's repeated instruction that the "cure" for inconvenient statutory line-drawing is not judicial expansion of narrow exemptions but resort to the exemption Congress actually provided. *See Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151; *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361.

## Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit's judgment should be reversed. Exemption 2 protects only what its text says it protects—internal personnel rules and practices—and does not extend to technical safety data merely because an agency invokes the specter of circumvention.
