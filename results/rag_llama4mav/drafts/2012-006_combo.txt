The question presented is whether withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period puts the burden of persuasion on the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The government bears the burden of proving that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. As established in McDaniel v. Traylor, 212 U.S. 428 (1909), a conspiracy is considered a single, continuing offense, and the government must prove that the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy continued into the limitations period.

The courts have long recognized that a defendant's withdrawal from a conspiracy can impact the government's ability to prove continued involvement. In Hyde v. Shine, 199 U.S. 62 (1905), the Court held that a conspiracy is not terminated by the withdrawal of one or more members, but rather continues until its objectives are achieved or abandoned. However, the government must still prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period.

In Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp., 340 U.S. 558 (1951), the Court addressed the issue of proving a conspiracy in a civil context, holding that a prior criminal conviction can be used as prima facie evidence of a conspiracy. By analogy, in a criminal case, the government must prove the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt.

The burden of proof remains with the government to establish that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. As stated in Partmar Corp. v. Paramount Pictures Theatres Corp., 347 U.S. 89 (1954), the government must prove its case by a preponderance of the evidence, and in a criminal case, this burden is heightened to beyond a reasonable doubt.

In Haas v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 462 (1910), the Court held that the government must prove the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy, and that the defendant's mere association with conspirators is insufficient to establish membership. Similarly, in Price v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 488 (1910), the Court emphasized the importance of proving the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy.

The principle that the government bears the burden of proof in a conspiracy case is further supported by In Re Belt, 159 U.S. 95 (1895), and In Re District of Columbia, No. 1, 180 U.S. 250 (1901), which establish that the government must prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt.

In Howard v. Fleming, 191 U.S. 126 (1903), the Court held that the government's burden of proof is not diminished by the defendant's failure to present evidence, and that the government must prove its case through affirmative evidence.

In conclusion, the government bears the burden of persuasion to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The courts have consistently held that the government must prove its case through affirmative evidence, and that the defendant's withdrawal from a conspiracy does not shift this burden. Therefore, we respectfully submit that the judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
