**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SENTENCING ENHANCEMENT FOR FIREARM POSSESSION DOES NOT TRANSFORM ROUSSOS’ DRUG CONSPIRACY CONVICTION INTO A “CRIME OF VIOLENCE” UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).**

Roussos’ drug conspiracy conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 is not a “crime of violence” as defined by 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that courts must apply the categorical approach when determining whether an offense qualifies as a “crime of violence.” *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990) (holding that the term “burglary” in the Armed Career Criminal Act must be defined by reference to the elements of the offense, not the facts underlying a particular conviction). Under this approach, a court looks only to the statutory elements of the offense and the fact of conviction, not to the particular facts of the case. *Id.* at 602.

Here, the sentencing enhancement for firearm possession did not alter the statutory elements of Roussos’ § 846 conviction. The enhancement was applied under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1), which provides for a two-level increase if “a dangerous weapon (including a firearm) was possessed” during the offense. The enhancement is based on conduct—possession of a firearm—not on an element of the offense itself. *See United States v. O’Brien*, 50 F.3d 718, 722 (8th Cir. 1995) (holding that § 2D1.1(b)(1) is a sentencing enhancement, not an element of the offense). Thus, the enhancement does not convert Roussos’ § 846 conviction into a “crime of violence” under § 924(c)(3).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that a sentencing enhancement does not change the nature of the underlying offense for purposes of the categorical approach. *See Shepard v. United States*, 544 U.S. 13, 20 (2005) (explaining that sentencing enhancements are not elements of the offense and do not alter the categorical inquiry). Because the enhancement here was not an element of Roussos’ § 846 conviction, it cannot transform that offense into a “crime of violence.”

**II. THE BOP’S PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE STATUTORY DEFINITION OF “CRIME OF VIOLENCE” AND THEREFORE EXCEEDS THE BOP’S AUTHORITY.**

The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 impermissibly expands the definition of “crime of violence” beyond the statutory text of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The Supreme Court has made clear that administrative agencies must “give effect to the unambiguously expressed intent of Congress.” *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). Here, the BOP’s interpretation of § 924(c)(3) conflicts with the statute’s plain language.

Section 924(c)(3) defines a “crime of violence” as an offense that either (A) has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, or (B) “by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force against the person or property of another may be used in the course of committing the offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). The BOP’s Program Statement, however, deems any drug offense enhanced for firearm possession a “crime of violence” based solely on the fact of enhancement, without regard to whether the offense “by its nature” involves a substantial risk of force. This interpretation is inconsistent with the statute’s text, which requires an inquiry into the nature of the offense, not the sentencing enhancement applied.

The Third Circuit has recognized that the BOP’s regulations must be consistent with the statute they purport to interpret. *See United States v. Johnson*, 964 F.2d 124, 127 (3d Cir. 1992) (holding that the BOP’s interpretation of its own regulations is entitled to deference only if it is consistent with the statute). Here, the BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 is not a permissible interpretation of § 924(c)(3) because it disregards the statutory requirement that the offense “by its nature” involve a substantial risk of force. The BOP’s interpretation thus exceeds its authority under *Chevron* and must be rejected.

**III. THE BOP’S ACTION VIOLATES ROUSSOS’ RIGHT TO EARLY RELEASE UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).**

Because Roussos’ § 846 conviction is not a “crime of violence” under § 924(c)(3), the BOP’s denial of early release eligibility based on Program Statement 5162.02 is contrary to law. The statute authorizing early release, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), provides for a reduction in custody for prisoners convicted of “nonviolent offenses.” The BOP’s regulations define “nonviolent offense” by reference to § 924(c)(3), but as discussed above, the BOP’s interpretation of § 924(c)(3) is inconsistent with the statute. Thus, the BOP’s denial of Roussos’ eligibility for early release is arbitrary and capricious in violation of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A).

The Third Circuit has held that the BOP’s discretion to grant early release is not unlimited. *See United States v. Means*, 984 F.2d 535, 537 (3d Cir. 1993) (holding that the BOP’s discretion must be exercised in accordance with the statute and regulations). Here, the BOP’s action is contrary to the plain language of § 3621(e)(2)(B) and its implementing regulations, as properly interpreted. The district court therefore erred in denying Roussos’ habeas petition.
