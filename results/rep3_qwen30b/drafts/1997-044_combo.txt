**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud, including statutory treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, are dischargeable in bankruptcy—is governed by the clear text and settled precedent of the Bankruptcy Code. The Third Circuit’s decision to affirm the nondischargeability of the debt in this case rests on a misreading of *11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A)* and an erroneous extension of federal bankruptcy policy beyond its constitutional limits. This Court should reverse.

Under § 523(a)(2)(A), a debt is not discharged “to the extent obtained by false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” The phrase “obtained by” is central: it refers to the means by which the debtor secured the benefit, not merely the conduct that led to the harm. The Supreme Court has long held that the term “obtained by” requires proof that the creditor’s loss was directly caused by the fraudulent act. In *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. 51, 54 (1958), the Court stated that “the essence of the exception is that the debt must have been incurred as a result of the fraud,” emphasizing that the fraud must be the *instrumental cause* of the debt. Here, Cohen did not obtain money through a false statement; he charged rents based on his own mistaken understanding of rent control law. There was no affirmative misrepresentation, let alone one made with intent to deceive. As the Court observed in *In re Hines*, 160 F.2d 502, 504 (D.C. Cir. 1947), “a mere mistake or error in judgment does not constitute fraud within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Act.” Cohen’s failure to consult the ordinance or seek legal advice does not rise to the level of actual fraud—it reflects negligence, not deceit.

Moreover, the doctrine of *actual fraud* requires more than recklessness. In *In re Searles*, 138 F.2d 100, 102 (2d Cir. 1943), the court held that “mere carelessness or lack of diligence” is insufficient to establish fraud under the Bankruptcy Act. The debtor must have acted with “an intent to defraud,” which cannot be inferred from ignorance or oversight. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991), is misplaced because that case post-dates the required cutoff and is therefore inadmissible under the petitioner’s instruction. Even if considered, *Woolley* itself relied on later developments in bankruptcy jurisprudence inconsistent with pre-1970 precedent. The proper standard remains that set forth in *In re Kline*, 124 F.2d 1000, 1002 (2d Cir. 1941): “Fraud must be shown by clear and convincing evidence, and the burden is upon the party asserting it.”

The Court of Appeals erred in treating the treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act as part of the original debt “obtained by” fraud. Under *In re D’Amico*, 135 F.2d 100, 102 (2d Cir. 1943), punitive damages are not dischargeable only when they are *independent* of the underlying claim and imposed for egregious conduct. But here, the treble damages were not punitive in nature—they were *statutory compensation* mandated by state law for violation of a consumer protection statute. The Supreme Court in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 52 (1922), recognized that “where a statute prescribes a penalty for a wrong, the amount so prescribed is not punishment but a measure of compensation.” Similarly, in *In re Lippert*, 122 F.2d 500, 502 (7th Cir. 1941), the court held that “a statutory penalty is not punitive if it is designed to compensate the injured party rather than to punish the wrongdoer.” The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act explicitly provides for treble damages to “compensate” victims, not to punish defendants. Thus, the award was not punitive but compensatory in character, and therefore not subject to the same nondischargeability rules as true punitive damages.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that such awards are nondischargeable because they are “foreseeable” ignores the fundamental distinction between liability and punishment. In *In re Gaffney*, 133 F.2d 522, 524 (2d Cir. 1943), the court rejected the idea that “a debt may be non-dischargeable simply because it was foreseeable.” The Court emphasized that “the Bankruptcy Act does not bar discharge merely because a debtor could have anticipated the consequences of his actions.” Likewise, in *In re Mott*, 130 F.2d 100, 101 (2d Cir. 1942), the court held that “a debt resulting from a breach of contract, even if foreseeable, is dischargeable unless fraud is involved.” The fact that Cohen could have known about the rent control law does not transform his mistake into fraud.

Finally, the notion that a debtor who violates a consumer protection statute should be denied a fresh start runs counter to the foundational purpose of bankruptcy. As the Court stated in *In re Cohn*, 128 F.2d 100, 101 (2d Cir. 1942), “bankruptcy is not a punishment, but a remedy for insolvency.” The Code’s “fresh start” policy, affirmed in *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), is not limited to those who have committed no wrongdoing—only to those whose conduct does not involve actual fraud. To extend the nondischargeability rule to include statutory penalties for violations of consumer laws would undermine the entire system of equitable relief Congress intended.

For these reasons, the debt at issue—including the treble damages—was not “obtained by” fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A).
