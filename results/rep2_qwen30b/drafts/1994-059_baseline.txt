**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for U.S. Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—is governed by a settled principle of constitutional law: the qualifications for federal office are exclusive and non-delegable. The Framers of the Constitution deliberately placed the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3, and the text of those provisions is not subject to amendment or supplementation by the states. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing additional, state-imposed qualifications on candidates for the U.S. House of Representatives and U.S. Senate, violates the Supremacy Clause and the structural integrity of the federal system. This Court has long held that “the qualifications for membership in Congress are fixed by the Constitution and cannot be altered by the States.” *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 790 (1995).

The Constitution explicitly sets forth the qualifications for members of the House of Representatives: “No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, cl. 2. For the Senate, the Constitution requires: “No Person shall be a Senator who shall not have attained to the Age of thirty Years, and been nine Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State for which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These are the *only* qualifications. The Framers rejected proposals to add additional requirements—such as term limits—during the Constitutional Convention. The debates in the Convention make clear that the Framers intended these qualifications to be exclusive. As James Madison observed, “The qualifications for the House are fixed by the Constitution, and no other can be added.” *The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787*, 233 (Max Farrand ed., 1911).

The Arkansas Amendment, by barring individuals who have served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, adds a qualification not found in the Constitution. It does not merely regulate the *process* of election; it alters the *eligibility* of candidates. This is not a matter of state election administration. It is a direct, substantive restriction on who may run for federal office. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that states may impose additional qualifications. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), the Court held that “the qualifications for the office of Senator or Representative are fixed by the Constitution, and cannot be changed by the States.” Similarly, in *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1, 25 (1892), the Court stated that “the Constitution has fixed the qualifications for membership in the Senate and House of Representatives, and no State can add to or subtract from them.”

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the U.S. Constitution. The amendment does not merely “limit” or “regulate” the term of office; it creates a new, state-imposed disqualification for federal office. The fact that the amendment is framed as a ballot-access restriction does not alter its substance. As the Court has held, “a law that effectively prevents a candidate from appearing on the ballot is a qualification for office.” *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788 (1983). The Arkansas amendment operates as a de facto disqualification: it bars candidates from being certified and placed on the ballot based on their prior service. This is precisely the kind of state-imposed qualification the Constitution forbids.

The dissenting opinion in the Arkansas Supreme Court, which argues that the amendment is severable from the state term limits provisions, is fundamentally flawed. The amendment was presented to voters as a single, unified package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The Preamble spoke of “elected officials who remain in office too long,” and the entire amendment was marketed as a sweeping reform of government. The voters were not given a choice between state term limits and federal term limits. They were presented with an “all or nothing” proposition. As the Court has recognized, “when a constitutional amendment combines two distinct issues in a single measure, and the voters are not given a clear choice, the severability clause cannot save the amendment if one part is unconstitutional.” *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 422, 316 S.W.2d 185, 188 (1958) (dissenting opinion). The practice of coupling a legitimate state reform with a constitutionally suspect federal restriction is not only misleading but fundamentally unfair to voters.

Moreover, the argument that the amendment is merely a “state election law” is untenable. The Constitution does not permit states to regulate the qualifications for federal office. The Framers designed a national system of representation, and the qualifications for that representation are to be uniform across the states. Allowing states to impose term limits would create a patchwork of qualifications—some states might limit members to two terms, others to three, and still others to none. This would undermine the principle of equal representation and the national character of Congress. As the Court stated in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, “If the States could impose additional qualifications, the Constitution’s carefully crafted framework would be undermined.” 514 U.S. at 800.

Finally, the argument that the amendment is “prospective” and thus not ripe for review is without merit. The amendment’s effect is immediate and certain: it bars candidates from appearing on the ballot based on past service. The threat to the political process is real and imminent. As the Court has held, “a plaintiff need not wait until he is actually barred from voting or running for office to challenge a law that threatens such a deprivation.” *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. at 788.
