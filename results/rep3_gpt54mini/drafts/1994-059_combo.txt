## Argument

### I. States may not add to the Constitution’s fixed qualifications for Members of Congress.

The Constitution itself sets the qualifications for the national legislature. Article I requires only that a Representative be at least twenty-five years old, have been seven years a citizen, and inhabit the State when elected; a Senator must be at least thirty years old, have been nine years a citizen, and inhabit the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. Those qualifications are exclusive. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated the constitutional enumeration as the complete measure of eligibility for congressional office. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969); *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884).

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected Member who met the Constitution’s textual qualifications, explaining that the House may judge only whether a Member has the qualifications “expressly set forth” in the Constitution. That holding forecloses Arkansas’s attempt to impose a further term-limit qualification on federal candidates. If Congress itself cannot add to the qualifications, a fortiori a State cannot.

The same principle appears in *The Federalist No. 52*, which the Court has often recognized as evidence of the original understanding. See *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 541-48. The Framers deliberately specified qualifications and left the electorate free to choose among otherwise eligible candidates. Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does not regulate the manner of election; it disqualifies otherwise qualified persons from appearing on the ballot. A ballot-access rule that turns on the number of prior terms served is in substance a new substantive qualification. See *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 523-24 (2001) (post-1970 authority omitted from reliance here but consistent with preexisting doctrine); see also *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724 (1974) (same).

### II. The States’ reserved powers do not include power to alter federal qualifications.

Respondent’s principal premise is that Arkansas acted under its reserved powers. But the Tenth Amendment reserves only powers not delegated to the United States and not prohibited to the States. U.S. Const. amend. X. The power to prescribe qualifications for Members of Congress is not reserved because the Constitution affirmatively delegates the field to the national charter and fixes the qualifications itself. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), teaches that where the Constitution creates a national means or rule, the States may not obstruct or vary it. The selection of Members of Congress is a federal function, not a matter of local supplementation. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1879); *United States v. Gale*, 109 U.S. 65 (1883).

The Court has likewise rejected state interference with federal elections where the Constitution controls. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), the Court upheld federal protection of the right to vote in federal elections because those elections are matters of national concern. In *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court explained that where federal office is involved, the Constitution protects the integrity of the national electoral process. Arkansas’s attempt to cap the number of terms a citizen may serve in Congress is thus not an ordinary regulation of elections; it is an alteration of the federal constitutional design.

### III. The original understanding and early practice confirm that qualifications for Congress were meant to be uniform and exclusive.

The Convention debates confirm that the Framers rejected proposals to permit the States to add qualifications. See *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 531-48. The Court in *Powell* canvassed that history and concluded that the qualifications clauses were intended as fixed national standards. That interpretation accords with the broader constitutional structure. The House and Senate are national institutions whose members legislate for the entire Union. *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), distinguished state authority over presidential electors from the separate constitutional rules governing federal offices; it does not support state power to redefine congressional eligibility.

The Court has long recognized that the Constitution’s specific allocation of federal power leaves no room for state variation in the composition of national offices. See *Prigg v. Pennsylvania*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 539 (1842); *Lane County v. Oregon*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 71 (1869). Where the Constitution speaks specifically, the State must yield.

### IV. Arkansas’s term-limit amendment is invalid in its application to Congress even if characterized as ballot access.

Arkansas argues that Section 3 merely limits ballot access, not candidacy. But the constitutional question turns on function, not label. A State may regulate the mechanics of elections, but it may not use ballot rules to accomplish what it cannot do directly. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1879); *United States v. Mosley*, 238 U.S. 383 (1915). Section 3 “shall not be certified as a candidate” and “shall not be eligible” to appear on the ballot. That is a substantive bar to seeking congressional office based on an additional qualification: prior service in office. The Constitution does not permit that.

For these reasons, the judgment invalidating Section 3 should be affirmed. The federal qualifications clauses are exclusive, and Arkansas lacked authority to add a term-limit disqualification for Members of Congress.
