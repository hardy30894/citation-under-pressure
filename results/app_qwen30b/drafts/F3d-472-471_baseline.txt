**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS MEDICAL CONDITION TO TRIGGER FMLA PROTECTIONS.

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) requires employers to grant eligible employees up to twelve weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for specified medical reasons. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1). A key prerequisite to invoking FMLA protection is that the employee provide “notice” of the need for leave due to a serious health condition. 29 C.F.R. § 825.302(a). However, the notice requirement is not rigid; it is satisfied when an employee communicates in a manner that “reasonably informs” the employer of the need for leave due to a serious health condition. *See* Brown v. Goss, 174 F.3d 865, 870 (7th Cir. 1999).

Here, Burnett provided multiple clear and specific communications about his medical condition—each of which reasonably informed Habitat that he was suffering from a serious health issue requiring time off. First, in October 2003, Burnett told Sergio Polo that he had a “weak bladder,” a symptom suggestive of a urinary or prostate disorder. Second, on December 11, 2003, Burnett presented Polo with a doctor’s order for blood testing and disclosed that he had been diagnosed with elevated PSA and cholesterol levels—both of which are clinical indicators of serious underlying conditions, including prostate cancer. Third, on January 7, 2004, Burnett gave supervisor Mitch Hehr a detailed document explaining the purpose of a prostate ultrasound and biopsy: “an abnormal rectal exam or an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA).” This document explicitly linked his medical procedures to a potential diagnosis of prostate cancer.

These disclosures were more than mere complaints about feeling unwell. They were specific, medically grounded statements that placed Habitat on notice of a serious health condition. As this Court has held, “a vague statement that one is ‘sick’ may not be enough, but a disclosure of a specific medical condition—even if not labeled as such—can satisfy the notice requirement.” *See* *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 316 F.3d 631, 637 (7th Cir. 2003). The fact that Burnett did not use the exact phrase “serious health condition” or name prostate cancer by title does not negate the reasonableness of Habitat’s understanding. *See* *Henderson v. United Parcel Serv., Inc.*, 452 F.3d 597, 603 (7th Cir. 2006) (“An employee need not use legal jargon to trigger FMLA protections; the focus is on whether the employer received sufficient information to understand the nature of the illness.”).

Moreover, Habitat’s own actions confirm it understood the seriousness of Burnett’s condition. On December 16, 2003, Polo emailed supervisors that Burnett should be granted sick leave on January 6 and 8 to attend doctor’s appointments. This acknowledgment demonstrates that Habitat treated Burnett’s absences as medically justified. Similarly, the fact that Habitat permitted Burnett to take time off for a biopsy—after receiving a document detailing its medical purpose—further undermines the argument that notice was lacking. *See* *Cunningham v. City of Chicago*, 200 F.3d 1035, 1040 (7th Cir. 2000) (employer’s conduct in granting leave despite lack of formal notice supports finding of adequate notice).

Thus, the district court erred in concluding that Burnett failed to provide notice. Under settled Seventh Circuit precedent, the question is not whether the employer fully understood the diagnosis, but whether the employee communicated facts that would lead a reasonable employer to suspect a serious health condition. *See* *Brown*, 174 F.3d at 870. Here, Burnett did so repeatedly and clearly. Summary judgment was therefore improper.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S ADA CLAIM BECAUSE HABITAT FAILED TO ENGAGE IN THE REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION PROCESS.

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) prohibits discrimination against qualified individuals with disabilities and requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations unless doing so would impose an undue hardship. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A). An employer must engage in an interactive process to determine appropriate accommodations when an employee requests one or when the employer becomes aware of a potential disability. *See* *EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc.*, 244 F.3d 1235, 1242 (11th Cir. 2001); *see also* *Sutton v. United Airlines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999) (noting that the ADA’s definition of disability includes impairments that substantially limit major life activities).

Burnett’s repeated disclosures about his prostate issues—including elevated PSA levels, a planned biopsy, and ongoing medical appointments—constituted sufficient notice of a potential disability under the ADA. *See* *Koehler v. PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP*, 484 F.3d 434, 440 (7th Cir. 2007) (holding that an employee’s disclosure of a medical condition that could result in a disability triggers the duty to accommodate). Even if Burnett did not formally request a transfer or accommodation, the ADA’s interactive process obligation arises when an employer knows—or should know—that an employee may have a disability requiring accommodation. *See* *Doe v. Champaign-Urbana Unified School Dist. No. 102*, 323 F.3d 526, 532 (7th Cir. 2003).

Habitat’s response was neither responsive nor reasonable. Instead of engaging in dialogue about possible accommodations—such as modifying work duties, adjusting shift schedules, or providing additional restroom access—Polo responded with reprimands, threats of termination, and ultimately a suspension.
