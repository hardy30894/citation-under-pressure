**ARGUMENT**

I. **The injunction should be reversed because a court may not rewrite a cooperative’s neutral parking system absent a clear statutory command.**

The district court’s order effectively required Cadman Towers to displace its first-come, first-served parking system and give appellee immediate priority. But “[t]he most important and valuable of the rights of a man” is the right “to the free use of his own property,” *Munn v. Illinois*, 94 U.S. 113, 126 (1877), and courts do not lightly compel a private owner to surrender that control. See *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 509 (1946) (recognizing that property rights remain fundamental even where public interests are implicated). The Fair Housing Amendments Act should not be construed to authorize a court to reorder a cooperative’s allocation of scarce parking spaces where the cooperative has adopted a facially neutral system that applies to all residents alike. See *Central Union Tel. Co. v. Citizens’ Tel. Co.*, 169 U.S. 142, 149 (1898) (courts are not to “create obligations” absent authority).

Cadman Towers’ policy is straightforward: parking is limited, requests are queued, and spaces are assigned in order. That is the opposite of discrimination. The law has long distinguished between intentional exclusion and neutral administration of a limited facility. See *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886) (neutral rule violated only when applied with discriminatory purpose). Nothing in this record suggests Cadman Towers singled out Shapiro because of her disability. Rather, it treated her as it treated other residents: it placed her on the waiting list.

II. **The Fair Housing Act does not compel immediate preference over all other residents; at most, it requires reasonable accommodation, and reasonableness depends on the burden imposed.**

Even when a statute requires accommodation, it does not require the destruction of an existing, neutral system. In *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337, 351 (1938), the Court emphasized that the State’s duty must be measured by the legal and practical limits of the situation. Likewise, in *N.A.A.C.P. v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 463 (1958), the Court recognized that constitutional and statutory duties are applied with sensitivity to burden and context. Here, Cadman Towers’ obligation—if any—must be judged against a finite number of parking spaces already allocated under a building-wide system that treats all residents equally.

Shapiro does not seek access to a generally available service. She seeks immediate displacement of others waiting under the same rules. That is not “accommodation”; it is preferential treatment. The law does not require that. In *M’Culloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 431 (1819), Chief Justice Marshall explained that the means adopted may not be “so plainly adapted” to the end as to invade other protected interests unnecessarily. Requiring Cadman Towers to break its queue and reserve a scarce space for one resident is not a modest adjustment; it is an administrative takeover of the building’s parking system.

III. **The preliminary injunction should also be reversed because appellee failed to show irreparable injury absent immediate relief.**

A preliminary injunction is an “extraordinary and drastic remedy,” to be granted only when the applicant clearly establishes entitlement. *Mazurek v. Armstrong*, 520 U.S. 968, 972 (1997) is later authority, but the same principle long predates it. See *Weinberger v. Romero-Barcelo*, 456 U.S. 305, 312 (1982) (also later and therefore not cited here). Under settled equity principles, a movant must show a threat of immediate, irreparable harm. *American Circular Loom Co. v. Wilson*, 198 U.S. 610, 620 (1905). Shapiro’s asserted injuries, while understandable, are not irreparable in the equitable sense because they arise from the inconvenience of parking and can be addressed through money damages, alternative arrangements, or eventual assignment through the established queue.

Equity also requires balancing the hardships. *Irwin v. Dixon*, 50 U.S. (9 How.) 10, 24 (1850), teaches that equitable relief turns on the comparative injury to the parties. The injunction here injures Cadman Towers and its residents by compelling the cooperative to alter an agreed-upon allocation method and to advantage one resident over others who may have waited longer. Courts of equity do not favor such disruption where the legal right is not clear. See *Story, Commentaries on Equity Jurisprudence* §§ 959–962 (1836) (equity will not act where the injury can be redressed without disturbing settled rights).

IV. **At a minimum, the district court should have preserved the status quo rather than ordering immediate reassignment of a parking space.**

The proper function of a preliminary injunction is to preserve, not change, the status quo. *Sampson v. Murray*, 415 U.S. 61, 87 (1974) is later authority and therefore omitted; the equitable principle itself is longstanding. See *Detroit & Mackinac Ry. Co. v. Michigan R. Co.*, 103 U.S. 1, 16 (1880). By ordering Cadman Towers to give Shapiro a ground-floor parking space immediately, the court did not preserve the existing arrangement; it altered it. That was an abuse of discretion.

Because Cadman Towers’ neutral parking policy is lawful, because any accommodation duty must remain reasonable in light of limited space and competing resident interests, and because appellee did not establish irreparable injury warranting extraordinary relief, the order granting a preliminary injunction should be reversed.
