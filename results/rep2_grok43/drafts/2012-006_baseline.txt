**ARGUMENT**

Withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period commences places upon the government the burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member during the actionable period. Once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the prosecution must establish the defendant's knowing participation in the conspiracy within the five-year window prescribed by 18 U.S.C. § 3282. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 399 (1957) (holding that "the statute of limitations begins to run from the time of the withdrawal").

This Court has long recognized that withdrawal is "an affirmative defense" that, when supported by evidence, terminates the defendant's liability for subsequent acts of co-conspirators. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). The government therefore bears the ultimate burden of persuasion on the element of membership during the limitations period. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 445 (1978) (requiring the prosecution to prove "beyond a reasonable doubt" every element of the offense, including the defendant's continued participation).

The court of appeals erred in relieving the government of that burden. By requiring only that the prosecution show the existence of some conspiracy, rather than the defendants' membership in it during the limitations period, the court inverted the allocation of proof established in *Grunewald* and *Gypsum*. The evidence that Moore and Gray had divided the enterprise before the limitations cutoff constituted sufficient evidence of withdrawal to trigger the government's obligation to prove continued membership beyond a reasonable doubt. Because the government failed to carry that burden, the convictions cannot stand.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
