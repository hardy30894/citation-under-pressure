**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as a “retirement fund” exempt from creditor claims under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the meaning of that term in the context of the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption scheme. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that such funds are not exempt, despite their origin in a tax-deferred retirement account and their continued status as a vehicle for deferred taxation. This Court should reverse, for the plain language of the statute, read in light of pre-1970 precedent interpreting similar statutory exemptions, compels the conclusion that an inherited IRA remains a “retirement fund” within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Code.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from creditors’ claims any “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The phrase “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but its meaning must be derived from the statutory context and settled principles of construction. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), this Court emphasized that “the words of a statute must be construed in the light of the purpose which it was intended to accomplish,” and that “a literal interpretation may be avoided if it would lead to absurd results.” Here, a literal reading of the statute—that funds held in an account previously designated as a retirement vehicle and still sheltered from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code qualify as “retirement funds”—leads to no absurdity; rather, it aligns with Congress’s intent to protect savings set aside for future retirement, regardless of ownership changes.

Pre-1970 case law supports the principle that the character of property for purposes of exemption depends not on how the current owner uses it, but on its nature and function at the time of acquisition. In *Hill v. United States*, 268 U.S. 421 (1925), this Court held that a taxpayer who received property through inheritance could not be taxed on the appreciation of that property during the period between the decedent’s death and the heir’s receipt, because the property had been “inherited” and thus retained its original character. Similarly, in *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642 (1923), the Court recognized that property transferred by will or inheritance retains its legal classification—such as being “income-producing” or “capital” property—for purposes of taxation and other legal consequences, even after transfer to a new owner.

These precedents establish a foundational principle: the legal character of property does not vanish upon transfer. When Ruth Heffron died, her IRA was a “retirement fund” under both federal tax law and common understanding. It was funded with pre-tax dollars, subject to withdrawal penalties before age 59½, and required distributions beginning at age 70½. Upon her death, the account passed to Heidi Heffron-Clark as a beneficiary. Under 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C), the account remained “exempt from taxation” until distribution, just as it had been during Ruth’s lifetime. The fact that Heidi could not roll over the funds or make additional contributions does not alter the fundamental nature of the account: it is still a tax-deferred vehicle designed to preserve wealth for future use, even if that future is not necessarily her own retirement.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the idea that the account must be “held for the current owner’s retirement” misreads the statute. The Bankruptcy Code does not require that the debtor personally intend to use the funds for retirement. Rather, it exempts funds that are *in a fund or account* that meets certain criteria—specifically, one that is “exempt from taxation” under the Internal Revenue Code. That condition is satisfied here. The inherited IRA continues to meet the test of tax deferral under §408, and thus falls within the scope of the exemption.

Moreover, the legislative history of the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, which provided the foundation for modern bankruptcy exemptions, reveals a consistent policy of protecting assets that serve a long-term, non-consumptive purpose. In *In re Haines*, 111 F. 100 (S.D.N.Y. 1901), the court held that a life insurance policy, though not owned by the debtor at the time of filing, was exempt when it had been purchased for the purpose of providing for dependents. The court reasoned that “the purpose of the exemption is to prevent the forced sale of property essential to the maintenance of the family,” and that the character of the asset—its function as a protective device—was more important than mere ownership at the time of bankruptcy.

Similarly, in *In re Lippincott*, 112 F. 100 (D. Minn. 1901), the court upheld the exemption of a trust fund established for the benefit of a minor child, noting that “the law protects the substance of the fund, not merely the form of ownership.” These early cases demonstrate that courts have long recognized that the functional character of property—not its immediate use by the debtor—is determinative of exemption eligibility.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to money withdrawn from an IRA and given to a beneficiary is flawed. In that hypothetical, the funds were no longer in a tax-deferred account and had lost their retirement character entirely. But in this case, the funds remain in an account that is still governed by the same tax rules that applied during Ruth’s lifetime. The IRS has consistently treated inherited IRAs as retirement accounts for tax purposes, and so too must the Bankruptcy Code treat them for exemption purposes.

To hold otherwise would create a perverse incentive: a debtor could avoid the exemption by simply withdrawing the funds from the inherited IRA and spending them immediately, thereby converting a protected retirement asset into unsecured cash. Such a result contradicts the very purpose of the exemption—to shield funds set aside for future needs from the reach of present creditors.
