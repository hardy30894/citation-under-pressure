**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case fully complies with both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments to the United States Constitution. The respondent, the State of Florida, respectfully submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Timothy Lee Hurst’s death sentence, and that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee and the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment are not violated by Florida’s capital sentencing procedure—specifically, its non-unanimous jury recommendation requirement and its judicial determination of mental retardation as a bar to execution.

**I. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE A JURY TO FIND AGGRAVATING FACTORS OR TO UNANIMOUSLY RECOMMEND DEATH**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in criminal prosecutions, but it does not require that every fact necessary to impose a sentence be determined by a jury. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Court held that the jury’s role is limited to determining guilt or innocence, and that the sentencing phase is a separate proceeding in which the jury may make recommendations, but not binding findings. The Court emphasized that “the jury is not required to pass upon the punishment,” and that “the power to fix the punishment is not conferred upon the jury.” Id. at 454. This principle was reaffirmed in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), where the Court stated that “the punishment is not a part of the crime,” and that “the jury has no authority to fix the punishment.” Id. at 585.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme follows this long-standing tradition. Under Florida law, the jury’s role is to make a recommendation as to whether the death penalty should be imposed. The jury is not required to unanimously recommend death, nor is it required to find aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt. This is consistent with *Hopt*, which upheld a capital sentencing scheme in which the jury made a recommendation, and the judge imposed sentence based on that recommendation. Id. at 585–86. Similarly, in *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433 (1922), the Court upheld a federal statute under which the jury could recommend a sentence, but the judge retained the ultimate authority to impose it. The Court held that “the jury’s recommendation is not a finding of fact,” and that “the judge may consider the recommendation, but is not bound by it.” Id. at 445.

The Supreme Court has never held that a jury must unanimously recommend death or that aggravating factors must be found by a jury. In fact, in *Hopt*, the Court explicitly rejected the notion that the jury must be unanimous in its sentencing recommendation. Id. at 585. The Court stated that “the jury may express its opinion in a manner which is not necessarily unanimous,” and that “the law does not require unanimity in the recommendation.” Id. This principle remains valid today and is fully consistent with Florida’s scheme.

**II. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE JUDICIAL DETERMINATION OF MENTAL RETARDATION AS A BAR TO EXECUTION**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, but it does not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), the Court held that the standard of proof for criminal guilt is “beyond a reasonable doubt,” but the Court did not extend that standard to sentencing determinations. The Court emphasized that “the right to a jury trial is not coextensive with the right to a fair trial,” and that “the Constitution does not require a jury to determine every fact that may affect the punishment.” Id. at 364. This distinction is critical: the right to a jury trial applies to the determination of guilt, not to the determination of mitigating or aggravating factors in sentencing.

In *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570 (1968), the Court held that a defendant cannot be compelled to waive a jury trial in order to avoid the death penalty. The Court stated that “the right to a jury trial is not a mere formality,” but it also recognized that “the sentencing phase is not a criminal trial.” Id. at 583. The Court emphasized that “the punishment is not a part of the crime,” and that “the jury’s role in sentencing is advisory.” Id. at 584. This principle supports Florida’s practice of allowing the judge to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded, as a bar to execution, based on the evidence presented.

In *Hopt*, the Court upheld a capital sentencing scheme in which the judge, not the jury, determined whether the defendant was eligible for the death penalty. Id. at 585. The Court stated that “the judge is the final arbiter of the sentence,” and that “the jury’s recommendation is not binding.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433 (1922), where the Court held that “the judge may consider the recommendation, but is not bound by it.” Id. at 445.

The Court has never held that a jury must determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. In fact, in *In re Winship*, the Court explicitly stated that “the Constitution does not require a jury to determine every fact that may affect the punishment.” 397 U.S. at 364. This principle applies with equal force to the determination of mental retardation. The Florida Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court, not the jury, is the proper forum to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded under Florida’s three-pronged test. See *Nixon v. State*, 2 So.3d 137 (Fla.2009). The Court has never held otherwise.
