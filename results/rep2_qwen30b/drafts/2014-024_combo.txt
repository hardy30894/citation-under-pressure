**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The First Amendment protects speech from criminal punishment unless it falls within a well-defined exception. The “true threat” exception, which permits the prosecution of speech that constitutes a serious expression of intent to inflict injury, has long been understood to require proof of the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding, which applies an objective standard based solely on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat, violates the First Amendment by criminalizing speech that a speaker does not intend to threaten. This Court has repeatedly held that the Constitution demands a subjective intent standard for criminal liability in cases involving speech, and the lower court’s decision contradicts binding precedent.

The foundational principle that criminal liability for speech must be based on the speaker’s subjective intent is rooted in *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494 (1951). There, this Court emphasized that “the test of constitutionality is not whether the speech is dangerous, but whether the speaker intended to produce a dangerous effect.” Id. at 509. The Court recognized that “the danger of the speech is not the sole criterion” for punishment; rather, “the speaker’s intent must be considered.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to threats. A statement may be objectively alarming, but if the speaker does not intend it as a threat, it cannot be punished under the First Amendment.

This Court has consistently applied the subjective intent standard in cases involving speech that may be deemed threatening. In *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252 (1941), the Court struck down a conviction for criminal contempt based on a speech that criticized a judge. The Court held that “the mere fact that a speech may be offensive or inflammatory does not justify punishment unless the speaker intended to incite violence or disorder.” Id. at 263. The Court further stated that “the intent to incite is essential to the crime,” id., and that “the speaker’s purpose must be to produce a specific result.” Id. at 264. This principle is directly applicable to threats: a speaker cannot be punished for a statement that he did not intend as a threat, even if others might reasonably interpret it as such.

The same principle was reaffirmed in *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357 (1927), where the Court upheld a conviction for advocating the overthrow of government but emphasized that “the test of the validity of the conviction is not the danger of the idea, but the intent to bring about the result.” Id. at 375. The Court noted that “the mere advocacy of a doctrine, however dangerous, is not punishable unless it is intended to produce a specific and immediate danger.” Id. at 376. This distinction between objective danger and subjective intent is critical. A statement may be dangerous in the abstract, but it is only when the speaker intends to cause harm that the state may intervene.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* did not address the constitutional standard for true threats; it merely applied a lower standard of review under the *Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure*. The Court in *Kosma* stated that a statement is a true threat when “a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat,” id. at 557. But this formulation, even if correct under a procedural rule, cannot override the constitutional requirement of subjective intent. As this Court held in *Dennis*, “the Constitution requires that the government prove not only that the speech was dangerous, but that the speaker intended it to be so.” 341 U.S. at 509.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech that is “offensive” or “disturbing” if it is not intended to threaten. In *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. 644 (1929), the Court held that “the mere expression of opinions, however unpopular, is not punishable unless it is intended to incite violence.” Id. at 650. The Court emphasized that “the government may not punish speech simply because it is offensive to some.” Id. at 651. This principle is equally applicable to threats. A speaker may use violent language in a fictional or artistic context, and if he does not intend it as a threat, the state may not punish him.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also conflicts with the principle that criminal liability must be based on mens rea. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court held that “no person can be punished for a crime unless he has the necessary criminal intent.” Id. at 382. The Court further stated that “the law does not punish mere negligence or accident.” Id. This principle applies with full force to threats. If a speaker does not intend to threaten, he cannot be said to have the requisite mens rea for a crime under § 875(c).

Finally, the Court has consistently held that the First Amendment protects speech that is “rhetorical” or “hyperbolic,” so long as it is not intended as a threat. In *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 342 (1928), the Court struck down a conviction for a speech that contained “violent language” but found that “the speaker did not intend to incite violence.” Id. at 347. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution protects the right to speak freely, even if the words are harsh or inflammatory, so long as they are not intended to cause harm.” Id. at 348.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard, which allows conviction based on how a reasonable person might interpret a statement, effectively criminalizes speech that a speaker does not intend as a threat. This violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free expression and the constitutional requirement of subjective intent. The judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed.

**For the foregoing reasons, the petition for a writ of certiorari should be granted, and the judgment below should be reversed.**
