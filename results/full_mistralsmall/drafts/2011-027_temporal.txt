**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine provides that when a state enters the Union, it takes title to the beds and banks of rivers that were navigable at the time of its admission. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). This rule is rooted in the principle that navigable waters are "public highways" dedicated to interstate and foreign commerce, and thus their beds are held in trust for the people. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 48 (1894). The test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was "capable of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). This inquiry is necessarily historical, focusing on the river’s condition at the time of statehood. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (holding that the navigability inquiry is "a question of fact to be determined by reference to the state of things at the time of the admission of the state").

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the navigability determination must be made as of the date of statehood. In *United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707, 716 (1950), the Court explained that the equal footing doctrine "looks to the time when the State became a member of the Union." Similarly, in *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14, the Court held that the question of navigability is determined by the river’s condition "at the time of the admission of the state." This historical inquiry ensures that the state’s title is fixed at the moment of its entry into the Union, reflecting the public trust purpose underlying the doctrine. *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 48.

**II. The Trial Court Properly Determined Navigability by Examining Historical Evidence of the Rivers’ Condition at Statehood.**

The trial court did not err in considering historical evidence to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. Courts routinely rely on historical records—such as explorers’ accounts, territorial surveys, and contemporaneous commercial records—to establish navigability as of the relevant date. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (relying on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75 (same). As this Court has recognized, such evidence is admissible under the ancient documents exception to the hearsay rule, as it was prepared in the ordinary course of business and has been in existence for more than twenty years. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 n.7 (1981) (citing *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563).

The trial court’s reliance on historical evidence was consistent with this longstanding practice. The court did not engage in speculation but instead examined records documenting the rivers’ use for commerce at the time of statehood. This approach comports with the settled rule that navigability is determined by the river’s "natural and ordinary condition," not by later modifications or uses. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563. The trial court’s findings are thus entitled to deference, as they are based on a proper historical inquiry into the rivers’ condition at the critical date.

**III. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Title or Its Ability to Seek Compensation for the Use of Riverbeds.**

The Federal Power Act (FPA) does not divest the State of its title to riverbeds or preempt its ability to seek compensation for their use. The FPA grants the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) authority to license hydroelectric projects but does not disturb state property rights. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946) (holding that the FPA "preserves the jurisdiction of the States over the waters within their borders"). The Court has repeatedly recognized that the FPA "does not interfere with the authority of the States over the waters within their borders." *Id.* at 176.

Moreover, the FPA explicitly contemplates that state law will govern proprietary rights, including the use of riverbeds. *Id.* at 161 n.6 (noting that applicants for FERC licenses must comply with state law regarding "beds and banks"). The Court has also held that the federal navigational servitude does not preempt state property rights unless Congress has clearly authorized such preemption. *Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954). Here, the State’s claim for compensation is based on its title to the riverbeds, a proprietary right that the FPA does not displace. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76.

**Conclusion**

The trial court correctly determined that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s statehood and that the State holds title to the riverbeds under the equal footing doctrine. The court’s reliance on historical evidence was proper, and the FPA does not preempt the State’s title or its ability to seek compensation. The judgment below should be affirmed.
