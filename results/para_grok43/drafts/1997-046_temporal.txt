**ARGUMENT**

I.

The Fourteenth Amendment affords no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings. The power to grant reprieves and pardons resides exclusively in the executive and constitutes an act of grace that creates no enforceable right in the recipient. United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833) (“A pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power intrusted with the execution of the laws”). The authority is “unlimited” and “not subject to legislative control.” Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866). Because the decision whether to extend clemency lies wholly within executive discretion, it generates no substantive expectation that due process can protect. See also Burdick v. United States, 236 U.S. 79, 89 (1915) (pardon “carries an imputation of guilt” and acceptance is a matter of choice, not entitlement).

The same principle precludes any procedural claim arising from the mere existence of a clemency review. The Constitution does not convert an executive act of grace into a stage of criminal adjudication. The Due Process Clause safeguards only those interests “recognized and protected by the law of the land.” The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 81 (1873). Clemency, being outside that body of law, supplies no such interest.

II.

The option of a voluntary interview likewise raises no Fifth Amendment compulsion. The privilege against self-incrimination “is a prohibition of the use of physical or moral compulsion to extort communications.” Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 106 (1908). It applies only when the state “compels” testimony; it does not forbid a prisoner from choosing to speak. Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 596 (1896) (“The object of the [Fifth Amendment] was to insure that a person should not be compelled, when acting as a witness in any investigation, to give testimony which might tend to show that he himself had committed a crime”). Because the interview is available solely at the prisoner’s request and may be declined without penalty, no compulsion exists. The regulation itself imposes no sanction for silence and therefore does not “require a waiver” of the privilege. See Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (protection triggered only by “compulsory process”).

Accordingly, neither the clemency process nor the voluntary interview implicates any constitutional right. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
