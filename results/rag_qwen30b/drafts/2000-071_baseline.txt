**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide its facilities for religious worship or instruction, even when other secular groups are permitted to meet after hours. Milford Central School’s decision to exclude the Good News Club from using its facilities does not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First or Fourteenth Amendments. The school’s policy is viewpoint-neutral, consistent with the Establishment Clause, and properly tailored to avoid the appearance of state endorsement of religion. The Second Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Milford, and this Court should do the same.

The foundation of the Club’s claim rests on a fundamental misreading of the First Amendment’s limits on government speech and forum regulation. The school did not create a “limited public forum” open to all comers; rather, it established a *limited community use policy* that explicitly excludes religious purposes. As this Court has long held, “a State need not open its property to all comers in order to satisfy the First Amendment.” *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 601 (1972). When a government chooses to open its facilities for certain non-religious, civic, or recreational uses, it may impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions to preserve the integrity of the forum and avoid violating the Establishment Clause.

Here, Milford’s policy is precisely such a restriction. The Community Use Policy prohibits “religious purposes” and requires applicants to certify that their use complies with that restriction. This is not a content-based or viewpoint-based exclusion; it is a categorical exclusion of religious activity. The Club’s own description of its meetings—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to “receive Jesus as [a child’s] personal Savior”—demonstrates that its activities are not merely “moral and character development” from a religious perspective, but *religious instruction and worship*. As this Court has recognized, “the line between religious instruction and moral instruction is not always clear,” but “when the activity is clearly religious in nature, the government may exclude it.” *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993).

The Club’s argument that its message is “teaching morals from a religious perspective” is legally insufficient. The First Amendment does not require a public forum to accommodate speech that is “quintessentially religious” simply because it touches on a “secular” subject. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, this Court rejected the notion that religious speech is categorically outside the scope of protected expression. But *Widmar* did not hold that religious speech must be permitted in every forum. Rather, it held that when a university opens a forum to student groups, it may not exclude religious groups based on the content of their speech. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271. That principle is inapplicable here because Milford did not open a forum to *any* student or community groups. It created a *limited* use policy that explicitly excludes religious purposes. The Club was not denied access because of its viewpoint; it was denied access because its proposed use falls outside the scope of the policy’s permitted uses.

The Club’s claim that Milford’s policy is “subject discrimination” rather than “viewpoint discrimination” is legally immaterial. The distinction between subject and viewpoint discrimination is not dispositive when the government has not created a public forum at all. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the First Amendment does not require the government to provide a forum for speech simply because it has chosen to allow some speech.” *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 727 (2000). Milford’s policy is not a denial of access to a forum; it is a decision not to create one for religious purposes. The Club’s activities are not “moral and character development” in the secular sense; they are religious instruction and worship. The fact that the Club’s message is couched in moral terms does not transform it into secular speech. As this Court has stated, “there is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter.” *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6.

Moreover, Milford’s concern that permitting the Club’s meetings would violate the Establishment Clause is not only reasonable but constitutionally justified. The Establishment Clause prohibits government from “endorsing” religion, even indirectly. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393. When a public school allows religious groups to meet on its premises, especially in a setting where children are present, there is a real risk that students will perceive the school as endorsing the religious message. This risk is not speculative; it is well-documented in the jurisprudence of this Court. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court recognized that “the appearance of state sponsorship of religious activity” is a legitimate concern. Id. at 394. Milford’s policy is designed to prevent that very appearance.

The Club’s reliance on *Board of Ed. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is misplaced. *Mergens* involved a high school that had created a “limited open forum” for student groups and then denied recognition to a Christian club. The Court held that the school could not deny recognition based on the religious content of the club’s speech. But *Mergens* did not create a new constitutional right to access public school facilities for religious purposes. It merely held that when a school opens a forum to student groups, it must do so without discrimination based on viewpoint. Here, no such forum was created. Milford’s policy is not a denial of access to a forum; it is a decision not to open one for religious purposes.

Finally, the Club’s claim under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA) fails. RFRA applies only to federal laws, not to state or local policies like Milford’s. *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S. 507, 520 (1997). Even if RFRA applied, it would not override the compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. The government’s interest in maintaining the separation of church and state is “compelling,” and Milford’s policy is the least restrictive means of achieving that interest.
