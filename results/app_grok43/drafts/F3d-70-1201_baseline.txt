I. The District Court Erred by Instructing the Jury to Disregard Evidence of Subsequent Remedial Measures That Was Properly Admitted for Impeachment.

Rule 407 permits evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered to prove “impeachment or, if controverted, the feasibility of precautionary measures.” Fed. R. Evid. 407. Morbark’s opening statement and cross-examination of Infinger created the false impression that the seventeen-inch chute remained unchanged and was still in daily use by the City of DeFuniak Springs, thereby placing feasibility and continued safety in issue. The district court correctly overruled the objection and permitted rebuttal testimony that the chute had been lengthened. See Muzyka v. Remington Arms Co., 774 F.2d 1309, 1313 (5th Cir. 1985) (evidence admissible “to rebut the impression” created by opposing counsel).

Once admitted for impeachment, the testimony could not be withdrawn without prejudice. An instruction to disregard evidence properly received for a legitimate purpose constitutes reversible error when the evidence is central to a contested issue. See United States v. Cohen, 888 F.2d 770, 776 (11th Cir. 1989) (limiting instruction erroneous where evidence was admissible and its exclusion would have left the jury with a materially misleading impression). The court’s subsequent admonition therefore stripped Wood of the only effective means of countering Morbark’s misrepresentation.

Because this is a strict-products-liability action, Rule 407’s exclusionary rationale does not apply. Liability turns on the product’s condition, not the defendant’s conduct; evidence of post-accident design changes therefore does not imply “culpable conduct.” See, e.g., Herndon v. Seven Bar Flying Serv., Inc., 716 F.2d 1322, 1327-28 (10th Cir. 1983); Unterburger v. Snow Co., 630 F.2d 599, 603 (8th Cir. 1980). The district court’s contrary ruling and its later instruction to disregard the impeachment evidence were therefore erroneous.

The error was not harmless. The jury was left with the uncorrected suggestion that the original design remained in use and was regarded as safe, directly undermining Wood’s claim that the seventeen-inch chute was unreasonably dangerous. A new trial is required. See O’Rear v. American Family Life Assurance Co., 968 F.2d 906, 909 (11th Cir. 1992) (new trial warranted when evidentiary error affects substantial rights).
