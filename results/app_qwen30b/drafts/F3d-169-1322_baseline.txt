**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the appellees on the claim that they violated the Agricultural Workers Protection Act’s (AWPA) registration and insurance provisions, 29 U.S.C. §§ 1841(b), 1842, and 1854(c)(1), because the evidence established that the Burtons and Little Rock *utilized* the services of a farm labor contractor (FLC) whose registration had lapsed and who failed to carry required liability insurance. Under the plain language of the AWPA and binding precedent, the appellees’ conduct constitutes a violation of the statute, and the workers are entitled to statutory damages under § 1854(c)(1). The district court’s contrary conclusion—based on a narrow, technical interpretation of “utilization” and “employment”—misapplies the law and undermines the Act’s remedial purpose.

**I. The Burtons and Little Rock “Utilized” the Services of a Farm Labor Contractor, Triggering Liability Under § 1842**

Section 1842 of the AWPA imposes a duty on any person who “utilizes” the services of a farm labor contractor to verify that the contractor is registered and insured. The statute defines “utilize” broadly: “the term ‘utilize’ means to employ, contract with, or otherwise use the services of a farm labor contractor.” 29 U.S.C. § 1802(10). This definition is not limited to formal employment or contractual relationships; it encompasses any use of a contractor’s services in the context of agricultural labor.

Here, the Burtons and Little Rock clearly “utilized” Luxama’s services. The Burtons contracted with Little Rock to grow and market snap beans, and in turn, the Burtons relied on Luxama to supply labor for the harvest. Luxama transported the workers, directed their work, and supervised the picking process. The Burtons, in turn, directed Luxama to specific fields and received the harvested beans for transport to Little Rock’s packinghouse. The Burtons’ role was not passive; they actively managed the harvest operation, including scheduling, field assignment, and final delivery. As the Supreme Court has held, “the term ‘employ’ in the context of labor statutes is to be construed broadly to effectuate the purposes of the legislation.” *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954). The same principle applies to “utilize” under the AWPA.

The district court’s conclusion that the Burtons did not “utilize” Luxama because they did not “employ” him under the FLSA is legally flawed. The AWPA’s definition of “utilize” is independent of the FLSA’s definition of “employ,” and the statute explicitly contemplates that a person may “utilize” a contractor without being an employer. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1802(10). The Burtons’ failure to verify Luxama’s registration and insurance—despite their direct involvement in the labor process—constitutes a violation of § 1842. The fact that Luxama was not formally hired by the Burtons is irrelevant; the statute targets conduct, not labels.

**II. The Burtons and Little Rock Violated the AWPA’s Registration and Insurance Provisions**

The Burtons failed to verify Luxama’s registration, which had lapsed in 1991 due to his failure to pay a fine. 29 U.S.C. § 1842 requires that any person who utilizes an FLC must “verify the registration of the farm labor contractor.” The Burtons did not do so. Similarly, Luxama failed to carry the required liability insurance, in violation of § 1841(b), and the Burtons and Little Rock failed to ensure compliance. The district court’s finding that the Burtons “did not know” Luxama’s registration was expired does not excuse their statutory duty. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 439 (1978) (“Ignorance of the law is no excuse”); *cf.* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (statutory duties are not excused by lack of knowledge).

Little Rock, as the entity that supplied the boxes, trailer, and financial support for the crop, also “utilized” Luxama’s services. It provided the infrastructure for the harvest and received the final product. The Burtons’ reliance on Little Rock to manage the crop and market the beans demonstrates that Little Rock was not a mere passive investor but an active participant in the labor process. The district court’s conclusion that Little Rock did not “utilize” Luxama’s services because it did not directly hire or supervise the workers ignores the statutory definition and the reality of the operation.

**III. The Workers Are Entitled to Statutory Damages Under § 1854(c)(1)**

The district court erred in concluding that the workers were entitled only to statutory damages under § 1854(c)(1) *if* a violation occurred. The statute provides that “any person who violates any provision of this chapter shall be liable to the affected agricultural worker for actual damages, including damages for personal injury or death.” 29 U.S.C. § 1854(c)(1). The phrase “any person who violates” includes those who “utilize” an unregistered or uninsured FLC. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 103-477, at 11 (1994)* (“The Act imposes liability on any person who uses the services of an unregistered or uninsured contractor.”).

The Burtons and Little Rock violated § 1842 by failing to verify Luxama’s registration and insurance. The resulting injury—death and serious injury to workers—was a direct consequence of that violation. The workers are therefore entitled to actual damages under § 1854(c)(1), not merely statutory damages. The district court’s interpretation, which limits recovery to statutory damages in all cases, contradicts the plain text of the statute and undermines its remedial purpose.
