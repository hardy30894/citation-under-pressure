ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment, as the lender’s omissions extended the rescission period to three years under settled principles governing notice and waiver.

I.

A party may rescind a mortgage transaction when the lender fails to furnish material disclosures before closing. This follows from the rule that “an elementary and fundamental requirement of due process in any proceeding which is to be accorded finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections.” Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). Rand supplied neither the three-day advance HOEPA disclosure nor clear notice of the post-transaction right to cancel. The Mouas therefore possessed the extended period in which to rescind.

II.

Any purported waiver of the advance-disclosure period must be knowing and voluntary. “A waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The handwritten statement copied at closing recited only the county’s delay in furnishing payoff figures; it did not disclose the statutory rights being surrendered or the consequences of the waiver. Because the Mouas lacked knowledge of the rights they were asked to relinquish, the waiver was ineffective.

III.

Simultaneous execution of the receipt and confirmation sections of the notice likewise failed to extinguish rescission rights. Equity will not enforce a release obtained before the time for reflection has expired. See Grymes v. Sanders, 93 U.S. 55, 62 (1876) (“A court of equity will not enforce a contract made under a mistake of material fact, where the mistake is not the result of negligence.”). The Mouas signed both sections on April 22, before the three-day period had run, at the direction of the closing agent. The confirmation therefore conferred no finality.

IV.

The inclusion of a prepayment penalty, forbidden by statute, independently triggered the extended rescission right. Where a lender exacts a charge the law prohibits, the borrower may avoid the transaction. See Marshall v. Vicksburg, 82 U.S. 146, 149 (1872) (equity relieves against an unlawful exaction secured by mortgage). The note’s prepayment term rendered the mortgage voidable for the full three-year period.

The judgment should be reversed.
