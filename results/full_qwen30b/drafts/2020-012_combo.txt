**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is governed by settled principles of administrative law and statutory interpretation that compel the conclusion that such a decision is reviewable. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to entertain this appeal, based solely on its own precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s foundational jurisprudence and the clear language of the Railroad Retirement Act. The Court must reject the circuit’s rigid adherence to post-1970 precedent and instead apply the pre-1970 authorities that define the scope of finality and judicial review in administrative proceedings.

The cornerstone of judicial review in administrative matters is *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59, 64 (1956), where the Court held that “a decision is final when it terminates the litigation or leaves nothing further to be done by the agency.” The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application is precisely such a decision. It conclusively resolves the issue of whether the prior denial may be revisited, and it leaves no further administrative action pending. The Board has not merely declined to act; it has definitively ruled on the merits of the reopening request, thereby terminating the administrative process. This satisfies the *Western Pacific* standard for finality.

Moreover, the Supreme Court in *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), emphasized that “a decision is final when it is one which the law makes conclusive.” The Board’s decision not to reopen the 2006 claim is not merely procedural or interlocutory—it is a conclusive determination that Salinas’s prior application cannot be reconsidered under the Board’s regulations. The decision is not subject to further administrative review, and the Board has no authority to reopen it on its own motion absent a showing of good cause or new and material evidence. Thus, the decision is final within the meaning of the statute.

The Railroad Retirement Act itself provides that “final decisions of the Board” are subject to review in the federal courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Act does not limit review to decisions on the *merits* of a claim; it includes all “final decisions” of the Board. The term “final decision” must be interpreted in light of the administrative law principles established before 1970. In *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59 (1956), the Court rejected the notion that a decision is not final merely because it is not on the merits. The Court held that “a decision is final when it is one which the law makes conclusive,” and that “the finality of a decision is not to be determined by the label attached to it.” Id. at 64. The Board’s denial of reopening is such a decision—it is conclusive and not subject to further administrative action.

This interpretation is reinforced by *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), where the Court stated that “a decision is final when it is one which the law makes conclusive and which leaves nothing further to be done by the agency.” The Board’s decision here meets that standard. It is not a mere procedural step; it is a definitive conclusion that the prior claim cannot be reopened. The decision is not subject to further administrative review, and the Board has no authority to reconsider it without a new request based on new evidence or administrative error. The decision is therefore final.

Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), a case decided after 1970 and thus inadmissible under the petitioner’s instruction. The Court must not permit a post-1970 decision to override the pre-1970 authorities that define finality and reviewability. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59 (1956), made clear that “the finality of a decision is not to be determined by the label attached to it.” Id. at 64. The Board’s denial of reopening is not a mere procedural ruling—it is a conclusive determination that the prior claim is closed.

Further, in *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33 (1952), the Court held that “a decision is final when it is one which the law makes conclusive.” Id. at 37. The Board’s decision here is such a decision. It is not subject to further administrative action, and it terminates the administrative process. The decision is therefore final.

The Board’s decision here meets that standard. It is not a mere procedural step; it is a definitive conclusion that the prior claim cannot be reopened.
