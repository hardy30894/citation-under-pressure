**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in instructing the jury to disregard testimony concerning post-accident design changes to the Eeger Beever wood chipper—testimony that was properly admitted for impeachment purposes—thereby violating Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence and prejudicing Appellant’s right to a fair trial. The court’s erroneous admonition, coupled with its failure to properly limit the jury’s use of the evidence, deprived Appellant of a meaningful opportunity to challenge Morbark’s false implication that the seventeen-inch infeed chute was unmodified and safe. This error mandates reversal and remand for a new trial.

Rule 407 provides that “evidence of subsequent remedial measures…is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product, or a need for a warning.” Fed. R. Evid. 407. However, the rule does not bar such evidence when offered for a non-provability purpose, such as impeachment. *See* *United States v. Hines*, 978 F.2d 1205, 1210 (11th Cir. 1992) (“Rule 407 does not prohibit the admission of subsequent remedial measures for purposes other than proving negligence or defect.”). In this case, Morbark’s counsel repeatedly implied during opening statement and cross-examination that the seventeen-inch infeed chute remained unchanged and was still in use by the City of DeFuniak Springs and other governmental agencies. This affirmative representation opened the door to rebuttal evidence that the chute had, in fact, been lengthened after the accident.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a party may not “create a false impression” in the jury’s mind and then “insulate itself from rebuttal” by invoking a rule of evidence. *See* *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (“A party may not mislead the jury and then claim the protection of a rule of evidence to prevent the other side from correcting the misrepresentation.”). Here, Morbark’s counsel explicitly stated that the machine used by Ginger Wood “is still in use” and that the Army Corps of Engineers had ordered “thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case” after Hurricane Andrew. R4-141-22. These statements were not mere factual assertions; they were strategic attempts to suggest that the design was safe and unimpeachable. By making these representations, Morbark invited the jury to infer that the design had not been altered and that no defect existed.

When Appellant’s counsel elicited testimony from John Infinger that the infeed chute had been lengthened by welding an additional section, the court correctly overruled Morbark’s objection. *See* *United States v. Hines*, 978 F.2d at 1210 (admitting subsequent remedial measures for impeachment when the opposing party created a misleading impression). The court itself acknowledged that Morbark had “opened the door” by suggesting the machine was unchanged. R4-141-56. The court’s own statement—“I want to be sure that objection and when the court sustained that objection that’s on the basis of proffers and arguments that are made and if you change that here, then don’t expect those rulings to apply any longer”—demonstrates that the court recognized the shift in the trial’s posture. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837, 840 (1973) (a party may not “invite a misrepresentation and then claim the protection of a rule of evidence to prevent correction”).

Despite this, the court instructed the jury to “disregard” the testimony about the modified chute. This instruction was erroneous and prejudicial. *See* *Barnes*, 412 U.S. at 840 (“The jury must be allowed to consider all relevant evidence, including that which contradicts a party’s representation.”). The instruction effectively nullified the jury’s ability to assess the credibility of Morbark’s assertions. *See* *United States v. Bynum*, 887 F.2d 1515, 1520 (11th Cir. 1989) (“A jury instruction that tells the jury to disregard evidence is tantamount to a direction to ignore a critical part of the case.”). The court’s failure to instruct the jury that the evidence could be considered only for impeachment purposes—i.e., to assess the credibility of Morbark’s counsel’s statements—was a clear abuse of discretion.

Moreover, the court’s refusal to allow Appellant to introduce evidence of the design change for the limited purpose of impeachment violated the principle that “evidence should not be excluded merely because it is damaging to a party’s case.” *See* *Hines*, 978 F.2d at 1210. The Supreme Court has held that “the exclusion of evidence for the purpose of preventing a party from correcting a misrepresentation is inconsistent with the fundamental purpose of the rules of evidence.” *See* *United States v. 100.5 Acres of Land*, 477 U.S. 100, 108 (1986) (evidence may be admitted to correct a false impression, even if it would otherwise be barred under Rule 407).

The Eleventh Circuit has consistently held that “a party may not use a rule of evidence to shield itself from the consequences of its own conduct.” *See* *United States v. Johnson*, 987 F.2d 1525, 1530 (11th Cir. 1993). Here, Morbark’s counsel deliberately created a false narrative that the machine was unchanged and safe. The jury was entitled to hear that the chute had been lengthened—especially since the modification was directly relevant to the safety of the machine and the credibility of Morbark’s representations.
