**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the removal period. The provision must be read to contain an implicit “reasonable time” limitation, the application of which is subject to federal-court review. Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S.Ct. 2491, 2495, 2505 (2001).

Section 1231(a)(6) states that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The text supplies no explicit durational limit. Yet “indefinite detention of aliens in the former category would raise serious constitutional concerns.” Id. at 2495. This Court therefore construes the statute “to contain an implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.” Id. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, “continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503.

The constitutional concerns are substantial. Detention must be tied to a legitimate governmental purpose; when removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the purpose evaporates. See Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896) (due process protects “all persons within the territory of the United States”). The statute “does not apply narrowly to ‘a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,’” but reaches aliens ordered removed “for many and various reasons.” Zadvydas, 121 S.Ct. at 2499. Preventive detention of this breadth, untethered to removal, cannot be reconciled with the Due Process Clause. See Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary standard—that detention remains lawful so long as “good faith efforts to effectuate . . . deportation continue” and the alien fails to show deportation is impossible—demands more than the statute permits. Zadvydas, 121 S.Ct. at 2505. That test would authorize detention “no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable” removal might be. Id. The statute, read in light of constitutional avoidance, rejects such an open-ended construction.

Petitioner satisfied the governing test. After the presumptively reasonable six-month period, he provided good reason to believe there was no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. The Government offered no sufficient rebuttal. Under these circumstances, § 1231(a)(6) no longer authorizes his detention. The judgment ordering release, subject to appropriate conditions of supervision, should be affirmed.
