**Argument**

The court of appeals erred in shifting to petitioner the burden to prove withdrawal from the conspiracy before the limitations period. In a criminal prosecution, the Government must prove every fact necessary to sustain conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. See Howard v. Fleming, 191 U.S. 126, 136 (1903) (“the guilt of the accused must be shown beyond a reasonable doubt”). That includes proof that the charged conspiracy existed during the limitations period and that petitioner remained a participant during that period. A conviction cannot stand on speculation that a defendant may have once been involved; the Government must establish criminal liability with competent proof. See First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Serv. Co., 391 U.S. 253, 289-90 (1968) (rejecting a claim where the party opposing summary judgment produced “no evidence” of conspiracy participation).

A conspiracy conviction is not insulated from limitations principles simply because the indictment alleges an ongoing agreement. In antitrust and civil conspiracy cases alike, the Court has recognized that limitations run from the acts or conduct within the statutory period, not from earlier conduct outside it. Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 401 U.S. 321, 338-39 (1971), held that the statute permits recovery only for damages caused by overt acts within the limitations period; it does not allow a plaintiff to reach back to stale conduct. American Pipe & Constr. Co. v. Utah, 414 U.S. 538, 554-55 (1974), likewise treats limitations as a real temporal boundary that is either tolled or not, not as a matter of burden-shifting to the defendant. Those principles apply with even greater force in a criminal case, where liberty is at stake and the Government bears the burden throughout.

Nor does the mere fact of a conspiracy allegation relieve the Government of proving timely participation. Conspiracy liability is derivative of membership in the agreement. McDaniel v. Traylor, 212 U.S. 428 (1909), described a fraudulent “combination and conspiracy” as tying claims together by reason of the defendants’ collective conduct. But that case does not suggest that a defendant must disprove continued participation once the Government has failed to establish it. To the contrary, it confirms that liability depends on the defendant’s own participation in the unlawful combination.

The court of appeals’ contrary rule also conflicts with the ordinary treatment of limitations defenses. Zenith Radio explained that a limitations defense can bar recovery for time-barred conduct unless the plaintiff proves a timely basis for relief. 401 U.S. at 338-39. In criminal cases, by parity of reasoning, the prosecution must prove that the offense continued into the limitations period or that the defendant remained a conspirator then. It is not the defendant’s burden to prove he withdrew; rather, withdrawal is a circumstance negating the Government’s proof of continuing participation.

The D.C. Circuit’s rule would improperly convert a failure of proof by the Government into an affirmative burden on the accused. That is inconsistent with the presumption of innocence and the requirement that guilt be shown beyond a reasonable doubt. Howard, 191 U.S. at 136. It also conflicts with the Court’s repeated insistence that criminal liability may not rest on conjecture or inference piled on inference without proof sufficient to support conviction. See Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp., 340 U.S. 558, 568-69 (1951) (recognizing that a prior verdict establishes only what the jury “necessarily found”).

If the Government contends that petitioner remained in the conspiracy until the limitations period, it must prove that fact beyond a reasonable doubt. If the evidence instead shows that the conspiracy’s leaders had split before the relevant period, and the Government offers no competent proof of petitioner’s continuing membership, the conviction cannot stand. The court of appeals’ burden shift relieved the Government of its constitutional obligation and should be reversed.
