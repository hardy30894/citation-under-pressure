**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition enters a criminal trial. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that "the process of formulating a rule of criminal responsibility... is one in which the legislature of each State has wide latitude" and that due process does not compel adoption of any particular test of criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally require a defendant to shoulder the entire burden of proving insanity, it follows that a State may likewise determine the procedural channel through which mental-disease evidence is presented and considered.

*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), reinforces this deference. There, the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, reasoning that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability" remain matters of state law, and that the Court should be "reluctant to take this step in the absence of some indication... that a change in that direction is required." *Id.* at 536. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is exactly the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States rather than federalize under the Due Process Clause.

This channeling approach also has deep historical roots. The insanity defense, as classically understood since *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treats mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant—not as a mechanism for piecemeal negation of the prosecution's mens rea proof. Arizona's rule preserves that traditional separation between the affirmative defense of insanity and the prosecution's burden on the elements of the offense; it does not eliminate consideration of mental illness but simply assigns it to the doctrinal category in which American law has historically placed it.

This Court has already sustained the exclusion of closely analogous psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, holding that the jurisdiction was entitled to resist "ingrafting the doctrine of partial responsibility onto our jurisprudence" without violating due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If wholesale exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutionally permissible in *Fisher*, then Arizona's considerably more modest rule—which still allows the same evidence full consideration on the question of insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. Due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Nor does the Fourteenth Amendment "assure uniformity of judicial procedure"; States remain free "to abolish old and to create new remedies... in the exercise of a wide discretion." *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 110–11 (1908).

This Court applied these principles directly to a state rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), upholding Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence over the defendant's objection that the rule improperly influenced the jury's assessment of guilt. The Court explained that "the process of adjudication... normally requires the drawing of lines," and that state evidentiary rules are entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation is, if anything, less intrusive: it does not exclude Clark's psychiatric evidence altogether, but channels it to the historically established vehicle—the insanity defense—through which such evidence has traditionally been evaluated.

The touchstone remains whether "the absence of that fair play" so infects the proceeding as to violate due process. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941). Here, the trial court admitted the full range of Clark's expert psychiatric testimony and permitted the factfinder to weigh it in adjudicating his insanity claim—the very evidence he now insists must also be available, unconstrained by that defense's burdens, to negate intent. Nothing in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), which concerned the allocation of the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be automatically admissible, without restriction, for an entirely separate evidentiary purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's psychiatric evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, freed from the burdens attending the insanity defense, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove beyond a reasonable doubt. That is precisely the kind of channeling rule sanctioned by *Leland*, *Powell*, *Fisher*, *Spencer*, and *Twining*. Arizona's rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people" as to be fundamental, *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
