The United States respectfully submits that the D.C. Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim under CERCLA § 107 is barred because the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B). As the court below recognized, the key issue is whether the Consent Decree "resolved [Guam's] liability" for the response action or response costs.

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for potentially responsible parties (PRPs) to seek recovery of costs associated with the cleanup of hazardous waste sites. Section 107 allows for a "cost-recovery" action, while § 113(f) permits a "contribution" action. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607, 9613(f). The statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, whereas the statute of limitations for a remedial § 107 action is six years. See CERCLA § 113(g)(2)(B), (g)(3).

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree did not "resolve [its] liability" is unpersuasive. The Decree required Guam to take specific remedial actions, including closing the Ordot Dump and designing a cover system. See Consent Decree 5-12, J.A. 94-101. This is sufficient to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), as the Sixth Circuit has held that a consent decree that requires a PRP to undertake remedial actions "resolves" its liability. See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015); see also Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019) (holding that a consent decree that requires a PRP to pay for remediation costs "resolves" its liability).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has made clear that § 113(f)(3)(B) is the exclusive remedy for PRPs that have resolved their liability through a settlement. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) ("§ 113(f) provides a cause of action for contribution in certain circumstances"). Allowing Guam to pursue a § 107 claim would undermine the settlement incentives created by § 113(f)(2), which shields settling PRPs from further contribution liability. See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (noting that CERCLA's settlement provisions are designed to encourage PRPs to settle with the government).

Guam's reliance on the disclaimer of liability in the Consent Decree is misplaced. As the Ninth Circuit has observed, "parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability." Asarco LLC v. Atl. Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). The disclaimer does not negate the fact that Guam assumed significant remedial obligations under the Decree.

Furthermore, Guam's argument that the Consent Decree does not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) because it was entered under the Clean Water Act, rather than CERCLA, is unavailing. The Decree required Guam to take remedial actions that are cognizable under CERCLA, including the "collection of leachate and runoff." See CERCLA § 101(24). The fact that the Decree was entered under a different statute does not change the fact that it "resolved [Guam's] liability" for the response action.

The D.C. Circuit's decision is consistent with the decisions of other circuits that have addressed this issue. See, e.g., Hobart Corp. v. Waste Management of Ohio, Inc., 758 F.3d 757, 767 (6th Cir. 2014) (holding that a consent decree that requires a PRP to undertake remedial actions triggers § 113(f)(3)(B)); Agere Systems, Inc. v. Advanced Environmental Technology Corp., 602 F.3d 204, 228 (3d Cir. 2010) (same).

In sum, the D.C. Circuit correctly held that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B), and that Guam's § 107 claim is therefore barred. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
