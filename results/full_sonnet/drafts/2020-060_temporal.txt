# ARGUMENT

## I. The 2004 Consent Decree's Express Disclaimer of Liability Means Guam Never "Resolved" Its Liability Within the Meaning of CERCLA's Contribution Provision.

A consent decree, though entered as a judicial order, "derives its force from the fact that the parties consented to it," and its meaning must therefore be gathered from what the parties actually agreed to, not from what a court might infer they intended. *Swift & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 311, 326 (1928). Where a decree's language is unambiguous, courts must give it effect according to its terms rather than looking behind it to divine some contrary purpose. *Hughes v. United States*, 342 U.S. 353, 356–57 (1952) (construing consent decree by its own text). The 2004 Consent Decree here could not be plainer: it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." That disclaimer is not surplusage to be read out of the agreement—it is the parties' considered choice of language, and it must be honored on its own terms just as any other negotiated provision of a decree would be. *Swift & Co.*, 276 U.S. at 326.

This Court has long recognized that agreements of this kind must be read as the parties wrote them, not as one party might wish them to have been written after the fact. *Hughes*, 342 U.S. at 357. Because Guam never admitted, and the decree never adjudicated, any liability for the contamination at Ordot, the decree did not "resolve" Guam's liability for that response action. Absent a resolution of liability, the three-year contribution clock never began to run, and Guam's cost-recovery claim remains governed by the ordinary six-year period.

## II. Ordinary Canons of Statutory Construction Forbid Reading CERCLA to Produce the Absurd Result That a Party's Claim Expires Before It Can Be Brought.

Even were the Consent Decree's language less clear, settled canons of construction would counsel against the reading the court below adopted. "All laws should receive a sensible construction," and "[g]eneral terms should be so limited in their application as not to lead to injustice, oppression, or an absurd consequence." *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868). So too, "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). Congress could not have intended a scheme in which a territory that spends years attempting compliance, only to have its settlement construed retroactively as a "resolution of liability," finds its contribution clock expired before it could reasonably have known the clock was running at all.

This concern is not merely academic; it is rooted in the basic principle that a limitations period runs from the point at which a cause of action actually accrues—that is, from an "actual breach" giving rise to a complete and present claim—not from some earlier, ambiguous event. *Reading Co. v. Koons*, 271 U.S. 58, 61 (1926). To hold that the three-year period began running in 2004, when Guam had neither admitted liability nor been adjudicated liable, and when the underlying remediation obligations continued for years afterward, inverts this settled principle. Where, as here, the statutory text is genuinely susceptible of two readings, this Court's obligation is to select the one that avoids "injustice, oppression, or an absurd consequence." *Kirby*, 74 U.S. at 487. Only the reading urged by Guam does so.

## III. Fundamental Fairness and the Common-Law Structure of Contribution Confirm That the Party Responsible for Creating the Hazard—Not the Sovereign Left to Clean It Up—Must Bear the Cost.

At common law, contribution existed to ensure that when one party is compelled to discharge an obligation properly shared by another, the paying party may recoup its proportionate share from those actually responsible. *Union Stock Yards Co. v. Chicago, B. & Q. Ry. Co.*, 196 U.S. 217, 224–26 (1905). That equitable purpose is defeated, not served, if a territory that never admitted fault, and that continued to bear remediation obligations for years after entering a decree, is deemed to have forfeited its right to recover from the actual polluter before it could reasonably have brought suit.

These concerns take on constitutional weight given the character of the interests at stake. A cause of action, once accrued, is itself a form of property, and rights of this kind—including rights running against the United States—are protected against arbitrary extinguishment. *Lynch v. United States*, 292 U.S. 571, 579 (1934) ("Rights against the United States arising out of a contract with it are protected by the Fifth Amendment."). Fundamental fairness requires, at minimum, that a party be afforded fair notice before a right is cut off. *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) (notice must be "reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances," to apprise a party of the risk to its interests). Guam received no such notice that its 2004 settlement—expressly disclaiming liability—would later be construed to have started a three-year clock foreclosing recovery from the very sovereign that built, operated, and filled the Ordot Dump for nearly half a century.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
