The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, but the NLRB has developed a standard for permitting employers to conduct polls. The NLRB's standard requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll (Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1989 WL 224426, citing Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)). However, several courts of appeals have rejected this standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice (e.g., NLRB v. A.P.W. Prods. Co., 316 F.2d 899 (2d Cir. 1963); Celanese Corp. of America v. NLRB, 279 F.2d 204 (7th Cir. 1960)).

In Celanese Corp. of America, 95 N.L.R.B. 664 (1951), the NLRB itself recognized that an employer may have a good-faith doubt about a union's majority status, even if the doubt is not objectively reasonable.

The NLRB's standard is overly restrictive and fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment (NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (8th Cir. 1965)). In Stoner Rubber Co., 123 N.L.R.B. 1440 (1959), the NLRB held that an employer's poll of employees was lawful, even though the employer did not have objective evidence of a loss of majority support.

In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union (cf. NLRB v. Gulfmont Hotel Co., 362 F.2d 588 (5th Cir. 1966)). The NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The Supreme Court has held that the NLRB's decisions must be based on substantial evidence (NLRB v. Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. 656 (1949)). In this case, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence.

Furthermore, the NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships (NLRB v. Sands Mfg. Co., 306 U.S. 332 (1939)). By allowing employers to conduct polls only when they have objective evidence of a loss of majority support, the NLRB's standard may actually destabilize collective-bargaining relationships (cf. NLRB v. Mayer, 313 F.2d 216 (6th Cir. 1963)).

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is not rational and consistent with the NLRA, and the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful.
