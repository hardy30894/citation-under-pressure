ARGUMENT

I. The Pat-Frisk of Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment Because the Officers Lacked Particularized, Reasonable Suspicion That He Was Armed and Dangerous.

The Fourth Amendment permits a limited weapons frisk only where an officer can identify "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). A frisk is justified only when the officer "observes unusual conduct which leads him reasonably to conclude in light of his experience that criminal activity may be afoot and that the persons with whom he is dealing may be armed and presently dangerous." Id. at 30. This standard is not satisfied by generalized unease; it requires suspicion tied to the particular individual searched. As the Supreme Court explained the same year it decided Terry, "[t]he police officer is not entitled to seize and search every person whom he sees on the street or of whom he makes inquiries" merely because the circumstances are suspicious in the abstract. Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968). The officer must point to facts specific to that individual indicating he is armed, not merely that he is present in a tense or high-crime setting.

Here, the officers' suspicion of Soares rested on two generalized observations: a momentary "furtive movement" toward the console before the car even stopped, and Soares's agitated, verbally abusive behavior after the stop. Neither supplies the individualized armed-and-dangerous suspicion Terry demands. The pre-stop movement was ambiguous and shared by the driver as well; it was not conduct distinctly suggestive of a weapon. And Soares's anger and profanity, however unpleasant, is conduct fully consistent with an innocent passenger frustrated by what he believed was a pretextual stop. Nervous or hostile behavior toward police, without more, has never been held the "specific and articulable fact" that a person is armed. See Terry, 392 U.S. at 21.

This case is unlike Terry itself, where the officer observed the suspects repeatedly walk past a store window and confer, conduct consistent with "casing" a target for a robbery, before frisking them. Id. at 6-7, 28. Soares's conduct — arguing with police during a nighttime stop, at gunpoint, after being falsely accused of "driving while Black" — is not comparable to conduct suggesting he was preparing to commit an armed offense. Where the individualized indicia present in Terry are absent, the intrusion of a frisk cannot stand.

Nor can the government rely on "good faith" or the officers' subjective unease to substitute for the objective showing Terry requires. "Good faith on the part of the arresting officers is not enough" to justify a Fourth Amendment intrusion. Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89, 97 (1964). Reid's own testimony that he "got a little uneasy" reflects precisely the kind of subjective, inarticulate hunch that the Fourth Amendment does not tolerate as a substitute for particularized fact. See Henry v. United States, 361 U.S. 98, 101 (1959) (suspicion alone cannot substitute for the objective showing the Fourth Amendment requires). As the Court has cautioned in the analogous probable-cause context, courts must assess "the factual and practical considerations of everyday life on which reasonable and prudent men, not legal technicians, act," Brinegar v. United States, 338 U.S. 160, 175 (1949), and those considerations do not support treating an angry, talkative passenger as one reasonably believed to be armed.

The distinction between the driver, Lee, and the passenger, Soares, further undermines the frisk. Lee voluntarily consented to a pat-frisk; no such consent existed as to Soares. The automobile context does not dilute the individualized-suspicion requirement; even in the automobile-search context, the Fourth Amendment demands "reasonable grounds" particular to the circumstances, not a blanket license to search all occupants of a stopped vehicle. See Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132, 153-54 (1925). And because "the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places," Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967), the officers were required to justify the frisk of Soares specifically — not simply invoke the tense atmosphere of the stop as a whole.

II. The Firearm Must Be Suppressed as the Fruit of the Unlawful Frisk.

Because the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment, the handgun discovered as a direct result of that unlawful search must be suppressed. "[A]ll evidence obtained by searches and seizures in violation of the Constitution is, by that same authority, inadmissible." Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). The gun here was recovered only because Lapolis pat-frisked Soares without the constitutionally required suspicion; the evidence is therefore tainted at its source and constitutes the "fruit of the poisonous tree." Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484-88 (1963). No intervening act of free will or independent source purges that taint: Soares's brief resistance to the frisk was a direct and predictable reaction to the unlawful intrusion itself, not an independent act breaking the causal chain. See id. at 486.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress should be reversed, and the handgun evidence should be suppressed as the product of an unconstitutional search.
