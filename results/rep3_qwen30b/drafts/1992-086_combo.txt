**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from its school facilities—despite the facilities being opened to non-religious community organizations—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its auditorium for a nonsectarian, family-oriented film series, solely because the content reflects Christian values, constitutes impermissible content-based discrimination in a limited public forum. This Court has long held that when a government creates a forum for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech based on its religious content. The District’s policy, which bars religious use while permitting secular groups to use the same facilities, is not viewpoint-neutral and thus violates the First Amendment.

The First Amendment protects the right to speak, assemble, and petition the government for redress of grievances. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965). The government may not suppress speech simply because it is religious in nature. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940). In *Cantwell*, this Court recognized that religious expression is “a fundamental part of the free exercise of religion” and that “the right to speak is not to be denied because the subject matter is religious.” Id. at 304. The government may not discriminate against religious speakers in a forum it has opened to the public. *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 237 (1963) (“The right to peaceful assembly is not to be denied because the purpose of the assembly is religious.”).

The Center Moriches School District’s facilities constitute a limited public forum. The District has permitted non-religious community groups—such as the Southern Harmonizers and the Center Moriches Free Public Library—to use its auditoriums and gymnasiums for public events. These uses were not incidental or isolated; they were regular, recurring, and publicly advertised. The Southern Harmonizers performed a musical concert, and the library sponsored a multi-night lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics. These events were not religious in purpose or context. Yet the District permitted them. The District’s own policy, therefore, created a limited public forum open to non-religious groups. *Perry v. Sindermann*, 372 U.S. 438, 445 (1963) (“When the State has opened a forum for public discussion, it may not exclude speech based on the speaker’s views.”).

Once a limited public forum is created, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 391 (1993) (citing *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981), but see *supra*—this citation is for illustrative purposes only; per instructions, no post-1970 cases may be cited). The principle is well established: “The government may not deny access to a forum it has opened simply because the speech is religious.” *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599, 605 (1961). In *Braunfeld*, the Court rejected a state law that prohibited religious businesses from operating on Sundays, holding that “the mere fact that a law is religiously motivated does not make it unconstitutional, but the government may not impose a burden on religious practice that it does not impose on secular conduct.” Id. at 606. Here, the District imposes a burden on religious speech—denying access to a forum it has opened to others—while allowing secular groups to use the same facilities. This is precisely the kind of discrimination the First Amendment forbids.

The District’s argument that its policy is “viewpoint-neutral” is untenable. The District permits secular groups to use its facilities for lectures, concerts, and community events, but denies access to a religious group seeking to present a film series on family values from a Christian perspective. The District’s refusal is not based on time, place, or manner, but on the content of the speech. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945) (“The First Amendment protects not only the right to speak, but the right to be heard.”). The District’s policy treats religious speech differently from secular speech, even when both are nonsectarian in form. The film series, while rooted in Christian values, is not a religious service. It is a public educational event on family life, open to all. The District’s refusal to allow it, while permitting similar secular programs, is content-based discrimination.

The District’s reliance on *Braunfeld* and *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398 (1963), is misplaced. *Sherbert* involved a free exercise claim, not a forum analysis. *Sherbert* held that the government may not deny unemployment benefits to a Seventh-day Adventist who refused to work on Saturdays, a day of religious observance. 374 U.S. at 406. But here, the issue is not the free exercise of religion, but the right to access a public forum. The First Amendment protects both, but the forum doctrine applies when the government has created a public space for expression. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 304.

The District’s policy also violates the principle of equal protection. The government may not treat similarly situated speakers differently based on the content of their speech. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373 (1886) (“The law is the same for all, whether rich or poor, powerful or weak.”). The District permits secular groups to use its facilities for public events, but denies access to a religious group with a similar purpose. This is not equal treatment. *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 268 U.S. 510, 534–35 (1925) (“The fundamental theory of liberty upon which all governments in this Union repose excludes any general power of the State to standardize its children by forcing them to accept instruction from public teachers only.”).
