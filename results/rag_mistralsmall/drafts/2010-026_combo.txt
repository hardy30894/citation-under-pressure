**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ninth Circuit erred in expanding FOIA Exemption 2 to shield ESQD arc maps that are not “internal personnel rules and practices.”

FOIA’s Exemption 2 permits withholding only “matters that are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit below adopted a “predominantly internal” test that lacks any textual or precedential foundation. This Court has long cautioned that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed” and are “explicitly exclusive.” Dep’t of Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976) (quoting S.Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 3 (1965)). The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” formulation—endorsed by the Ninth Circuit—finds no support in the statute’s plain terms or this Court’s decisions.

This Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to expand Exemption 2 beyond its statutory bounds. In Rose, the Court rejected a broad reading of Exemption 2, emphasizing that the exemption applies only to “trivial matters” such as parking and lunch-hour rules. Id. at 363. The Court contrasted these mundane matters with “more sensitive” information, which Congress addressed in other exemptions. Id. at 369. The “predominantly internal” test conflates Exemption 2 with Exemption 7(F), which protects law enforcement techniques that could endanger safety. See, e.g., United States v. Alger, 152 U.S. 384, 388 (1894) (distinguishing between internal rules and sensitive operational details).

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with this Court’s instruction that FOIA exemptions must be “clearly delineated.” Rose, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The “predominantly internal” test is amorphous and invites agencies to shield operational details under the guise of internal practices. This undermines FOIA’s core purpose: ensuring public access to government records unless a specific exemption applies. See id. at 360 (quoting S.Rep. No. 813 at 3).

II. The ESQD arc maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices” and do not risk circumvention of agency regulation.

The Navy’s ESQD arc maps are operational safety tools, not internal personnel rules. They are used to design storage facilities and organize ammunition operations—functions that are not “personnel rules and practices” under any reasonable construction. See Murray v. Joe Gerrick & Co., 291 U.S. 315, 318 (1934) (distinguishing between internal rules and operational practices).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. Hardy involved a law enforcement manual governing search warrants—clearly an internal practice. Id. at 655. The ESQD arc maps, by contrast, are not used to regulate personnel or govern agency operations in the same way. They are technical tools for safety planning, akin to engineering diagrams or hazard assessments. See Cole v. Young, 351 U.S. 536, 543 (1956) (distinguishing between personnel rules and operational functions).

Moreover, the maps do not present a risk of circumvention. The Navy’s claim that disclosure would allow “reverse engineering” of ammunition storage is speculative and unsupported by the record. The maps are not classified, and the Navy has not explained how their disclosure would circumvent any regulation. See United States v. Hendee, 124 U.S. 309, 314 (1888) (requiring specific evidence of circumvention risk).

III. The Ninth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s precedents on FOIA’s structure and purpose.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA’s exemptions must be narrowly construed. See, e.g., Rose, 425 U.S. at 361; United States v. MacDaniel, 32 U.S. 1, 10 (1833) (construing exemptions strictly). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test eviscerates this principle by allowing agencies to shield operational details under Exemption 2.

The Court has also warned against conflating Exemption 2 with other exemptions. In Rose, the Court distinguished Exemption 2 from Exemption 7(F), which protects law enforcement techniques that could endanger safety. 425 U.S. at 369. The Ninth Circuit’s approach blurs this line, treating operational safety data as if it were a personnel rule. This is inconsistent with FOIA’s structure and purpose.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit erred in expanding Exemption 2 to shield the ESQD arc maps. The maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices,” and their disclosure does not risk circumvention of agency regulation. The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and remand for disclosure of the requested documents.
