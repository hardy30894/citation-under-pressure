**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Carpenter’s procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that a prisoner must not only raise a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel but must do so in a procedurally proper manner. *See Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) (“[A]n ineffective-assistance claim may constitute cause for a procedural default if the petitioner can show that the defaulted claim is ‘so novel’ that the prisoner could not reasonably have been expected to raise it.”); *see also Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446, 451 (2000) (holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse another default unless it is "sufficiently substantial" to meet the carrier standard).

The Sixth Circuit’s approach conflates the standards for cause and prejudice. A claim of ineffective assistance of counsel must itself be properly exhausted or excused before it can serve as cause to excuse another default. *See Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 489 (“[T]he mere fact that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is easier to prove in a particular case does not excuse the default of the underlying claim.”). The Court has made clear that a prisoner cannot bootstrap a procedurally defaulted claim into cause for another default. *See id.* at 490 (“[A]n ineffective-assistance claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim.”).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule would undermine the finality of state court judgments and encourage piecemeal litigation. *See Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 732 (1991) (“[A] state prisoner’s federal habeas petition should not be entertained unless the prisoner has first exhausted his state remedies.”). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would permit prisoners to circumvent exhaustion requirements and evade the procedural rules of state courts. *See id.* at 731 (“[T]he exhaustion doctrine is designed to give the state courts a full and fair opportunity to resolve federal constitutional claims before those claims wind their way through the federal court system.”).

**II. The Sixth Circuit erred in finding that Carpenter’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective.**

Even if this Court were to assume, arguendo, that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of his sufficiency claim, the Sixth Circuit erred in finding that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective. To establish ineffective assistance of counsel, a prisoner must show both that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that Carpenter’s counsel was ineffective is unsupported by the record and misapplies *Strickland*.

The Sixth Circuit relied on the fact that Carpenter’s counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence on direct appeal. However, this Court has held that a claim of insufficient evidence is not cognizable on habeas review unless it was raised and rejected on the merits in state court. *See Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 635 (1993) (“[A] federal habeas court may not award relief to a state prisoner on the ground that the evidence was insufficient to convict unless the state court’s rejection of the claim was both ‘contrary to’ and ‘involved an unreasonable application of clearly established Federal law.’”). Carpenter’s counsel cannot be faulted for failing to raise a claim that was not ripe for review under *Brecht*.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s finding of prejudice is speculative. *Strickland* requires a showing that “there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different.” *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694. The Sixth Circuit did not identify any evidence that would have undermined the sufficiency of the evidence supporting Carpenter’s conviction. Without such evidence, the court’s prejudice finding is unsupported. *See id.* at 696 (“[T]he defendant must show that the errors actually had an adverse effect on the defense.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Sixth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim, and the Sixth Circuit erred in finding that Carpenter’s appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective.
