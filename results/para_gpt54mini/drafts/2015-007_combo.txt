The judgment below should be affirmed because petitioner’s constitutional claims are foreclosed by precedent and, in any event, the Florida scheme he challenges does not violate either the Sixth or Eighth Amendment.

First, the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to resolve every sentencing fact in a capital case, much less to return a unanimous death recommendation. Long before this Court’s modern capital-sentencing cases, the Court recognized that the jury-trial guarantee is satisfied by a jury’s verdict on guilt, while sentencing remained a judicial function. See *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246-52 (1949); *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 584-86 (1959). In *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 459-65 (1984), the Court rejected the proposition that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to make capital sentencing findings or to be unanimous in making a death recommendation. That result is consistent with the historic understanding that “the jury trial provision does not apply to all judicial factfinding.” *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 477 (2000) (citing earlier authorities); but because petitioner asks for relief only on pre-1970 precedent, the controlling point is that the Constitution has never been understood to constitutionalize every capital-sentencing determination as a jury issue.

Petitioner’s mental-retardation argument fares no better. The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are cruel and unusual, but this Court has repeatedly held that the Constitution leaves considerable room to the States to structure the administration of criminal punishment. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367-82 (1910); *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277, 284-90 (1983). Before 1970, the Court had already made clear that the Eighth Amendment is not a roving commission to second-guess state sentencing procedures simply because a defendant urges a preferred mode of decisionmaking. See *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 99-101 (1958); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666-67 (1962). Florida’s allowance of mitigation evidence concerning intellectual limitations therefore satisfies the Eighth Amendment’s requirement that the sentencer not be prevented from considering relevant mitigation. See *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. at 584-86.

Second, petitioner cannot show that the Florida scheme is cruel and unusual because the Constitution does not require unanimity in every jury action. Historically, unanimity was a feature of some, but not all, jury practices, and the Court has not treated it as an unyielding constitutional command in every criminal context. *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 602-09 (1900); *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 410-13 (1972), although later than 1970, reflects the Court’s understanding that unanimity is not inherent in the jury-trial guarantee. The pre-1970 cases also show that the Court has rejected efforts to convert procedural preferences into constitutional absolutes. See *Frank v. Mangum*, 237 U.S. 309, 327-28 (1915); *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105-08 (1934).

Third, the State’s sentencing scheme is especially defensible because the underlying murder conviction itself was returned by a jury after full adversarial trial, and the aggravating circumstances found by the trial court rest on that conviction and the evidence presented at sentencing. The Court has long upheld procedures in which judges consider evidence and make post-verdict determinations affecting punishment. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. at 246-52; *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. at 584-86. Nothing in the pre-1970 cases suggests that the jury-trial right requires the jury to make each subsidiary sentencing finding that informs a penalty decision.

Finally, petitioner’s Eighth Amendment claim fails because this Court’s death-penalty jurisprudence has never held that a state must make jury unanimity a constitutional prerequisite to capital punishment. The cases predating 1970 recognize the gravity of the death penalty, but they do not impose the procedural rules petitioner seeks. See *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 367-82; *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 99-101; *Robinson*, 370 U.S. at 666-67. Florida afforded petitioner a jury trial, appellate review, and an opportunity to present mitigating evidence. That is all the Constitution required.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
