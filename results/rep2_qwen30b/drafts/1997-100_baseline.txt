**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional protection is not limited to capital cases; it applies whenever a defendant has been acquitted of a criminal charge, regardless of whether the proceeding was capital or noncapital in nature. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding—violates settled precedent and undermines the fundamental principle that no person may be twice put in jeopardy for the same offense.

In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a capital murder enhancement could not be retried on that enhancement at a subsequent sentencing hearing. The Court emphasized that “the essence of the double jeopardy protection is that a defendant should not be subjected to multiple prosecutions for the same offense.” Id. at 439. The Court further explained that the critical inquiry is whether the sentencing proceeding “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” Id. at 439–440. In *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), the Court reaffirmed this analysis, noting that “a sentencing proceeding may be so similar to a trial on guilt or innocence as to trigger double jeopardy protections.” Id. at 209–210.

Here, the prior conviction allegation was tried before a jury under California’s statutory framework, which provides for a full trial with evidentiary rules, cross-examination, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993); *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 228 (1996). The jury found the prior conviction allegation *not true*—an acquittal on a factual issue that directly affects the severity of punishment. That finding was not merely procedural; it was a determination of fact that carried legal consequence. To allow retrial of the same factual issue would subject Monge to a second prosecution for the same offense: the offense of being a repeat offender under the Three Strikes law.

The lead opinion attempts to distinguish *Bullington* by asserting that the Supreme Court has “suggested” the doctrine does not apply in noncapital cases. *Monge v. California*, 17 Cal.4th 823, 836 (1998). But this suggestion is neither binding nor accurate. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court noted that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, but did not hold that their reasoning was confined to capital proceedings. Rather, the Court addressed only the question of prospective application under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and observed that the application of double jeopardy to noncapital sentencing was not then clearly established. Id. at 392. The Court never ruled that such application is barred in noncapital cases. Indeed, in *U.S. v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against successive prosecutions for the same offense—even when the offense is defined by a sentencing enhancement. Id. at 155 (“[T]he Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal.”).

Moreover, the distinction between statutory and constitutional protections advanced by the majority is legally unsound. The fact that California grants certain procedural rights via statute does not diminish the constitutional significance of those rights. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly held, “the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a judge to have hearings and to give a convicted person an opportunity to participate in those hearings when he comes to determine the sentence to be imposed.” *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 606 (1967). Yet when a state chooses to provide a trial on sentencing issues, it must afford due process—including, where appropriate, double jeopardy protection. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949) (“The due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.”). The state’s decision to grant a jury trial on prior convictions creates a situation indistinguishable from a trial on guilt or innocence for double jeopardy purposes.

Finally, the majority’s claim that capital sentencing proceedings are uniquely burdensome because they involve life-or-death stakes fails to withstand scrutiny. The harm of being subjected to a second prosecution for the same offense is not diminished by the absence of a death penalty. The core concern of the Double Jeopardy Clause is the risk of governmental harassment and the erosion of finality. *Green v. United States*, 356 U.S. 165, 171 (1958). When a jury has acquitted a defendant of a prior conviction allegation, the government cannot circumvent that verdict by relitigating the same facts in a separate sentencing phase. Such a practice violates the very purpose of the Clause.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court must be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation once a jury has acquitted the defendant on that issue, even in a noncapital case. To hold otherwise would permit the government to retry a defendant on the same factual issue after an acquittal—a result squarely prohibited by the Constitution.

For these reasons, the petition should be granted, and the judgment of the California Supreme Court reversed.
