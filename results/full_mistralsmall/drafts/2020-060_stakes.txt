**ARGUMENT**

I. **GUAM’S SECTION 107 CLAIM IS TIMELY AND UNAFFECTED BY THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE**

The District of Columbia Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA triggered § 113(f)(3)(B) of the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery action. The court below conflated the concepts of “resolving liability” and “incurring response costs,” a distinction this Court has repeatedly emphasized. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 61 (1998) (noting that CERCLA liability attaches to those who “arrange for disposal” of hazardous substances, not merely those who later pay for cleanup). The Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability to the United States for the Ordot Dump’s contamination; it merely compelled Guam to undertake remedial actions under the Clean Water Act (CWA), 33 U.S.C. § 1362 *et seq.* As such, it did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), and Guam’s § 107 claim remains timely.

A. **The Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability Under CERCLA**

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that the Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability rests on an erroneous interpretation of § 113(f)(3)(B). That provision permits contribution claims only where a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has cautioned that “resolving liability” under CERCLA requires a determination of responsibility for the underlying contamination—not merely an agreement to perform cleanup work. *See Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004) (holding that § 113(f)(3)(B) applies only where a party has “admitted liability or been adjudged liable” in a settlement).

Here, the Consent Decree explicitly disclaimed any admission of liability by Guam. *See Consent Decree*, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam Jan. 24, 2008), at 3 (“[T]his Decree is based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.”). This disclaimer is dispositive. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding no “resolution of liability” where the settlement expressly disclaimed liability). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding misreads § 113(f)(3)(B) by treating a CWA enforcement action as a CERCLA liability determination. *See Guam v. United States*, 966 F.3d 1, 22 (D.C. Cir. 2020) (erroneously conflating CWA compliance obligations with CERCLA liability).

B. **Guam’s § 107 Claim Accrued When It Incurred Response Costs, Not When the Consent Decree Was Entered**

CERCLA § 107(a)(4)(B) permits cost-recovery actions by any person who “incurred necessary costs of response.” 42 U.S. C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). The statute of limitations for such claims runs from the date the plaintiff incurs the costs, not from the date of a prior settlement. *See United States v. Colorado & E. R.R. Co.*, 50 F.3d 1530, 1535 (10th Cir. 1995) (“The limitations period for a § 107 claim begins to run when the plaintiff first incurs response costs.”). Guam incurred its response costs between 2004 (when the Consent Decree was entered) and 2011 (when the Ordot Dump was closed). *See* Amended Complaint ¶¶ 15, 25 (alleging that Guam expended over $160 million in remediation costs). Thus, its § 107 claim is timely, as it was filed in 2017—well within CERCLA’s six-year limitations period. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that the Consent Decree triggered § 113(f)(3)(B) and thus barred § 107—rests on a misapplication of *Aviall*. In *Aviall*, the Supreme Court held that a party that has resolved its liability in a settlement may seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B), but it did not hold that such a settlement precludes a § 107 claim. 543 U.S. at 167. The Court expressly distinguished between § 107 and § 113, noting that § 107 provides a direct cause of action for incurred costs, while § 113 provides a derivative contribution action. *Id.* at 161–62. The D.C. Circuit’s holding thus conflicts with Supreme Court precedent.

II. **THE NAVY’S LIABILITY AS A “RESPONSIBLE PARTY” UNDER CERCLA § 107(A) IS CLEAR**

The Navy is a “covered person” under CERCLA § 107(a) because it “arranged for disposal” of hazardous substances at the Ordot Dump. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(3). The Supreme Court has held that a party may be liable under § 107(a)(3) if it “exercised control over the hazardous substances” and “disposed of them.” *Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. at 66. The Navy undisputedly operated the Ordot Dump from the 1940s through the Vietnam War, disposing of munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange. *See* Amended Complaint ¶ 11. This conduct falls squarely within CERCLA’s liability scheme.

The D.C. Circuit’s suggestion that the Navy’s relinquishment of sovereignty over Guam in 1950 insulated it from liability is meritless. CERCLA liability is not tied to sovereignty; it is tied to the act of arranging for disposal. *See id.* at 55 (liability attaches to “those who were involved in the chain of disposal”). The Navy’s continued use of the Ordot Dump after 1950—even as Guam assumed ownership—demonstrates its ongoing role in arranging for disposal. *See id.* at 66 (finding liability where a parent company “operated” a facility despite not owning it).

III. **GUAM’S CLAIMS DO NOT IMPLICATE THE “SINGLE REMEDY” RULE**

The D.C. Circuit erroneously invoked the so-called “single remedy” rule to bar Guam’s § 107 claim. That rule, derived from *United States v. Cannons Eng’g Corp.*, 899 F.2d 79, 87 (1st Cir. 1990), holds that a party cannot pursue both § 107 and § 113 for the same response costs. However, the rule applies only where a party has already sought contribution under § 113. *See id.* Here, Guam has not pursued a § 113 claim; it has brought a § 107 claim for costs it incurred. The D.C. Circuit’s application of the rule was thus premature and erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed. The 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability under CERCLA, and its § 107 claim is timely. The Navy is a responsible party under § 107(a), and Guam’s claims do not implicate the “single remedy” rule. The Court should remand with instructions to reinstate Guam’s complaint.
