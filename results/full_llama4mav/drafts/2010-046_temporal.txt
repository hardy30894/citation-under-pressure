ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Is Not Appealable

The State's appeal of the district court's October 27 Order must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The Order is not final within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1291. As this Court has held, a civil contempt order is ordinarily not appealable until the district court has adjudicated the contempt motion and applied sanctions. See SEC v. Naftalin, 407 F.2d 1003 (8th Cir. 1969); Fox v. Capital Co., 96 F.2d 684 (3d Cir. 1938). The October 27 Order clearly contemplates further proceedings, as it requires the State to show cause why it should not be held in contempt if it fails to transfer $250 million to the Receiver by November 5, 2008. This is not a final order, and the State's appeal must be dismissed.

The State argues that awaiting sanctions will not enable it to raise its challenges to the underlying order, citing United States v. United Mine Workers of America, 330 U.S. 258 (1947). However, that case does not support the State's position. In United Mine Workers, the Supreme Court held that a party may not challenge an underlying order in a contempt proceeding if it had the opportunity to appeal the order and failed to do so. Id. at 294-95. Here, the State had the opportunity to appeal the original consent orders and the order appointing the Receiver, but failed to do so.

II. The Petition for Writ of Mandamus Should Be Denied

The State's petition for a writ of mandamus should also be denied. The five factors set forth in Bauman v. United States Dist. Court are not met in this case. See Ex parte Fahey, 332 U.S. 258 (1947); Roche v. Evaporated Milk Ass'n, 319 U.S. 21 (1943). First, the State has an adequate means to attain relief through a direct appeal of a final order. Second, the State will not be damaged or prejudiced in a way not correctable on appeal. Third, the district court's order is not clearly erroneous as a matter of law. The order is based on the consent orders and the receivership, which the State did not contest at the time.

The State's reliance on cases such as In re National Labor Relations Board, 124 F.2d 209 (7th Cir. 1941), is misplaced. In that case, the court issued a writ of mandamus to compel the district court to vacate an order that was beyond its jurisdiction. Here, the district court's order is not beyond its jurisdiction, and the State has not shown that it is clearly erroneous.

In conclusion, the State's appeal and petition for writ of mandamus should be denied. The district court's order is not final and appealable, and the State has not met the requirements for issuance of a writ of mandamus.
