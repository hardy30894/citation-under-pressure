**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Statute of Limitations is Triggered by Knowledge of the Act, Not Knowledge of the Legal Violation.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a plaintiff must have knowledge that the underlying act was *imprudent* to trigger the three-year statute of limitations under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). Under the law of this Court, a statute of limitations is triggered by knowledge of the *act* itself, not knowledge of the legal violation. In *United States v. Blumenthal*, 332 U.S. 539 (1947), the Court explained that a person is guilty of a crime if he knows the *act* is being done, not necessarily that it is illegal. Id. at 543. This principle applies equally to the discovery rule in civil statutes of limitations. Here, Sulyma knew that Intel invested the retirement funds in alternative assets. He did not need to know that those investments were legally *imprudent* for the statute of limitations to begin to run. By requiring Sulyma to possess legal sophistication regarding the prudence of the investments, the Ninth Circuit effectively read the "actual knowledge" requirement out of the statute.

**II. "Actual Knowledge" Includes Constructive Knowledge.**

The Ninth Circuit also erred by ignoring the concept of constructive knowledge. The Court has held that "actual knowledge" is satisfied if the facts are such that a reasonable person would know. *United States v. Morton Salt Co.*, 338 U.S. 632, 635 (1950). Intel disclosed the nature of the alternative investments and the strategy behind them through Fund Fact Sheets and other documents. Sulyma had constructive knowledge of these facts because they were available to him. The fact that Sulyma chose not to read the documents does not negate the statute of limitations. As the Court noted in *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22 (1953), a person is guilty of a crime if he knows the *act* is being done, not necessarily that it is illegal. Id. at 25. Similarly, a plaintiff is barred by the statute of limitations if the facts are such that a reasonable person would have known of the breach, regardless of whether the plaintiff actually read the relevant documents.

**III. A Plaintiff’s Failure to Read Disclosures Does Not Reset the Statute of Limitations.**

Finally, the Ninth Circuit created a new rule that a plaintiff’s lack of recollection regarding reading documents creates a genuine issue of material fact. This rule is inconsistent with established principles of constructive knowledge. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court held that a defendant has "actual knowledge" of a fact if the facts are such that a reasonable person would know. Id. at 397. Here, Intel provided the information. Sulyma’s testimony that he did not recall seeing the documents is insufficient to overcome the presumption of constructive knowledge. The statute of limitations began to run when the investments were made and disclosed, not when Sulyma finally chose to review the materials. Therefore, the district court properly granted summary judgment for Intel.
