**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privileges of conducting activities within the forum state, such that the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). The mere fact that a product sold elsewhere eventually reaches a forum state does not, by itself, establish the minimum contacts required for specific personal jurisdiction. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (citing *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980)); but see *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987) (reaffirming *World-Wide Volkswagen*). The Supreme Court has consistently held that jurisdiction must be based on the defendant’s own conduct, not on the fortuitous or unforeseeable actions of third parties. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297.

In this case, Ford Motor Company did not purposefully direct its activities toward Montana. It did not design, manufacture, or sell the vehicle in Montana. The vehicle was assembled in Kentucky and first sold to a dealership in Washington State. The dealership sold it to an Oregon resident, who later transferred it to a Montana purchaser. The tire failure occurred on a Montana highway, but the injury was not caused by any act or omission of Ford within Montana. The causal chain between Ford’s conduct and the injury is severed by the intervening actions of multiple independent third parties. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. The mere foreseeability that a product might eventually reach a forum state is insufficient to establish jurisdiction. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana by advertising and selling parts in the state is legally unsound. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 478 Mont. 1, 297 P.3d 1200 (2013). The sale of replacement parts, even if distributed through a network of dealerships, does not constitute purposeful availment when the parts are not used in the manufacture or repair of the vehicle that caused the injury. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). In *Hanson*, the Court held that a corporation’s mere presence in a state through a subsidiary was insufficient to establish jurisdiction where the claim arose from the operation of a trust in another state. Similarly, Ford’s sale of parts in Montana does not create a sufficient nexus to the injury caused by a tire failure in a vehicle that never entered Ford’s distribution chain in Montana.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires that the exercise of jurisdiction be reasonable. *Schaffer v. Heitner*, 433 U.S. 186, 204 (1977). The burden on the defendant must be weighed against the forum state’s interest in adjudicating the dispute. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476. Here, Montana has no substantial interest in the litigation. Ford has no offices, employees, or manufacturing facilities in Montana. The vehicle was not sold or serviced in Montana. The injury occurred on a public highway, but the cause of the injury—tire tread separation—was not the result of any action by Ford within Montana. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476. The state’s interest in protecting its residents is not sufficient to justify jurisdiction where the defendant has not purposefully directed its activities toward the state.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Conway v. Benefis Health System*, 2013 MT 73, 297 P.3d 1200, and *Newbury v. State Farm*, 2008 MT 156, 184 P.3d 1021, is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance coverage and the collateral source rule, not personal jurisdiction. *Conway* and *Newbury* did not address the constitutional limits on jurisdiction. They are inapposite. The Court in *Hanson* emphasized that “the mere fact that a product may be used in a state does not subject the manufacturer to jurisdiction there.” *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253. The same principle applies here: the fact that a Ford vehicle was driven in Montana does not subject Ford to jurisdiction in Montana.

The Court in *World-Wide Volkswagen* held that “a manufacturer’s general awareness that its products will be distributed through channels that may reach the forum State is not enough.” *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297. The same is true here. Ford did not target Montana. It did not advertise in Montana. It did not sell vehicles in Montana. The vehicle was not even registered in Montana at the time of the accident. The injury was not foreseeable as a result of Ford’s conduct in Montana. *Asahi Metal Industry Co.*, 480 U.S. at 113.

The Due Process Clause also requires that the defendant have fair notice that it may be haled into court in a particular state. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. Ford had no reason to anticipate that it would be subject to litigation in Montana. The vehicle was sold in Washington and Oregon. The chain of title and use was entirely outside Ford’s control. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. To hold otherwise would violate fundamental notions of fair play and substantial justice. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316.
