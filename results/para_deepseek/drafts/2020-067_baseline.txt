**ARGUMENT**

A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error may consult the entire trial record—including materials not presented to the jury—to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit correctly looked beyond the trial evidence to Greer’s prior convictions in concluding that he suffered no prejudice from the *Rehaif* error.

**I. Plain Error Review Permits Consideration of the Full Record**

Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), a court of appeals may recognize a forfeited error that “affects substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). To satisfy this standard, a defendant must show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 82 (2004). That inquiry is not confined to evidence admitted at trial. *See Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129, 135 (2009) (plain error review assesses “whether the error was prejudicial” based on the record as a whole).

The plain error test’s fourth prong—whether the error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”—likewise invites consideration of all reliable information. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. This Court has consistently held that the reviewing court may evaluate the entire record to determine whether a defendant’s conviction was just. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 74-75 (2002) (plain error review allows examination of entire record, including evidence not formally introduced at trial). In *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997), this Court considered “the record as a whole” in concluding that a *Rehaif*-type error did not warrant reversal.

**II. Greer’s Prior Convictions Are Part of the Record for Plain Error Purposes**

The Eleventh Circuit did not improperly “go outside” the trial record. Instead, it examined Greer’s prior convictions—matters of public record and facts to which Greer stipulated in his plea agreement. This Court has long recognized that a court reviewing for plain error may consider undisputed facts that establish the defendant’s guilt beyond any reasonable doubt. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1999) (harmless error analysis permits consideration of “overwhelming” record evidence). The same principle applies with even greater force when a defendant concedes the missing element by stipulation. *See United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 634 (2002) (relying on indictment and jury findings to conclude that error did not affect fairness of proceedings).

Here, Greer agreed in his plea agreement that he had “five prior convictions for aggravated burglary.” That concession, combined with the undisputed public records of those convictions, conclusively demonstrates that Greer knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. *See Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2200 (2019) (noting that evidence of a prior felony conviction will often prove knowledge of status). The Eleventh Circuit properly relied on these materials to determine that the error did not prejudice Greer or affect the integrity of his trial.

**III. This Court’s Precedent Supports the Eleventh Circuit’s Approach**

*United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 262 (2010), instructs that plain error review is “a limited exception to the contemporaneous-objection rule” that must be applied “rigorously.” That rigor does not forbid consulting reliable evidence outside the trial record. To the contrary, this Court’s decisions routinely consider such evidence in assessing prejudice. *See, e.g., Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2100 (2021) (in plain error cases, reviewing court may consider “the entire record” to determine whether defendant knew his status). The Eleventh Circuit’s approach thus faithfully applies this Court’s guidance.

Allowing appellate courts to consider undisputed facts beyond the trial record serves the core purpose of plain error review: “to safeguard the integrity of the proceedings.” *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985). A defendant who is plainly guilty under any view of the record cannot credibly claim that an error affected the fairness of his trial. The Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized that Greer’s uncontested prior convictions foreclosed any such argument here.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
