# ARGUMENT

**A COMPANY THAT PURCHASES DEFAULTED DEBT AND COLLECTS IT FOR ITS OWN ACCOUNT IS A CREDITOR, NOT A "DEBT COLLECTOR," UNDER THE FDCPA**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates "debt collectors," not creditors. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Petitioners ask this Court to erase that distinction by holding that any entity which acquires debt after default automatically becomes a "debt collector," regardless of whether it collects for itself or for another. That theory contradicts the statutory text, this Court's precedent, and the structure Congress enacted.

## I. The Statutory Text Distinguishes Debt Collectors From Creditors Based on Agency, Not the Default Status of the Debt

Section 1692a(6) defines a "debt collector" as any person "who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). This Court has recognized that the operative phrase requires collection of debts "owed [to] ... another." *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). Santander collected debts owed to *itself*—debts it had purchased and now held as creditor—not debts "owed or due another."

Petitioners attempt to manufacture ambiguity by arguing that "another" could refer to whoever originally held the debt, even after a subsequent sale extinguished that relationship. But as the Eleventh Circuit correctly explained in rejecting an identical argument, "our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether [the purchaser] regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to [the purchaser]; our inquiry is whether [the purchaser] regularly collects on debts owed or due another *at the time of collection*." *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (emphasis added); *accord Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) ("The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another.'"). When Santander engaged in the collection conduct challenged here, the debts were owed to Santander alone. It was not acting on behalf of CitiFinancial or anyone else.

Petitioners' reliance on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii)—which excludes from the debt-collector definition "any person collecting ... any debt ... owed or due another ... which was not in default at the time it was obtained"—inverts the statute's logical structure. That provision is an *exclusion* from an otherwise-applicable inclusive definition; it presupposes that the person already satisfies one of the threshold definitions of "debt collector" by collecting debt "owed or due another." Only then does the exclusion ask whether the debt was in default when acquired. As the court below correctly held, "the overall structure of § 1692a(6) makes clear that when assessing whether a person qualifies as a 'debt collector,' we must first determine whether the person satisfies one of the statutory definitions given in the main text of § 1692a(6) before considering whether that person falls into one of the exclusions." Because Santander never satisfied the threshold requirement of collecting debt "owed or due another," the default-status exclusion in (F)(iii) is simply irrelevant to Santander's status. *See Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314 ("[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. [Instead] ... he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements.").

## II. Petitioners' Default-Timing Rule Would Rewrite the Statute and Contradicts Its Purpose

Petitioners' rule—that acquiring debt after default transforms a creditor into a debt collector—finds no textual footing. Congress could easily have written the definition to turn on default status; instead, it turned on *whose* debt is being collected. The legislative history confirms this: Congress excluded "mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others" from FDCPA coverage "so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing." S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3–4 (1977), *reprinted in* 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. That provision addressed *servicers acting for others*, not entities like Santander that purchase debt outright and collect for their own account.

The FDCPA's stated purpose reinforces this reading: "It is the purpose of this subchapter to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors." 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Congress targeted the unique incentive problems of third-party collectors—who have no ongoing relationship with the debtor and profit only from aggressive collection—not creditors collecting their own accounts, who bear the reputational and business consequences of their own conduct. See *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 294 (analyzing the Act's application based on the nature of the collection relationship, not the debt's history).

Petitioners' theory would also produce absurd and unadministrable results. Under their approach, a "company such as Santander—which, as a consumer finance company, lends money, services loans, collects debt for itself, collects debt for others, and otherwise engages in borrowing and investing its capital—would be subject to the FDCPA for all of its collection activities simply because one of its several activities involves the collection of debts for others." That cannot be squared with a statute whose liability provisions require that "all the statutory elements at the time of and in connection with the prohibited conduct" coincide. A violation of § 1692e, for example, requires proof that the *defendant was acting as a debt collector* at the time of the alleged misrepresentation—not that it once occupied that role with respect to a different creditor's account.

## III. Petitioners' Complaint Confirms Santander Acted as Creditor, Not Debt Collector

The complaint itself forecloses petitioners' theory. It alleges that CitiFinancial sold petitioners' loans to Santander for "a few cents on the dollar," and that only *after* this purchase did Santander engage in the challenged collection conduct. Nowhere does the complaint allege that Santander collected on behalf of CitiFinancial after the sale. To the contrary, the debts became Santander's own. Having purchased the receivables outright, Santander stood in the shoes of a creditor collecting its own debt—conduct Congress placed outside the FDCPA's reach.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
