**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Reach an Alien Whose Reentry Occurred Before Its Effective Date.**

Section 241(a)(5) applies when the Attorney General finds that “an alien has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed.” The conduct the statute identifies is reentry. Petitioner reentered in 1982—fifteen years before the statute became effective. The text does not say “who reentered before or after this section’s enactment,” nor does it say “who is found in the United States after the statute’s effective date.” At most, the statute is silent on temporal reach.

A statute is not to be construed as retroactive unless “the language expressly declares or necessarily implies” that result. *United States v. American Sugar Refining Co.*, 202 U.S. 563, 576 (1906). If the words are not so clear as to compel a retrospective construction, the statute operates prospectively. *Sohn v. Waterson*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 596, 601 (1873). “The policy of the law is against giving statutes a retrospective operation.” *Lowry v. International Bhd. of Boilermakers*, 259 U.S. 1, 9 (1922). This canon applies with full force to immigration laws. *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536, 559 (1884).

The Tenth Circuit inferred congressional intent from the statute’s failure to carve out pre-enactment reentrants. That inverts the presumption. The question is not whether Congress excluded old cases; it is whether Congress expressly included them. Here, there is no expression at all. The term “has reentered” is grammatically indifferent to the date of reentry. The presumption resolves that ambiguity prospectively.

The Attorney General cannot avoid this result by saying that petitioner was “found in” the United States after 1997. Continued presence is not a new reentry. In immigration law, “entry” is an event, not a continuing condition. *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947). If continued presence were enough, the word “reentered” would be surplusage. Congress knows how to reach aliens “who are present” in the country; it did not use that language here.

**II. Applying the Statute to a Pre-1997 Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive.**

A retrospective law “attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past.” *St. Louis & Iron Mountain Ry. Co. v. Paul*, 173 U.S. 404, 409 (1899). The 1997 reinstatement statute does exactly that. It permanently bars an alien who has once reentered from “any” relief under the Immigration and Nationality Act, including adjustment of status. Before 1997, no statute imposed that categorical bar on petitioner’s completed 1982 reentry. The new law therefore changed the legal meaning of a past act.

The deportation cases the government may invoke are different. In *Harisiades v. Shaughnessy*, 342 U.S. 580 (1952), and *Galvan v. Press*, 347 U.S. 522 (1954), Congress had made “membership” in the Communist Party—wherever and whenever it occurred—an express ground of removal. The Court upheld those statutes only after finding that Congress had clearly chosen a retrospective reach. No comparable expression appears here. The phrase “reinstated from its original date” describes the administrative force of the prior order once the statute is lawfully invoked; it does not state that the alien’s reentry may have occurred before the statute existed.

The lower court’s “no protectable expectation” rationale is insufficient. The rule against retroactive statutes is a canon of statutory construction, not a penalty rule. Even if deportation is not criminal punishment, *Mahler v. Eby*, 264 U.S. 32, 39 (1924), the presumption against retroactivity remains. An alien’s lack of a right to remain does not permit a court to rewrite a statute to reach past conduct unless Congress plainly said so. *American Sugar Refining*, 202 U.S. at 576; *Chew Heong*, 112 U.S. at 559.

At the very least, the presumption requires this Court to construe the statute to apply only to illegal reentries occurring after its effective date. That construction avoids the serious retroactivity question. Because Congress did not clearly say otherwise, the judgment below should be reversed.
