**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the ESQD documents fall within Exemption 2. That exemption protects only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The statutory text and this Court’s precedent require a narrow construction that the “predominantly internal” test adopted below cannot satisfy.

FOIA mandates disclosure unless material falls within one of nine “explicitly exclusive” exemptions that “are to be interpreted narrowly.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)); *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). Exemption 2 therefore reaches only rules and practices “related solely” to internal personnel matters. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The Court in *Rose* recognized that disclosure of certain internal materials could risk circumvention of agency regulation, but it tied that concern to documents whose “disclosure may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” *Id.* at 369. Nothing in *Rose* authorizes withholding on the ground that documents are merely “predominantly internal.”

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” formulation from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), expands Exemption 2 beyond both the statutory language and *Rose*. The phrase “related solely” does not admit a “predominantly” qualifier. Materials that concern facility planning, explosive-storage limits, and public-safety distances are not “solely” about internal personnel rules; they directly affect surrounding communities and are therefore outside the exemption’s text.

The ESQD documents illustrate the mismatch. They quantify blast radii and quantity-distance arcs used to site magazines and limit explosive loads. Their disclosure would reveal information about potential effects on nearby civilian areas, not internal hiring, training, or disciplinary practices. Because the documents are not confined to “internal personnel rules and practices,” they fall outside Exemption 2 regardless of any asserted risk of circumvention.

The Navy’s affidavits confirm that the arcs serve long-term planning and safety compliance, not personnel administration. Allowing withholding whenever documents are “predominantly internal” would permit agencies to shield any operational data under a label the statute does not contain. Such an approach contravenes the command that exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for disclosure or consideration of other exemptions.
