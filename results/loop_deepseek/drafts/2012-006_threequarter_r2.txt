**ARGUMENT**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a charged conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period. This fundamental principle, rooted in the presumption of innocence and the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, compels reversal. *See Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) (requiring proof of “every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged”); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895) (holding that the presumption of innocence places on the prosecution “the burden of proving every element of the crime charged beyond a reasonable doubt”); *Hopt v. Utah*, 120 U.S. 430, 440–41 (1887) (requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt of each element of the offense). A conspiracy is a continuing offense; it must extend into the limitations period for a conviction to stand. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910) (a conspiracy “is a partnership in criminal purposes” that “may have continuation in time” and the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period). Consequently, the jury must find that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy within the statutory period.

Withdrawal from a conspiracy, when it occurs before the limitations period, does not shift the burden of persuasion to the defendant. Rather, it negates an essential element of the government’s case—membership during the limitations period. This Court has long recognized that a defendant’s unilateral acts may sever his connection to the conspiracy, but withdrawal is an affirmative defense only where the government has already established membership within the limitations period. *See Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 452–53 (1949) (Jackson, J., concurring) (noting the “continuing nature of the conspiracy” and the need for the government to prove “the defendant’s participation in the common plan”); *United States v. Nearing*, 252 U.S. 489, 499–500 (1920) (requiring the government to prove that the defendant “was a member of the conspiracy” during the relevant period). Critically, the government must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt that, notwithstanding any evidence of withdrawal, the defendant remained a member during the relevant time. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (“[I]t is not a permissible legislative device to relieve the prosecution of its burden of proving every essential element of the offense.”); *Miles v. United States*, 103 U.S. 304, 312 (1880) (the government must prove “every ingredient of the crime” beyond a reasonable doubt).

The lower court erred by effectively requiring petitioners to disprove their membership during the limitations period. The government’s evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy split up before the limitations period began. That evidence, at a minimum, created a reasonable doubt as to whether petitioners were still part of any single, ongoing conspiracy. The jury should have been instructed that the government had the burden of proving membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt, and that any evidence of withdrawal—even if insufficient to constitute an affirmative defense—could be considered in determining whether the government had carried its burden. The court’s failure to place this burden squarely on the government cannot be excused as harmless error, because the jury could have credited the withdrawal evidence and still convicted on the mistaken belief that the defendants bore the burden of proving they were not members during the limitations period.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed, and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions on the burden of persuasion.
