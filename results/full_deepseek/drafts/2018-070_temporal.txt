**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) adopts state law as surrogate federal law only to fill gaps in federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether a gap exists. That reading contradicts the plain text, purpose, and this Court’s precedent.

**I. OCSLA’s Text and Purpose Require a Gap in Federal Law Before State Law May Apply**

OCSLA provides that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting condition. It means state law operates only where federal law does not already occupy the field. This Court recognized as much in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), explaining that OCSLA was designed to “define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the outer Continental Shelf. Because federal law was “never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself,” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953), Congress borrowed state law to fill the inevitable gaps.

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied this gap-filling principle in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), holding that state law may be assimilated only when there is a “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. That decision, decided before 1970, reflects the proper understanding of OCSLA. The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of the gap requirement reads the limiting phrase “to the extent that” out of the statute.

**II. The Fair Labor Standards Act Leaves No Gap for State Wage and Hour Laws**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”), 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219, is a comprehensive federal scheme governing wages, overtime, and meal periods. It establishes minimum wage and overtime standards and provides a federal remedy for violations. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves state wage and hour laws only within the states’ own territorial jurisdiction. On the outer Continental Shelf—an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction under OCSLA, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1)—the savings clause does not create a gap. Federal law already covers the subject matter.

This Court has long held that comprehensive federal regulatory schemes preempt state law in areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction. *See United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 384 U.S. 224, 230 (1966) (federal law governs activities on federal enclaves). The FLSA’s detailed provisions—including regulations on meal periods, 29 C.F.R. § 785.19—demonstrate that Congress intended federal law to be the exclusive source of wage and hour protections on the OCS. No gap exists.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Improperly Expand State Law Beyond Constitutional Limits**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow state law to apply even when federal law fully addresses the subject. That approach conflicts with the constitutional concerns that originally limited OCSLA’s adoption of state law. In *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293–94 (1958), this Court upheld the prospective adoption of state law under the Assimilative Crimes Act only because that Act “fills a gap” in federal criminal law. The Court emphasized that Congress may adopt state law as federal law only where federal law is “deficient.” *Id.* at 294. OCSLA’s 1975 amendment, which allowed prospective adoption of state law, did not alter the gap-filling requirement. *Sharpnack* confirms that state law cannot displace comprehensive federal law.

**IV. The District Court Correctly Dismissed Newton’s Claims**

Because the FLSA leaves no gap, California’s wage and hour laws cannot apply as surrogate federal law under OCSLA. The district court properly granted judgment on the pleadings. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding should be reversed.
