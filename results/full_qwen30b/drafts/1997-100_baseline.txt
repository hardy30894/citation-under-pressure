**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional guarantee is not limited to trials on the underlying criminal charge; it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense” after an acquittal or a reversal based on insufficient evidence. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings involving prior conviction allegations—contravenes settled precedent and undermines the fundamental protection against governmental harassment through successive prosecutions.

The Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a defendant is subjected to a second trial on the same factual issue, even if that issue is collateral to the main charge. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a capital sentencing enhancement—specifically, the aggravating circumstance that he had committed a prior felony—could not be retried on that same issue after the state appellate court reversed the trial court’s finding due to insufficient evidence. The Court emphasized that the sentencing hearing in a capital case “is not a mere formality” but “a critical stage of the criminal proceeding” that “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 439, 440. The Court concluded that “the State’s interest in obtaining a final determination of the sentencing issue is not sufficient to justify a second prosecution for the same offense,” particularly when the first proceeding ended in a reversal based on evidentiary insufficiency. *Id.* at 440.

The lead opinion in *Monge* attempts to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it was a capital case. But the Supreme Court has never held that *Bullington*’s reasoning is confined to capital proceedings. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), the Court observed that *Bullington* and *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), were “capital cases,” and that their reasoning was “based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Id.* at 392. Yet the Court did not hold that the double jeopardy protection in those cases was limited to capital cases. Indeed, the Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to any “second prosecution for the same offense,” regardless of the severity of the punishment. *U.S. v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 155 (1997) (“The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.”); *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995) (“The Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense.”).

The California Supreme Court’s assertion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply in noncapital cases because sentencing proceedings are “not trials on guilt or innocence” is legally untenable. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a proceeding must be a “trial on guilt or innocence” to trigger double jeopardy protection. In *Bullington*, the Court explicitly rejected the idea that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable simply because the proceeding is part of sentencing. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. The Court held that the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence”—such as the presentation of evidence, the burden of proof, and the finality of the determination—were present in the capital sentencing hearing, and that these characteristics “make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 439–440.

The same hallmarks are present in the noncapital sentencing proceeding at issue here. The trial court conducted a formal, jury-tried hearing on the prior conviction allegation. The prosecution bore the burden of proving the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. The defense had the right to cross-examine witnesses, to present evidence, and to challenge the authenticity of the documents. The court took judicial notice of the prior conviction, but the prosecution also introduced a “prison packet” and an abstract of judgment. The trial was not a mere administrative formality; it was a full adversarial proceeding with procedural safeguards. When the appellate court reversed the finding due to insufficient evidence, the state was barred from retrying the same issue—precisely the protection the Double Jeopardy Clause was designed to ensure.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection in noncapital cases is misplaced. *Williams* held that due process does not require a trial on sentencing issues, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court in *Williams* emphasized that sentencing is not a trial on guilt, and that judges may rely on information obtained outside the courtroom. *Id.* at 245–246. But that does not mean that when a state chooses to provide a trial on a sentencing issue—such as a prior conviction—it may then strip the defendant of the constitutional protection against retrial after a reversal based on insufficient evidence. The state’s decision to grant a jury trial under Penal Code § 1025 does not diminish the defendant’s constitutional rights; it enhances them. The fact that the state has chosen to provide a trial does not mean it may then subject the defendant to a second prosecution on the same issue after a reversal based on insufficient evidence.

The dissent’s argument that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because the prior conviction is not “the same offense” is also flawed. The offense in question is not the prior crime itself, but the *allegation* that the defendant has suffered a prior conviction.
