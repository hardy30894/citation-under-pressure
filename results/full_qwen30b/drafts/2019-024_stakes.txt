**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA, 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), bars a claim where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” in the context of a fiduciary duty claim under § 1104. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may lack “actual knowledge” of a breach even when the information was available and disclosed, misreads the plain language of the statute and contradicts binding precedent. The Court should reverse.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplies the “Actual Knowledge” Standard by Requiring Knowledge of Imprudence, Not Just the Transaction

The statute provides that an action under § 1104 “may not be commenced more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The phrase “actual knowledge” is not defined in ERISA, but this Court has long held that “actual knowledge” means “knowledge of the facts that constitute the breach,” not knowledge of the legal wrong. *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1831 (2015). In *Tibble*, the Court emphasized that the statute of limitations “begins to run when the plaintiff knows of the facts that constitute the breach,” not when the plaintiff knows the breach violates ERISA. Id. at 1832. This principle is rooted in the common law of statutes of limitations, where the trigger is the plaintiff’s awareness of the operative facts, not the legal consequences.

The Ninth Circuit, however, created a new and impermissible standard: that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge not only of the transaction but also of its imprudence. *Sulyma v. Intel Corp.*, 935 F.3d 1058, 1065 (9th Cir. 2019). The court held that “actual knowledge” requires awareness that the fiduciary acted imprudently. Id. This interpretation is contrary to *Tibble* and the settled understanding of the statute. The Court in *Tibble* rejected the idea that “actual knowledge” requires knowledge of the legal standard of prudence. Id. at 1832 (“The statute does not require knowledge of the law.”). To require knowledge of imprudence—i.e., a legal conclusion—would effectively rewrite the statute to require knowledge of the law, which *Tibble* explicitly forbids.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also contradicts this Court’s precedent in *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), which the court itself cited but misapplied. In *Blanton*, the trustees were parties to a transaction that violated ERISA. The court held that the statute of limitations began to run when the trustees “had actual knowledge of the transaction,” not when they learned it was unlawful. Id. at 991–92. The court explicitly rejected the argument that knowledge of the law was required. Id. The same logic applies here: if a plaintiff knows the investment was made—i.e., that the fund allocated 20–25% to hedge funds—then the “transaction” has occurred, and the limitations period begins. Whether that allocation was imprudent is a legal question, not a factual one that triggers the statute.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Creates a Dangerous Loophole That Undermines ERISA’s Purpose

The Ninth Circuit’s rule allows plaintiffs to avoid the statute of limitations by simply failing to read disclosures, even when those disclosures are clear, comprehensive, and accessible. This result is inconsistent with ERISA’s goal of promoting fiduciary accountability and ensuring timely resolution of disputes. As this Court has recognized, “ERISA’s statutory framework is designed to provide certainty and finality in the administration of employee benefit plans.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1830. A rule that permits a plaintiff to delay suit indefinitely by claiming ignorance of information he could have easily accessed undermines that purpose.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s approach is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Tibble* that the limitations period “begins to run when the plaintiff knows of the facts that constitute the breach.” Id. at 1831. Here, Intel provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and Annual Disclosures—documents that explicitly disclosed the allocation to alternative investments, the strategy behind them, and the associated risks. These documents were available on Intel’s website and were accessible to Sulyma during his employment. The fact that Sulyma did not read them is not a basis for excusing the statute of limitations. As this Court has held, “a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by failing to investigate.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1832. The availability of information, not the plaintiff’s personal diligence, is the relevant inquiry.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with the Text and Purpose of ERISA

ERISA’s three-year limitations period is a “statute of repose,” designed to prevent stale claims and to encourage prompt resolution of disputes. *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1831. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that such statutes must be applied with precision to avoid undermining their purpose. Id. The Ninth Circuit’s rule, which allows a claim to proceed even when the plaintiff had access to all material facts and chose not to examine them, transforms the limitations period into a mere formality. It permits plaintiffs to “sit on their rights” indefinitely, relying on the excuse of non-reading, while fiduciaries are left vulnerable to claims years after the events in question.

This Court should not countenance such a result. The statute of limitations under § 1113(2) is triggered by “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not by the plaintiff’s subjective failure to read. When a fiduciary discloses the material facts—such as the allocation of assets to alternative investments—the breach is complete, and the limitations period begins. The plaintiff’s failure to read the disclosure does not excuse the running of the statute. To hold otherwise would render the limitations period meaningless and invite abuse.
