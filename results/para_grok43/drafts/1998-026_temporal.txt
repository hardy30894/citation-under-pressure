The respondent submits that a district court's failure to advise a defendant of the right to appeal, in violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32, furnishes no ground for collateral relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 when the defendant already knew of that right and elected not to appeal.

A collateral attack may not serve as a substitute for a timely appeal. Sunal v. Large, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947). Only where exceptional circumstances render the judgment void or fundamentally unfair does habeas corpus lie. Id. at 179. The mere omission of a required judicial statement does not constitute such an exceptional circumstance.

This Court has applied that principle to an analogous omission under Rule 32. In Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the district court failed to afford the defendant an opportunity to speak before sentence, as Rule 32(a) then required. The Court held that "the failure of the District Court to follow the mandate of the Rule is not of itself an error which would justify relief under § 2255." Id. at 428. The same reasoning governs here. Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant learns of the right to appeal. When an evidentiary hearing establishes that the defendant already possessed that knowledge, the rule's purpose has been fulfilled and the omission occasions no prejudice.

Harmless-error principles confirm the result. A judgment may be set aside on collateral attack only when the error affected substantial rights. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946). Knowledge of the right to appeal negates any claim that the defendant was deprived of a meaningful opportunity to seek review. The court below therefore correctly concluded that the Rule 32 violation was harmless and that habeas relief is unavailable.

For these reasons the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
