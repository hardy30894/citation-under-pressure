**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing appellants’ WARN Act claims on the basis of releases signed under coercive conditions that violated the Act’s core protections. The releases are invalid because they were not knowing and voluntary, were obtained in circumvention of the WARN Act, and are void as against public policy.

**I. The Releases Are Invalid Because They Were Not Knowing and Voluntary**

A waiver of federal statutory rights must be “knowing and voluntary.” *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 704 (1945). This standard requires that employees have “a full understanding of their rights” and “adequate time” to consider the waiver. *O’Hare v. General Marine Transport Corp.*, 740 F.2d 160, 168 (2d Cir. 1984). Here, DHL gave drivers only six days—from December 5 to December 11—to decide whether to accept the ten-week plan and sign a release waiving all WARN claims. That period is presumptively inadequate. *See EEOC v. Sundance Rehabilitation Corp.*, 466 F.3d 490, 498 (6th Cir. 2006) (holding that a seven-day consideration period for ADEA waivers is “the minimum” and may be insufficient under the circumstances). Moreover, DHL did not provide any explanation of the WARN Act rights being waived. *Cf. Pugh v. Tribune Co.*, 521 F.3d 686, 694 (7th Cir. 2008) (requiring “clear and unambiguous” waiver of ERISA rights). The releases also required employees to simultaneously complete a job rebid form, creating confusion and pressure. Such “economic duress” vitiates consent. *United Steelworkers of America v. North Star Steel Co.*, 5 F.3d 39, 44 (3d Cir. 1993) (duress invalidates WARN release).

**II. The Releases Were Obtained in Violation of the WARN Act**

The WARN Act requires employers to provide 60 days’ notice before a mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). An employer may not circumvent this obligation by offering severance in exchange for early resignation without first giving the required notice. *Local 217, Hotel & Restaurant Employees Union v. MHM, Inc.*, 976 F.2d 805, 809 (2d Cir. 1992) (WARN notice must precede any “employment loss”). DHL announced the closure on November 10, 2008, but the releases were signed as early as December 11—before the 60-day period expired on January 9, 2009. By inducing employees to resign early, DHL effectively shortened the notice period for those workers. *See Dill v. City of Edmond*, 155 F.3d 1193, 1198 (10th Cir. 1998) (WARN Act prohibits “piecemeal” layoffs that avoid notice). The releases themselves acknowledge that employees were “giving up my employment relationship” and “any recall rights.” This is precisely the “employment loss” the Act seeks to delay. *Carpenters District Council v. Dillard Department Stores*, 15 F.3d 1275, 1281 (5th Cir. 1994) (WARN notice must be given before any employee is separated).

**III. The Releases Are Void as Against Public Policy**

The WARN Act embodies a “strong public policy” favoring advance notice to allow workers to prepare for job loss. *Kildea v. Electro-Wire Products, Inc.*, 144 F.3d 400, 405 (6th Cir. 1998). A release that waives future WARN claims without the employee having received the statutory notice is void. *Bledsoe v. Emery Worldwide Airlines*, 1999 WL 334948, at *4 (6th Cir. 1999) (unpublished) (release of WARN claims invalid where employer failed to provide required notice). Here, DHL gave no individual WARN notice to the 506 workers who signed releases; it merely negotiated with the union. The WARN Act requires notice to “each affected employee” or their representative. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). Even if union notice sufficed, the releases were signed before the 60-day period ended, meaning the employees never received the full benefit of the notice. *See 20 C.F.R. § 639.3* (employer cannot “circumvent” WARN by offering severance in lieu of notice). The releases therefore contravene the Act’s purpose and are unenforceable.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for proceedings on the merits of appellants’ WARN Act claims.
