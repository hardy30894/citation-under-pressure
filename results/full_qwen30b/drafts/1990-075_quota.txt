**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), constitutes a valid and constitutional delegation of legislative authority to the Attorney General, and the subsequent sub-delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) Administrator is fully authorized under settled principles of administrative law and separation of powers. The Toubys’ challenge fails because Congress has provided an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s exercise of power, and the delegation is consistent with the Court’s long-standing jurisprudence affirming Congress’s ability to empower executive officials to act in response to urgent public safety threats.

**I. Congress Has Provided an Intelligible Principle for Temporary Scheduling Under § 811(h)**

The non-delegation doctrine does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to the Executive Branch so long as it establishes an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee’s exercise of discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This principle is not a rigid standard but a flexible one, requiring only that Congress set forth “some standard to guide the exercise of the delegated power.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 377 (1989). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress has provided clear criteria, even when those criteria are broad or general. *Id.* at 376–77.

Section 811(h) satisfies this test by mandating that the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only if it poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” and only after considering specific, enumerated factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These factors are not vague or amorphous; they are concrete, policy-oriented benchmarks that reflect Congress’s judgment about what constitutes a legitimate basis for emergency regulation. The requirement that the Attorney General consider the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ evaluation further ensures that medical and scientific expertise informs the decision. *Id.* § 811(h)(4). This structure is no less precise than the standards upheld in *Mistretta*, where Congress directed the Federal Sentencing Commission to consider “the nature and circumstances of the offense, the history and characteristics of the defendant, and the need for the sentence imposed” — a framework that the Court found sufficient to satisfy the intelligible principle test. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 377.

The Toubys’ argument that the delegation is impermissible because it allows the creation of new crimes is foreclosed by precedent. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that the mere power to criminalize activity renders a delegation unconstitutional. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 521 (1935), were invalidated not because they created crimes, but because they lacked any standard to guide the President’s discretion. In contrast, § 811(h) provides multiple, specific criteria that must be met before a substance may be temporarily scheduled. The presence of such standards renders the delegation constitutionally sound.

**II. The Sub-Delegation to the DEA Administrator Is Fully Authorized**

Congress may delegate authority to an executive agency so long as the delegation is consistent with the statutory framework and the agency is acting within the scope of its statutory mandate. *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001). The Attorney General’s authority to delegate functions under the Controlled Substances Act is explicitly recognized in 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which authorizes the sub-delegation of “functions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This regulation, issued under the Attorney General’s statutory authority, is a valid exercise of administrative discretion.

The DEA Administrator’s role in temporary scheduling is not independent but derivative. He acts only upon the Attorney General’s authority and under the constraints of § 811(h). The Administrator’s actions are subject to notice-and-comment procedures under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 553, and are reviewable in the courts for arbitrary or capricious action. *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983). The Toubys’ claim that the sub-delegation is invalid because the DEA is not a “legislative” body is irrelevant; the Constitution does not require that delegated authority be exercised only by elected officials. *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579, 635 (1952) (Jackson, J., concurring) (noting that Congress may delegate power to administrative agencies to carry out legislative functions).

**III. Judicial Review Is Not Precluded, and the Delegation Is Not Unconstitutional**

The Toubys argue that § 811(h)(6)’s provision that “no court shall have jurisdiction to review any order issued under this subsection” precludes constitutional review. But this provision does not bar review of constitutional challenges. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977), holds that “the availability of judicial review is presumed” and that Congress must show “clear and convincing” evidence to foreclose review of constitutional claims. No such evidence exists here. The legislative history of § 811(h) reflects Congress’s intent to allow judicial scrutiny of the Attorney General’s compliance with the statutory criteria, not to immunize the delegation from constitutional scrutiny. *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987).
