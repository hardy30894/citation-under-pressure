The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Clause is intended to protect individuals from discriminatory state action based on their membership in a particular class or group, not from arbitrary or irrational treatment as an individual. As the Court held in Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the unlawful administration of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination. (Snowden, 321 U.S. at 8). The Court further stated that "a discriminatory purpose is not presumed; there must be a showing of 'clear and intentional discrimination.'" (Id. at 8, quoting Gundling v. Chicago, 177 U.S. 183, 186 (1900)).

Moreover, in Tigner v. Texas, 310 U.S. 141 (1940), the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause allows states to make classifications, as long as they are not arbitrary or unreasonable. (Tigner, 310 U.S. at 147). The Court emphasized that the Constitution does not require things which are different in fact or opinion to be treated in law as though they were the same. (Id., citing Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920)).

In this case, respondent Olech did not allege membership in a class or group. Rather, she claimed that the Village's demand for a 33-foot easement was motivated by a "spiteful effort to 'get' her" (Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, 160 F.3d 386, 387 (7th Cir. 1998)). Allowing a "class of one" claim would effectively constitutionalize run-of-the-mill zoning and land-use disputes, which is not the intended purpose of the Equal Protection Clause. The Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a cause of action for a "class of one."
