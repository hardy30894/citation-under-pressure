**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding it had subject matter jurisdiction over this declaratory judgment action. The Pressls’ claim arises solely under Virginia state law and concerns the interpretation and scope of a privately held flowage easement. No federal question is presented, and the mere fact that the easement was created in connection with a federally licensed hydroelectric project does not confer federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 or 16 U.S.C. § 825p. Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, its dismissal of the complaint must be vacated, and the case must be remanded to state court.

**I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 Because the Pressls’ Claim Does Not Raise a Federal Question.**

The jurisdiction of federal courts under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 is limited to cases “arising under the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States.” A claim “arises under” federal law only when the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on the construction or interpretation of federal law. *Gully v. First National Bank*, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936). Where the claim is based entirely on state law, even if federal law is tangentially involved, federal jurisdiction is not proper. *Hartford Fire Ins. Co. v. California*, 113 S. Ct. 1497, 1502 (1993) (citing *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 112); see also *Patsy v. Board of Regents*, 457 U.S. 496, 500 (1982) (citing *Gully*).

Here, the Pressls’ claim is grounded in Virginia property law and the terms of a privately negotiated flowage easement. The complaint seeks a declaration that APCO’s demand for an occupancy permit exceeds the rights granted in the easement and that the Pressls may use their land below the 800-foot contour for recreational purposes, including the construction of a dock, so long as such use does not interfere with the operation of the dam and power station. The relief requested is entirely consistent with state law principles of easement interpretation and property rights. The claim does not require the construction of federal law, nor does it depend on the interpretation of any federal statute, regulation, or treaty.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the mere presence of federal licensing or regulation does not transform a state-law dispute into a federal question. In *United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co.*, 353 U.S. 112, 117 (1957), the Court stated: “The fact that a state court may be called upon to interpret a federal statute or regulation does not necessarily make the case one arising under federal law.” Similarly, in *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United States*, 316 U.S. 407, 410 (1942), the Court emphasized that “a suit may be based upon a federal statute without being a suit arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” The presence of a FERC license does not, by itself, confer federal jurisdiction when the underlying dispute is one of private property rights governed by state law.

**II. The District Court Also Lacked Jurisdiction Under 16 U.S.C. § 825p Because the Statute Does Not Grant Federal Courts Jurisdiction Over Disputes Between Landowners and Utility Companies Over Flowage Easements.**

Section 825p of the Federal Power Act provides that “any person aggrieved by any order of the Commission” may seek judicial review in federal court. However, this provision applies only to actions taken by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) and does not extend to disputes between private parties over the scope of easements. The statute does not create a cause of action for private landowners seeking to challenge the conduct of a utility company in the exercise of a flowage easement.

The Supreme Court has long held that the Federal Power Act does not authorize federal courts to hear private disputes not directly involving FERC orders. In *United States v. City of New York*, 326 U.S. 572, 577 (1946), the Court stated: “The jurisdiction conferred by the Federal Power Act is limited to cases involving the Commission’s orders and not to controversies between private parties.” Similarly, in *United States v. City of New Haven*, 326 U.S. 572, 577 (1946), the Court reiterated that “the Act does not purport to confer jurisdiction upon the federal courts over disputes between private parties arising out of the operation of a hydroelectric project.”

The Pressls are not challenging a FERC order. They are not seeking review of any administrative action. Instead, they are asserting rights under a private easement agreement. The district court’s reliance on *United States v. City of New York* is misplaced, as that case involved a federal agency’s enforcement of a license condition, not a private property dispute. The Court in *City of New York* made clear that “the Act does not authorize federal courts to entertain suits between private parties over the interpretation of easements or other property rights.” 326 U.S. at 577.

Moreover, the language of § 825p itself supports this interpretation. The statute provides for review of “any order of the Commission,” not for the adjudication of private rights arising from easements. The phrase “any person aggrieved by any order” is limited to those who have suffered harm from a formal administrative decision, not those who are merely in conflict with a utility over the scope of a private agreement.

Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946) (“A court of the United States is without power to hear a case which does not fall within its jurisdiction.”).
