**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), protects a criminal defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy” for the same offense. This protection extends beyond the guilt phase to any proceeding that determines whether a defendant will suffer additional punishment for a prior act. The California Supreme Court erred in holding that the Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings involving prior conviction allegations. Retrial of such allegations after an appellate finding of insufficient evidence violates the Double Jeopardy Clause.

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial After a Finding of Insufficient Evidence**

The fundamental guarantee of the Double Jeopardy Clause is that no person shall be subjected to a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896). An acquittal may be express or implied, and it includes a determination that the evidence is legally insufficient to support a conviction. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904). Once a court of competent jurisdiction has finally resolved that the prosecution failed to prove its case, the defendant may not be retried. *Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141, 143 (1962). Here, the California Court of Appeal held that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon in the prior assault. That determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal. Retrial would place Monge twice in jeopardy for the same allegation.

**II. The Determination of a Prior Conviction Allegation Is a Criminal Proceeding Subject to Double Jeopardy**

The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to any proceeding that is “criminal” in nature and that subjects the defendant to the risk of additional punishment. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1874). The Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense, *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), and it applies whenever the State seeks to impose a sanction that is “essentially criminal.” *Id.* at 718. A prior conviction allegation under California’s Three Strikes law doubles the defendant’s prison term—a severe, additional criminal punishment. The proceeding to determine the truth of that allegation is therefore a criminal proceeding within the meaning of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Supreme Court has recognized that sentencing proceedings that require findings of fact beyond the elements of the underlying offense are “a separate criminal proceeding” subject to due process protections. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967). In *Specht*, the Court held that a defendant facing an enhanced sentence under a sex offender statute was entitled to “the full panoply of the relevant protections which due process guarantees,” including the right to confront witnesses and to offer evidence. *Id.* at 610. The same logic compels application of the Double Jeopardy Clause. If the State must prove a fact beyond a reasonable doubt to increase punishment, and if the defendant is entitled to a jury trial and the right to confront witnesses, then the proceeding is sufficiently “trial-like” to trigger double jeopardy protections. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 245-46 (1949) (distinguishing informal sentencing from trial-like proceedings). California itself provides a statutory right to a jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the rules of evidence for prior conviction allegations. These hallmarks confirm that the proceeding is criminal in nature.

**III. The California Supreme Court’s Distinction Between Capital and Noncapital Sentencing Is Unsupported**

The California Supreme Court relied on the fact that the United States Supreme Court has never explicitly extended double jeopardy protections to noncapital sentencing. But the Court has never held that such protections are limited to capital cases. The reasoning of *Specht* applies with equal force here: when a sentencing determination requires proof of a fact that increases punishment, the defendant is entitled to the same protections as in a trial on guilt. *Specht*, 386 U.S. at 610. The Double Jeopardy Clause is a fundamental right that “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717, and that protection does not vanish simply because the proceeding occurs after a conviction. The State’s failure to prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt is an acquittal on that allegation, and retrial is barred.

**IV. The Court of Appeal’s Insufficiency Finding Is an Acquittal That Bars Retrial**

In *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. at 130, the Court held that an appellate court’s reversal for insufficient evidence is equivalent to an acquittal. The same principle applies here. The California Court of Appeal determined that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon. That determination is a final judgment on the merits of the prior conviction allegation. Retrial would subject Monge to a second determination of the same factual issue, in violation of the Double Jeopardy Clause. *See Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957) (holding that a defendant may not be retried for a greater offense after being impliedly acquitted of it).

**CONCLUSION**

The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of a prior conviction allegation after an appellate finding of insufficient evidence. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
