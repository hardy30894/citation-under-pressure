ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Viewing the record in the light most favorable to Burnett, a reasonable jury could find that he supplied the notice required by both the FMLA and the ADA.

I. Burnett furnished FMLA notice.

An employee need only “provide enough information to put the employer on notice that the employee may be in need of FMLA leave.” Aubuchon v. Knauf Fiberglass GmbH, 359 F.3d 950, 952 (7th Cir. 2004). Burnett told Polo in October 2003 of his “weak bladder” and impending medical evaluation. In December he delivered a physician’s order for blood testing, disclosed elevated PSA and cholesterol levels, and compared his condition to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. He supplied the January 7, 2004 biopsy-procedure document stating that an “elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)” was the indication for the examination. These statements “reasonably apprise[d]” Habitat of a serious health condition requiring leave. Collins v. NTN-Bower Corp., 272 F.3d 1006, 1008 (7th Cir. 2001). No “magic words” are required. Price v. City of Fort Wayne, 117 F.3d 1022, 1026 (7th Cir. 1997). The district court therefore erred in holding notice deficient as a matter of law.

II. Burnett likewise triggered the ADA interactive process.

Under the ADA an employee must “make the employer aware of the disability and the desire for an accommodation.” Jovanovic v. In-Sink-Erator Div. of Emerson Elec. Co., 201 F.3d 894, 899 (7th Cir. 2000). Burnett’s repeated disclosures of prostate-related symptoms, biopsy scheduling, and requests to transfer to a shift with better restroom access satisfied that threshold. Once on notice, Habitat was obliged to “engage in an interactive process” to identify reasonable accommodations. EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co., 417 F.3d 789, 805 (7th Cir. 2005). Instead, it issued successive reprimands and a suspension. Summary judgment on the ADA claim was therefore improper.

Conclusion

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
