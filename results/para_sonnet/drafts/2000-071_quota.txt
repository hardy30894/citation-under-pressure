# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Community Use Policy Draws a Reasonable, Viewpoint-Neutral Line That the First Amendment Permits.

A school that opens its facilities for after-hours civic use creates, at most, a limited public forum, and it "may be justified in reserving [its forum] for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics" so long as the "restrictions are reasonable and are not an effort to suppress expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker's view." *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 800, 806 (1985). A limited forum "need not be all things to all persons"; a governmental entity "may confine its forum to the limited and legitimate purposes for which it was created." *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Milford's Policy does exactly that: it opens the building for "social, civic and recreational" uses "pertaining to the welfare of the community," while expressly excluding "religious purposes." That is a content-based, subject-matter limitation of precisely the kind *Cornelius* and *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 49 (1983), recognize as permissible in a limited forum, because the government "may be justified in reserving [the forum] for certain groups."

This Court's decision in *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), does not compel a different result. There the school had opened its forum to "social, civic, or recreational uses," and the film at issue merely presented family and child-rearing values from a religious perspective; the Court held the exclusion of that particular film was unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination because the school permitted secular discussion of the same subject—family values—while excluding the religious view of it. *Id.* at 393-94. Milford, by contrast, has never permitted any group, secular or religious, to conduct the functional equivalent of worship, proselytizing, and devotional exercises on its premises. The line Milford draws is not "religious perspective on a permitted topic" versus "secular perspective on that topic"; it is "instruction and discussion" versus "worship, proselytization, and prayer"—the very distinction this Court identified as constitutionally significant in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), when it asked, without resolving, whether "singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles" might, in context, constitute "worship" rather than "speech."

The record answers that question here. The Good News Club meeting opens and closes with prayer, includes an "invitation" for children to accept Christ as personal savior, and instructs children to pray a specific prayer of salvation. That is not moral instruction "from a religious viewpoint," as in *Lamb's Chapel*; it is religious worship and evangelism itself. Milford's exclusion of that category of activity—applied evenhandedly to any group proposing to conduct devotional exercises—is reasonable subject-matter regulation, not viewpoint suppression, and it survives scrutiny under *Cornelius* and *Perry*.

## II. Milford's Concern That Admitting the Club Would Convey State Endorsement of Religion Independently Justifies the Exclusion.

Even if the Policy touched viewpoint at its margins, Milford's judgment was independently justified by its obligation to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. A school "may exercise a degree of supervision and control" over the use of its facilities that would be unconstitutional if applied to a traditional public forum, precisely because a school's imprimatur carries special weight with impressionable children. *Board of Educ. of Westside Cmty. Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250-53 (1990) (plurality opinion) (emphasizing that schools may guard against the appearance that religious speech in school facilities bears the school's endorsement, particularly for younger students).

The concern is not speculative. This Court has repeatedly recognized that elementary-school children are particularly susceptible to subtle pressures to participate in religious exercises and to perceive school-sponsored access as school endorsement. *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (noting the "heightened concerns with protecting freedom of conscience from subtle coercive pressure" applicable to public school students). The Club here targets six-to-twelve-year-old children, meets immediately after the school day, on school property, and invites those children—drawn from the same classrooms they occupied moments before—to recite a specific prayer accepting Jesus Christ as their personal savior. Under the familiar framework of *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971), a school district acts on solid constitutional ground when it declines to lend its facilities, immediately after the instructional day, to a program whose primary effect a reasonable child could understand as school-endorsed religious conversion.

The Second Circuit in *Bronx Household of Faith v. Community School District No. 10*, 127 F.3d 207 (2d Cir. 1997), likewise recognized that a school may distinguish between religious speech about a permissible subject and religious worship itself, sustaining a school's authority to exclude the latter from a limited forum created for civic and social purposes. Milford's Board acted consistently with that precedent, and with this Court's admonition in *Cornelius* that reasonableness, not strict scrutiny, governs limited-forum line-drawing. 473 U.S. at 806-08.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
