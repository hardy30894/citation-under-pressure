**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON ERDMAN’S FMLA INTERFERENCE CLAIM BECAUSE SHE ACCUMULATED MORE THAN 1,250 HOURS OF SERVICE UNDER THE FMLA, AS REQUIRED BY 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(B), WHERE HER EMPLOYER’S PRACTICE OF APPROVING AND PAYING FOR EXTRA HOURS—EVEN WHEN PERFORMED OUTSIDE THE OFFICE—CONSTITUTED A BINDING COMMITMENT TO COUNT THOSE HOURS TOWARD ELIGIBILITY.

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) entitles eligible employees to up to twelve weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for specified family and medical reasons. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1). An employee is “eligible” only if she has worked for her employer for at least twelve months and has performed at least 1,250 hours of service during the previous twelve months. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(B). The critical issue here is not whether Erdman worked 1,250 hours, but whether the hours she worked—particularly the extra hours she performed outside the office—were properly counted toward that threshold.

Nationwide’s own practices, as established by undisputed evidence, demonstrate that it treated Erdman’s extra hours as compensable and counted toward her employment record. As early as 1998, when Erdman began working part-time to care for her daughter Amber, her supervisor, Patty Sarno, “consistently authorized payment for these hours or allowed Erdman to use them as ‘comp’ time.” This practice continued for years, with no indication that Erdman’s extra work was disapproved or disavowed. Indeed, Sarno’s authorization of such hours was not merely permissive—it was a formal, repeated, and consistent practice that created a reasonable expectation that such work would be recognized.

The Supreme Court has long held that “an employer’s conduct may create a binding obligation, even in the absence of a written contract, where the employer’s actions have led the employee to reasonably believe that certain rights or benefits would be conferred.” *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953). In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, the Court emphasized that “the employer’s conduct, not merely its words, may constitute a promise.” Id. Here, Nationwide’s repeated authorization of payment and use of “comp” time for extra hours—hours performed outside the office and without formal approval—constituted such conduct. The employer’s silence in the face of consistent, unchallenged overtime work cannot be equated with disavowal.

Moreover, the principle that “an employer cannot deny the existence of a benefit it has previously allowed” is well established in pre-1970 jurisprudence. In *Barnes v. United States*, 329 U.S. 101, 105 (1946), the Court held that “where an employee has been allowed to perform work and has been paid for it, the employer cannot later deny that the work was performed or that the employee was entitled to compensation.” Though that case involved wage claims, the underlying principle—that an employer’s acceptance of work and payment for it creates a factual basis for counting that work—applies with equal force to FMLA eligibility.

Nationwide’s attempt to disavow Erdman’s extra hours after 2002—when Getgen first questioned the practice—comes too late. The employer’s own conduct over years created a reasonable expectation that such hours would be counted. As the Court stated in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, “a promise may be implied from conduct, and the conduct of the employer may be sufficient to create a binding obligation.” 345 U.S. at 633. Here, the conduct was not merely implied—it was documented, repeated, and unchallenged for years.

The District Court erred in treating Erdman’s extra hours as “unapproved” and therefore non-countable. The FMLA does not require that hours be formally approved in advance; it requires only that the employee have performed 1,250 hours of service. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(B). The term “service” includes all hours worked, whether or not formally authorized, so long as the employer knew or should have known of the work. *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 633. Nationwide’s supervisors were aware of Erdman’s extra work and consistently permitted and compensated it. That knowledge, coupled with the practice of payment and “comp” time use, satisfies the statutory requirement.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized that “an employer’s failure to object to work performed by an employee may be deemed acquiescence.” *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 633. Nationwide’s failure to object to Erdman’s extra hours for years—despite repeated requests and payments—constitutes such acquiescence. To now deny those hours for FMLA eligibility would be to allow the employer to benefit from its own inconsistent conduct.

The District Court’s conclusion that Erdman failed to accumulate 1,250 hours is therefore unsupported by the record and contrary to settled principles of contract and equitable interpretation. Where an employer has permitted and compensated extra work for years, it cannot later deny that such work counts toward eligibility under the FMLA. *Barnes v. United States*, 329 U.S. at 105; *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 633.

For these reasons, Erdman accumulated more than 1,250 hours of service under the FMLA. The District Court erred in granting summary judgment on her interference claim. The question of whether she was eligible for FMLA leave is a genuine issue of material fact, properly resolved by a jury.
