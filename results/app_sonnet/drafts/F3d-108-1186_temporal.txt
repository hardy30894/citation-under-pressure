**ARGUMENT**

**I. Although Unit's objection to the verdict forms was general, the confusion those forms produced constituted plain error affecting Unit's substantial rights and is properly reviewable on appeal.**

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 51 provides that "[n]o party may assign as error the giving or the failure to give an instruction unless that party objects thereto before the jury retires to consider its verdict, stating distinctly the matter objected to and the grounds of the objection." Unit recognizes that its objection—that the forms were "extremely difficult to understand"—did not identify with precision the specific defect that produced the double deduction of Enron's repair costs. Rule 51's specificity requirement, however, does not foreclose appellate review where the error is so fundamental that it affects the fairness and integrity of the proceeding. The Second Circuit confronted precisely this tension in *Troupe v. Chicago, Duluth & Georgian Bay Transit Co.*, 234 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1956), where Judge Frank held that a civil litigant's failure to lodge a specific objection to erroneous instructions does not bar review when the error is "plain" and works a miscarriage of justice on the jury's verdict. The defect here is of that character: the verdict forms permitted—indeed invited—the jury to deduct Enron's repair costs from Unit's Form 1 award for substantial performance and then award Enron the identical sum again as a set-off on Form 3, effectively erasing Unit's recovery twice for the same loss. That structural flaw did not depend on any factual dispute; it was baked into the forms themselves and could have been avoided by a single clarifying instruction. Because the error goes to the very structure of the damages calculation rather than to a nuance of contract law, this Court should reach the merits of Unit's challenge notwithstanding the imprecision of its trial objection.

**II. The district court erred in refusing to resubmit the ambiguous verdict to the jury before it was discharged.**

Even if this Court declines to reach the instructional error directly, the district court independently erred in refusing Unit's request—made before the jury was discharged—that the jury be asked to clarify its verdict. The Supreme Court addressed an analogous situation in *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd.*, 369 U.S. 355 (1962), where a jury's answers to special interrogatories appeared internally inconsistent. The Court held that a trial judge confronted with an ambiguous or seemingly inconsistent verdict has an obligation to resolve the ambiguity through the jury itself, while it remains available to explain its own intent, rather than to permit the court or the parties to reconstruct that intent after the fact. The rationale is straightforward: the jury, not the court, is the finder of fact, and only the jury can say with certainty what result it meant to reach. That principle applies with full force here. The verdict returned by the jury—an award of $70,768.02 to Unit on Form 1 and a set-off of $86,494.30 to Enron on Form 3—is susceptible to at least two readings: either the jury already netted the set-off into its Form 1 figure, or it intended two independent, additive awards. Rather than resolve that genuine ambiguity by returning the jury to its room, as *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores* required, the district court simply picked the interpretation most unfavorable to Unit. That was error.

**III. The district court's construction of the verdict improperly disregarded the jury's evident intent to award Unit net damages of $70,768.02.**

Finally, even accepting that the jury was discharged before clarification could be obtained, the district court's chosen construction of the verdict cannot stand because a fully reasonable, harmonizing reading of the two forms was available and consistent with the evidence and instructions. In *Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 372 U.S. 108 (1963), the Supreme Court confronted seemingly inconsistent jury findings and held that courts must strive to harmonize a jury's answers whenever a fair reading of the record permits it, rather than adopt a construction that nullifies the jury's evident intent. The arithmetic of this case supplies exactly such a harmonizing reading. The unpaid invoices totaled $157,262.36. Subtracting Enron's proven repair cost of $86,494.30 from that figure yields $70,767.02—the near-exact sum of the Form 1 award, $70,768.02 (a one-dollar rounding variance). That correspondence is not coincidence; it demonstrates that the jury had already performed the substantial-performance deduction the instructions required and arrived at a net figure for Unit's recovery. The jury's Form 3 entry of $86,494.30 as a "set-off" was, on this reading, a bookkeeping notation of the deduction it had already made—not a second, independent award to Enron. The district court's approach—treating the Form 3 figure as an additional, stacked deduction—converted a single cost item into a double deduction that the jury's own arithmetic contradicts. Under *Gallick*, the district court was obligated to adopt the construction that gives coherent effect to the jury's calculations, not the construction that eliminates Unit's recovery altogether.

For these reasons, the district court's judgment that Unit take nothing should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02, or, alternatively, for a new trial on damages.
