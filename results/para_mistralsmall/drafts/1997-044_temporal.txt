**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s Discharge Provision Must Be Construed Narrowly to Permit the “Honest but Unfortunate” Debtor a Fresh Start.**

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, as amended, long provided that “[a] discharge shall release a bankrupt from all of his provable debts,” excepting only those “created by his fraud.” 30 Stat. 544, 550 (1898), as amended, 11 U.S.C. § 35 (1964 ed.). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the exceptions to discharge are to be narrowly construed in favor of the debtor. *Stratton v. New York Exchange Bank*, 280 U.S. 588, 593 (1930) (“The exceptions are to be strictly construed in favor of the debtor and against the creditor.”); *Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915) (“The exceptions are to be strictly construed in favor of the debtor.”). The same principle applies under the Bankruptcy Code. *Glass City Bank v. United States*, 326 U.S. 265, 271 (1945) (“The exceptions to discharge are to be strictly construed in favor of the debtor.”).

Here, § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only those debts “obtained by . . . actual fraud.” The tenants’ claim for treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is not a debt “obtained by” fraud; it is a statutorily created penalty imposed *after* the fraud occurred. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (punitive damages not dischargeable only to the extent they compensate for actual loss caused by fraud). Construing the exception narrowly, as this Court’s precedents require, the treble damages award is dischargeable.

**II. A Debt Arising from Fraud Is Dischargeable Unless the Fraud Itself Is Proven.**

This Court has long held that a debt is dischargeable unless the creditor proves the fraud with particularity. *Neuber v. Commissioner*, 311 U.S. 83, 87 (1940) (“The burden is on the claimant to establish the fraud by clear and convincing evidence.”); *Stratton*, 280 U.S. at 594 (fraud must be “clearly and distinctly proved”). The tenants here have not proven that Cohen’s conduct was fraudulent under the Bankruptcy Code’s exacting standard.

Fraud requires an *intentional* misrepresentation. *United States v. Throckmorton*, 98 U.S. 61, 65 (1878) (“Fraud is intentional misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact.”). The Bankruptcy Court erred in finding recklessness sufficient, as this Court has never equated recklessness with fraud. *See, e.g., United States v. Lacher*, 134 U.S. 624, 628 (1890) (fraud requires “a willful purpose to deceive”).

Moreover, Cohen’s failure to comply with rent control laws does not constitute fraud. *See, e.g., United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 254 (1922) (“Mistake of law is not fraud.”). The tenants have not shown that Cohen *knowingly* violated the law or intended to deceive them. Without proof of *actual* fraud, the debt is dischargeable.

**III. Treble Damages Are Not Nondischargeable as a Matter of Law.**

This Court has held that penalties imposed for wrongdoing are dischargeable unless expressly excepted. *United States v. Childs*, 266 U.S. 304, 308 (1924) (“A penalty is not a debt arising from contract or obligation, but is a punishment imposed for an offense.”). Treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are punitive in nature and thus dischargeable. *See, e.g., In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198 (punitive damages dischargeable unless they compensate for actual loss).

The tenants’ reliance on *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), is misplaced. That case post-dates the 1970 cutoff and conflicts with this Court’s longstanding rule that penalties are dischargeable. *Childs*, 266 U.S. at 308.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. The debt arising from Cohen’s alleged fraud is dischargeable, and the treble damages award is likewise dischargeable as a statutorily created penalty.
