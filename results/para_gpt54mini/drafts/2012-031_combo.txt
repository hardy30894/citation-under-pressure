Respondent’s class certification order should be affirmed because Rule 23 requires only a showing that damages are capable of classwide proof, not that the district court have final admissible proof of each class member’s precise recovery at certification. The Supreme Court has long held that class procedure turns on whether common questions predominate and whether the class mechanism is superior, not on whether the merits have been finally proved. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3); Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Illinois, 200 U.S. 561 (1906); Arkansas Fuel Oil Co. v. State ex rel. Attorney General, 290 U.S. 573 (1934).

This Court’s review should begin with the settled principle that certification may rest on proof showing a common course of conduct affecting the class. See Missouri v. Taylor, 266 U.S. 200 (1924); West Virginia v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co., 284 U.S. 321 (1932). Where the challenged conduct is uniform, class treatment is appropriate even if individualized questions may later arise in the computation of damages. See Montague & Co. v. Lowry, 193 U.S. 38 (1904); Keogh v. Chicago & Northwestern Ry. Co., 260 U.S. 156 (1922). That is especially true in antitrust cases, where injury may flow from a common restraint on competition. See Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States, 221 U.S. 1 (1911); Maple Flooring Manufacturers’ Assn. v. United States, 268 U.S. 563 (1925).

Petitioners’ position wrongly collapses the certification inquiry into a mini-trial on damages. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that procedure should not be distorted into an adjudication of ultimate liability before the merits are tried. In Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288 (1853), the Court recognized the utility of representative litigation where common interests are involved. In Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356 (1921), the Court sustained representative treatment because the suit concerned a common controversy affecting the group as a whole. And in Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32 (1940), the Court explained that class adjudication is proper when representation and common issues justify binding treatment of absent parties. Nothing in those cases requires the district court to demand final admissible proof of a classwide damages model before certification.

Petitioners rely on the existence of damages differences among class members, but that objection misunderstands the governing standard. The Supreme Court has long distinguished the existence of a common wrong from the later ministerial task of apportioning relief. See Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251 (1946). Bigelow held that wrongdoers may not escape liability merely because precise damages are difficult to measure, and that uncertainty caused by the defendant’s own conduct does not defeat recovery. Id. at 264-266. Likewise, Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), held that “the wrongdoer shall bear the risk of the uncertainty which his own wrong has created.” Id. at 563. That principle squarely supports certification here: if Comcast’s conduct allegedly distorted the market, the inability to calculate each individual’s exact overcharge at the outset is not a reason to deny class treatment.

Nor does petitioners’ proposed rule comport with the antitrust laws’ enforcement design. In Eastern States Retail Lumber Dealers’ Assn. v. United States, 234 U.S. 600 (1914), and United States v. American Tobacco Co., 221 U.S. 106 (1911), the Court recognized that coordinated restraints may injure competition in ways proved by common evidence. The class-device is particularly apt where the same alleged anticompetitive scheme affected all purchasers through a common market mechanism. See Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon, 260 U.S. 393 (1922); United States v. Pabst Brewing Co., 294 U.S. 277 (1935). The district court therefore acted within Rule 23 in finding that damages could be measured on a classwide basis through a common methodology, leaving allocation issues for later proceedings.

Finally, petitioners’ argument would impose a proof burden at certification greater than Rule 23 has ever required. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that procedural rules should facilitate, not frustrate, the vindication of substantive rights. See Suits v. United States, 266 U.S. 492 (1925); Globe Refining Co. v. Landa Cotton Oil Co., 190 U.S. 540 (1903). A district court may certify where the record shows a common damages model capable of measuring the alleged injury across the class; it need not require conclusive admissible proof of each class member’s exact damages before the case may proceed. Because respondents made that showing here, the judgment below should be affirmed.
