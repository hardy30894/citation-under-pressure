The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to mean that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). However, the Court has also emphasized that the OCSLA is not intended to create a duplicative or conflicting body of law. See Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473, 480 (1981).

The Fifth Circuit has developed a test for determining when state law is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law under the OCSLA. In Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is only adopted as surrogate federal law when there is a "significant void or gap" in federal law. This test has been reaffirmed in subsequent Fifth Circuit decisions, including Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), and Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc., 713 F.3d 208, 213 (5th Cir. 2013).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case is inconsistent with the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the OCSLA. By holding that state law can apply whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law, the Ninth Circuit has effectively created a duplicative body of law on the outer Continental Shelf. This is contrary to the Supreme Court's holding in Gulf Offshore, which emphasized that the OCSLA is intended to provide a uniform body of law.

Furthermore, the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims. See 29 U.S.C. § 201, et seq. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), allows for more protective state laws to apply, but this does not mean that state law can supplant federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. As the Fifth Circuit held in Continental Oil, the FLSA's comprehensiveness means that there is no gap for state law to fill.

Other circuits have also recognized the importance of a uniform body of law on the outer Continental Shelf. See, e.g., Smith v. Penrod Drilling Corp., 960 F.2d 456, 459 (5th Cir. 1992); Laredo Offshore Constructors, Inc. v. Hunt, 754 F.2d 1223, 1231 (5th Cir. 1985). The Ninth Circuit's decision is an outlier that creates uncertainty and inconsistency in the application of the OCSLA.

In light of the Supreme Court's decisions in Rodrigue and Gulf Offshore, as well as the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the OCSLA in Continental Oil and subsequent cases, it is clear that the OCSLA permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Newton's claims under California law should be dismissed.

The correct interpretation of the OCSLA is supported by the principles of statutory construction, which require that a statute be interpreted in a manner that is consistent with its purpose and the overall statutory scheme. See, e.g., King v. St. Vincent's Hosp., 502 U.S. 215, 221 (1991); United States v. Locke, 471 U.S. 84, 96 (1985). In this case, the purpose of the OCSLA is to provide a uniform body of law on the outer Continental Shelf, and the Ninth Circuit's decision undermines this purpose.
