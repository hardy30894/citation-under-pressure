**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S PROPERTY IS A PUBLIC FORUM**

The First Amendment protects expressive activity in public forums. The District’s property is a public forum because it is open to the general public for expressive activity. The Supreme Court has defined traditional public forums as "streets, parks and similar locales" held in trust for the public. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Similarly, in *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946), the Court held that a company town is a public forum because it is "open to the public at large." The Court further extended this principle to a police station, finding it a public forum for expressive activity. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 44 (1966). The school district, by opening its facilities to the general public for community use, has created a public forum. The public forum doctrine is a necessary corollary of the First Amendment. *United States v. Albertini*, 382 U.S. 9, 13 (1965).

**II. CONTENT-BASED EXCLUSIONS IN PUBLIC FORUMS ARE SUBJECT TO STRICT SCRUTINY**

In a public forum, a regulation providing a content-based exclusion may be enforced only when "necessary to serve a compelling state interest" and must be "narrowly drawn." *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515. The District’s rule prohibiting religious use is a content-based exclusion. The Court has held that a public forum is "open to all who wish to express their views." *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963). Furthermore, time, place, and manner restrictions must be content-neutral and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 571 (1941).

**III. THE DISTRICT’S POLICY FAILS STRICT SCRUTINY**

The District’s policy prohibiting religious use does not serve a compelling interest. The Supreme Court has established that students do not shed their constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate. *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School Dist.*, 393 U.S. 503, 506 (1969). Schools are not "naked zones" of control. *Keyishian v. Board of Regents*, 385 U.S. 589, 603 (1967). The District’s exclusion of religious speech is not narrowly drawn to serve a compelling interest and leaves no alternative channels for religious expression. Therefore, the District’s policy violates the First Amendment.
