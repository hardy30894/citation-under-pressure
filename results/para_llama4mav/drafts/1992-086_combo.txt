The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series on family values and child rearing constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech.

The character of the public property at issue determines the extent of permissible governmental regulation of expressive activity. Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 276 U.S. 593, 603 (1929); Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515-516 (1939). The School District's facilities are a limited public forum, as they have been opened for use by certain groups for expressive activity. In such a forum, the government's power to regulate expression is limited. The regulation of expression must be viewpoint-neutral and reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum. See Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951); Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 561-562 (1948).

The School District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series, which is a form of expression, is content-based. The District denied the request because the film series was deemed "church related" and thus fell within the prohibition on using school premises for "religious purposes" under Local Rule No. 7. This exclusion is not viewpoint-neutral, as it discriminates against religious speech. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 161-162 (1939).

The Supreme Court has held that, in a limited public forum, the government may not exclude speech based on its content unless the exclusion is necessary to serve a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that interest. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574-575 (1941); Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 571-572 (1942). The School District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series does not meet this standard. The District's interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation is not a compelling state interest that justifies the exclusion. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947); McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 211-212 (1948).

Furthermore, the School District has not consistently applied its policy of excluding religious groups from using its facilities. The District has allowed various groups to use its facilities for purposes that, while not necessarily religious, have some incidental religious connotations. This inconsistent application of the policy undermines the District's claim that the exclusion is viewpoint-neutral. See Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 294-295 (1951); Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949).

In conclusion, the School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use its facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The exclusion is not justified by a compelling state interest and is not viewpoint-neutral. Therefore, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that the School District's actions were unconstitutional.
