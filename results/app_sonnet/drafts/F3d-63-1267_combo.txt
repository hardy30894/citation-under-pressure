ARGUMENT

I. STELWAGON'S ROBINSON–PATMAN AWARD MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL DAMAGES WITH THE DEGREE OF CERTAINTY THE CLAYTON ACT REQUIRES.

A plaintiff seeking treble damages under Section 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a), must do more than establish a violation of Section 2(a) of the Robinson-Patman Act. Liability and damages are analytically distinct inquiries. A showing that a seller's price differential "may be substantially to lessen competition," 15 U.S.C. § 13(a), satisfies the Federal Trade Commission's competitive-injury standard for injunctive relief, see FTC v. Morton Salt Co., 334 U.S. 37, 46 (1948) (explaining that Congress intended the Act "to reach not merely to actual harm but also ... incipient" injury to competition), but it does not relieve a private treble-damage plaintiff of its independent burden to prove that it suffered actual pecuniary injury flowing from the discrimination, and the amount of that injury. See Automatic Canteen Co. v. FTC, 346 U.S. 61, 74 (1953) (recognizing that the Robinson-Patman Act's competitive-injury provisions and the burdens attending private recovery are not coextensive). As this Court and the Supreme Court have long recognized, "the general rule is that a wrongdoer is not entitled to complain that damages ... cannot be measured with the exactness and precision that would otherwise be possible," but that rule does not excuse a plaintiff from proving the fact and approximate amount of damage by evidence that is more than speculative. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931). It is settled that "the uncertainty which defeats recovery ... is uncertainty as to the fact of the damage and not as to its amount." Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co., 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927). Conversely, where the fact of damage itself is not fairly established, and the amount rests on nothing but conjecture, recovery cannot stand. Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264-65 (1946) (holding that while damages need not be proved with "mathematical precision," a jury "may not render a verdict based on speculation or guesswork").

Here, Stelwagon's damages proof consisted almost entirely of an internal, after-the-fact projection of lost profits that assumed, without evidentiary foundation, that every unit Standard Roofing and Celotex purchased from Tarmac at a lower price would otherwise have been purchased from Stelwagon at Stelwagon's own margins. That assumption ignores the requirement, reaffirmed in Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 395 U.S. 100, 114 n.9 (1969), that a plaintiff must show that the challenged conduct was a "material cause" of its injury and must offer "data from which the amount of the loss may be ascertained as a matter of reasonable inference," rather than a chain of unsupported extrapolations. See also Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp., 370 U.S. 690, 702 (1962) (damages must flow from the antitrust violation itself, not from the plaintiff's general business misfortune). Stelwagon offered no market analysis, no evidence of the elasticity of demand among contractor-customers, and no accounting for the many non-price factors—delivery times, existing relationships, product substitution—that determine which distributor a contractor patronizes. Nor did Stelwagon account for Tarmac's evidence, unrebutted at trial, that its price differentials to Standard and Celotex reflected legitimate functional and volume-based distinctions of the kind the Supreme Court has recognized as lawful. See FTC v. Sun Oil Co., 371 U.S. 505, 519-20 (1963) (functional discounts reflecting genuine differences in distribution services do not violate Section 2(a)); FTC v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 363 U.S. 536, 549 (1960) ("discriminat[ion] in price" means only a difference not justified by cost or function). Where, as here, the plaintiff's own damages witness could not explain the derivation of the loss figures beyond stating that they represented "what we think we would have made," the jury verdict rested on exactly the kind of guesswork Bigelow forbids.

The district court recognized as much when it found the jury's $2,272,000 verdict "unsupported by the evidence and ... grossly excessive," Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc., 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994), and reduced the antitrust award by more than eighty percent. But remittitur cannot cure a total absence of a reasoned evidentiary basis; it can only correct an excessive but otherwise sufficiently grounded award. Because Stelwagon's damages theory was speculative from its inception, no amount—whether $450,383.60 or any other figure derived from the same defective methodology—can stand as "actual damages" within the meaning of Section 4. The judgment awarding treble damages under the Clayton Act should therefore be vacated in its entirety, not merely reduced.

II. IN THE ALTERNATIVE, THE COURT SHOULD REMAND FOR A NEW TRIAL LIMITED TO DAMAGES.

Should this Court conclude that Stelwagon's evidence, though thin, was not so wholly speculative as to preclude any recovery, the proper remedy is a new trial on damages, not affirmance of a figure this record cannot support. A verdict resting on an erroneous damages methodology cannot be salvaged by judicial remittitur to an arbitrarily chosen lesser sum; the jury must reassess damages under a proper evidentiary framework. See Bigelow, 327 U.S. at 265 (damages must be "guided by proper instructions" applied to competent evidence, not judicial approximation).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the treble-damages judgment entered against Tarmac under Section 4 of the Clayton Act.
