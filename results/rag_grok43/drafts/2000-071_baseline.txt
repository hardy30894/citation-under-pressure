**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Was Permissible Subject-Matter Discrimination in a Limited Public Forum.**

Milford’s Community Use Policy opens school facilities after hours only for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” while expressly barring “religious purposes.” This creates a limited public forum. In such a forum, “the State may be justified in reserving its forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics,” *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995), provided the restriction is reasonable and viewpoint-neutral.

The Club’s proposed meetings consisted of prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories, missionary narratives, and invitations to accept Christ as personal Savior. These activities constitute religious instruction and worship, not the discussion of a secular subject from a religious perspective. The district court and Second Circuit correctly found that the Club sought access for “religious instruction and prayer rather than the teaching of values from a religious viewpoint.” *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (distinguishing religious worship from a film series addressing child-rearing “from a Christian perspective”).

Unlike the film in *Lamb’s Chapel*, which addressed a permitted secular topic, or the student newspaper in *Rosenberger*, which addressed university news and politics, the Club’s program had no secular anchor. Its “challenge and invitation” segments explicitly called for conversion. Milford therefore engaged in content-based, not viewpoint-based, exclusion when it denied access for religious worship. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (recognizing that “religious worship” may be treated differently from other speech).

**II. Any Viewpoint Discrimination Was Justified by the Establishment Clause.**

Even assuming the exclusion implicated viewpoint, Milford’s interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplied a compelling justification. *See Board of Ed. of Westside Cmty. Schs. (Dist. 66) v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 253 (1990) (plurality) (noting that equal-access policies at the elementary level raise distinct endorsement concerns). Elementary-school children are “impressionable” and likely to perceive official endorsement when religious instruction occurs on school property immediately after the school day. *See Bender v. Williamsport Area Sch. Dist.*, 475 U.S. 534, 553 (1986) (Burger, C.J., dissenting) (warning against “state support of religion” perceived by students). Milford’s policy therefore served the compelling interest of maintaining strict separation between government facilities and religious indoctrination of young children.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
