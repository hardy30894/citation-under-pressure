**ARGUMENT**

The Patent Act does not permit a design-patent plaintiff to recover the infringer’s *entire* profits when the patented design covers only a component of a larger multicomponent product. Section 289 authorizes recovery of “the total profit” only for the “article of manufacture to which the patented design or colorable imitation has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. That text requires identification of the relevant article of manufacture, and this Court has long held that design-patent remedies are limited by the scope of the patented design. See Gorham Co. v. White, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511, 528 (1872); Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444–45 (1885). The Federal Circuit’s rule allowing recovery of all profits from an end product, even where the patented design covers only a part of that product, cannot be reconciled with the statute.

This Court’s decisions confirm that design-patent damages are tied to the thing actually designed. In Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc., 580 U.S. 53 (2016), the Court rejected the Federal Circuit’s categorical approach that the “article of manufacture” must always be the consumer product as sold. The Court explained that, in ordinary usage, an “article of manufacture” may be “a product sold to a consumer or a component of that product.” Id. at 58. The Court therefore held that “the relevant ‘article of manufacture’ for a design patent may be either a product sold to a consumer or a component of that product.” Id. at 67. That holding necessarily forecloses automatic recovery of the infringer’s profits on the entire end product when the patented design is only a component.

The statutory phrase “total profit” does not alter that conclusion. Section 289 permits recovery of the infringer’s “total profit” only from the “article of manufacture to which the patented design or colorable imitation has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. As the Court explained in Samsung, Congress used the term “article of manufacture” rather than “product” and thus signaled that the relevant unit may be less than the finished article sold to consumers. 580 U.S. at 60–62. Reading “total profit” to mean total profit on the entire product regardless of the scope of the patented design would collapse the statutory limitation and make the “article of manufacture” language meaningless. See id. at 65–67.

Longstanding design-patent precedent points the same way. In Dobson, the Court refused to allow recovery of carpet profits attributable to unpatented portions, warning against damages untethered to the actual design contribution. 114 U.S. at 444–46. Later cases likewise treated design-patent recovery as limited to the patented appearance, not the whole commercial article. See Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 234 F. 79, 82–83 (2d Cir. 1916); Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 377 U.S. 476, 507 (1964) (noting that patent remedies must be confined to the patented subject matter); General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp., 461 U.S. 648, 653–54 (1983) (patent damages compensate only for the infringement proved); Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1854) (damages must be commensurate with the injury caused by the infringement).

The Federal Circuit’s entire-profits rule also conflicts with the broader patent law principle that damages must reflect the value of the invention actually appropriated. In Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), the Court held that a patentee seeking damages based on an entire product must “separate or apportion” the infringer’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features. Although Garretson involved utility patents, its basic principle applies with special force here because design patents protect only the ornamental design, not the functionality or commercial value of unrelated product features. See also Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614–16 (1912).

The government’s contrary position would produce absurd and punitive outcomes. A patented icon, bezel, or screen arrangement could sweep in profits from a smartphone’s processor, memory, software ecosystem, and brand goodwill—even though those profits were generated by unpatented technology and business choices. Nothing in § 289 authorizes such a windfall. The Court has repeatedly rejected constructions of patent remedies that exceed compensation for the specific infringement proved. See Aro Mfg. Co., 377 U.S. at 507; Devex, 461 U.S. at 655–56.

Nor is there any administrability problem. Samsung already recognized that the proper inquiry is the “article of manufacture” analysis addressed in this Court’s precedent. 580 U.S. at 67. The factfinder can determine, based on the record, the relevant article of manufacture and then award the infringer’s total profit from that article—not from the unrelated parts of the larger product. That approach honors both the text and the Court’s decision in Samsung.

The judgment below should be reversed. Under § 289, where the patented design is only a component, damages must be limited to the infringer’s profits attributable to the relevant article of manufacture, not the entire end product.
