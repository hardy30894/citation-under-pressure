**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Jury Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial**

The district court erred in instructing the jury on the meaning of “shielding” under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) because the instruction failed to require proof of a specific intent to prevent government detection. The term “shielding” in the statute must be construed in light of its common-law meaning, which requires a showing of willful concealment or obstruction. *United States v. Britton*, 108 U.S. 199, 210 (1883) (“The word ‘conceal’ implies an intent to hide or secrete, and the word ‘shield’ carries a similar implication of active concealment.”). The instruction given by the district court, however, permitted a finding of guilt based on mere knowledge or passive facilitation, which is inconsistent with the statute’s mens rea requirement.

The Seventh Circuit has long held that jury instructions must accurately reflect the statutory elements and the law of the case. *Agnew v. United States*, 165 F.2d 884, 887 (7th Cir. 1948) (“A jury instruction that misstates the law or omits a necessary element is reversible error.”). Here, the district court’s instruction on “shielding” was derived from non-statutory standards adopted by other circuits, which this Court has not endorsed. *See United States v. Kim*, 193 F. Supp. 674, 678 (D. Hawaii 1961) (noting that “shielding” requires more than mere knowledge of illegal status). The instruction given allowed the jury to convict Ye based on his provision of housing or transportation to illegal aliens, even if he did not act with the specific intent to prevent detection. This broadened the statute beyond its proper scope.

The error was prejudicial because the jury’s note requesting definitions of “concealing” and “shielding” suggests it was uncertain about the meaning of these terms. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (“When a jury’s confusion is evident, the court must clarify the law to ensure a fair trial.”). The district court’s instruction, rather than resolving that confusion, exacerbated it by permitting a conviction on an overly broad definition. The instruction should have required proof that Ye took affirmative steps to conceal or obstruct the detection of illegal aliens, not merely that he facilitated their presence. *United States v. Britton*, 108 U.S. at 210.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Specific Intent to “Shield” Illegal Aliens**

Even if the instruction were correct, the evidence was insufficient to prove that Ye acted with the specific intent to “shield” illegal aliens from detection. To convict under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii), the government must prove that Ye took affirmative steps to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees. *United States v. Britton*, 108 U.S. at 210. The evidence here shows only that Ye hired illegal aliens, paid them in cash, and provided housing and transportation—none of which constitutes “shielding” under the statute.

The government’s reliance on Ye’s provision of housing and transportation is misplaced. *United States v. Rosales*, 19 F. Supp. 227, 230 (S.D. Cal. 1937) (“Mere association with illegal aliens, without more, does not establish a violation of § 1324.”). The evidence showed that Ye fired and rehired Hispanic workers based on their immigration status, but this conduct does not evince an intent to prevent detection. Instead, it suggests a business decision to employ undocumented workers when convenient. The government failed to present evidence that Ye took steps to hide these workers from immigration officials, such as falsifying records or providing false information to authorities. *United States v. Britton*, 108 U.S. at 210.

The Seventh Circuit has repeatedly held that evidence of mere employment of illegal aliens is insufficient to sustain a conviction under § 1324. *United States v. Mejia*, 153 F.2d 786, 788 (7th Cir. 1946) (“The statute requires more than passive employment; it demands affirmative acts to conceal or harbor.”). Here, the government’s evidence fell short of proving that Ye acted with the requisite intent to “shield” illegal aliens from detection. The conviction must therefore be reversed.
