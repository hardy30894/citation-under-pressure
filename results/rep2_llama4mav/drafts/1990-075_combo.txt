The Controlled Substances Act's delegation of authority to the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. The Constitution grants Congress the power to make laws, but it is well established that Congress may delegate authority to executive branch officials when it provides an intelligible principle to guide their actions (J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). 

The intelligible principle test has been met in this case. The Act provides specific factors for the Attorney General to consider when temporarily scheduling a substance, including the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health (21 U.S.C. § 811(c)). These factors provide sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, as seen in cases such as Yakus v. United States, where the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the Price Administrator to fix commodity prices based on "fair and equitable" standards (321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944)). Similarly, in Fahey v. Mallonee, the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to regulate the operations of savings and loan associations, finding that the Board's actions were guided by "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the field (332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947)).

The Attorney General's authority to temporarily schedule substances is also limited by the requirement that the substance pose an "imminent hazard to the public safety" (21 U.S.C. § 811(h)). This limitation provides a clear standard for the Attorney General to follow, similar to the standard upheld in United States v. Rock Royal Co-operative, where the Court found that the Secretary of Agriculture's authority to regulate milk prices was guided by a clear standard (306 U.S. 533, 577-78 (1939)).

Furthermore, the Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the DEA Administrator is authorized by Congress. The Act grants the Attorney General the authority to delegate his functions to other officials (21 U.S.C. § 871(a)). In United States v. Grimaud, the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the Secretary of Agriculture to make rules and regulations governing forest reserves, and the Secretary's subsequent sub-delegation of authority to forest officers (220 U.S. 506, 515-16 (1911)). Similarly, in C.C. C. & St. L. Ry. Co. v. Board of Public Works, the Court upheld a sub-delegation of authority from the Interstate Commerce Commission to a committee of state officials (222 U.S. 156, 165-66 (1911)).

The non-delegation doctrine has been applied leniently by the courts, and delegations of authority have been upheld in a wide range of contexts (Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-94 (1892); Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496-97 (1904)). In Panama Oil Co. v. Ryan, the Court noted that the non-delegation doctrine is not violated when Congress provides a clear standard for the delegatee to follow (293 U.S. 388, 426 (1935)). Here, the Controlled Substances Act provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, and the sub-delegation to the DEA Administrator is authorized by Congress.

In conclusion, the Controlled Substances Act's delegation of authority to the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances is constitutional. The Act provides an intelligible principle to guide the Attorney General's actions, and the sub-delegation to the DEA Administrator is authorized by Congress. The convictions of the Toubys should be upheld.
