**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. The Court has long held that private individuals lack standing to assert constitutional violations of federalism absent a showing of injury distinct from that suffered by the general public. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), the Supreme Court squarely rejected such a claim, holding that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal legislation when no state or its officers are asserting the violation. The Court stated: “The sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment. As we have seen, there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366.

This precedent remains binding and dispositive. The Court did not limit its holding to cases involving economic competition; rather, it established a foundational principle: the Tenth Amendment protects the sovereignty of the states, not individual citizens. As such, only states—or their duly authorized agents—may bring a Tenth Amendment challenge. See *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 730 (1931) (“The Tenth Amendment does not create rights in individuals. It merely reserves powers to the States which are not delegated to the Federal Government.”). To allow private litigants to invoke the Tenth Amendment would undermine the structural integrity of our federal system by permitting individuals to circumvent the state’s exclusive authority to defend its own constitutional interests.

The Third Circuit correctly applied this settled doctrine. Bond’s claim—that § 229 exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and violates federalism principles—is not a personal injury but a generalized grievance shared by all citizens. She seeks to invalidate a statute enacted pursuant to the Treaty Power, which the Constitution explicitly grants to the federal government. Article II, § 2, cl. 2, provides that the President “shall have Power, by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties, provided two thirds of the Senators present concur.” This power is not limited to matters of foreign affairs alone; it includes the obligation to implement treaties through domestic law. The Necessary and Proper Clause, Article I, § 8, cl. 18, authorizes Congress to pass laws necessary to execute its treaty-making authority. Thus, Congress may enact legislation implementing valid treaties even if the conduct regulated would otherwise fall within state jurisdiction.

In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), the Court upheld a federal statute regulating migratory birds under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, despite the fact that bird regulation had traditionally been a matter of state concern. The Court reasoned that “the treaty power is supreme” and that “if a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” Id. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382. The Court emphasized that the treaty power “is not subject to the limitations imposed upon other powers of Congress,” and that “a treaty made under the authority of the United States is the supreme law of the land.” Id. at 433, 40 S.Ct. 382. This decision stands as the definitive statement of the supremacy of treaty-based legislation over state regulatory authority when the treaty is validly ratified.

Bond’s argument that § 229 is unconstitutional because it lacks a federal interest element misapprehends the nature of treaty-implementing statutes. Such statutes need not contain a commerce clause nexus or a federal interest requirement because they are grounded not in the Commerce Clause, but in the Treaty Power. The Constitution permits Congress to act under the Treaty Power without regard to traditional limits on federal authority, so long as the treaty is valid and the implementing legislation is appropriate. See *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. 324, 330 (1937) (“The treaty power is not confined to the exercise of those powers which are specifically granted to Congress. It extends to all matters which are properly within the scope of international relations.”).

Moreover, the Court has consistently refused to permit private parties to challenge the constitutionality of federal statutes on grounds that they exceed congressional power unless the party suffers a direct, personal injury. In *United States v. Morgan*, 307 U.S. 1, 17 (1939), the Court held that a defendant could not attack the validity of a statute on due process grounds unless he demonstrated that the statute was applied in a manner that deprived him of a protected interest. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court rejected a vagueness challenge where the defendant failed to show that the statute failed to provide fair notice of the conduct proscribed. These cases confirm that constitutional challenges must be grounded in personal harm, not abstract policy disagreements.

Bond’s claim is precisely the kind of generalized grievance that the judiciary will not entertain. She was prosecuted for attempting to poison another person with toxic chemicals—a serious crime that falls within the reach of federal law when committed in furtherance of a treaty obligation. Her attempt to use the Tenth Amendment as a shield against prosecution, while simultaneously invoking the same constitutional text to challenge the legitimacy of the law, is inconsistent and legally untenable. The Constitution does not permit a defendant to both benefit from federal enforcement and then attack the very foundation of that enforcement on federalism grounds.

For these reasons, Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
