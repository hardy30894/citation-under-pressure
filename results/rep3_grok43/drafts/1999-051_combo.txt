I.

A state prisoner must exhaust available state remedies before seeking federal habeas relief. *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886). This requirement, rooted in comity, bars federal courts from adjudicating claims the state courts have not had a fair opportunity to address. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950); *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944). When a prisoner fails to present a claim in accordance with state procedural rules, the claim is defaulted and federal review is precluded absent a showing of cause and prejudice. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486-87 (1953).

II.

Petitioner’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was never presented on direct appeal and was rejected as untimely when first raised in an application to reopen. That procedural default bars federal review unless petitioner can demonstrate cause. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). Petitioner seeks to establish cause through a separate claim that appellate counsel rendered ineffective assistance by omitting the sufficiency argument. Yet that ineffectiveness claim was itself rejected by the state courts as untimely and therefore is also defaulted.

III.

A defaulted claim cannot supply the cause necessary to overcome another default. Cause must arise from an objective factor external to the defense that impeded compliance with state procedure. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 359 U.S. 394, 407 (1959). An unexhausted ineffectiveness allegation does not meet this standard, for it remains subject to the same exhaustion mandate that governs all constitutional claims. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 115 (1935). Allowing one defaulted claim to excuse another would nullify the exhaustion doctrine and permit successive layers of unpresented claims to evade state procedural bars. *Rogers v. Richmond*, 365 U.S. 534, 547-48 (1961).

IV.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding—that a defaulted ineffectiveness claim may nevertheless constitute cause—contradicts the principle that federal habeas jurisdiction extends only to claims first fairly presented to the state courts. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963). Because petitioner never obtained a state adjudication of his ineffectiveness allegation on the merits, that allegation cannot serve as cause. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938). The district court therefore lacked authority to reach the defaulted sufficiency claim.

V.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
