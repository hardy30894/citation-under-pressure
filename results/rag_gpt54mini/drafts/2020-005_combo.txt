This Court should hold that the Due Process Clause permits specific jurisdiction when the defendant’s forum conduct is sufficiently related to the plaintiff’s claims, even if no single in-forum act is the strict “cause” of injury. The governing line of cases has long rejected the notion that due process requires the plaintiff’s claim to arise solely from a forum contact that is the immediate cause of harm. Rather, the question is whether the defendant has engaged in forum activities that make it “reasonable and just” to subject it to suit there, and whether those activities are connected to the asserted cause of action. Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952); International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945).

International Shoe itself replaced rigid territorial formalism with a fairness-based inquiry, holding that jurisdiction is proper where maintenance of the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” 326 U.S. at 316. The Court explained that a defendant’s “continuous and systematic” forum activity may support jurisdiction, and that the relationship among the contacts, the forum, and the litigation controls. Id. at 317-319. That framework does not impose an inflexible causation requirement. To the contrary, later decisions have repeatedly focused on relatedness and fairness, not strict but-for causation. Perkins, 342 U.S. 437; McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957).

McGee is especially instructive. There, jurisdiction rested on a single insurance contract delivered into the forum, even though the insurer’s broader business was elsewhere. 355 U.S. at 223. The Court upheld jurisdiction because the suit was based on a contract “which had substantial connection” with the forum. Id. at 223. McGee thus confirms that due process permits jurisdiction when the defendant has deliberately served the forum market and the suit is meaningfully connected to that forum activity. A requirement that the forum contact itself must be the direct cause of injury would unduly narrow McGee and International Shoe.

The Court’s motor-vehicle cases point in the same direction. In Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a nonresident motorist based on the privilege of using the state’s highways and the state’s power to protect those injured there. Wuchter v. Pizzutti, 276 U.S. 13 (1928), likewise approved the nonresident-motorist framework, emphasizing that a state may condition highway use on amenability to suit arising from in-state accidents. See also Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad, 346 U.S. 338 (1953). Those cases do not turn on where the defendant designed or manufactured the vehicle; they turn on the defendant’s deliberate exploitation of the forum and the forum’s interest in providing redress for harms occurring there. That is precisely the kind of forum relationship present here.

Ford’s contrary position would resurrect the formalism the Court has repeatedly rejected. In Metzger Motor Car Co. v. Parrott, 233 U.S. 36 (1914), the Court recognized that automobile use in the forum can justify liability-based regulation and litigation. In Clark v. Wells, 203 U.S. 164 (1906), and Wells Fargo & Co. Express v. Ford, 238 U.S. 503 (1915), the Court accepted that jurisdictional rules are satisfied when the defendant has adequate notice and a sufficient forum nexus to the controversy. And in Provident Savings Life Assurance Society v. Ford, 114 U.S. 635 (1885), the Court recognized that a corporation may be subject to suit where it has availed itself of the privilege of doing business through local agents. Minnesota Commercial Men’s Assn. v. Benn, 261 U.S. 140 (1923), further confirms that a nonresident corporation may be haled into a state’s courts when it has purposefully invoked the benefits of that state’s regulatory and commercial environment.

Perkins reinforces the point most directly. The Court there stated that where a foreign corporation’s activities make it reasonable and just to subject it to suit, jurisdiction comports with due process. 342 U.S. at 445-448. Nothing in Perkins or International Shoe requires that the forum contact be the immediate mechanical cause of the injury. Rather, the relevant inquiry is whether the defendant’s forum conduct is sufficiently connected to the litigation and whether asserting jurisdiction is fair. Ford’s advertising, selling, and servicing of the same model of vehicle in Montana created a substantial forum relationship to the very claims that the vehicle was defectively designed, inadequately warned, and negligently marketed for use in Montana.

A strict causation rule would also produce arbitrary results inconsistent with the Court’s precedents. It would permit a manufacturer to cultivate a forum market, profit from that market, and nevertheless avoid suit there when its product later injures a forum resident in the very state in which the product was expected to be used. Due process does not require such an artificial result. International Shoe, 326 U.S. 310; McGee, 355 U.S. 220; Perkins, 342 U.S. 437.

The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
