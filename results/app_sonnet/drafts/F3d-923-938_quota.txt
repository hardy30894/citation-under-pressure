ARGUMENT

I. THE TAX COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE NET TRANSFERS FROM MERCO'S AFFILIATES DID NOT CONSTITUTE BONA FIDE INDEBTEDNESS RUNNING DIRECTLY TO MERUELO.

Subchapter S establishes "a pass-through system under which corporate income, losses, deductions, and credits are attributed to individual shareholders in a manner akin to the tax treatment of partnerships." Bufferd v. Comm'r, 506 U.S. 523, 525 (1993). A shareholder may deduct losses only to the extent of his basis in stock and in "any indebtedness of the S corporation to the shareholder." 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1)(B). The controlling regulation requires that the debt be "bona fide indebtedness of the S corporation that runs directly to the shareholder." Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). Nothing in the regulation or the statute requires that the funds physically pass through the shareholder's own hands before reaching the corporation.

Courts have long recognized that transfers routed through commonly controlled entities may nonetheless create bona fide indebtedness running to the individual who directs and controls those entities. In Estate of Bean v. Comm'r, 268 F.3d 553 (8th Cir. 2001), the court held that a "back-to-back loan" structure—where funds moved from a shareholder-controlled entity to the S corporation—could satisfy the direct-indebtedness requirement because the shareholder retained the economic reality of a lender notwithstanding the intermediate corporate form. The Tax Court itself has applied this same principle where a taxpayer, like Meruelo, dominates all entities in the chain of transfer. See Yates v. Comm'r, T.C. Memo. 2001-280 (funds moved among commonly owned entities may be treated as a loan from the shareholder where the taxpayer controls the flow of funds); Culnen v. Comm'r, T.C. Memo. 2000-139 (examining whether the debt, in substance, reflects the shareholder's own credit rather than an independent corporate obligation). The touchstone, as the Eighth Circuit explained in Oren v. Comm'r, 357 F.3d 854, 857 (8th Cir. 2004), is whether "the taxpayer is the true lender," not whether the paperwork labels an intermediate S corporation as the payor.

Meruelo satisfies that test. He alone controlled Merco and each of the affiliates that advanced funds to it. Every affiliate transfer was memorialized on Merco's books as an account payable and reconciled annually against Meruelo's promissory note establishing a $10 million unsecured line of credit to Merco at a stated six percent interest rate. Carreras, Meruelo's accountant, testified that each year he charged Meruelo's line of credit for the amount of Merco's net payable to its affiliates, precisely the mechanism contemplated by Estate of Bean and Yates for converting affiliate advances into shareholder debt. The Tax Court's insistence that the money must travel directly from Meruelo's own bank account to Merco elevates form over the substance that Oren and Culnen instruct courts to examine.

II. MERUELO MADE AN ACTUAL ECONOMIC OUTLAY SUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT HIS CLAIMED BASIS.

Even under the "actual economic outlay" doctrine, Meruelo's basis claim should stand. That doctrine, articulated in Perry v. Comm'r, 54 T.C. 1471 (1970), aff'd, 469 F.2d 1234 (5th Cir. 1972), and refined in Gilday v. Comm'r, T.C. Memo. 1982-241, requires that the shareholder show he has "left himself poorer in a material sense" by the transaction—that he, not merely a related entity, has borne the economic burden of the corporation's debt.

This Court's decision in Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985), confirms that basis may arise even when a bank, rather than the shareholder, is the formal creditor, so long as the shareholder is the party the creditor looks to for repayment. Selfe adopted the "incorporated pocketbook" theory, under which the S corporation is treated merely as a conduit for funds that are, in economic substance, the shareholder's own. Selfe, 778 F.2d at 772. The same reasoning governs here: Meruelo originally funded the affiliate network with $4,985,035 of personally borrowed capital—an outlay the Commissioner concedes established basis—and continued to bear the risk of nonpayment across the affiliated entities he owned. His ownership interests, his personal guarantee obligations, and his consistent treatment of the affiliate transfers as draws on his own $10 million line of credit to Merco demonstrate that he, and not some independent third party, stood to lose if Merco's debts went unpaid.

The Tax Court's contrary finding rests on the mistaken premise that an "actual economic outlay" must be a single, direct cash transfer from shareholder to corporation. Neither Perry nor Gilday nor Selfe imposes such a rigid requirement; each looks to the substance of who bears the ultimate economic risk. Because Meruelo bore that risk through his interlocking ownership of Merco's affiliates and his formalized line-of-credit arrangement, he made the requisite economic outlay to support his claimed $9 million basis.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Tax Court's decision should be reversed, and this Court should hold that Meruelo established bona fide indebtedness of Merco running directly to him in the amount claimed.
