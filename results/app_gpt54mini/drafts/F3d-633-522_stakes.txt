**Argument**

The judgment should be reversed because DHL’s severance agreements did not lawfully extinguish appellants’ WARN claims. The WARN Act creates a substantive statutory right to advance notice, and the district court erred in treating the union-negotiated releases as effective waivers of that right absent the clear, knowing, and voluntary relinquishment the statute and governing labor law require. See *Local Joint Executive Bd. of Culinary/Bartender Trust Fund v. Las Vegas Sands, Inc.*, 244 F.3d 1152, 1159-60 (9th Cir. 2001); *Wright v. Universal Mar. Serv. Corp.*, 525 U.S. 70, 79-82 (1998).

First, WARN rights are not ordinary contract claims. Congress enacted WARN to provide workers and their families with advance warning of plant closings and mass layoffs. 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2104; *United Steelworkers of Am. v. North Star Steel Co.*, 5 F.3d 39, 42 (3d Cir. 1993). Because WARN imposes a statutory duty on employers, the ability to surrender claims under it must be evaluated with particular care. See *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80-81 (a waiver of federal rights must be “clear and unmistakable”); *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 740-41 (1981) (individual employees cannot be presumed to have waived statutory protections through collective bargaining).

The releases here do not satisfy that standard. Although the form mentioned the “Federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act,” the surrounding circumstances show no knowing waiver of accrued WARN claims. DHL announced the shutdown on November 10, 2008; by then the WARN violation, if any, had already occurred or was in progress. Appellants were then presented with short-fuse severance elections—some drivers had only days to decide while also completing rebid forms. The Supreme Court has repeatedly required a waiver of statutory employment rights to be both clear in text and voluntary in fact. *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80-82; *Metz v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 39 F.3d 1482, 1486-87 (10th Cir. 1994).

Second, the releases were obtained through collective bargaining over severance benefits, not by individual negotiation of WARN claims. That matters because a union may bargain over some employment terms, but it cannot lightly waive employees’ federal statutory causes of action unless the waiver is explicit and unmistakable. *Barrentine*, 450 U.S. at 740-46; *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80-82. WARN is designed to protect workers directly; a union’s agreement to severance terms cannot be read to surrender each worker’s accrued WARN claim unless Congress’s demanded clarity is present. See *Lingle v. Norge Div. of Magic Chef, Inc.*, 486 U.S. 399, 408-10 (1988) (statutory rights are analytically distinct from collective-bargaining rights).

Third, the structure of the agreements confirms that the releases were not intended as bona fide consideration for relinquishing WARN rights. DHL’s own form described the severance payment as “beyond anything to which I am already or otherwise entitled,” yet WARN liability is precisely an entitlement that may already have accrued if notice was deficient. A release given in exchange for benefits that the employer may already owe cannot establish a knowing relinquishment unless the worker is fairly apprised of the claim being surrendered and the value of the exchange. See *Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co.*, 415 U.S. 36, 52-54 (1974) (statutory rights are not waived by indirection and require independent scrutiny); *O’Hare v. Global Natural Res., Inc.*, 898 F.2d 1015, 1017-18 (5th Cir. 1990).

Fourth, DHL’s reading would undermine WARN’s remedial purpose. The Act is meant to deter abrupt layoffs by imposing advance-notice obligations and damages for violations. 29 U.S.C. § 2104; *United Steelworkers*, 5 F.3d at 42. If employers could avoid liability by rushing severance offers to employees after announcing a shutdown, the statutory notice requirement would be transformed into a bargaining chip. Courts reject interpretations that would nullify a federal labor statute’s deterrent force. See *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 77-78; *Barrentine*, 450 U.S. at 740-41.

Finally, the releases should not be enforced against the certified class because the question is not simply whether some workers signed a form, but whether the employer validly extinguished a classwide statutory claim. The record shows a mass shutdown, standardized forms, and compressed deadlines for hundreds of workers. Under these circumstances, DHL bore the burden of proving a clear, voluntary waiver by each employee, and it did not do so. See *Hampton v. Ford Motor Co.*, 561 F.3d 709, 714-15 (7th Cir. 2009); *Morais v. Central Beverage Corp. Union Employees’ Supplemental Ret. Plan*, 167 F.3d 709, 712-13 (1st Cir. 1999).

Because the severance agreements did not validly waive WARN rights, the class may pursue statutory damages under 29 U.S.C. § 2104. The judgment should be reversed.
