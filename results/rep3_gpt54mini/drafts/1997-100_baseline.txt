**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial after an appellate finding of insufficient evidence because the State has already had a full and fair opportunity to prove the allegation once. In **Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1, 16–18 (1978)**, the Court held that reversal for evidentiary insufficiency is the equivalent of an acquittal and that a second trial would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause. That principle should apply here. California’s prior-conviction allegation procedure is not a mere administrative formality; it is a contested adjudication of a fact that increases punishment, and the State bears the burden of proof. See **People v. Tenner, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 565–66, 24 Cal. Rptr. 2d 840, 862 P.2d 840 (1993)**; **In re Yurko, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 862–63, 112 Cal. Rptr. 513, 519 P.2d 561 (1974)**.

The California Supreme Court erred in treating noncapital sentencing as categorically exempt from double jeopardy. The question is not whether sentencing generally requires all the procedures of a trial on guilt, but whether the proceeding at issue has become trial-like in the relevant constitutional sense. In **Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430, 438–46 (1981)**, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a sentencing proceeding has “the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” The Court reaffirmed that approach in **Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203, 209–12 (1984)**, holding that an adverse capital sentencing finding based on insufficiency of proof bars retrial. And in **Poland v. Arizona, 476 U.S. 147, 155–57 (1986)**, the Court explained that once the sentencer has resolved factual issues in the defendant’s favor, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes another attempt to prove the same aggravating facts.

Those principles fit this case. California does not treat prior-conviction allegations as informal sentencing considerations. The Legislature has required that the allegation “must be tried” to a jury or, as here, to the court. Cal. Penal Code § 1025. California decisions have further imposed proof beyond a reasonable doubt, confrontation rights, and evidentiary constraints. See **Tenner, 6 Cal. 4th at 566**; **People v. Reed, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224, 228 n.6, 52 Cal. Rptr. 2d 106, 914 P.2d 184 (1996)**; **People v. Myers, 5 Cal. 4th 1193, 1201, 22 Cal. Rptr. 2d 911, 858 P.2d 301 (1993)**. Where the State chooses to convert a sentencing enhancement into a discrete adjudicatory proceeding, with proof and factfinding on a contested element that increases the penalty, the proceeding cannot be insulated from the ordinary consequences of an evidentiary failure.

The Court’s reliance on the proposition that sentencing generally lacks the full panoply of trial rights proves too much. **Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 246–51 (1949)** upheld judicial discretion to consider out-of-court information at ordinary sentencing, but it did not involve a legislatively mandated, adversarial, burden-of-proof proceeding on a discrete enhancement allegation. Nor did **Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389, 398–99 (1995)** or **United States v. Watts, 519 U.S. 148, 154–57 (1997)** address retrial after a formal insufficiency ruling. Those cases confirm only that a sentencing court may consider relevant conduct in selecting a sentence within lawful limits; they do not authorize repeated prosecution of an enhancement allegation after the State has failed to prove it.

The State will likely rely on **Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721 (1998)**, but that decision should not control this Court’s constitutional analysis if the question is presented independently. To the extent Monge suggests a categorical rule, it departs from the logic of **Bullington** and **Rumsey**, which focus on the character of the proceeding and the finality of an insufficiency determination. See **Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439–46**; **Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209–12**. The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” **North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)**. When the State fails to prove the fact that authorizes a harsher punishment, it has lost that contest. It may not be given a second chance. See **Burks, 437 U.S. at 11, 16–18**.

This Court should reverse and hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on the prior conviction allegation after the Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient.
