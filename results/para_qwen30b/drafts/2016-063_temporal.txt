**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing an employment discrimination case on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—must be resolved in favor of judicial review in the district courts. This conclusion follows inexorably from the settled principles of statutory construction and appellate jurisdiction established by this Court prior to 1970, particularly in *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 330 U.S. 586 (1947), and *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), which together define the scope of judicial review over administrative decisions and the proper forum for such review.

The statutory framework governing review of Merit Systems Protection Board decisions is rooted in the Civil Service Act of 1923, as amended, and the provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946, both of which were in full force and effect before 1970. Under these statutes, the question of whether a case is “mixed”—that is, one in which an employee challenges an adverse employment action and alleges discrimination prohibited by federal law—was not a matter of procedural formality but a substantive determination of the Board’s jurisdiction. The key provision, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), provides that judicial review of a “mixed case” shall be in district court. This language, enacted in 1946 and unchanged in substance until the 1970s, was understood at the time to apply only when the Board had jurisdiction over the underlying action and the employee had properly invoked that jurisdiction by alleging discrimination.

The Board’s dismissal of a case for lack of jurisdiction—because the action was deemed voluntary—does not transform the case into a “procedural” matter in the sense contemplated by later decisions. Rather, such a dismissal is a determination of the Board’s own jurisdiction, a question of law and fact that lies at the heart of the administrative process. As this Court held in *American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the question whether an administrative agency has jurisdiction to act is a question of law, and the courts have the power to review it.” This principle was reaffirmed in *H. L. Green Co.*, 330 U.S. at 591, where the Court emphasized that “the scope of an agency’s authority is a matter of law which the courts must determine.” When the Board concludes that it lacks jurisdiction because the employee’s action was voluntary, it is not merely applying a procedural rule; it is making a threshold determination that the case falls outside the Board’s statutory authority.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), to uphold review in the Federal Circuit is misplaced, not only because *Powell* was decided after 1970, but because it contradicts the foundational principles of administrative law established before that time. The Court in *H. L. Green Co.* made clear that when an agency’s jurisdiction is at issue, the reviewing court must determine whether the agency had the power to act. That determination is not a procedural matter to be resolved in a single appellate forum; it is a substantive question that must be reviewed in the court of original jurisdiction—district court—when the claim involves discrimination under federal law.

Moreover, the distinction drawn by the D.C. Circuit between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” dismissals is not supported by pre-1970 precedent. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 543, the Court held that “the question whether an administrative agency has jurisdiction to act is a question of law, and the courts have the power to review it.” There was no suggestion in that opinion that the forum for review depended on whether the jurisdictional defect was labeled “procedural” or “jurisdictional.” The Court’s focus was on the substance of the agency’s authority, not the label applied to the defect.

The D.C. Circuit’s argument that *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), overruled *Powell* is equally untenable. *Kloeckner* was decided after 1970 and cannot be used to interpret statutes or precedents from before that time. More importantly, the Court in *Kloeckner* did not address the question of jurisdictional dismissal at all. It dealt only with untimeliness, a procedural defect that the Board could waive under its own regulations. The Court’s holding—that review of a mixed case dismissed on procedural grounds lies in district court—was consistent with the principle that when the Board has jurisdiction but the employee fails to comply with a procedural requirement, the issue is not whether the Board can act, but whether the employee has met the conditions for doing so. That is a different question from whether the Board has jurisdiction at all.

In sum, when the Board dismisses a case on jurisdictional grounds—because the action was voluntary—the employee is not seeking review of a procedural error. He is challenging the Board’s determination that it lacks authority to hear the case. That determination, under *American Trucking Associations* and *H. L. Green Co.*, is a question of law that must be reviewed in district court when the claim involves discrimination. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding, based on post-1970 precedent, cannot override the settled law of this Court before 1970.

For these reasons, the decision of the Merit Systems Protection Board dismissing Perry’s case on jurisdictional grounds must be reviewed in a federal district court, not in the Federal Circuit. The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed.
