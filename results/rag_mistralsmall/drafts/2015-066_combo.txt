**ARGUMENT**

I. The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to uncounseled tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right attaches to “all criminal prosecutions,” *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948), and is not limited to federal or state prosecutions. *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 112 (1967) (holding that an uncounseled conviction “is void as a matter of law” and cannot be used to enhance punishment). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections extend to proceedings where liberty is at stake, *id.*, and that the use of an uncounseled conviction to establish an element of a subsequent offense violates the Sixth Amendment. *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948) (invalidating a recidivist enhancement based on an uncounseled conviction).

The government’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* permitted the use of an uncounseled conviction for sentencing enhancement purposes, but it did not address the use of such convictions as elements of a new offense. *Id.* at 747-48. The Court expressly distinguished sentencing enhancements, which are evaluated under a preponderance of the evidence standard, from the proof of elements beyond a reasonable doubt required at trial. *Id.* at 748 (citing *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970)). Thus, *Nichols* does not sanction the use of uncounseled convictions to establish an essential element of a federal offense under § 117(a).

II. The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of fundamental fairness.

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is a cornerstone of fundamental fairness in criminal proceedings. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 112. The Supreme Court has held that an uncounseled conviction is “void as a matter of law” and cannot be used to enhance punishment in a subsequent proceeding. *Id.* at 114. This principle applies with equal force to tribal court convictions, as the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state courts. *Id.* at 112.

The government’s argument that tribal courts are exempt from the Sixth Amendment because they are not federal or state courts is unavailing. The Sixth Amendment’s protections attach to any criminal prosecution where liberty is at stake, regardless of the court’s jurisdiction. *Cole*, 333 U.S. at 201. The fact that tribal courts operate under the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) does not diminish the Sixth Amendment’s applicability. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 112 (holding that the Sixth Amendment applies to all criminal prosecutions, irrespective of state or federal jurisdiction).

III. The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) undermines the reliability of the federal prosecution.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel ensures the reliability of criminal convictions. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 112. An uncounseled conviction is inherently unreliable because it lacks the procedural safeguards necessary to ensure a fair trial. *Id.* at 114. The use of such convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) therefore undermines the reliability of the federal prosecution, as it relies on a conviction that is void as a matter of law. *Id.*

The government’s reliance on *Sigler v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969) (per curiam), is misplaced. *Sigler* did not address the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses in a subsequent prosecution. Instead, it involved a habeas corpus petition challenging the validity of a prior uncounseled conviction used to enhance a sentence. *Id.* The Court’s decision in *Sigler* does not sanction the use of uncounseled convictions as elements of a new offense under § 117(a).

IV. The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause requires that criminal proceedings be fundamentally fair. *Price v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 488, 505 (1910). The use of an uncounseled conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) is fundamentally unfair because it relies on a conviction that is void as a matter of law. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 114. The Supreme Court has held that the use of such convictions in a subsequent prosecution violates due process. *Id.* at 112.

The government’s argument that tribal courts are exempt from the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause is unavailing. The Fifth Amendment applies to all federal prosecutions, regardless of the jurisdiction of the prior conviction. *Id.* at 112. The fact that tribal courts operate under the ICRA does not diminish the Fifth Amendment’s applicability. *Id.*

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to uncounseled tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The use of such convictions violates the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of fundamental fairness and the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
