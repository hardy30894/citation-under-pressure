**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Possesses No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

This Court has long held that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). In *Dumschat*, the Court rejected a due process challenge to a state clemency board’s refusal to explain its denial of a commutation application, concluding that the inmate had no constitutionally protected liberty interest in the clemency process itself. *Id.* at 465. That holding controls here.

The Sixth Circuit’s lead opinion attempted to distinguish *Dumschat* by asserting that Ohio’s clemency procedure is “an integral part of the … system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.” *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956). But the Court has never extended that reasoning to executive clemency. As Judge Nelson correctly observed below, “the Supreme Court has sometimes been dubious about the wisdom of pushing the logic of its decisions to the extreme.” *Woodard v. Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 107 F.3d 1178, 1187 (6th Cir. 1997) (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). Indeed, *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985)—the case on which the Sixth Circuit relied—involved a first appeal as of right, not a wholly discretionary clemency proceeding. *Evitts* itself recognized that constitutional protections do not extend to collateral proceedings such as habeas corpus. *Id.* at 394 (citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). Clemency is far more remote from trial than habeas, and “the great distance that lies between the trial and appeal stage, on the one hand, and the executive clemency stage on the other” counsels against imposing due process requirements. *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1187 (Nelson, J., dissenting).

Ohio’s clemency process is entirely discretionary. The Governor enjoys “almost unfettered discretion” under Article III, § 11 of the Ohio Constitution. *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1186 (Nelson, J., dissenting). The General Assembly may not “prescribe substantive regulations limiting the Governor’s discretion.” *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520 (1994). Because no state law creates a legitimate claim of entitlement to clemency, no liberty interest arises. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465. The Sixth Circuit’s remand for a determination of “minimal” due process protections therefore lacks any constitutional foundation.

**II. The Voluntary Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976). Ohio’s clemency procedure does not compel any inmate to speak. The regulation provides that the inmate may *request* a personal interview, but nothing requires participation. *See* Ohio Death Penalty Clemency Procedure, Part IV C.2. As the district court correctly held, the interview is purely voluntary.

The Sixth Circuit erred by suggesting that the option of an interview might impose an “unconstitutional condition.” *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1190 (lead opinion). But the state cannot be said to coerce a waiver of Fifth Amendment rights when it merely offers a voluntary proceeding. *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 316. In *Baxter*, the Court held that an inmate’s decision to remain silent in a prison disciplinary hearing did not violate the Fifth Amendment because the inmate was “not compelled to be a witness against himself.” *Id.* at 317. So too here. Woodard faces no penalty for declining the interview; the clemency review proceeds regardless. Any risk of self-incrimination is “theoretical” and “premature.” *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1188 (Nelson, J., dissenting) (quoting *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 324). This Court should not countenance a federal injunction based on speculation about future events that may never occur.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s dismissal of the complaint should be affirmed.
