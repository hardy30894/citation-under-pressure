## Argument

### I. Arizona’s evidentiary rule constitutes a permissible exercise of state authority to define the elements of criminal offenses and the defenses thereto.

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a State to treat evidence of mental disease or disease as negating the *mens rea* element of a crime. This Court has long recognized that “the State is free to define the elements of its criminal offenses” and to allocate the burden of proof with respect to affirmative defenses. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 208 (1977). In *McMillan v. Pennsylvania*, 477 U.S. 79, 85 (1986), the Court reaffirmed that “the States possess broad authority under their police powers to regulate the procedures under which criminal laws are implemented.” Arizona’s decision to confine evidence of mental disease or defect to the insanity defense—rather than allowing it to negate *mens rea*—is a substantive policy choice that respects the traditional distinction between the elements of a crime and affirmative defenses. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798–99 (1952) (upholding a state’s requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt).

### II. The rule does not violate due process because it does not preclude the defendant from presenting a meaningful defense.

A defendant’s right to present relevant evidence is not absolute. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 55 (1987). A State may exclude evidence if its probative value is outweighed by the danger of confusion, prejudice, or misleading the jury. *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998). Here, Arizona did not bar Clark from introducing expert testimony about his mental state; it merely channeled that evidence to the insanity defense, where the law placed the burden on him. The trial court allowed Clark to present his expert evidence in support of insanity, but he failed to satisfy that burden. Because the State is not required to “run the risk of having the jury consider evidence of mental disease” in evaluating *mens rea*, it may adopt a rule that “ensures that the insanity defense remains the exclusive vehicle for considering mental disease” in criminal cases. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 49–50 (1996) (plurality opinion) (upholding exclusion of voluntary intoxication evidence to negate *mens rea*).

This Court’s decision in *Egelhoff* is directly analogous. There, the Court upheld a Montana statute that prohibited a defendant from presenting evidence of voluntary intoxication to prove that he lacked the requisite mental state. 518 U.S. at 56. The plurality emphasized that “the State may be entitled to decide that the defendant’s intoxication is not a relevant factor in determining his *mens rea*” because the State’s definition of *mens rea* may incorporate a normative judgment about culpability. *Id.* at 50–51. Similarly, Arizona may decide that mental disease or defect is relevant only to the question of legal insanity—a determination that speaks to culpability in a different, more fundamental way than the specific intent element of the crime. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 536 (1968) (plurality opinion) (observing that the insanity defense serves as a “concession to the infinite variety of mental states” that the criminal law cannot fully accommodate within the *mens rea* framework).

### III. This Court has consistently upheld state rules that limit the use of mental condition evidence to the insanity defense.

In *Leland v. Oregon*, the Court rejected a due process challenge to Oregon’s rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. 343 U.S. at 799. The Court noted that the State could constitutionally require the defendant to bear the burden of proving insanity, and that the defendant’s evidence of mental condition was not improperly excluded because it was considered under the insanity defense. *Id.* at 797–98. Here, as in *Leland*, Arizona’s rule does not prevent Clark from presenting his expert evidence; it simply assigns that evidence to a specific legal category. *See also Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 233–34 (1987) (upholding a state’s requirement that the defendant prove self-defense by a preponderance of the evidence, even though the evidence could negate the *mens rea* of murder).

The distinction between *mens rea* and insanity is a long-standing feature of Anglo-American criminal law. Insanity addresses the defendant’s capacity to understand the wrongfulness of his actions, while *mens rea* focuses on his specific intent at the time of the act. Arizona’s rule preserves this distinction and avoids the confusion that would arise if juries were permitted to consider the same evidence under two different legal standards. The State has a legitimate interest in “avoiding the risk of jury confusion and the potential for inconsistent verdicts” that could result from allowing mental condition evidence to be used for both purposes. *See Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 314.

### IV. Conclusion

Because Arizona’s evidentiary rule is a reasonable exercise of state authority, does not preclude the defendant from presenting a defense, and is consistent with this Court’s precedents, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
