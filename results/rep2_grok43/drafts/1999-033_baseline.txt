**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not authorize a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one.” The Clause “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). It forbids “class legislation” that singles out a defined group for adverse treatment while leaving others untouched. The Civil Rights Cases, 109 U.S. 3, 24 (1883). A plaintiff who alleges only that government officials acted out of personal animus toward her individually, without claiming membership in any class or group, therefore fails to state a claim under the Clause.

This Court has consistently required an allegation of class-based discrimination. In The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 81 (1873), the Court explained that the Clause was intended to prevent “hostile and discriminating legislation against” particular classes. Later decisions have reiterated that the Clause addresses “the denial of equal laws” to members of a class, not “individual wrongs.” See, e.g., Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954); Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976). The Seventh Circuit’s contrary rule—that a single plaintiff may prevail by showing that state action was “motivated solely by a ‘spiteful effort to “get” him’”—finds no support in these precedents.

The decisions on which the court of appeals relied do not establish a class-of-one theory. Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County, 260 U.S. 441 (1923), and Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. Commission, 488 U.S. 336 (1989), involved systematic undervaluation or overvaluation of an entire category of property; each plaintiff was treated differently from others within the same class. Neither case holds that an isolated, individualized demand, standing alone, violates the Equal Protection Clause.

Recognizing a class-of-one claim would transform the Clause into a general prohibition on arbitrary or malicious governmental action. That function is already performed by other constitutional provisions and by state tort law. See, e.g., Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693, 701 (1976). The Equal Protection Clause was not designed for that purpose, and the judgment below should be reversed.
