The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” gloss on Exemption 2 contravenes the statutory text and the settled rule that exemptions from disclosure must be confined to their literal terms. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2) exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” That language admits no additional qualifier. Morgan v. United States, 304 U.S. 1, 22 (1938), long ago held that an agency “may not impose upon the parties any greater burden than the statute itself imposes.”

The phrase “related solely” is restrictive, not expansive. As this Court explained in another context, when Congress uses “solely,” it means “to the exclusion of all else.” United States v. Morgan, 307 U.S. 183, 190 (1939). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test substitutes a looser standard that permits withholding whenever internal use predominates, even if the documents also serve external regulatory or safety functions. That substitution enlarges the exemption beyond the words Congress enacted.

The same principle of strict construction appears throughout the Court’s pre-FOIA administrative decisions. In Service v. Dulles, 354 U.S. 363, 388 (1957), the Court refused to imply additional exceptions to a statute requiring disclosure of agency action, stating that “the power of an administrative officer to withhold information must be found in the statute itself.” Likewise, Greene v. McElroy, 360 U.S. 474, 507 (1959), cautioned that “courts must be alert to prevent the unauthorized exercise of power” by agencies seeking to withhold information. Vitarelli v. Seaton, 359 U.S. 535, 545 (1959), applied the same rule to personnel records, holding that an agency could not withhold documents merely because they touched internal operations; the statute had to authorize the withholding.

The Navy’s documents calculate blast effects for ammunition stored near civilian communities. They therefore affect persons outside the agency. FCC v. Schreiber, 381 U.S. 279, 291 (1965), rejected an agency’s attempt to withhold information that, though generated internally, bore directly on the rights of private parties. The same reasoning applies here: the ESQD materials are not “related solely” to internal personnel rules when their principal consequence is felt by the surrounding public.

Peters v. Hobby, 349 U.S. 331, 345 (1955), further confirms that agencies may not enlarge exemptions by administrative interpretation. There the Court set aside an agency’s broader reading of its own confidentiality regulation because the regulation could not “be construed to authorize what the statute itself does not permit.” The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard does precisely that.

Because the documents fall outside the narrow statutory language of Exemption 2, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
