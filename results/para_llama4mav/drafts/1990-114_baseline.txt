ARGUMENT

I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR STATE COURT JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE AGE DISCRIMINATION IN EMPLOYMENT ACT (ADEA)

The ADEA prohibits employers from discriminating against employees on the basis of age, but it exempts certain individuals, including "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question presented here is whether Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption.

The Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" and therefore are excluded from the ADEA's protection. As the First Circuit noted in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), "policymaking" is a part of the function of judging, as judges create law by filling the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. Missouri's appointed judges exercise considerable policymaking responsibility, particularly in their supervisory roles. For example, the Missouri Supreme Court is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar, and for resolving disputes within the judicial hierarchy. See Mo. Const. art. V, Sec. 4; In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc).

The legislative history of the ADEA exemption supports the conclusion that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." Although the conference report accompanying the 1972 amendments to the ADEA states that the exemption should be construed narrowly, it also indicates that the exemption applies to "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The state judiciary is a critical "agency" of state government, and appointed judges are policymakers within the meaning of the exemption.

II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR STATE COURT JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT

The Equal Protection Clause requires that state laws be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is rationally related to several legitimate government interests, including the need to ensure that judges are physically and mentally capable of performing their duties, and the desire to promote a representative judiciary by creating opportunities for younger judges, including women and minorities. See Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The rational basis test is a deferential standard of review, and the Court should not second-guess the Missouri legislature's judgment regarding the need for a mandatory retirement requirement. See Murgia, 427 U.S. at 314-317. As the Eighth Circuit correctly held, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement is rationally related to legitimate government interests, and therefore does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
