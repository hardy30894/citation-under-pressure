The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. Under the settled “intelligible principle” doctrine, Congress may confer discretion on executive officers if it supplies a policy and standards to guide the exercise of that discretion. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 421 (1935); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 425-26 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The CSA easily satisfies that test. Congress identified the public-safety objective, specified the factors to be considered, and required consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services before scheduling action is taken. 21 U.S.C. § 811(a), (b), (c), (h). That is a paradigmatic case of lawful delegation. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 426-27.

This Court has long upheld delegations in technically complex and fast-changing fields. In Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 693-94 (1892), the Court sustained a statute authorizing the President to apply tariff consequences upon specified findings, explaining that Congress may leave to another branch the ascertainment of facts on which statutory operation depends. In Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813), the Court upheld trade restrictions triggered by a presidential proclamation. In Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), the Court sustained administrative authority to exclude “inferior” tea because Congress had supplied an ascertainable standard. And in United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-18 (1911), the Court held that Congress may authorize officials to “fill up the details” of a statutory scheme, even where violations of valid regulations are criminally punishable.

Petitioners’ reliance on Panama Refining and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those decisions invalidated statutes that gave officials broad power without sufficient congressional standards. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 430; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. By contrast, the CSA tightly cabins temporary scheduling to circumstances presenting an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” directs consideration of specified factors, limits the duration of the temporary order, and requires notice and consultation. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1)-(4). That structure resembles the delegations sustained in Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424-27, and American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), not the standardless grants rejected in 1935.

Nor does the criminal character of the CSA change the constitutional analysis. Congress may authorize administrative action that carries criminal consequences when the underlying delegation is valid. Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 517-19; see also United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892). The relevant question is whether Congress has set the legislative policy and standards; it plainly has. J.W. Hampton, 276 U.S. at 409; Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-26. Temporary scheduling is thus an execution of legislatively supplied policy, not an exercise of free-floating lawmaking.

Petitioners’ subdelegation challenge fails for an independent reason. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the drug laws. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Long before 1970, this Court recognized that executive functions may be carried out through subordinate officers under departmental authority. See Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 318-19 (1903); United States v. George, 228 U.S. 14, 24-25 (1913). In addition, the Attorney General had authority to assign functions within the Department of Justice to the DEA Administrator by regulation. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). Nothing in the CSA suggests that Congress intended the Attorney General personally to perform every temporary scheduling act himself, and the statutory command that the Attorney General act through an administratively workable enforcement structure confirms the contrary. See Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 518; Buttfield, 192 U.S. at 496.

The CSA is also the sort of regulatory measure for which delegation has always been especially appropriate. Congress was confronting a rapidly evolving drug market in which manufacturers could evade fixed schedules by creating synthetic analogues faster than legislation could be enacted. In such a setting, the Constitution permits Congress to authorize prompt executive response so long as it supplies intelligible limits. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-26; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250; American Power & Light, 329 U.S. at 105. Congress did so here. The judgment below should be affirmed.
