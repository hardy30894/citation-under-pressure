ARGUMENT

The district court erred in designating Bryshun Genard Furlow as an "armed career criminal" under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and as a "career offender" under the Sentencing Guidelines. Furlow's prior convictions for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina and first-degree arson in Georgia do not qualify as predicate offenses under the ACCA and the Guidelines.

I.

Furlow's Conviction for Distribution of Crack Cocaine in South Carolina is Not a Predicate "Serious Drug Offense" or "Controlled Substance Offense"

The ACCA defines a "serious drug offense" as an offense under state law involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute a controlled substance, for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a "controlled substance offense" similarly, as an offense under federal or state law punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year that prohibits the manufacture, import, export, distribution, or dispensing of a controlled substance, or the possession of a controlled substance with intent to manufacture, import, export, distribute, or dispense. USSG § 4B1.2(b).

Furlow's 2016 conviction for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina does not qualify as a predicate offense under either definition. Under South Carolina law, distribution of crack cocaine is punishable by a maximum term of imprisonment of five years for a first offense, and up to ten years for a subsequent offense. S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-375(B)(2)(a). Because Furlow's prior conviction was for a first offense, it does not meet the ACCA's requirement of a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more. See United States v. Simmons, 649 F.3d 237, 243-44 (4th Cir. 2011) (en banc) (holding that a prior conviction is only considered a predicate offense if the defendant could have received the requisite sentence).

Moreover, the Guidelines definition of a "controlled substance offense" excludes offenses that are punishable by less than one year of imprisonment. USSG § 4B1.2(b). As Furlow's prior conviction was punishable by up to five years, but was actually sentenced as a first offense, it is unclear whether it meets the Guidelines definition. However, this Court has held that a prior conviction must be punishable by more than one year of imprisonment to qualify as a predicate offense under the Guidelines. See United States v. Hinkle, 832 F.3d 569, 574-75 (5th Cir. 2016) (cited with approval in United States v. Mills, 917 F.3d 324, 330 (4th Cir. 2019)). Therefore, Furlow's conviction for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina is not a predicate "controlled substance offense."

II.

Furlow's Conviction for First-Degree Arson in Georgia is Not a Predicate "Violent Felony" or "Crime of Violence"

The ACCA and the Guidelines both enumerate "arson" as a qualifying predicate offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). However, this Court has held that a prior conviction for arson only qualifies as a predicate offense if it meets the generic definition of arson. See United States v. Whitley, 529 F.3d 150, 155-57 (2d Cir. 2008) (cited with approval in United States v. Gatson, 776 F.3d 405, 410 (6th Cir. 2015)).

Under Georgia law, first-degree arson is defined as knowingly damaging a dwelling or other structure without consent, with the intent to damage or recklessly causing damage by means of fire or explosive. Ga. Code Ann. § 16-7-60(a). This definition is broader than the generic definition of arson, which requires malicious burning. See United States v. Misleveck, 735 F.3d 983, 988 (7th Cir. 2013) (holding that a prior conviction for arson under a state statute that did not require malicious burning did not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA). Therefore, Furlow's conviction for first-degree arson in Georgia is not a predicate "violent felony" or "crime of violence."

Furthermore, this Court has applied the categorical approach to determine whether a prior conviction qualifies as a predicate offense. See United States v. Doctor, 842 F.3d 306, 308-09 (4th Cir. 2016) (per curiam). Under this approach, the Court looks to the elements of the prior conviction, rather than the underlying facts. Id. As the Georgia first-degree arson statute is broader than the generic definition of arson, Furlow's conviction does not qualify as a predicate offense.

In sum, Furlow's prior convictions for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina and first-degree arson in Georgia do not qualify as predicate offenses under the ACCA and the Guidelines. Therefore, the district court erred in designating him as an "armed career criminal" and a "career offender." This Court should vacate Furlow's sentence and remand for resentencing.
