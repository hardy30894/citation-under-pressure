**ARGUMENT**

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). A police officer may conduct a protective pat-down search—commonly known as a “frisk”—only when he has a reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant the intrusion.” *Id.* at 21. Here, the officers lacked any such reasonable suspicion when they removed Michael Soares from the vehicle and conducted a pat-frisk. The district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress the firearm, and this court must reverse.

The initial stop of the green Lexus was lawful. The failure to illuminate headlights constitutes a traffic violation under Massachusetts law, and the officers had probable cause to stop the vehicle. *See United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 422 U.S. 873, 880 (1975) (noting that traffic violations provide a valid basis for a stop, even if not the sole reason). However, the legality of the stop does not justify the subsequent frisk. The Fourth Amendment requires that each search be independently justified. *See United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. 220, 224 (1960).

The officers’ decision to frisk Soares was not supported by reasonable suspicion. The only conduct cited by the officers as justifying the frisk was Soares’s verbal abuse and his rocking back and forth in the seat. While such behavior may be annoying or provocative, it does not rise to the level of reasonable suspicion that a person is armed and dangerous. *See Adams v. Williams*, 407 U.S. 143, 147 (1972) (holding that “mere nervousness, fidgeting, or evasiveness” is insufficient to justify a frisk). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the Fourth Amendment protects the individual’s right to be free from arbitrary intrusions by law enforcement.” *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 482 (1963).

The officers’ reliance on “furtive movements” observed before the stop is equally unavailing. The movements—wherein the front-seat passenger (Soares) and the driver gestured toward the console and door—were not observed by the officers until after the car had already begun to move. *See United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 229 (1985) (noting that “fleeting or ambiguous movements” do not justify a frisk). Moreover, the officers did not observe any movement in the back seat, and the conduct of the driver and Soares was not clearly indicative of weapon concealment. *See United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 422 U.S. at 880 (holding that “a mere suspicion” based on appearance or conduct is insufficient to justify a frisk).

The officers’ decision to remove Soares from the car and conduct a pat-frisk was not based on any articulable facts suggesting danger. The Supreme Court has held that “a frisk is justified only when the officer has reason to believe that the person is armed and dangerous.” *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. at 27. Here, the officers had no information that Soares had a weapon, no indication that he had touched a concealed object, and no evidence that he had attempted to conceal anything. The fact that Soares was agitated and used profanity does not justify a frisk. *See Adams v. Williams*, 407 U.S. at 147. The Court has made clear that “the mere fact that a person is nervous or hostile does not justify a search.” *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. at 482.

Furthermore, the officers’ decision to conduct a frisk was not based on a reasonable belief that Soares posed a threat to their safety. The officers had not been threatened, no weapon had been seen, and no attempt to flee had occurred. *See United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. at 224. The presence of two additional officers at the scene—Lapolis and Brooks—does not justify a frisk absent a reasonable suspicion of danger. *See United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. at 229. The mere presence of backup officers does not transform a lawful stop into a lawful frisk.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that “the Fourth Amendment does not permit a search based on a hunch or a generalized suspicion.” *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. at 27. The officers’ actions here were based on a subjective sense of unease, not on objective facts. *See United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 422 U.S. at 880. The Court has rejected the notion that “a police officer may frisk a person simply because he feels uneasy.” *Adams v. Williams*, 407 U.S. at 147. The officers’ “uneasiness” was not grounded in any specific, articulable facts. *See United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. at 224.

Finally, the Supreme Court has emphasized that “the reasonableness of a search depends on the totality of the circumstances.” *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. at 21. Here, the totality of the circumstances—including the absence of any indication of weapon possession, the lack of any threat to officer safety, and the mere presence of verbal hostility—does not support a frisk. *See United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. at 229. The officers’ actions were not reasonable under the Fourth Amendment.
