**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. No decision of this Court before 1970 supports such a bifurcated competency inquiry. To the contrary, the established constitutional standard for competency—whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him,” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam)—applies equally to all stages of a criminal proceeding, including the entry of a guilty plea and the waiver of counsel.

**I. The *Dusky* Standard Governs Competency for All Purposes**

This Court has never distinguished between competency to stand trial and competency to waive constitutional rights. In *Dusky*, the Court articulated the single test for competency: whether the defendant possesses the capacity to understand the proceedings and to assist counsel. 362 U.S. at 402. That test was reaffirmed in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 386 (1966), which held that a trial court must conduct a competency hearing whenever “sufficient doubt” exists. Neither *Dusky* nor *Pate* suggests that a different, higher standard applies when a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” requirement—that a defendant must have the capacity to weigh alternatives rationally—is simply a restatement of the *Dusky* standard. A defendant who has a rational understanding of the proceedings necessarily possesses the ability to make a reasoned choice among available options. The *Dusky* test already encompasses that capacity. There is no historical support for a separate, more demanding inquiry.

**II. Pre-1970 Precedent Does Not Support a Heightened Standard**

The Ninth Circuit relied on *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), for the proposition that a guilty plea requires “utmost solicitude.” *Boykin* held that a court must ensure a plea is knowing and voluntary, but it did not alter the competency standard. 395 U.S. at 242–43. *Boykin* requires that the record demonstrate a defendant’s understanding of the rights waived; it does not demand a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial.

Similarly, *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), does not support the Ninth Circuit’s rule. In *Westbrook*, the Court reversed a conviction because the record failed to show that the defendant’s waiver of counsel was knowing and intelligent. 384 U.S. at 150. The Court noted that the defendant “may be competent to stand trial” but that the record did not demonstrate a valid waiver. *Id.* That observation merely underscores that a competency finding alone does not automatically establish a valid waiver; the waiver must also be knowing and intelligent. *Westbrook* did not create a separate competency standard for waivers. It simply required that the trial court examine the circumstances of the waiver to ensure it was voluntary and informed—a requirement already satisfied by the *Dusky* competency determination and the subsequent colloquy.

**III. The State Court Applied the Correct Standard**

The Nevada trial court found Moran competent to stand trial under *Dusky* and *Pate*. That finding was supported by two psychiatric evaluations. When Moran later sought to waive counsel and plead guilty, the court conducted a colloquy to ensure his waiver was knowing and intelligent. The court asked Moran about his medication, his understanding of the charges, and his desire to discharge counsel. Moran’s monosyllabic responses do not demonstrate incompetence; they reflect a deliberate, albeit self-destructive, choice. The *Dusky* standard does not require a defendant to make wise decisions—only rational ones.

The post-conviction hearing confirmed that Moran was competent under *Dusky*. The Ninth Circuit erred by requiring the state court to apply a “reasoned choice” standard that has no basis in this Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence. Because the state court applied the correct constitutional standard, its competency determination is entitled to deference.

**Conclusion**

The Due Process Clause requires only that a defendant be competent to stand trial under *Dusky* and *Pate*. A trial court need not conduct a separate inquiry into a defendant’s capacity for “reasoned choice” before accepting a guilty plea or waiver of counsel. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
