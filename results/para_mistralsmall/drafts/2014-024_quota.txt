**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

The Third Circuit erred by upholding Elonis’s conviction on an objective standard that dispenses with any showing of subjective intent. This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment permits punishment of “true threats” because such statements “by their very utterance . . . inflict great harm and have limited social value.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (quoting *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam)). But the Court in *Black* also made clear that the exception is “limited in scope to words that ‘by their very utterance’ cause harm,” and that the speaker must intend his words to be taken as a threat. *Id.* at 360 (emphasis added). The Court rejected Virginia’s argument that “true threats” could be defined solely by their effect on the listener, explaining that such an approach would “undermine[] the constitutionally required intent element.” *Id.* at 365.

This Court’s precedents confirm that the First Amendment requires a showing of subjective intent in true-threat prosecutions. In *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886 (1982), the Court upheld a judgment against civil rights leaders for boycott-related violence, but it emphasized that liability turned on proof that the defendants “acted with the intent to further an unlawful goal.” *Id.* at 918. Similarly, in *Hess v. Indiana*, 414 U.S. 105 (1973), the Court reversed a conviction for disorderly conduct where the evidence failed to show that the defendant intended his speech to incite imminent lawless action. *Id.* at 108-09. These cases establish that when speech is alleged to fall outside First Amendment protection, the government must prove the speaker’s subjective intent.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a reasonable listener’s perception suffices—is foreclosed by this Court’s decision in *Black*. The Court in *Black* expressly rejected the notion that the First Amendment permits punishment of speech based solely on its tendency to frighten listeners. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 364-65. The Court cautioned that “a speaker’s reasonable prediction that his speech will be interpreted as a threat is not sufficient for liability,” because such an approach would “criminalize a substantial amount of constitutionally protected speech.” *Id.* at 365. The Third Circuit’s objective standard thus conflicts with this Court’s holding that the First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent in true-threat prosecutions.

II. The Third Circuit’s objective standard conflicts with this Court’s precedents governing the mens rea required for criminal liability.

This Court has repeatedly held that criminal statutes require proof of a culpable mental state unless Congress clearly indicates otherwise. *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 70 (1994) (citing *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952)). In *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), the Court held that the government must prove a defendant knew his weapon was capable of automatic fire before convicting him under the National Firearms Act. *Id.* at 619. Similarly, in *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419 (1985), the Court required proof that a defendant knew his conduct was unlawful before convicting him of food stamp fraud. *Id.* at 426.

Section 875(c) is silent as to mens rea, and this Court has held that such silence does not dispense with the requirement of a culpable mental state. *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 70. The Third Circuit’s objective standard—which permits conviction even when the speaker did not intend his words to be taken as threats—thus conflicts with this Court’s precedents governing the mens rea required for criminal liability.

III. The Third Circuit’s rule is untenable because it would criminalize vast amounts of protected speech.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard would chill constitutionally protected speech by permitting prosecutions based on how listeners *perceive* statements, rather than what the speaker *intended*. This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech that, while controversial or offensive, does not constitute true threats. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“[A] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest . . . .”). The Third Circuit’s rule would permit the government to prosecute individuals for speech that, while frightening to some listeners, was not intended as a threat—such as political hyperbole, rap lyrics, or theatrical performances. *See Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708 (holding that a statement about shooting the President was protected political hyperbole).

This Court has repeatedly warned against laws that chill protected speech by permitting prosecutions based on listener reaction. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444 (1969), the Court held that speech may be punished only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” *Id.* at 447. The Third Circuit’s objective standard would permit prosecutions under § 875(c) even when the speaker lacked the intent to threaten, thus chilling protected speech.

IV. The Third Circuit’s rule is inconsistent with the holdings of other circuits.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard is out of step with the majority of circuits, which require proof of subjective intent in true-threat prosecutions. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all require proof that the speaker intended his statements to be taken as threats. *See United States v. Jeffries*, 692 F.3d 488, 497 (6th Cir. 2012) (requiring subjective intent); *United States v. Morales*, 108 F.3d 1031, 1037 (9th Cir. 1997) (same); *United States v. Orozco-Santillan*, 903 F.2d 1246, 1249 (9th Cir. 1990) (same). The Third Circuit’s contrary rule thus creates a circuit split that warrants this Court’s review.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s decision and remand for further proceedings consistent with the First Amendment’s requirement of subjective intent in true-threat prosecutions.
