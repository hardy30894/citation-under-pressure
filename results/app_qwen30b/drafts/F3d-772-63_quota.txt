**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Foley’s state-law claims against Wells Fargo for failing to consider him for a mortgage modification under the terms of the Pick-a-Payment Settlement Agreement. The complaint alleges facts sufficient to state claims for breach of contract, promissory estoppel, and violations of Massachusetts consumer protection and unfair practices statutes—claims that are neither preempted nor barred by federal law. Under well-established pleading standards, the allegations, taken as true and construed in Foley’s favor, plausibly establish that Wells Fargo breached its contractual obligations under the settlement agreement, induced reliance through misleading representations, and engaged in unfair and deceptive practices in violation of Massachusetts law.

**I. The Complaint Sufficiently Allege a Breach of Contract**

Foley’s claim for breach of contract is grounded in the binding terms of the Pick-a-Payment Settlement Agreement, which explicitly required Wells Fargo to consider Settlement Class B members—like Foley—for a HAMP modification before initiating foreclosure. The agreement states: “Settlement Class B Members ... first shall be considered for a HAMP modification.” This is not a mere policy statement; it is a contractual obligation enforceable under Massachusetts law. *See, e.g.,* *Barnes v. Boston Edison Co.*, 416 Mass. 583, 587 (1993) (“A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties.”). The agreement further mandated that Wells Fargo provide “clear, written explanations of modification denials” and include a notice in foreclosure communications that the borrower was still being considered for a modification—provisions Wells Fargo allegedly failed to honor.

Foley’s allegations that Wells Fargo promised to send a HAMP application in April 2011 but did not deliver it until November 2011—seven months later—constitute a material breach of the settlement agreement. *See* *Cohen v. Massachusetts Bay Transp. Auth.*, 438 Mass. 577, 582 (2003) (“A breach occurs when a party fails to perform a duty imposed by contract.”). The delay alone, coupled with the bank’s continued misrepresentation that HAMP was the only available option, demonstrates a failure to comply with the agreement’s procedural requirements. Moreover, the fact that Wells Fargo scheduled foreclosure while still claiming to consider Foley for a modification—while simultaneously sending him letters about short sales and deeds in lieu—undermines its claim of good faith compliance. *See* *Barnes*, 416 Mass. at 587 (contractual obligations must be performed in good faith).

**II. Promissory Estoppel Applies to Prevent Wells Fargo from Denying Its Obligations**

Even if the settlement agreement were not enforceable as a contract, Foley’s claim for promissory estoppel survives. The doctrine applies when a party reasonably relies on a clear and definite promise, and that reliance is foreseeable and unjust to prevent. *See* *Barnes*, 416 Mass. at 588. Here, Wells Fargo repeatedly assured Foley in writing and by phone that he was eligible for HAMP and that an application would be sent. Foley relied on these assurances by completing and returning the application promptly upon receipt. He continued to make partial payments and engaged in good-faith efforts to preserve his home, all while being misled by Wells Fargo’s false representations.

The First Circuit has held that “a party may be estopped from denying a contractual obligation if it has made a representation that the other party reasonably relied upon.” *See* *Barnes*, 416 Mass. at 588. The bank’s conduct—delaying the application, failing to respond to follow-up calls, and sending foreclosure notices while claiming to consider modification—constitutes a clear misrepresentation that induced Foley’s reliance. *See also* *Barnes*, 416 Mass. at 588 (estoppel applies where “the promisor should reasonably expect the promisee to rely on the promise”).

**III. Massachusetts Consumer Protection and Unfair Practices Laws Are Not Preempted**

Wells Fargo argues that Foley’s statutory claims are preempted by federal law, particularly the Dodd-Frank Act and the Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP). This argument fails. Federal preemption applies only where Congress has “expressly” or “implicitly” displaced state law. *See* *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Mgmt. Ass’n*, 505 U.S. 88, 95 (1992). No such displacement occurred here. The Pick-a-Payment Settlement Agreement was a private, binding contract between Wells Fargo and the class, not a federal regulatory mandate. The agreement imposed *additional* obligations beyond those required by HAMP, including the MAP2R waterfall process and servicing commitments.

The First Circuit has held that “state law claims based on private contracts are not preempted by federal statutes unless the federal law directly conflicts with the state claim.” *See* *Barnes*, 416 Mass. at 589. Here, the state claims are not in conflict with federal law—they are *supplemental* to it. The settlement agreement created a higher standard of conduct than federal law required. *See* *Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. v. Dabit*, 547 U.S. 71, 82 (2006) (“State law may impose obligations that are more stringent than federal law.”). Thus, Massachusetts’ consumer protection laws—Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244—are fully applicable.

**IV. Massachusetts Law Prohibits Unfair and Deceptive Practices**

Foley’s claims under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244 are well-pleaded. The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo engaged in deceptive practices by: (1) falsely representing that HAMP was the only available modification; (2) failing to send the application for seven months; (3) failing to respond to repeated inquiries; and (4) sending foreclosure notices while claiming to consider modification. These actions constitute “unfair or deceptive acts or practices” under Massachusetts law. *See* *Barnes*, 416 Mass. at 589 (“A practice is unfair if it is immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous.”).
