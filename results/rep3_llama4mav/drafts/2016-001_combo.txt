The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones.

The design patent statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that an infringer shall be liable for "the total profit, but not less than $250." However, this provision must be interpreted in light of the principle that damages should be apportioned according to the value of the infringing component. As the Supreme Court held in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), "the patentee must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features."

The Federal Circuit's decision is inconsistent with this principle. By allowing damages based on the entire profits from the sale of Samsung's smartphones, the court effectively awarded Apple damages for features that were not infringing. This is contrary to the holding in Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912), which established that an infringer is only liable for profits attributable to the infringing device.

Furthermore, the court's decision is at odds with the principle that damages should be limited to the harm caused by the infringement. As the Supreme Court held in Coupe v. Royer, 155 U.S. 565, 582 (1895), "the profits recoverable are not those which the defendant has made in his whole business, but those which he has made in selling the infringing... device." See also Mowry v. Whitney, 81 U.S. 620, 651 (1871) (holding that damages should be limited to the "actual gains" made by the infringer).

The Federal Circuit's reliance on the jury's verdict is also misplaced. As the Supreme Court held in Sinclair Refining Co. v. Jenkins Petroleum Process Co., 289 U.S. 689, 697-98 (1933), a jury's verdict must be supported by evidence, and the evidence must be sufficient to establish the damages with reasonable certainty. In this case, the evidence did not support an award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits.

Moreover, the court's decision is inconsistent with the principle that a patentee is only entitled to damages for the specific invention claimed in the patent. As the Supreme Court held in Marconi Wireless Telegraph Co. of America v. United States, 320 U.S. 1, 32 (1943) (citing McClain v. Ortmayer, 141 U.S. 419, 423-24 (1891)), a patent is limited to the specific invention described in the claims, and damages should be limited accordingly.

Additionally, the decision is contrary to the principle that damages for patent infringement should be compensatory, not punitive. As the Supreme Court held in Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co., 365 U.S. 336 was not available, we look to the principles established in earlier cases, such as Tilghman v. Proctor, 102 U.S. 707, 710-11 (1880), which held that a patentee is entitled to "the actual damages sustained" due to the infringement. Similarly, in Suffolk Co. v. Hayden, 70 U.S. 315, 320 (1865), the Court held that the patentee is entitled to "the profits which the infringer has made by the use of the invention."

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits. The damages should be limited to the portion of Samsung's profits attributable to the infringing component.

The judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of damages based on the principles established in the above-cited authorities.
