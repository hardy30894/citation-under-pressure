**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit correctly held that Comcast failed to demonstrate that the District Court abused its discretion in certifying the class. A district court certifying a class under Rule 23(b)(3) must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to ensure that common questions predominate. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350-51 (2011). That analysis “may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits,” *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 317 (3d Cir. 2008), but it does not require a district court to resolve all merits disputes before certification. *Amgen Inc. v. Connecticut Retirement Plans & Trust Funds*, 568 U.S. 455, 465-66 (2013).

Here, the District Court properly applied that standard. After four days of evidentiary hearings, multiple expert reports, and extensive argument, the court found that respondents had demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that antitrust impact and damages could be proven through common evidence. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). That finding is entitled to deference and may be reversed only if “clearly erroneous.” *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985).

Comcast’s primary argument—that the District Court failed to require adequate admissible evidence of class-wide damages—misapprehends the law. Rule 23 does not require that damages be calculable “with mathematical precision” at the certification stage. *In re Urethane Antitrust Litig.*, 768 F.3d 1245, 1266 (10th Cir. 2014). Nor does it require a showing that damages will be identical across the class. *In re Rail Freight Fuel Surcharge Antitrust Litig.*, 725 F.3d 244, 252 (D.C. Cir. 2013). Instead, the question is whether “damages are capable of measurement and quantification on a class-wide basis” through common evidence. *In re Whirlpool Corp. Front-Loading Washer Prods. Liab. Litig.*, 722 F.3d 838, 854 (6th Cir. 2013). The District Court answered that question in the affirmative, based on Dr. McClave’s regression models showing that Comcast’s clustering caused supra-competitive prices. 264 F.R.D. at 183-88.

Comcast’s attacks on Dr. McClave’s methodology go to weight, not admissibility. A district court may consider expert testimony at certification even if the expert’s analysis is “imperfect” or subject to criticism. *In re Blood Reagents Antitrust Litig.*, 783 F.3d 183, 188 (3d Cir. 2015). Indeed, “respondents need not show that their expert’s testimony will be admitted at trial; they need only demonstrate that the testimony is ‘not fatally flawed.’” *In re Asacol Antitrust Litig.*, 907 F.3d 42, 54 (1st Cir. 2018). The District Court expressly found that McClave’s model was “sufficiently reliable to be used to calculate damages on a class-wide basis.” 264 F.R.D. at 186.

Moreover, the Third Circuit correctly rejected Comcast’s argument that the District Court applied an incorrect legal standard regarding the geographic market. As the court noted, Comcast’s proposed standard—that the relevant geographic market is “each class member’s residence”—would render the concept of a geographic market “nonsensical.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 157 n.5. The District Court properly applied the standard from *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 337 (1962), which recognizes that a geographic market may be “as small as a single metropolitan area.” The Philadelphia DMA meets that standard.

Finally, Comcast’s suggestion that the District Court improperly resolved merits issues finds no support in the record. The District Court simply assessed whether common evidence could prove impact and damages—the very inquiry Rule 23 requires. *See Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 33-35 (2013) (recognizing that a district court may “evaluate the merits of a plaintiff’s underlying claim” when that claim is “relevant to the predominance inquiry”). The District Court did not err, let alone abuse its discretion.

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
