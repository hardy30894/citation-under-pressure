**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 289 Limits Recovery to Profits Attributable to the Article of Manufacture to Which the Patented Design Is Applied.**

35 U.S.C. § 289 provides that an infringer of a design patent “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit” made “from the article of manufacture to which [the] design * * * has been applied.” The statutory text thus ties the profit award to the specific “article of manufacture” embodying the design, not to any larger product in which that article may be incorporated. When a design patent claims only a component, the “article” is that component.

The phrase “article of manufacture” has long been understood to encompass components as well as finished products. See, e.g., *Diamond v. Chakrabarty*, 447 U.S. 303, 308 (1980) (recognizing that “manufacture” denotes “the production of articles for use from raw or prepared materials”); *Roper Corp. v. Litton Sys., Inc.*, 757 F.2d 1266, 1269 (Fed. Cir. 1985) (design patents may protect “a design for an article of manufacture”). Historical precedent confirms that profits are measured only from the article actually embodying the design. See *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 222 F. 902, 904 (2d Cir. 1915) (limiting recovery to profits on the patented case, not the entire piano); *Young v. Grand Rapids Refrigerator Co.*, 268 F. 966, 974 (6th Cir. 1920) (same, for refrigerator hardware).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—that the entire smartphone is the “article” whenever any component is patented—contradicts this authority and renders the statutory phrase “to which such design * * * has been applied” surplusage. See *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (courts must give effect to every clause). It also conflicts with the principle that design-patent scope is defined by the claim, which may be limited to a portion of an article. See *Gorham Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511, 517 (1871).

**II. Apportionment Is Required When the Patented Design Is Not the Sole Driver of Consumer Demand.**

Even under § 289, an award of “total profit” cannot exceed the profit fairly attributable to the patented design. Precedent requires apportionment where the design is applied only to a component. See *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885) (profits must be apportioned to the design’s contribution); *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390, 402 (1940) (apportionment required when infringing element is separable). The jury here received no instruction limiting recovery to the component, and the record contains no evidence that the patented design drove demand for the entire phone. The judgment therefore rests on an erroneous legal premise and must be vacated.
