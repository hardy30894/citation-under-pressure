**ARGUMENT**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. The Pressls’ complaint seeks only a declaration of rights under a Virginia flowage easement. Federal-question jurisdiction therefore does not exist under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 or 16 U.S.C. § 825p.

I. The well-pleaded complaint rule confines jurisdiction to cases in which a federal question appears on the face of the complaint. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). The complaint alleges that APCO’s permit requirement exceeds the rights granted by the 1960 easement and asks the court to declare the parties’ respective property interests under that instrument. No federal statute or regulation is invoked as an element of the claim.

A state-law claim triggers federal jurisdiction only when it “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial.” Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 258 (2013). Interpretation of the easement turns on its text and Virginia property law; the FERC license is not an essential element. See Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314-15 (2005). Any effect the license may have on APCO’s authority is, at most, a federal defense that cannot support removal. Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386, 393 (1987).

II. Section 825p grants district courts jurisdiction over actions “to enforce any liability or duty created by” the Federal Power Act or FERC orders. 16 U.S.C. § 825p. The Pressls seek no such enforcement; they ask only that APCO be limited to the rights the easement itself confers. The statute therefore supplies no jurisdictional basis.

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, its judgment must be vacated and the case remanded to state court.
