The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class absent adequate admissible evidence that antitrust damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement and quantification. Class actions demand that common questions predominate, a requirement rooted in the equitable origins of representative suits. Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302 (1853) (representative decree binds only when interests are “joint” and proof is uniform); Supreme Tribe of Ben Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921) (same); Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) (class relief available only when the named parties fairly represent absent members).

In antitrust actions, damages must be established by evidence that is neither speculative nor individualized. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages not to be determined by mere speculation or guess); Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co., 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (proof must rest on facts from which the jury can make a reasonable estimate).

Where, as here, the proposed class spans thousands of subscribers across multiple counties, common proof of injury and damages is indispensable. Thomsen v. Cayser, 243 U.S. 66, 88 (1917) (antitrust plaintiffs must show injury common to all); Keogh v. Chicago & Northwestern Railway Co., 260 U.S. 156, 162 (1922) (recovery requires evidence of the extent of the injury applicable to the claimant). Chattanooga Foundry & Pipe Works v. City of Atlanta, 203 U.S. 390, 396 (1906), confirms that antitrust remedies turn on concrete proof of harm, not abstract theories. Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co., 245 U.S. 531, 534 (1918) (same); Straus v. American Publishers' Assn., 231 U.S. 222, 234 (1913) (damages must be proven with reasonable certainty).

The record contains no such common, admissible evidence. Petitioners’ expert models rest on assumptions that vary by subscriber location, contract terms, and viewing preferences—precisely the individualized inquiries that preclude predominance. Because the district court accepted these models without requiring proof that damages can be measured uniformly for every class member, its certification order rests on an erroneous legal standard and must be reversed.
