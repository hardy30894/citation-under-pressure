## ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings, and this Court’s precedents permit the use of valid but uncounseled convictions to prove elements of subsequent offenses.

### I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Govern Tribal Court Proceedings

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies only to “the federal government and the states.” *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 372 (1979); *see also United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978) (affirming tribal sovereignty). Indian tribes are “distinct, independent political communities” retaining “attributes of sovereignty over both their members and their territory.” *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1978). The Bill of Rights does not constrain tribal governments. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Congress enacted the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, to impose certain procedural requirements on tribal courts, but that Act does not guarantee a right to appointed counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. *See Santa Clara Pueblo*, 436 U.S. at 62-64.

Because tribal court convictions are not “Sixth Amendment” convictions, they cannot be “uncounseled” in the constitutional sense. The district court properly held that Bryant’s tribal convictions are constitutionally valid and may serve as predicates under § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary view improperly imposes federal constitutional standards on sovereign tribal institutions, undermining comity and tribal self-governance. *See Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997).

### II. *Nichols v. United States* Permits the Use of Uncounseled Convictions to Establish Guilt in Recidivist Prosecutions

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994), this Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction—valid under *Scott* because no imprisonment was imposed—could be used to enhance a sentence. The Court rejected the argument that such convictions are “so unreliable that they should be disregarded entirely.” *Id.* at 747. *Nichols* overruled *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980), which had suggested that uncounseled convictions might not be usable for enhancement. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748-49.

The Ninth Circuit attempted to distinguish *Nichols* as a “sentencing case,” but this Court has made clear that “recidivist statutes do not punish the defendant for his prior convictions, but for the last offense committed.” *Id.* at 747. Section 117(a) uses prior convictions as an element of the offense, but the same reasoning applies: the defendant is punished for the current domestic assault, not for the earlier offenses. *See United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (“None [of the punishment] is for the prior convictions or the defendant’s ‘status as a recidivist.’”) (citation omitted). The use of a prior conviction as an element does not alter the constitutional calculus. Every circuit to address this issue—except the Ninth—has held that *Nichols* allows the use of uncounseled convictions as predicates for § 117(a). *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011).

### III. Reliability Concerns Do Not Bar Consideration of Valid Tribal Convictions

The Ninth Circuit relied on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), to impose a per se rule barring uncounseled tribal convictions from proving elements of federal crimes. *Ant* is inconsistent with *Nichols*. In *Nichols*, this Court acknowledged reliability concerns but held that the Sixth Amendment does not require exclusion of uncounseled convictions from sentencing proceedings. 511 U.S. at 747-48. The Court noted that the Sentencing Guidelines allowed a defendant to challenge the reliability of a prior conviction, *id.*, but it did not hold that reliability concerns categorically bar use. *Ant* went further than *Nichols* by requiring that tribal court convictions must “guarantee a right to counsel that is, at minimum, coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right.” *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014). This imposes a condition that Congress itself chose not to require in the ICRA.

Moreover, a tribal court conviction that complied with ICRA procedures is presumptively reliable. *See California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202, 215-16 (1987) (recognizing tribal courts’ ability to adjudicate matters fairly). The Ninth Circuit’s paternalistic approach disrespects tribal sovereignty and ignores that “tribal court systems . . . have undergone major improvements” and “are entitled to respect.” *Wilson*, 127 F.3d at 811.

### IV. A Tribal Conviction Remains Valid Even If a Prison Sentence Was Unconstitutionally Imposed

Even assuming *arguendo* that Bryant’s prison sentence might have violated the Sixth Amendment if imposed in state or federal court, that does not invalidate the underlying conviction. In *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 661-62 (2002), this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could not support a suspended prison sentence, but it did not vacate the conviction itself. *See also United States v. Acuna-Reyna*, 677 F.3d 1282, 1284-85 (11th Cir. 2012) (same). The conviction remains “valid” for purposes of recidivist statutes. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748. Thus, even if the tribal court’s imposition of a prison sentence were unconstitutional in a federal context, Bryant’s conviction for domestic assault remains intact and may serve as a predicate.

### V. Congress’s Intent in Enacting § 117(a) Supports Using Tribal Court Convictions

Congress enacted § 117(a) to address the epidemic of domestic violence against Native American women. *See Violence Against Women and Department of Justice Reauthorization Act of 2005*, Pub. L. No. 109-162, § 909, 119 Stat. 2960, 3084. The statute explicitly references “Indian tribal court” convictions as predicate offenses. Congress understood that tribal courts often do not provide counsel, yet chose to include those convictions. The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines congressional intent and hamstrings enforcement of a statute designed to protect vulnerable victims. *See United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (Congress has “plenary power” to legislate in Indian affairs).

### Conclusion

Because tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment, and because *Nichols* permits use of uncounseled convictions for enhancement and element purposes, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. The tribal court convictions are valid predicates under § 117(a), and Bryant’s conviction should be affirmed.
