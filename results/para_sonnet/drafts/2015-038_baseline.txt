# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that only "public lands" within the boundaries of a conservation system unit (CSU) shall be deemed part of that unit, and that non-public lands "shall not be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). Petitioner's theory—that this provision categorically exempts state, Native, and private inholdings from *any* NPS regulation whatsoever—cannot be squared with the statute's actual text.

The hovercraft ban at issue, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not a regulation "applicable solely to public lands within" CSUs. It is a regulation of general applicability that governs conduct throughout NPS-administered areas, public and non-public alike, precisely because Congress recognized that the Park Service must retain authority to manage activities occurring on inholdings that are geographically enclosed within park boundaries. Section 103(c)'s carve-out is triggered only by regulations that apply *solely* to public lands; a regulation that applies to all lands within the unit, without regard to ownership, falls outside the exemption altogether. This Court has long instructed that statutory text is the beginning and end of interpretation where, as here, the language is unambiguous. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984) ("If the intent of Congress is clear, that is the end of the matter."). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon's "interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute."

Petitioner's reading would also produce an absurd patchwork of jurisdiction within park boundaries—federal authority evaporating and reappearing acre by acre depending on subsurface title, river-bed ownership, or corporate conveyance status. Congress does not legislate by such indirection. *See Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc.*, 531 U.S. 457, 468 (2001) ("Congress ... does not alter the fundamental details of a regulatory scheme in vague terms or ancillary provisions.").

## II. ANILCA § 1319 CONFIRMS THAT PREEXISTING FEDERAL REGULATORY AUTHORITY SURVIVES

Section 1319 of ANILCA independently forecloses petitioner's argument. That provision states that "[n]othing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions." 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (emphasis added). NPS's general authority to regulate activities within units of the National Park System long predates ANILCA and derives from the Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1 *et seq.*, and other statutes of general applicability. Because § 103(c) does not "specifically set forth" a repeal of that preexisting authority as applied to activities like hovercraft use, § 1319 preserves it intact.

## III. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT'S NAVIGATIONAL SERVITUDE AND OTHER SOURCES OF AUTHORITY SUPPORT NPS'S REGULATION REGARDLESS OF SUBMERGED-LAND TITLE

Even assuming, *arguendo*, that title to the Nation River's bed passed to Alaska under the Submerged Lands Act or the Equal Footing Doctrine, that fact would not divest the federal government of regulatory authority over activities occurring on the surface of navigable waters within a national preserve. As Justice Scalia observed, "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters ... by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75, 112 (2005) (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). Ownership of the riverbed and regulatory authority over activities on the water are analytically distinct; petitioner conflates the two in an effort to manufacture a jurisdictional vacuum that Congress never intended.

## IV. RESPONDENT'S POSITION IS CONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT'S DEFERENCE PRINCIPLES

To the extent any ambiguity remains in the statute's application to hovercraft regulation, NPS's longstanding interpretation—that its general regulations apply throughout CSUs regardless of subsurface ownership—is entitled to deference under *Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 844. Petitioner's suggestion that NPS's post-1996 enforcement posture undermines that deference is unavailing, because "[a]gency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the *Chevron* framework." *National Cable & Telecommunications Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Services*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005). Regardless of any prior enforcement lapses, NPS's current interpretation is the one that actually comports with the statutory text, and that is what matters under *Chevron*.

## V. THIS AS-APPLIED CHALLENGE PROVIDES NO BASIS FOR THE SWEEPING RELIEF PETITIONER SEEKS

Finally, this case comes to the Court as an as-applied challenge to a single regulation—the hovercraft ban—as applied to a single stretch of river. The lower courts properly limited their analysis accordingly, and petitioner did not contest that characterization on appeal. Any ruling by this Court should be similarly confined; nothing in the record supports the broader proposition that NPS lacks all regulatory authority over non-public inholdings within Alaska's CSUs. Sweeping relief of the kind petitioner requests would be particularly inappropriate given the practical and administrative consequences such a holding would have for the integrated management of park resources that Congress contemplated when it enacted ANILCA as a comprehensive scheme for balancing conservation and access in Alaska's unique landscape.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals affirming summary judgment for respondent should be affirmed.
