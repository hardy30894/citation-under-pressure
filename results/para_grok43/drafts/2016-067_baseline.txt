ARGUMENT

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s],” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a term the statute defines to reach “any person who . . . regularly collects or attempts to collect . . . debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” Id. (emphasis added). Santander purchased the loans outright and thereafter sought to collect them for its own account. That conduct places Santander outside the statutory definition.

The text is dispositive. The phrase “owed or due another” refers to debts owed to a third party at the time of collection. Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (“The Act’s definition of the term ‘debt collector’ includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect . . . debts owed [to] . . . another’” (alteration in original)). Because Santander held the debts as owner, no “another” existed. See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1316 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[A] person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition of the term.”); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’”).

The default status of the loans at purchase is irrelevant. The Act distinguishes debt collectors from creditors by whether the collector acts for another or for itself, not by when the debt fell into arrears. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (defining “creditor” as “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt”); S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698 (“The Committee intends the term ‘debt collector’ . . . to cover all third persons who regularly collect debts for others.”). Petitioners’ contrary reading would convert every purchaser of defaulted paper into a debt collector, a result the statutory structure rejects. See also Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012) (recognizing the mutual exclusivity of the terms but still requiring collection “for another”).

The complaint alleges only that Santander acquired the loans and then collected them on its own behalf. That conduct satisfies none of the three statutory routes to “debt collector” status. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The district court and the court of appeals therefore correctly held that the FDCPA does not apply.
