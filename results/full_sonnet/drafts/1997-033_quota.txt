# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 1407(a) REQUIRES REMAND AND FORECLOSES SELF-ASSIGNMENT UNDER SECTION 1404(a)

Section 1407(a) commands that any action transferred by the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This Court has repeatedly instructed that "shall" is mandatory language admitting no judicial discretion. See *Lopez v. Davis*, 531 U.S. 230, 241 (2001) (Congress' use of "shall" "creates an obligation impervious to judicial discretion"). The Panel—not the transferee district court—possesses the remand power, and it must exercise that power once pretrial proceedings conclude.

Milberg's countermotion asked the District Court to invoke § 1404(a) to "transfer" the case to itself. But a court cannot transfer a case to itself; transfer, by definition, requires movement between two different districts. See *Black's Law Dictionary* 1503 (6th ed. 1990) (defining "transfer" as conveyance from one person or place to another). Section 1404(a) authorizes a district court to transfer a civil action "to any other district." 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) (emphasis added). The word "other" is not surplusage; this Court has long held that statutes must be construed to give effect to every word. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) ("It is our duty 'to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.'") (quoting *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883)). A transferee court sitting in Arizona cannot "transfer" a case to the District of Arizona under § 1404(a); it can only retain what § 1407(a) already requires it to relinquish.

The Ninth Circuit majority below did not dispute this textual analysis. It candidly acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to grant mandamus relief based on contrary "case law." Pet. App. 12. That is precisely backward. When statutory text is unambiguous, judicial invention of contrary practice cannot substitute for the words Congress enacted. See *Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992) ("[C]ourts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.").

## II. THE COURTS OF APPEALS' CONTRARY PRACTICE RESTS ON POLICY PREFERENCES, NOT STATUTORY AUTHORITY

The line of authority permitting self-transfer traces to *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), which rejected the statutory command as "absurd" on policy grounds rather than grounding its holding in the text of § 1407(a). *Id.* at 124–25. Subsequent decisions, including *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), simply followed *Pfizer* without independent textual analysis. Even the district court candidly confronting this question in *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971), admitted "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district."

Judge Kozinski's dissent below correctly identified this as "an egregious, oft-repeated error." Pet. App. 15 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). As he explained, "[i]t's hard to imagine a clearer statutory command, or one more widely ignored." *Id.* The fact that the Multidistrict Panel later "enshrine[d] this unlawful practice into one of its rules," J.P.M.L.R.P. 14(b), cannot cure the defect. This Court has made clear that agency or judicial rules cannot override clear statutory text. *See Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) ("It is axiomatic that an administrative agency's power to promulgate legislative regulations is limited to the authority delegated by Congress.").

## III. SECTION 1407(a) REFLECTS A DELIBERATE CONGRESSIONAL CHOICE TO PRESERVE PLAINTIFFS' VENUE RIGHTS FOR TRIAL

Congress designed § 1407(a) to permit consolidation only for the limited, coordinated purpose of pretrial proceedings—principally discovery—while preserving the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial and other dispositive matters. This structure reflects the traditional principle that "plaintiffs retain their ancient right to select the forum," Pet. App. 19 (Kozinski, J., dissenting), a right this Court has long recognized as an important feature of federal civil litigation. *See Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert*, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947) (discussing plaintiff's privilege to select forum as a substantial consideration in venue analysis).

Had Congress intended to grant transferee courts plenary authority to retain cases indefinitely for trial, it would have said so, just as it did in § 1404(a) with respect to ordinary venue transfers. Instead, Congress imposed a mandatory remand requirement precisely to prevent transferee courts from converting temporary custody for pretrial coordination into permanent jurisdiction over the merits. This Court's admonition against reading statutes to render express limitations meaningless applies with full force. *See TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) ("[W]e are hesitant to adopt an interpretation of a congressional enactment which renders superfluous another portion of that same law.").

## IV. PETITIONER SUFFERED THE PRECISE HARM SECTION 1407(a) WAS DESIGNED TO PREVENT

Petitioner Lexecon was transferred to Arizona for coordinated pretrial proceedings in multidistrict litigation to which it was not even initially a party in that capacity, then—after those proceedings concluded—was forced to try its separate malpractice claims against Milberg in a forum with no independent connection to the controversy. This is the exact "unseemly" practice of transferee courts "glomm[ing] onto" cases entrusted to them "for limited purposes" that Judge Kozinski identified. Pet. App. 18 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). The Ninth Circuit's suggestion that no prejudice existed because petitioner had already litigated in Arizona for over a year, Pet. App. 10, inverts the inquiry: the extended presence in an improper forum is the harm that mandatory remand was designed to avoid, not a justification for perpetuating it.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that § 1407(a)'s mandatory remand provision precludes a transferee court from invoking § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial, and should reverse the judgment below.
