Milford did not violate the Good News Club’s free speech rights, because the school did not exclude the Club from a forum opened for speech on the same subject matter. Milford’s community-use policy opened its facilities only for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” while expressly forbidding “religious purposes.” When the Club sought to meet for singing, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and prayer, Milford reasonably concluded that the proposed use was religious worship and instruction, not speech within the policy’s limited forum. A public owner “may confine the forum to the limited and legitimate purposes for which it was created.” Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515–16 (1939). The Constitution does not require a school to surrender control of its property to private groups for uses outside the scope of the forum it has created. See Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47–48 (1966); Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 405–06 (1953).

The First Amendment protects speech, not a right to use government property contrary to neutral forum limits. The government may regulate access to its property to preserve the property for the purpose to which it is dedicated. See Greer v. Spock, 424 U.S. 828, 836–40 (1976) (recognizing that the government “is not required to indefinitely retain the open character of the facility”); cf. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 575–76 (1941) (upholding reasonable regulation of expressive activity in light of public order). Milford did not single out the Club because of disagreement with its viewpoint on morals; it excluded the Club because the Club’s proposed activities were religious in nature and therefore fell outside the forum. A forum owner may draw subject-matter lines. See Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 571–72 (1942) (recognizing that the Constitution does not compel protection of all utterances in all settings).

In any event, Milford’s decision was justified by the Establishment Clause. The Constitution forbids a public school from appearing to sponsor or endorse religious exercise by young children on school premises. “The First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state.” Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947). That wall is especially high in the elementary-school setting, where children are impressionable and school authority carries particular weight. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212–13 (1948) (invalidating religious instruction on public-school property); Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 424–25 (1962) (rejecting state-composed prayer in public schools); School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 223–24 (1963) (prohibiting school-sponsored Bible reading); Epperson v. Arkansas, 393 U.S. 97, 106–07 (1968) (forbidding school action that aligns government with religious doctrine).

Milford reasonably could conclude that permitting the Good News Club’s meetings would blur the line between private religion and public-school sponsorship. The Club’s program included prayer, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Jesus as Savior; in the elementary-school context, that activity risked precisely the kind of state entanglement and coercive appearance the Establishment Clause forbids. See McCollum, 333 U.S. at 209–12; Engel, 370 U.S. at 430–31; Schempp, 374 U.S. at 222–23. The school was not required to choose between violating the Free Speech Clause and risking an Establishment Clause violation. When a forum limitation and a constitutional anti-establishment concern point in the same direction, the school may act to preserve neutrality. See Everson, 330 U.S. at 17–18; Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313–14 (1952) (emphasizing that government may accommodate religion only without becoming its sponsor).

Accordingly, Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was a permissible application of its limited-use policy and, independently, a justified effort to avoid a perceived Establishment Clause problem. The judgment should be affirmed.
