**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously held that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lacks jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected oil and gas exploration barge. The plain text of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, coupled with the limited regulatory reach of the Coast Guard over uninspected vessels, compels the conclusion that OSHA’s citation was lawful.

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

The Act applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). A drilling barge engaged in extracting oil from the seabed is not a “vessel in navigation” within the traditional maritime meaning. *The Steamboat Co. v. Joliffe*, 187 U.S. 123, 134 (1902) (inspection laws apply only to vessels “navigating” and “carrying passengers or freight”). *Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 484, 485 (1957) (a dredge moored during operations is not a “vessel in navigation” for Jones Act purposes). *The Blackheath*, 195 U.S. 361, 367 (1904) (admiralty jurisdiction over navigable waters does not extend to stationary structures attached to the land). Like those structures, Rig 52 was stationary while drilling—its sole purpose—and thus was not “used for transportation on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870). The fact that it could be moved does not transform it into a navigational vessel. *Evans v. G. &. A. Steamboat Co.*, 231 U.S. 297, 301 (1913) (a barge used primarily as a storage facility not a vessel). Consequently, Rig 52 is a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Mere jurisdiction is insufficient; the agency must *actually* regulate the same condition. This Court has long held that preemption requires an actual conflict, not speculative overlap. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (preemption necessary only when “the matter is so fully covered by federal law as to leave no room for state action”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 grants it power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. It has no authority to prescribe comprehensive safety standards for drilling operations. *See The Steamboat Co. v. Joliffe*, 187 U.S. 123, 134 (1902) (inspection laws apply only to vessels “navigating” and “carrying passengers or freight”). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate the working conditions that gave rise to this explosion—well control, blowout prevention, and general workplace safety—means it has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over those conditions. *Hines*, 312 U.S. at 67 (exemptions in remedial statutes are construed narrowly); *see also Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (preemption requires “clear and manifest purpose of Congress”).

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on Post-1970 Precedents Is Misplaced**

The court below rested on its own decisions in *Clary* and *Donovan*, both decided after the OSH Act’s 1970 enactment and thus inapplicable to this pre-1970 analysis. But even under pre-1970 law, workers on uninspected drilling barges are not “seamen” entitled to an industry-wide exemption. *The Blackheath*, 195 U.S. at 367 (“The structure was not a vessel … and the jurisdiction of admiralty does not extend to it.”). Rig 52’s employees performed industrial oil well work, not traditional maritime duties. Their working conditions are analogous to those on a fixed platform—areas indisputably covered by OSHA. *The Steamboat Co. v. Joliffe*, 187 U.S. at 134 (a structure not used for navigation is not a vessel subject to maritime law). *See also Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 217 (1917) (maritime law governs only “maritime contracts and maritime torts”; stationary structures are not maritime). Overlapping regulation is not the anomaly the lower court feared; it is the norm when maritime safety touches landside operations. *The Blackheath*, 195 U.S. at 367 (admiralty does not “extend[] to structures so attached to the land as to be part of the shore”).

In sum, Rig 52 is a workplace, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over its working conditions, and OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment below should be reversed.
