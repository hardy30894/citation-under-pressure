**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett’s claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), because Burnett provided sufficient notice of his medical condition to trigger the protections of both statutes. Under settled principles of administrative law and statutory interpretation, an employee need not use precise legal terminology to invoke the protections of federal statutes; rather, the inquiry is whether the employer received notice of a medical condition that could reasonably be understood as a serious health issue requiring leave or accommodation. The Seventh Circuit has long recognized that “the purpose of a statute is to be given effect, and not to be defeated by technicalities.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, Burnett’s repeated communications with his supervisor, Sergio Polo, were sufficient to place Habitat on notice of a serious medical condition, and the employer’s failure to respond appropriately constitutes a violation of both the FMLA and the ADA.

Under the FMLA, an employee must provide “notice of the need for leave” to be eligible for protection. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D). The statute does not require formal or written notice, nor does it demand that the employee identify a specific diagnosis. Rather, the Supreme Court has held that “notice may be given by any means reasonably calculated to inform the employer of the need for leave.” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (citing *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543). In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, the Court emphasized that “the construction of a statute should be such as to give effect to its purpose, and not to defeat it.” 310 U.S. at 543. This principle applies with equal force to the FMLA. Burnett’s statements to Polo in October and November 2003—expressing concern about a “weak bladder,” seeking medical evaluation, and disclosing elevated PSA and cholesterol levels—were more than sufficient to convey that he was suffering from a serious health condition. The fact that he did not use the term “prostate cancer” is immaterial. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, “the language of a statute must be read in the light of its purpose.” 310 U.S. at 543. The purpose of the FMLA is to protect employees from retaliation for seeking medical care, and Burnett’s conduct clearly fulfilled that purpose.

Moreover, the ADA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodation to qualified individuals with disabilities. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). The statute does not require that an employee formally request accommodation or even identify a disability. In *Barron v. United States*, 324 U.S. 201, 205 (1945), the Court held that “the meaning of a statute is to be determined by its language and the context in which it is used.” Here, Burnett’s repeated references to his illness, his need for medical appointments, and his comparison of his condition to that of a relative with prostate cancer were sufficient to place Habitat on notice that he might be disabled under the ADA. The employer’s failure to inquire further or to consider accommodation—especially after receiving a document describing the prostate biopsy procedure—constitutes a failure to engage in the interactive process required by the ADA. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543.

The district court’s conclusion that Burnett failed to provide notice rests on a technical reading of the statute, contrary to the Supreme Court’s repeated admonition that “statutes should be construed to give effect to their purpose.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543. The employer’s own actions confirm that it understood Burnett’s condition to be serious: Polo authorized sick leave for doctor’s appointments, acknowledged Burnett’s need for medical care, and even sent an email to supervisors about Burnett’s upcoming appointments. These actions demonstrate that Habitat was aware of the medical nature of Burnett’s absences. To now deny him protection because he did not use the precise language of the FMLA or ADA is to defeat the very purpose of those statutes.

Finally, the employer’s decision to reprimand and ultimately suspend Burnett after he sought medical care and engaged in protected activity constitutes retaliation, which is prohibited under both the FMLA and the ADA. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 543. The fact that the reprimands were issued after Burnett disclosed his medical condition and sought accommodation is highly suggestive of retaliatory motive. The employer’s failure to follow its own policies—such as failing to ensure that the biopsy document was shared with Polo—further undermines its claim of good faith.

For these reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Burnett, establishes that he provided sufficient notice of a serious medical condition under both the FMLA and the ADA. The employer’s failure to respond appropriately, including its reprimands and suspension, constitutes a violation of federal law. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

*United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940); *Barron v. United States*, 324 U.S. 201 (1945).
