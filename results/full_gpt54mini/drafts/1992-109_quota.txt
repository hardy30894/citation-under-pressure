The Due Process Clause does not require a special “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has long held that the constitutional floor for competency is whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). In Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), the Court reaffirmed that formulation for trial competency and rejected attempts to recast competency as a more searching inquiry into whether the defendant’s decision-making is optimal. See also Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993).

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” rule cannot be squared with this Court’s competency cases. In Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993), the Court rejected the proposition that competence to plead guilty or waive counsel requires a higher level of mental functioning than competence to stand trial. The Court explained that “the competence that is required of a defendant seeking to waive his right to counsel is the competence to waive the right, not the competence to represent himself.” Id. at 399. A court need not ask whether the defendant’s decision is wise, prudent, or well considered; the question is whether he is competent under the Dusky standard and whether the waiver itself is knowing and voluntary. Id. at 400-01.

That distinction is critical. Competency asks about the defendant’s mental capacity. The validity of the waiver asks whether the choice was made knowingly and voluntarily. As this Court has recognized, those are separate inquiries. See Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938); Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975). Faretta held that a defendant may proceed without counsel if he “knowingly and intelligently” forgoes that right, but it did not constitutionalize a higher competency threshold. Faretta, 422 U.S. at 835. Likewise, Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), requires that a guilty plea be an intelligent and voluntary waiver of constitutional rights, but Boykin does not alter the competency standard itself. Id. at 242-44.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach conflates capacity with choice quality. Under its rule, a defendant who is mentally competent under Dusky could still be deemed incompetent because his decision appears irrational to a reviewing court. That is not the Constitution’s command. In Indiana v. Edwards, 554 U.S. 164 (2008), this Court later recognized a limited state authority to deny self-representation to a defendant who is competent to stand trial but lacks the mental capacity to conduct trial proceedings alone. But Edwards confirms the baseline: competency to waive rights remains distinct from whether self-representation will be effective or sensible. Id. at 174-78. Edwards did not adopt a “reasoned choice” standard for pleas or waivers; it addressed a narrow self-representation problem not presented here. See also McKaskle v. Wiggins, 465 U.S. 168 (1984).

Nor does constitutional law require the trial judge to probe whether a defendant has made the best possible decision. A guilty plea is valid if it is entered voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently, see Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), and if the defendant is competent under Dusky. A court’s duty is to ensure those requirements are met, not to impose an additional substantive judgment about the defendant’s reasoning process. See Marshall v. Lonberger, 459 U.S. 422 (1983); North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25 (1970).

Here, the state court found Moran competent to stand trial under psychiatric evaluations and accepted his waiver after colloquy confirming his understanding of the rights relinquished. That is the proper constitutional inquiry. The Ninth Circuit erred by demanding more. Under Godinez, once the defendant is competent under Dusky, the remaining question is whether the plea and waiver were knowing and voluntary. 509 U.S. at 400-01. Because due process does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
