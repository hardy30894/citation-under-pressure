ARGUMENT

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that “only those regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units shall be applicable to” non-federal lands. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), applies to all lands and waters administered by the National Park Service and therefore is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands.” See United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (“[T]he plain meaning of legislation should be conclusive.”).

This reading is confirmed by the statute’s structure. ANILCA defines “public lands” to exclude lands “conveyed to the State” or to Native corporations. 16 U.S.C. § 3102(3). Congress therefore knew how to distinguish federal from non-federal ownership, yet chose to limit the exemption to regulations that apply solely to public lands. See Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., 534 U.S. 438, 452 (2002) (“Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”).

The Service’s organic authorities independently authorize the regulation. See 54 U.S.C. §§ 100101(a), 100751. Those statutes contain no geographic limitation to federally owned land. See Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 539 (1976) (“The power over the public land thus entrusted to Congress is without limitations.”).

Petitioner’s contrary construction would require the Service to enforce different rules on adjacent parcels within the same unit, producing the checkerboard regulation Congress sought to avoid. See FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000) (“[T]he words of a statute must be read in their context and with a view to their place in the overall statutory scheme.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the hovercraft rule survives § 103(c) follows directly from the text. See National Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs., 545 U.S. 967, 986 (2005) (“Only a judicial precedent holding that the statute unambiguously forecloses the agency’s interpretation” displaces it.). No such precedent exists.

The same textual analysis defeats the claim that ANILCA silently repealed preexisting Service authority. See Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 551 (1974) (“[R]epeals by implication are not favored.”). ANILCA § 1319 expressly preserves “existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207.

Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed.
