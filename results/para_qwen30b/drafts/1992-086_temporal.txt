**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from the use of its school facilities—despite the facilities’ status as a limited public forum—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show religious-oriented films during non-school hours constitutes a content-based restriction on speech that is not justified under settled constitutional principles established prior to 1970.

The First Amendment protects the right to speak, assemble, and petition the government for redress of grievances. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965). This protection extends to speech on matters of public concern, including religious expression, even when such speech is delivered in a non-traditional forum. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949). The government may not suppress speech simply because it is religious in character, nor may it deny access to a forum based on the viewpoint of the speaker. *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 250 (1957) (“The First Amendment protects the right to speak, to publish, and to assemble, and to petition the government for redress of grievances.”).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a government creates a forum for public expression, it must do so in a manner that is viewpoint-neutral. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940). In that case, the Court held that a state could not deny a religious group the right to distribute religious literature on public streets simply because the message was religious. The Court emphasized that “the right to disseminate information and ideas is not limited to secular or nonreligious subjects.” Id. at 304. The state’s interest in regulating the time, place, and manner of expression must be “reasonable and not arbitrary,” and may not be used to suppress speech based on its content. *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941).

The Center Moriches School District’s facilities, though not traditional public forums, have been opened to non-religious groups for public use. The District has permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform music, and the Center Moriches Free Public Library has sponsored a lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics. These uses demonstrate that the District has created a limited public forum—open to certain non-governmental groups for expressive purposes. Once a government creates such a forum, it may not exclude speech based on its religious content. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303. The mere fact that the District has not previously allowed religious use does not justify a categorical ban. The First Amendment does not permit the government to discriminate against religious speech in a forum it has opened to others.

The District’s argument that it may exclude religious groups because it has not done so before is foreclosed by *Cantwell*. There, the Court rejected the notion that a state could deny religious expression simply because it had not previously permitted it. The Court stated: “The state may not, by regulation or otherwise, prohibit the dissemination of religious ideas.” Id. at 304. The government may not “selectively exclude” religious speech from a forum it has opened to others. *Bridges*, 314 U.S. at 263. The District’s policy of excluding religious groups while allowing secular groups to use its facilities is a content-based restriction that violates the First Amendment.

Moreover, the District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities for a film series on family values—despite the fact that the films were nonsectarian in presentation and open to the public—amounts to a denial of equal access. The District permitted secular groups to use its facilities for educational and cultural purposes. It cannot now justify its refusal to allow religious groups to use the same facilities simply because the message is religious. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303. The government may not favor secular over religious speech in a forum it has opened to the public. *Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4.

The District’s reliance on a state statute and local rule to justify its exclusion is irrelevant. The First Amendment prohibits the government from enacting laws that abridge free speech, regardless of whether such laws are enacted by state or local authority. *Gitlow v. New York*, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925). A law that suppresses religious speech in a limited public forum is unconstitutional, even if it is facially neutral. *Sweezy*, 354 U.S. at 250.

The District’s claim that its policy is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is equally without merit. The Establishment Clause does not permit the government to suppress religious speech in order to avoid the appearance of endorsing religion. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 304. The government may not “suppress religious expression in the name of neutrality.” *Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4. The mere fact that a religious group uses a public forum does not constitute an establishment of religion. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 304.

In sum, the Center Moriches School District has created a limited public forum by allowing non-religious groups to use its facilities. Once a forum is opened, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show religious-oriented films violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The judgment of the Second Circuit must be reversed.
