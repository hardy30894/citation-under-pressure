**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING REMAND BECAUSE THIS CASE DOES NOT ARISE UNDER FEDERAL LAW.**

The Pressls’ complaint sought only state-law declaratory relief regarding the scope of their rights under a flowage easement. A case “arises under” federal law only if the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint necessarily raises a federal issue. *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987). Here, the Pressls’ claims are grounded in state property law and do not implicate federal issues. The mere fact that APCO removed the case based on a federal license does not convert the action into a federal case. *Id.* at 399 (“[A] case may not be removed to federal court on the basis of a federal defense . . . even if the defense is anticipated in the plaintiff’s complaint.”).

APCO’s reliance on *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), is misplaced. In *Gulf Offshore*, the Supreme Court held that a federal statute governing outer continental shelf activities provided federal jurisdiction where the plaintiff’s claim implicated federal interests. *Id.* at 477–78. Unlike *Gulf Offshore*, the Pressls’ claims do not depend on interpretation of the Federal Power Act or FERC’s licensing authority. The flowage easement’s validity and scope are governed by Virginia law, and the Pressls seek no relief under federal law. *See Merrell Dow Pharms. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 817 (1986) (holding that a federal issue must be “necessary” to the plaintiff’s claim, not merely “incidental”).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT LACKED JURISDICTION UNDER 16 U.S.C. § 825p.**

Section 825p grants federal district courts jurisdiction over “all controversies involving contracts between a licensee and a public utility.” 16 U.S.C. § 825p. However, this provision applies only where the controversy “arises from the licensee’s obligations under the Federal Power Act.” *See Town of Norwood v. FERC*, 906 F.2d 1216, 1220 (4th Cir. 1990). The Pressls’ dispute concerns the interpretation of a private flowage easement, not APCO’s obligations under its FERC license. The easement predates APCO’s federal license and was negotiated between private parties. Thus, § 825p does not confer jurisdiction. *See Town of Norwood*, 906 F.2d at 1220 (limiting § 825p to disputes “directly related to the licensee’s federal obligations”).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING THE COMPLAINT ON THE MERITS.**

Even if federal jurisdiction existed (which it does not), the district court erred in dismissing the Pressls’ complaint. The flowage easement does not grant APCO unbridled discretion to prohibit all structures below the 800-foot contour. Under Virginia law, easements are construed in light of their purpose. *See, e.g., Meade v. Hampton, 218 Va. 37, 40 (1977)*. The easement’s purpose is to allow APCO to flood the land as needed for hydroelectric operations—not to prohibit all recreational use. The Pressls’ proposed dock would not interfere with APCO’s rights, as it would not obstruct water flow or require permanent structures. *See Restatement (Third) of Property (Servitudes) § 4.10 cmt. d* (2000) (easements should be interpreted to permit reasonable use consistent with their purpose).

The district court also erred in requiring the Pressls to exhaust administrative remedies before the FERC. Exhaustion is required only where Congress has clearly mandated it. *See, e.g., Darby v. Cisneros*, 509 U.S. 137, 144 (1993). The Federal Power Act does not impose such a requirement for disputes over private easements. APCO’s Occupancy and Use Permit is an extra-contractual demand, not a condition of its FERC license. Thus, the Pressls were not required to seek FERC review before filing suit.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this state-law dispute. The judgment below must be vacated, and the case remanded to the state court for further proceedings.
