**ARGUMENT**

The answer to the Question Presented is **no**. A fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may not be enhanced **solely** because counsel’s performance was excellent and the results were impressive. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the lodestar is the “reasonable fee,” and that quality and results are ordinarily reflected in the lodestar itself, not added on top of it. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 430 n.3, 434-37 (1983); *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897-901 (1984); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 564-68 (1986).

The governing premise is that a fee must be “reasonable,” not congratulatory. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433-34. The lodestar method—reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable rate—produces a presumptively sufficient award because it already captures the factors that ordinarily matter most, including the attorney’s skill and the results obtained. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897-900. The Court in *Blum* rejected the argument that “quality of representation” and “results obtained” justify an enhancement absent specific proof that the lodestar failed to account for them. Id. at 898-901. The Court explained that those considerations are typically “subsumed” within the lodestar calculation. Id. at 898-900.

That principle is not new. Long before the modern fee-shifting cases, the Court emphasized that fee awards should compensate for necessary legal work, not serve as a bonus for success. See *The Baltimore*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 377, 388-89 (1869); *The Connemara*, 103 U.S. 754, 757-58 (1881). In *The Baltimore*, the Court held that compensation must be tied to the services rendered, not to an after-the-fact appraisal of the outcome. 75 U.S. at 388-89. Likewise, *The Connemara* treated fee awards as reimbursement for actual legal effort, not as a premium for prevailing. 103 U.S. at 757-58. That same understanding informs the federal fee-shifting statutes: “reasonable” means adequately compensatory, not enriched by a success multiplier. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433-37.

Respondents’ position is reinforced by the Court’s longstanding treatment of litigation results as an element already reflected in ordinary fee-setting, not an independent basis for enhancement. In *Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society*, the Court reiterated that attorney’s fees are a matter of statutory authorization and that courts may not freely expand fee awards beyond what Congress has provided. 421 U.S. 240, 262-71 (1975). *Alyeska* did not create the modern lodestar, but it confirmed the core separation-of-powers principle that fee awards must remain faithful to the statute. Id. at 262-71. That principle is inconsistent with a discretionary bonus untethered to any deficiency in the lodestar. See also *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 719-20 (1967) (declining to infer fee shifting absent statutory authorization).

The Court’s precedents also make clear that enhancement cannot rest on a court’s subjective admiration for counsel’s work. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901. To permit a multiplier based solely on “quality” and “results” would invite precisely the kind of open-ended valuation *Blum* rejected. Id. at 899-901. A lodestar enhancement is permissible only if the fee applicant proves that the lodestar does not already reflect the relevant consideration. Id. at 898-901. That is a demanding showing, and it cannot be satisfied merely by pointing to excellent lawyering or a favorable outcome. Id. at 900-01.

Nor may respondents recast success as “exceptional” to evade the lodestar rule. The Court has repeatedly warned that excellence and victory are not enough by themselves. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 434-37; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901. Otherwise, every strong civil-rights case would become a candidate for a bonus, defeating the lodestar’s function as the ordinary measure of reasonableness. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897-900. Fee-shifting statutes were enacted to ensure access to counsel, not to create a market for judicial premiums. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 429-37; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897-901.

In short, the Court should hold that a fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute cannot be enhanced **solely** on the basis of superior performance and favorable results. The lodestar already accounts for those features in the usual case, and a multiplier requires something more—specific proof that the lodestar was not reasonable without it. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901; *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433-37. On that settled rule, the judgment below should be reversed.
