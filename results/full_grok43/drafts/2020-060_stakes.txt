**ARGUMENT**

The 2004 consent decree did not resolve Guam’s liability to the United States “for some or all of a response action” within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Guam therefore retains its right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a), 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a), which is subject to a six-year limitations period that has not expired.

Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” The statute’s text requires an actual resolution of CERCLA liability. See Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) (describing the provision as creating an “express” contribution right “during or following” specified events). The 2004 decree, entered in a Clean Water Act enforcement action, contains no such resolution. It states expressly that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. Courts have recognized that a disclaimer of liability weighs heavily against finding resolution under § 113(f)(3)(B). See, e.g., Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015).

The decree’s substantive obligations—payment of a civil penalty and construction of a cover system—arose solely under the Clean Water Act, 33 U.S.C. §§ 1251 et seq. The complaint alleged only unpermitted discharges of pollutants; it did not invoke CERCLA or seek recovery of response costs for hazardous substances. Although CERCLA defines “remedial action” to include leachate collection, 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24), that definition does not convert a Clean Water Act settlement into a CERCLA resolution of liability. See United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (emphasizing CERCLA’s distinct liability scheme).

Because the decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s § 107(a) claim remains available. The statute of limitations for that claim is six years from completion of the remedial action, 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B), and has not run. Allowing the decree to extinguish the longer limitations period would produce the very anomaly Congress sought to avoid when it preserved distinct cost-recovery and contribution remedies. See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
