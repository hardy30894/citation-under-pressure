**ARGUMENT**

**THE PLAINTIFFS’ AWARD OF TREBLE DAMAGES, ATTORNEY’S FEES, AND COSTS IS NOT EXCEPTED FROM DISCHARGE UNDER 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A).**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The plain language of this provision limits the exception to debts that represent the value of “money, property, [or] services” that the debtor actually “obtained” through fraud. The award of treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs here—calculated as a multiple of the rent overcharge—is not a debt “for” anything the debtor obtained from the plaintiffs. It is, instead, a punitive penalty imposed by state law wholly unrelated to the value of the benefit the debtor fraudulently acquired.

**I. The Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Limits Nondischargeability to the Value of Property or Money Obtained by Fraud.**

The Bankruptcy Code must be interpreted according to its plain meaning. *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, *to the extent obtained by*” fraud. The phrase “to the extent obtained by” operates as a limitation: only the portion of a debt that corresponds to the actual value of the money or property fraudulently acquired is nondischargeable. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that “the amount of punitive damages was not a debt *for* money obtained by fraud within the meaning of section 523(a)(2)(A)”). As the Ninth Circuit explained, “to the extent obtained by” modifies “money, property, services, or credit,” and thus punitive damages “are not excepted from discharge.” *Id.*

The Third Circuit’s contrary interpretation reads the limiting phrase “to the extent obtained by” out of the statute. Congress’s choice of the word “obtained” reflects a focus on the *benefit* the debtor received, not on the *harm* the creditor suffered. *See Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. 213, 220 (1998) (noting that “obtained by” language “focuses attention on the debtor’s use of fraud to acquire property”). But the Court in *Cohen* did not address the precise question presented here: whether that focus extends to punitive, non-compensatory awards that exceed the actual value of property obtained.

**II. Treble Damages, Attorney’s Fees, and Costs Are Not “Debts” for Money “Obtained” Through Fraud.**

Treble damages are a form of civil punishment, not compensation for property the debtor obtained. This Court has long recognized that punitive damages serve to “punish the defendant for his wrongful conduct and to deter others from engaging in similar conduct.” *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Grp., Inc.*, 532 U.S. 424, 432 (2001). Because treble damages are not designed to return to the creditor the value of the money or property the debtor fraudulently acquired, they cannot be said to constitute a debt “for” such money or property.

The majority of circuits to consider this issue have held that punitive or non-compensatory damages are not excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A). *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198; *In re Chambers*, 348 F.3d 650, 656 (7th Cir. 2003) (holding that state-law punitive damages are not excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are not a debt “for” money, property, or services obtained by fraud). The Fifth Circuit similarly holds that “punitive damages are not ‘for’ money, property, or services obtained by fraud,” and are dischargeable. *In re Scroggin*, 364 F.3d 592, 597 (5th Cir. 2004). The Third Circuit’s decision below conflicts with these authorities.

**III. The “Fresh Start” Policy of the Bankruptcy Code Requires a Narrow Construction of Discharge Exceptions.**

Exceptions to discharge are to be construed narrowly in favor of the debtor to effectuate the Bankruptcy Code’s central purpose: providing the “honest but unfortunate debtor” with a “fresh start.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). This policy dictates that “exceptions to discharge [should be] confined to those plainly expressed” by Congress. *Kawaauhau v. Geiger*, 523 U.S. 57, 62 (1998). Because § 523(a)(2)(A) does not plainly express an intention to except punitive, non-compensatory damages from discharge, the debtors’ “fresh start” must prevail.

**IV. The Decision Below Conflicts with the Principle That Bankruptcy Law Defines the Scope of Discharge Exceptions.**

The Third Circuit relied on New Jersey’s characterization of treble damages as compensation. But federal law, not state law, defines the scope of discharge exceptions. *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 284 n.10 (noting that “the scope of the exception to discharge is a federal question”). Because § 523(a)(2)(A) focuses on what the debtor *obtained*, not the creditor’s measure of damages, state-law labels are irrelevant. *See In re Chambers*, 348 F.3d at 656 (“[T]he question whether a debt is excepted from discharge is a federal question, not a state law question.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The plain text of § 523(a)(2)(A) limits nondischargeability to the actual value of money, property, or services obtained through fraud. Punitive treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not debts “for” anything the debtor obtained; they are penalties imposed by state law. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
