**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause requires the Government to prove every element of the charged conspiracy offense beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant's membership persisted into the limitations period.**

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This bedrock guarantee "requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which [the defendant] is charged." *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 698 (1975). Where a fact is essential to establish that the defendant committed the offense at all—as opposed to merely mitigating an already-proven crime—the burden of persuasion may not be shifted to the accused. *See id.* at 703-04.

Continued participation in a conspiracy during the limitations period is not an affirmative defense collateral to guilt; it is the very fact that makes prosecution of that defendant for that conspiracy timely and lawful. A conspiracy prosecution requires proof that the defendant was a member of the agreement charged, and where the indictment alleges a single continuing conspiracy extending into the limitations window, the defendant's continued adherence to that agreement during the window is an element of the offense as charged. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977), confirms that "the State must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt with respect to every element of the offense charged." Withdrawal evidence does not introduce a new justification or excuse; it rebuts the Government's proof of the temporal element of continued membership. Once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, the Government's burden to establish that element beyond a reasonable doubt does not evaporate—it must be satisfied by evidence showing that, notwithstanding the claimed withdrawal, the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the charged period.

This allocation follows directly from *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 522-23 (1995), which held that the jury, not the judge, must find every element of a federal offense beyond a reasonable doubt, and from *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000), which reaffirmed that any fact essential to the imposition of criminal liability must be proved to the fact-finder beyond a reasonable doubt. Petitioners' continued membership during the limitations period is precisely such a fact.

**II. At common law, withdrawal terminates a conspirator's liability for subsequent acts, and the temporal scope of a defendant's membership has always been treated as part of the Government's proof of the conspiracy charged.**

This Court has long recognized that withdrawal from a conspiracy has substantive legal consequences bearing directly on the scope of the defendant's liability. In *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912), the Court explained that a conspiracy, once formed, is presumed to continue until its purposes are abandoned or accomplished, and that a member who has actually withdrawn is no longer accountable for the acts of the remaining conspirators done in furtherance of the scheme thereafter. The logic of *Hyde* is that liability for continuing conspiratorial conduct tracks continuing membership; a conspirator who has left the conspiracy stands outside its ongoing criminal enterprise from that point forward.

*United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270, 274-75 (2003), reaffirmed that a conspiracy does not automatically terminate merely because its immediate objective is temporarily frustrated; it continues "until the conspirators abandon the conspiracy or achieve their goal." The corollary of that rule is equally important here: because the Government defines the temporal boundaries of the conspiracy it charges, and because the existence of the conspiracy "during" the charged period is squarely part of what the indictment must establish, evidence tending to show that a particular defendant was no longer part of that continuing agreement during the relevant window goes to whether the Government has, in fact, proved the conspiracy it charged as to that defendant. It is not a freestanding excuse that leaves the underlying charge intact while merely mitigating punishment; it is a fact directly bearing on whether the essential temporal element of the charged crime—continued agreement during the limitations period—has been proved.

**III. Treating withdrawal as an affirmative defense that shifts the ultimate burden to the defendant is inconsistent with the settled allocation of burdens in conspiracy prosecutions and threatens conviction on less than the constitutionally required proof.**

Requiring petitioners to bear the burden of persuasion on withdrawal would permit the jury to convict without the Government ever having proved, to the requisite degree of certainty, that petitioners' membership extended into the period necessary to sustain the prosecution. That result cannot be squared with *Winship*'s core command that "no man shall lose his liberty unless the Government has borne the burden of ... convincing the fact-finder of his guilt" to the standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 363-64. Once petitioners came forward with evidence that the conspiracy's architects had split and that petitioners had ceased participation before the limitations period began, the Government was obligated to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that petitioners nonetheless remained members during that period. The courts below erred in relieving the Government of that burden and in affirming convictions obtained without proof of an essential element to the constitutionally required standard.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
