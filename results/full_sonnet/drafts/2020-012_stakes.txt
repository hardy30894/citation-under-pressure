# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 355(f)'s Text Confers Jurisdiction Over Any Board Decision That Satisfies Ordinary Principles of Finality.

The Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, incorporated into the Railroad Retirement Act by 45 U.S.C. § 231g, authorizes judicial review of "any final decision" of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). Congress did not limit that grant to decisions "made after a hearing" or to decisions resolving the underlying merits of an original application. The statute's only qualifier is finality itself.

This Court has long applied a "pragmatic" approach to finality, asking whether the challenged action marks "the consummation of the agency's decisionmaking process" and is one by which "rights or obligations have been determined, or from which legal consequences will flow." Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154, 177–178 (1997) (quoting Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman Corp., 333 U.S. 103, 113 (1948)). The Board's refusal to reopen Salinas's 2006 application meets both criteria. After a full evidentiary hearing, a Board hearing officer issued a decision that ended the administrative process; the Board had nothing further to decide. And that decision fixed Salinas's rights: it foreclosed any possibility that his disability annuity would be measured from his 2006 application rather than his 2013 one, a difference of seven years of benefits. Under this Court's settled finality doctrine, that is a "final decision" in every sense that matters. See FTC v. Standard Oil Co. of Cal., 449 U.S. 232, 239 (1980) (finality turns on whether agency action has "such an effect" that judicial intervention is proper); Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967) (finality doctrine serves to permit review of agency action that imposes "practical" consequences on regulated parties).

## II. Califano v. Sanders Does Not Control Because the Social Security Act's Judicial-Review Provision Contains Textual Limitations That Section 355(f) Lacks.

The Fifth Circuit's contrary rule traces to decisions borrowing wholesale from this Court's reasoning in Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99 (1977). But Sanders arose under a materially different statute. Section 205(g) of the Social Security Act authorizes review only of "any final decision of the Secretary made after a hearing." 42 U.S.C. § 405(g) (emphasis added). This Court held that a decision declining to reopen a prior claim falls outside that provision because such decisions are typically discretionary and do not require, and often do not receive, a hearing. Sanders, 430 U.S. at 108 ("Congress' judgment that agencies should be encouraged to reopen closed claims . . . would be frustrated" by treating every refusal to reopen as reviewable).

Section 355(f) contains no comparable "made after a hearing" restriction. Nothing in its text excludes reopening decisions from the category of "final decisions" subject to review. Where Congress uses different language in analogous statutes, this Court does not import limitations from one into the other. See, e.g., Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) ("[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," the omission is presumed intentional). Sanders' rationale—protecting agency discretion to decide informally, without a hearing, whether to reopen—has no purchase here, where the Board in fact convened a hearing and issued a reasoned decision applying its own regulatory standards for reopening. See 20 C.F.R. § 261.2.

## III. The D.C. Circuit's Approach in Stovic Correctly Applies These Principles and Should Guide This Court.

The D.C. Circuit confronted this precise question and held that "the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations." Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 826 F.3d 500, 502 (D.C. Cir. 2016). The court reasoned that Sanders' holding was "tightly tied" to the specific hearing requirement in the Social Security Act and that no such requirement appears in the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act's review provision. Id. at 504. That reasoning tracks this Court's own admonition that jurisdictional statutes must be read according to their terms, not by analogy to superficially similar but textually distinct provisions. See Kucana v. Holder, 558 U.S. 233, 251–252 (2010) (declining to infer statutory limits on judicial review that Congress did not enact).

This Court's decision in ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270 (1987), confirms that reopening decisions are amenable to judicial review when, as here, the agency's denial rests on an application of legal or regulatory standards rather than pure, unreviewable discretion. There, the Court distinguished between unreviewable refusals to reopen based on "new evidence" and reviewable refusals resting on claims of "material error." Id. at 278–280. The Board's hearing officer here evaluated Salinas's claim against the regulatory four-year reopening window of 20 C.F.R. § 261.2—a legal standard, not an unreviewable exercise of grace. That decision is the kind of reasoned, standard-bound agency action Congress intended courts to review.

## IV. The Fifth Circuit's Rule Produces Anomalous and Unjust Results.

Under the Fifth Circuit's rule, a railroad worker wrongly denied benefits has no recourse to challenge a Board decision refusing to correct that error, no matter how compelling the new evidence or how clear the administrative mistake. That result contradicts Congress's evident purpose in enacting a broad judicial-review provision for the protection of employees dependent on the Railroad Retirement system. This Court should hold that the Board's denial of Salinas's request to reopen his 2006 application is a "final decision" reviewable under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), reverse the judgment below, and remand for consideration of the merits of Salinas's petition.
