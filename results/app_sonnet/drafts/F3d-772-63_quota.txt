UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS
FOR THE FIRST CIRCUIT

No. 13-2527

JONATHAN FOLEY, Plaintiff-Appellant,
v.
WELLS FARGO BANK, N.A., Defendant-Appellee.

**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Applied an Overly Demanding Pleading Standard.**

A complaint survives dismissal if it states "a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007). Plausibility exists where the pleaded facts "allow[] the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged." Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). This Court reviews dismissal de novo, accepting the complaint's well-pleaded facts and drawing all reasonable inferences for the plaintiff. Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014); Watterson v. Page, 987 F.2d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 1993). Measured against that standard—not the heightened certainty the district court effectively demanded—Foley's complaint states plausible claims on every count.

**II. Foley Adequately Alleged Breach of Contract as a Third-Party Beneficiary of the Settlement Agreement.**

Under Massachusetts law, a non-signatory may enforce a contract if the contracting parties "clearly and definitely" intended to confer a direct benefit upon that person. Anderson v. Fox Hill Village Homeowners Corp., 424 Mass. 365, 366 (1997). The inquiry turns on "the language and circumstances of the instrument," Rae v. Air-Speed, Inc., 386 Mass. 187, 195 (1982), and intent to benefit a third party may be found even where that party is identified only as a member of a defined class. Cerberus Partners, L.P. v. Gadsby & Hannah LLP, 430 Mass. 152, 157 (1999).

The Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement expressly creates "Settlement Class B," of which Foley is a member, and mandates that such borrowers "first shall be considered for a HAMP modification" and, failing that, "shall be considered for a MAP2R modification" through a defined "waterfall" process. The agreement further imposes "servicing commitments" requiring written explanations of denials and specified communications during foreclosure proceedings. These are not vague aspirations; they are enforceable obligations running directly to identified class members like Foley. See Anderson, 424 Mass. at 366.

Foley alleged that Wells Fargo never considered him for MAP2R, never applied the waterfall analysis, and instead pursued foreclosure while sending him "short sale" and "deed in lieu" letters—the opposite of the loan-preservation review the settlement guaranteed. Those factual allegations, taken as true, plausibly show breach of a contractual duty owed directly to Foley. Rae, 386 Mass. at 195. The district court erred by demanding proof, rather than plausible allegations, of breach at the pleading stage. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.

**III. Foley's Chapter 93A Claim Is Independently Viable.**

Chapter 93A reaches conduct that is "immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous." Kattar v. Demoulas, 433 Mass. 1, 14 (2000). A plaintiff need only show unfair or deceptive conduct and a causal link between that conduct and his injury. Klairmont v. Gainsboro Restaurant, Inc., 465 Mass. 165, 174 (2013). Foley alleged that Wells Fargo repeatedly represented, for seven months, that HAMP was his only available modification path, delayed sending the HAMP application, falsely claimed non-receipt of his completed application, ignored his follow-up calls, and steered him toward short sale and deed-in-lieu options—all while quietly scheduling foreclosure without ever running the MAP2R waterfall it had promised. Such conduct plausibly qualifies as unfair or deceptive under the "rascality" standard of Kattar, and Foley's stalled modification and impending foreclosure plausibly satisfy Klairmont's causation requirement. Dismissal on this count was likewise premature.

**IV. Federal Law Does Not Preempt Foley's State-Law Claims.**

Wells Fargo's preemption argument fails because Foley does not seek to impose a state-law lending requirement on the bank; he seeks to enforce the bank's own voluntary contractual commitment. Courts have consistently held that HOLA and its implementing regulations do not preempt claims to enforce a bank's independently assumed contractual obligations, because such claims do not "regulate" the terms of credit within the meaning of the preemption regulations. See Aguayo v. U.S. Bank, 653 F.3d 912, 918 (9th Cir. 2011); Sovereign Bank v. BJ's Wholesale Club, Inc., 533 F.3d 162, 175 (3d Cir. 2008) (state-law claims that merely require a bank to honor its own contractual undertakings are not preempted). The settlement agreement here is Wells Fargo's own bargain, freely negotiated to resolve federal Truth-in-Lending litigation; enforcing its terms imposes no substantive lending standard beyond what Wells Fargo already promised. Preemption principles designed to protect national banks' lending discretion have no application to a bank's breach of its own settlement.

**V. Foley's Chapter 244 Claim Should Not Have Been Dismissed.**

Massachusetts foreclosure procedure demands strict compliance with statutory and contractual prerequisites before a mortgagee may exercise the power of sale. See U.S. Bank Nat'l Ass'n v. Ibanez, 458 Mass. 637, 646–47 (2011) (requiring strict adherence to statutory foreclosure prerequisites). Because the settlement agreement conditioned any foreclosure-related communication on continued modification consideration, and Wells Fargo scheduled foreclosure without satisfying that condition, Foley's Chapter 244 claim likewise states a plausible cause of action.

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the dismissal of Counts I, II, and III and remand for further proceedings.
