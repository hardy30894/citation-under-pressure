**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621–634, does not extend to appointed state court judges because they fall within the statutory exemption for “appointees on the policymaking level.” Congress enacted this exception not merely to exclude high-level executive and legislative officials, but to preserve the integrity of the entire tripartite structure of government—especially the judiciary—by recognizing that judges, like other senior officials, exercise substantial discretion in shaping the law and administering justice. The plain language of 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) unambiguously includes appointed state judges within this category, and no “clear statement” of congressional intent is required to override the text and structure of the statute.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office… or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The petitioners, though initially appointed by the Governor under Missouri’s Non-Partisan Court Plan, are not “elected” and thus do not fall under the first exemption. But they are unquestionably “appointees on the policymaking level.” The term “policymaking” is not confined to the executive or legislative branches; it encompasses any official whose decisions have broad, enduring consequences for the administration of government. As the First Circuit held in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judicial decisions “fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” This is not mere theory—it is the lived reality of the judiciary.

Judges do not merely apply pre-existing rules; they interpret ambiguous statutes, resolve conflicts in precedent, and establish legal standards that govern future conduct. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), “the Constitution is not a static document, but a living instrument” that evolves through judicial interpretation. Every decision by a state judge—whether in a civil case, a criminal appeal, or a constitutional challenge—has the potential to shape public policy, influence future litigation, and affect the rights of individuals and institutions. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, has the authority to adopt rules governing the conduct of the bar, to resolve internal disputes within the judiciary, and to set binding precedent across the state. See *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985); *In re Rules of Circuit Court of Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 757 S.W.2d 590 (Mo. 1988). These are not administrative tasks; they are core policymaking functions.

The petitioners argue that judges are not policymakers because their role is “to decide cases between litigants involving questions of law in which there are no interstices or lacunae to fill.” App. Br. at 10. But this view is both factually and legally flawed. It ignores the reality that nearly every case presents some legal uncertainty—whether in statutory construction, constitutional interpretation, or the application of common law principles. Even in “easy” cases, judges must exercise judgment in selecting among competing interpretations. As the Supreme Court has long held, “the judicial function is not merely to apply rules, but to interpret them in light of changing circumstances.” *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974). To deny that judges make policy is to deny the very nature of the judicial role.

Moreover, the legislative history cited by the petitioners—specifically, the Joint Explanatory Statement that the exemption applies to “cabinet officers” and “persons with comparable responsibilities”—does not limit the exemption to the executive branch. The phrase “comparable responsibilities” is broad and inclusive. It is not reasonable to suppose that Congress intended to exclude judges from this category while including other high-level officials. Indeed, the EEOC itself has acknowledged that “the powers and responsibilities of appointed and elected judges are identical,” and that the distinction between them is irrelevant to the policymaking exemption. Brief for EEOC as Amicus Curiae at 9. To read the exemption narrowly would render it meaningless in the context of state judiciaries, which are integral to state governance.

Finally, the principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* supports the conclusion that Congress intended the exemption to apply to judges. If Congress had meant to exclude appointed judges from the exemption, it would have done so explicitly—especially given that elected judges are already excluded under the first clause of § 630(f). The absence of such a qualification suggests that Congress intended the exemption to cover all appointees who exercise policymaking authority, regardless of branch.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges at age 70 is rationally related to a legitimate state interest and thus satisfies the rational basis test under the Fourteenth Amendment. The petitioners’ equal protection claim fails because the state has a compelling interest in ensuring the competence, efficiency, and public confidence in its judiciary.
