The District Court's admission of improper testimony from two Assistant District Attorneys ("ADAs") and a police lieutenant was reversible error, as it allowed these witnesses to vouch for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, provide impermissible legal conclusions, and interpret evidence that the jury was equally capable of analyzing. Such testimony is barred by longstanding principles of evidence law. See, e.g., United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 139-40 (2d Cir. 1948) (reversing conviction where expert witness vouched for credibility of other witnesses); Marx & Co. v. Diners' Club, Inc., 405 F.2d 477, 479-80 (2d Cir. 1968) (reversing judgment where expert witness provided legal conclusions); McCormick on Evidence § 12 (1954) (noting that witnesses may not testify to matters of law).

The admission of this testimony was not harmless, as it directly addressed the two most contested issues in the case: the credibility of Ramos and Rivera, and whether they had probable cause for their actions. See Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (holding that error is not harmless if it affects the substantial rights of the parties); cf. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (noting that a prosecutor's improper statements can be particularly prejudicial because of their "weight of the government's imprimatur").

Appellants are entitled to a new trial with proper jury instructions. Under New York law, the offense of "obstructing governmental administration" requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. See People v. Lupinacci, 191 Misc. 251, 254 (N.Y. Co. Ct. 1948) (noting that an essential element of the offense is that the officers be engaged in lawful performance of their duties); People v. Hare, 284 N.Y.S. 702, 704 (N.Y. App. Div. 1936) (same). The District Court should have instructed the jury accordingly.

Furthermore, Appellants' version of events, if believed, would allow a jury to infer that Ramos and Rivera acted with malice, entitling them to a punitive damages instruction. See Smith v. Warden, 86 F.2d 619, 621 (2d Cir. 1936) (noting that punitive damages may be awarded where defendant's actions are "wanton, malicious, or oppressive"); cf. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893) (noting that punitive damages are available where defendant's actions demonstrate "wantonness, or . . . wilful or malicious wrong"). 

The District Court's denial of Appellants' motion for judgment as a matter of law was proper, as the photographs taken by the security camera did not definitively rule out either side's version of events. See Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Chamberlain, 288 U.S. 333, 343 (1933) (noting that a court should not disturb a jury's verdict unless the evidence is "so conclusive that reasonable minds could not differ"); cf. Gunning v. Cooley, 281 U.S. 90, 94 (1930) (noting that a court should not direct a verdict where there is "substantial evidence" supporting the opposing party's claim).

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
