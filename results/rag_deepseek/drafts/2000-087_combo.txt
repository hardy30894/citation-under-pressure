**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. Properly construed, the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Any broader reading would raise grave constitutional concerns that this Court must avoid.

**I. The Statute’s Text and Purpose Limit Detention to a Reasonable Time**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The statute does not specify an unlimited duration. When Congress intends to authorize indefinite detention, it says so plainly. *See, e.g.,* 18 U.S.C. § 4246 (civil commitment of dangerous persons). The absence of such language here indicates that Congress contemplated detention only until removal is effected. *Cf. Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (deportation statutes must be construed “with great strictness” in favor of the alien). The statute’s basic purpose—ensuring the alien’s presence for removal—is satisfied once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. Continued detention then serves no statutory objective.

**II. Indefinite Detention Raises Serious Constitutional Concerns**

The Constitution forbids the government from imprisoning a person indefinitely without the procedural protections required for criminal punishment. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 235 (1896) (holding that aliens cannot be subjected to “infamous punishment” without judicial trial). Deportation proceedings are civil, not criminal. *United States ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi*, 287 U.S. 129, 133 (1932). But indefinite civil detention—especially of a resident alien who has been admitted to the country—implicates the fundamental liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 540 (1952) (recognizing that “the right to bail” in deportation cases turns on “the likelihood of the alien’s appearance” and danger to the community, not on punitive grounds).

This Court has long distinguished between detention at the border and detention of aliens who have entered the United States. *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 188 (1958) (paroled alien “in the United States” for purposes of § 243(h) but not for all purposes). A resident alien like petitioner has “entered” the United States and is entitled to constitutional protections. *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193, 197 (1958) (excluded aliens on parole are not “within the United States” for all purposes). Indefinite detention of such an alien without any prospect of removal would transform civil detention into a de facto life sentence—a result the Constitution does not tolerate.

**III. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires a Reasonableness Limitation**

Where a statute is ambiguous, this Court construes it to avoid serious constitutional questions. *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 201 (1957) (construing supervision statute to avoid “grave constitutional issues”). Here, reading § 1231(a)(6) to authorize indefinite detention would raise due process concerns of the highest order. The statute must therefore be read to include an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957) (deportation statutes should be interpreted “in the light of the Constitution’s demands”).

The government’s interpretation—that detention may continue as long as “good faith efforts” to remove persist—would permit confinement for years or even decades. *See United States ex rel. Mensevich v. Tod*, 264 U.S. 134, 137 (1924) (deportation to a country that does not exist is impossible). Such a result is inconsistent with the statute’s purpose and with the principle that deportation is not punishment. *Phillippides v. Day*, 283 U.S. 48, 52 (1931) (deportation is a civil proceeding, not a criminal penalty). When removal becomes impossible, continued detention serves only to punish—a function Congress did not authorize.

**IV. The Government’s Reading Would Permit Punishment Without Trial**

The government argues that petitioner may be held indefinitely because he is a risk to the community or a flight risk. But those factors are relevant only to the conditions of release, not to the authority to detain. *Carlson*, 342 U.S. at 540 (bail may be denied based on danger or flight risk, but only within the statutory framework). The statute itself provides for supervised release under § 1231(a)(3). Indefinite detention without any meaningful review violates due process. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237 (imprisonment without judicial safeguards is unconstitutional).

Petitioner has been in custody for years after the removal period expired. No country will accept him. His detention is indefinite. This Court should hold that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and that once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is unlawful.
