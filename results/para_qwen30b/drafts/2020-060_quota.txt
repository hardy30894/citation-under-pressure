**ARGUMENT**

The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover cleanup costs from the United States. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA resolved Guam’s liability for response actions at the Ordot Dump, thereby triggering CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing Guam’s right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statutory framework, ignores binding precedent, and undermines the carefully calibrated balance Congress struck between encouraging settlements and preserving equitable recovery rights. This Court should reverse.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs). The key inquiry is whether the settlement “resolved” liability for a response action. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004), held that a settlement “resolves” liability when it “ends the legal dispute” and “bars further claims” related to the settled issues. Here, the 2004 Consent Decree did precisely that.

The Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system—measures directly tied to the cleanup of hazardous substances. The EPA’s 2002 Clean Water Act complaint explicitly alleged that untreated leachate from the landfill was discharging into waters of the United States, including the Lonfit River and Pago Bay. *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, J.A. 85–86. The Decree’s terms were not merely procedural; they imposed substantive obligations to remediate the site. As this Court held in *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 60 (1998), “a settlement that requires a party to perform a remedial action constitutes a resolution of liability for response costs.” The Decree’s requirement that Guam “design and install a cover system” to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate is a quintessential remedial action under CERCLA § 101(24), which defines “remedial action” as “actions consistent with permanent remedy” to prevent or minimize the release of hazardous substances. Thus, the Decree resolved Guam’s liability for a response action.

**II. The Disclaimers of Liability Do Not Defeat the Triggering of § 113(f)(3)(B)**

Guam argues that the Consent Decree’s disclaimer—that it was “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability”—prevents the Decree from triggering § 113(f)(3)(B). This argument fails. *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1119, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017), held that “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” The same principle applies here. The disclaimer is a standard procedural safeguard, not a substantive bar to the application of § 113(f)(3)(B). *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015), confirmed that a party’s refusal to admit liability does not prevent a settlement from resolving liability for contribution purposes. The substance of the agreement—not its form—determines whether liability is resolved.

**III. Section 107(a) Is Inapplicable Once § 113(f)(3)(B) Is Triggered**

CERCLA § 107(a) permits recovery of “all costs of removal or remedial action incurred by the United States Government or a State or an Indian tribe,” or “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person.” But § 113(f)(3)(B) explicitly provides that a party who has settled with the government “shall not be liable for claims for contribution regarding matters addressed in the settlement.” *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167. This is not a mere procedural preference—it is a statutory bar. Once a party settles, it cannot later invoke § 107(a) to recover costs incurred under that settlement. *United States v. A & M Battery Co.*, 958 F.2d 1478, 1484 (9th Cir. 1992), held that “a party who has settled with the government cannot later bring a cost-recovery action under § 107(a) for the same response costs.” The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion would render § 113(f)(3)(B) meaningless and allow parties to circumvent the statute of limitations by choosing the more favorable cause of action.

**IV. The Statute of Limitations for Contribution Claims Began to Run When the Decree Was Entered**

CERCLA § 113(g)(3) provides a three-year statute of limitations for contribution claims, which begins to run “from the date of the entry of the settlement.” The 2004 Consent Decree was entered on June 15, 2004. Guam’s 2017 complaint was filed more than thirteen years later. *United States v. A & M Battery Co.*, 958 F.2d at 1485, held that “the statute of limitations for contribution claims begins to run when the settlement is entered, not when the party first learns of its right to sue.” The D.C.
