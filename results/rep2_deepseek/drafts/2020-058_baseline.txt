**ARGUMENT**

**A Grant of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an “Admission” into the United States Under 8 U.S.C. § 1255 Because It Provides a Lawful Entry After Inspection and Authorization.**

Section 1255(a) permits adjustment of status for an alien “who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The INA defines “admission” and “admitted” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under § 1254a satisfies this definition because, by the very nature of obtaining nonimmigrant status, the alien undergoes inspection and is deemed admitted. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017); *Flores v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Servs.*, 718 F.3d 548, 552-53 (6th Cir. 2013).

**A. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Establishes That TPS Recipients Are Considered “Admitted.”**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This statutory language accomplishes more than merely conferring a status—it deems the alien to be in the same position as one who has been lawfully admitted as a nonimmigrant. When an alien obtains nonimmigrant status, that status necessarily flows from a lawful admission after inspection. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960. Congress’s decision to “consider” TPS recipients as being in lawful nonimmigrant status thus logically incorporates the inspection and admission that ordinarily accompanies that status.

Contrary to the Third Circuit’s view, *Sanchez v. Sec’y U.S. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 967 F.3d 242 (3d Cir. 2020), the INA does not require that “admission” be a physical event that occurs at the border. Although § 1101(a)(13)(A) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States,” that definition does not foreclose the possibility that an alien already present in the country may be deemed admitted through a subsequent grant of lawful status. The Board of Immigration Appeals has long recognized that “an adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). Because TPS places the alien in lawful nonimmigrant status, it too constitutes an admission for purposes of adjustment.

**B. The Statutory Context and Structure Confirm That TPS Satisfies § 1255(a)’s Admission Requirement.**

Congress’s use of the phrase “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” in § 1254a(f)(4) mirrors the language used in § 1258(a), which applies to “any alien lawfully admitted to the United States as a nonimmigrant who is continuing to maintain that status.” (Emphasis added). The parallel phrasing indicates that § 1254a(f)(4) equates being in lawful nonimmigrant status with being lawfully admitted as a nonimmigrant. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 961-62. The Third Circuit’s effort to distinguish these provisions by parsing “admission” and “status” as separate concepts ignores this textual connection.

Moreover, reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a)’s admission requirement does not render any statutory provision superfluous. Section 1255(a) imposes a threshold eligibility requirement; § 1255(c)(2) separately bars adjustment for aliens who have failed to maintain lawful status. Both provisions operate independently. A TPS recipient who entered without inspection satisfies § 1255(a) because the TPS grant constitutes an admission, and also satisfies § 1255(c)(2) because § 1254a(f)(4) deems the alien to have maintained continuous lawful status. The dual requirements serve distinct functions and are not redundant.

**C. The Purpose of TPS Supports Reading a Grant of TPS as an Admission.**

TPS was designed to provide humanitarian relief to nationals of countries experiencing ongoing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Construing § 1254a(f)(4) to permit adjustment of status for TPS recipients who entered without inspection furthers this humanitarian purpose. It allows TPS recipients who have established deep ties to the United States—including family relationships with U.S. citizens—to obtain permanent residence without being forced to return to dangerous home countries. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964.

The Third Circuit’s contrary interpretation undermines this purpose by compelling TPS recipients who entered illegally to either leave the United States—potentially to unsafe conditions—or forgo the opportunity to adjust status. Congress could not have intended such an anomalous result, particularly when TPS recipients who entered lawfully are permitted to adjust without difficulty. *Id.* at 962.

**D. The Agency’s Longstanding Interpretation Supports Petitioners’ Position.**

Although the Third Circuit cited a 1991 opinion of the INS General Counsel for the proposition that TPS does not constitute an admission, that opinion predates the modern USCIS Policy Manual, which now recognizes that certain grants of status may serve as admissions. More importantly, the Sixth and Ninth Circuits—the only circuits to have squarely addressed this question prior to the Third Circuit—both held that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission under § 1255. *Flores*, 718 F.3d at 552-53; *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960. Their reasoning is consistent with the text, structure, and purpose of the INA, and this Court should adopt it.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
