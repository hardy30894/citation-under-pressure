# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Exempts OSHA Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Authority Over the Working Conditions at Issue

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA jurisdiction only for "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards." The statute's use of the active verb "exercise" requires more than the bare existence of regulatory power; it requires that the power actually have been brought to bear on the working conditions in question. This Court has long insisted that federal displacement of an otherwise applicable regulatory scheme be measured by what Congress and the delegated agency have actually done, not by what they might do. See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (state regulation is not superseded "unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress").

The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels—covering fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire arrestors, and signaling lights, 46 U.S.C. § 4102—address navigational and life-saving equipment. They do not exercise authority over the occupational hazards of oil and gas exploration, such as blowout-prevention procedures, that caused the injuries here. Because the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over these working conditions, Section 4(b)(1) does not apply, and OSHA's citation stands on solid statutory ground.

## II. Napier's Field-Preemption Rule Does Not Extend to Minimal, Unexercised Coast Guard Authority

Petitioner's reliance on a field-occupation theory analogous to Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605 (1926), is misplaced. In Napier, the Court found state safety-appliance regulation displaced because the Interstate Commerce Commission had promulgated pervasive federal rules governing the very locomotive equipment at issue, occupying the field so thoroughly that no room remained for the states to add requirements of their own. No comparable comprehensive exercise of authority exists here. The Coast Guard's uninspected-vessel rules are narrow and equipment-specific; they leave untouched the drilling and well-control practices at issue in this case. Absent the kind of pervasive regulatory occupation present in Napier, there is no basis to infer that Congress intended silent, unexercised Coast Guard authority to oust OSHA.

## III. Concurrent Regulation of Vessel Safety Is Permissible Absent Actual Conflict

This Court has repeatedly tolerated overlapping safety regulation of vessels by different regulators where no genuine conflict exists. In Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1 (1937), the Court upheld a state's regulation of tug-and-barge towing operations notwithstanding federal inspection laws, because Congress had not manifested an intent to exclude such regulation. Similarly, in Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), the Court sustained a municipal smoke-abatement ordinance as applied to federally licensed vessels, reasoning that regulation of matters of local concern survives absent an actual, operative federal rule addressing the same conduct. Id. at 445–48. The same principle governs here: the Coast Guard's failure to regulate drilling-related occupational hazards leaves room for OSHA's complementary regulation without producing any conflict Congress sought to avoid.

## IV. OSHA's Remedial Purpose Supports Broad Construction of "Workplace" Coverage

The Act is remedial legislation, and this Court has long construed such statutes generously to achieve their protective purpose. See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111 (1944) (holding that the term "employee" under the National Labor Relations Act is not limited by common-law definitions but must be construed with reference to the statute's purposes and the evils it was designed to remedy). Likewise, this Court has recognized that when Congress enacts broad protective language, courts should give that language its natural, expansive scope rather than importing limitations Congress did not write into the statute. See United States v. American Trucking Assns., 310 U.S. 534 (1940). Section 4(a)'s broad reference to "employment performed in a workplace" plainly reaches a barge actively engaged in drilling operations on state waters, staffed by employees performing industrial tasks. The Secretary's interpretation of her own statute, reached through reasoned application to these facts, warrants respect from this Court in construing the scope of the Act's coverage.

## V. The Workers' Functional Role, Not the Vessel's Documentation Status, Governs

To the extent petitioner suggests that its employees' presence aboard a vessel automatically triggers exclusive maritime regulation, this Court's precedents establish that coverage turns on the function performed, not on the vessel's Coast Guard classification. See South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251 (1940) (seaman status depends on the nature of the work performed and its relationship to navigation, not merely on presence aboard a vessel); International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50 (1926) (extending seaman's remedies to a stevedore because the nature of his work, not his formal job title, determined his status). Employees engaged in oil and gas extraction from a stationary drilling barge perform industrial, not seafaring, functions, reinforcing that the barge was a "workplace" subject to OSHA under Section 4(a) and that the Coast Guard's unexercised authority over uninspected vessels does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1).

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
