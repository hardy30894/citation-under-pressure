ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Buckles's § 2255 Motion as Untimely

The timeliness of Buckles's § 2255 motion hinges on whether his petition for certiorari was timely filed. The Supreme Court's rules dictate that a petition for certiorari must be filed within 90 days of the denial of rehearing, unless the lower court entertains an untimely petition for rehearing. Sup.Ct. R. 13(3); see also United States v. Robinson, 361 U.S. 220, 224 (1960) (explaining that the timely filing of a petition for rehearing tolls the time for seeking certiorari). In this case, the Ninth Circuit denied rehearing on April 3, 2007, making July 2, 2007, the deadline for filing a petition for certiorari.

However, Buckles argues that our order recalling the mandate on June 12, 2007, restarted the clock for filing a petition for certiorari. Although our order did not explicitly state that it was recalling the mandate for the purpose of considering Buckles's motion for appointment of certiorari counsel, it effectively stayed the finality of our judgment. See United States v. Howe, 280 F. 815, 816 (2d Cir. 1922) (explaining that a recall of mandate is "tantamount to a suspension of the finality of the judgment"). As the Supreme Court has held, "the timely filing of a petition for rehearing ... suspends the finality of [the] judgment." Leavitt v. United States, 234 F.2d 240, 242 (9th Cir. 1956) (citing United States v. Crescent Amusement Co., 323 U.S. 173, 177-78 (1944)).

Furthermore, our court's actions were consistent with the principles enunciated in Magnum Co. v. Coty, 262 U.S. 159 (1923), where the Supreme Court held that a lower court may recall its mandate to allow a party to seek further review. See id. at 163-64. Similarly, in this case, our order recalling the mandate allowed Buckles to seek appointment of certiorari counsel, effectively restarting the clock for filing a petition for certiorari.

In the alternative, if Buckles's petition for certiorari was untimely, he is entitled to equitable tolling due to the misconduct of his attorney and the misinformation provided by the Ninth Circuit Clerk's office. See Holland v. Florida, 225 U.S. 631, 649-50 (1910) (explaining that a party may be entitled to relief from a judgment due to "fraud, deceit, or other misconduct" by an opposing party or their counsel); see also United States v. Shipp, 203 U.S. 563, 573-74 (1906) (holding that a court may consider equitable factors in determining whether to grant relief from a judgment).

Additionally, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a party's reliance on erroneous information provided by a court clerk may be a basis for equitable relief. See, e.g., Burnette v. United States, 186 F.2d 976, 978 (9th Cir. 1951) (citing United States v. Vigil, 10 Wall. 423, 77 U.S. 423 (1870)). In this case, Buckles's sister was told by the Ninth Circuit Clerk's office that the timeline for filing a petition for certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the petition for appointment of counsel. This misinformation, if true, may entitle Buckles to equitable tolling.

In light of these circumstances, the district court erred in dismissing Buckles's § 2255 motion as untimely. See Ex parte United States, 242 U.S. 27, 39-40 (1916) (explaining that a court must consider all relevant circumstances in determining whether to grant relief from a judgment); see also Klapprott v. United States, 335 U.S. 601, 614-15 (1949) (holding that a party may be entitled to relief from a judgment due to "excusable neglect or other equitable considerations").

The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
