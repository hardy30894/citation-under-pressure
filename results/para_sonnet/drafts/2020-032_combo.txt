# ARGUMENT

## I. A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES CANNOT REVIVE AN OTHERWISE MOOT CONTROVERSY BECAUSE ARTICLE III REQUIRES A LIVE DISPUTE OVER A REAL AND SUBSTANTIAL INTEREST

This Court has long held that "the exercise of judicial power under Art. III of the Constitution depends on the existence of a case or controversy," and that a federal court has "never been empowered to decide moot questions or abstract propositions." **North Carolina v. Rice**, 404 U.S. 244, 246 (1971) (quoting United States v. Alaska S.S. Co., 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920)). The doctrine traces to the foundational principle that judicial power extends only to actual controversies, not to advisory opinions. **Muskrat v. United States**, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911) (holding that Article III courts may not resolve disputes lacking "the nature of a case or controversy"). Where "the question sought to be adjudicated has been mooted by subsequent developments," a federal court is without power to render a decision. **California v. San Pablo & Tulare R. Co.**, 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893).

Petitioners' claim for nominal damages does not supply the "real and substantial controversy" that Article III demands. **Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth**, 300 U.S. 227, 240-41 (1937) (requiring a controversy "admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts"). A request for a token sum, untethered to any actual injury, is precisely the kind of abstract vindication of rights that this Court has refused to entertain. As this Court explained in **Ashwander v. TVA**, 297 U.S. 288, 325 (1936), courts do not "pass upon abstract, hypothetical or contingent questions," and the availability of a purely nominal remedy—one that in no way depends on proof of actual harm—cannot convert an abstract dispute into a concrete one.

GGC's voluntary revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct eliminated the only live dispute in this case. This Court has repeatedly held that voluntary cessation of challenged conduct moots a case where, as here, there is no reasonable expectation that the conduct will recur. **United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Ass'n**, 166 U.S. 290, 308-09 (1897) (dismissing challenge to conduct that had ceased). The policies petitioners challenged no longer exist; the "case or controversy" that Article III requires evaporated when the college amended its rules.

## II. NOMINAL DAMAGES ARE NOT "DAMAGES" IN ANY MEANINGFUL SENSE BECAUSE THEY VINDICATE NO ACTUAL INJURY

Petitioners' theory rests on the premise that a request for nominal damages is functionally equivalent to a request for compensatory damages for purposes of maintaining a live case. That premise cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated recognition that damages in a civil action must be tied to actual, provable injury. In **Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.**, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931), this Court explained that "[t]he rule which precludes the recovery of uncertain damages applies to such as are not the certain result of the wrong, not to those damages which are definitely attributable to the wrong and only uncertain in respect of their amount." Nominal damages, by contrast, are not "attributable to the wrong" in any measurable sense—they are a fiction employed to mark a technical violation where no injury has been shown.

This distinction is not merely semantic. The very purpose of a damages remedy at common law was to make the plaintiff whole for a demonstrated loss. See **Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.**, 245 U.S. 531, 533 (1918) (Holmes, J.) (observing that legal causation and injury are necessary predicates to a damages recovery). Where, as here, petitioners disclaimed any actual injury and rested their prayer for relief solely on a nominal award, they sought nothing more than a symbolic declaration of wrongdoing dressed in the language of damages. Such a request does not present the kind of "specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character" that satisfies Article III. **Aetna Life Ins. Co.**, 300 U.S. at 241.

## III. NEITHER GRADUATION NOR POLICY REVISION LEAVES ANY REMEDY FOR A FEDERAL COURT TO AWARD

This Court has consistently dismissed cases as moot where the practical significance of the underlying dispute has been extinguished by intervening events, even where some nominal interest is asserted. In **Mills v. Green**, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), this Court dismissed an appeal as moot because "the decision of [the] question ... could be of no practical benefit to [plaintiff]," notwithstanding a technical live claim. So too here: with the Freedom of Expression Policy rescinded, the Student Code of Conduct amended, and Uzuegbunam himself no longer enrolled at GGC, no decree this Court could issue would have any effect on the parties' primary conduct. The residual claim for a token sum does not alter this calculus; it is the very definition of a case that "could be of no practical benefit."

This Court's decision in **Little v. Bowers**, 134 U.S. 547, 556 (1890), confirms that a court will not proceed to adjudicate constitutional questions when the underlying practical dispute has been resolved by the parties' own conduct outside of litigation. GGC's voluntary correction of the challenged policies—undertaken before any adjudication of their validity—removed the practical stakes of this litigation just as surely as the events in Little removed the stakes there.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
