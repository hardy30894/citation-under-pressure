The Ninth Circuit erred in reversing the district court's grant of summary judgment to Intel. The Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) imposes a three-year statute of limitations on actions alleging breach of fiduciary duty, which begins to run "from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly noted that "actual knowledge" requires more than just knowledge of the underlying transaction; it requires knowledge that the transaction was imprudent. However, it incorrectly held that a plaintiff's self-serving testimony that they did not read or recall reading the disclosures is sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact.

The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "actual knowledge" under ERISA, but the Court's decisions in related contexts suggest that actual knowledge is not solely a matter of a plaintiff's subjective awareness. In Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101 (1989), the Court held that a plan administrator's interpretation of a plan is reviewed de novo unless the plan gives the administrator discretionary authority. This decision implies that a plaintiff's knowledge of a plan's terms and disclosures can be sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if the plaintiff does not subjectively understand the implications of those disclosures.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's own precedent in Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), and Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), supports the conclusion that a plaintiff's actual knowledge is not limited to their subjective awareness. In these cases, the court held that a plaintiff's knowledge of a transaction or event is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if the plaintiff does not know that the transaction or event violates ERISA.

In this case, Intel disclosed the relevant information about the investments to Sulyma through various documents hosted on two websites. Sulyma accessed some of this information on the websites, but he testified that he was not actually aware that his retirement accounts were invested in alternative investments while working at Intel. However, Sulyma's self-serving testimony is insufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact. The record shows that Intel provided Sulyma with numerous disclosures, including Fund Fact Sheets, a Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, a Summary Plan Description, and Annual Disclosures, which explained the investment strategy and possible risks.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment was based on its finding that Sulyma's testimony created a dispute of material fact. However, this decision ignores the fact that Sulyma had access to the relevant information and failed to read or recall it. As the Court held in Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882 (1996), ERISA fiduciaries are required to act "solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries" and must exercise "the care, skill, prudence, and diligence ... that a prudent man acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use." 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1). Intel complied with its disclosure obligations under ERISA, and Sulyma's failure to read or recall the disclosures should not toll the statute of limitations.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's grant of summary judgment to Intel should be reinstated. The three-year statute of limitations under ERISA bars Sulyma's claims because he had actual knowledge of the breach or violation more than three years before filing the action. The fact that Sulyma chose not to read or could not recall having read the disclosures provided by Intel is not sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact.
