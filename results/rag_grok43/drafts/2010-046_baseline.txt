The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act. That statute authorizes such relief only when crowding is the “primary cause” of an Eighth Amendment violation and “no other relief will remedy” it. The record establishes both predicates.

The district court found, after extensive hearings, that severe overcrowding produced systemic failures in medical and mental-health care, resulting in preventable deaths and suffering. These conditions constitute cruel and unusual punishment. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (“the length of time each inmate spent in isolation was simply one consideration among many” in assessing whether “conditions in the isolation cells continued to violate the prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment”); Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (Eighth Amendment reaches deprivations denying “the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities”). Overcrowding was not merely correlative; it was the root cause that rendered existing facilities and staffing incapable of delivering constitutionally adequate care.

No narrower remedy suffices. Prior stipulated orders and incremental injunctive relief had failed. See Hutto, 437 U.S. at 687 (“The District Court had given the Department repeated opportunities to remedy the cruel and unusual conditions”). The court therefore concluded that only a reduction to 137.5 percent of design capacity would permit delivery of adequate medical services. That determination rests on the same equitable authority recognized in Costello v. Wainwright, 430 U.S. 325, 326 (1977) (per curiam), where a single-judge court could order population reduction or capacity expansion to cure unconstitutional overcrowding.

The order is narrowly drawn and extends no further than necessary. It leaves the State free to choose among release mechanisms, new construction, or transfers, consistent with the PLRA’s requirement that relief be the least intrusive means of correcting the violation. Because the findings demonstrate both statutory prerequisites, the order does not violate the Act.
