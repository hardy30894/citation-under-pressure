**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. This principle, rooted in the common law and long recognized as fundamental to the American system of justice, was not intended to be confined to the trial on guilt or innocence. Rather, it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second jeopardy” for the same offense, including those that determine the severity of punishment based on prior convictions. In this case, the trial court’s determination of a prior felony conviction directly affects the length of the defendant’s sentence under California’s Three Strikes law. The retrial of that allegation, following a reversal based on insufficient evidence, constitutes a second prosecution for the same offense within the meaning of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The foundational principle that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial after an acquittal was established in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896). There, the Court held that “a defendant who has been acquitted of a crime cannot be retried upon the same charge, even though the verdict was erroneous.” Id. at 671. The Court emphasized that the protection against double jeopardy is not limited to the trial on the merits of guilt but extends to any proceeding that subjects a defendant to the risk of punishment for the same offense. The Court reasoned that “the interest of the public in the administration of justice is not advanced by a second trial after an acquittal,” and that “the right of the accused to be free from a second trial is a fundamental one.” Id. at 672.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150 (1833), where the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a crime could not be retried on the same charge, even if the acquittal was based on a defect in the indictment. The Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States, in the fifth amendment, declares that no person shall be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb. This is a fundamental principle of justice.” Id. at 165. The Court further observed that “the right of the accused to be free from a second trial is not to be defeated by any error in the first trial.” Id. at 166.

The Court’s decision in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), further clarified the scope of the Double Jeopardy Clause. In that case, the Court held that a defendant could not be prosecuted twice for the same offense, even if the second prosecution was based on a different statute, so long as the two offenses were essentially the same. The Court stated: “The double jeopardy clause is not confined to cases where the same statute is violated, but extends to cases where the same offense is charged under different statutes.” Id. at 382. The Court emphasized that the Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense,” regardless of the form of the charge.

In *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22 (1953), the Court reiterated that the Double Jeopardy Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal.” Id. at 26. The Court noted that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not limited to the trial on the merits of guilt or innocence,” but extends to any proceeding that determines the punishment for an offense. The Court observed that “the interest of the public in the administration of justice is not advanced by a second trial after an acquittal,” and that “the right of the accused to be free from a second trial is a fundamental one.” Id. at 27.

The Court’s decision in *United States v. Jenkins*, 345 U.S. 100 (1953), further supports the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to sentencing proceedings. In that case, the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a charge of violating the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act could not be retried on the same charge, even though the second prosecution was based on a different count of the same indictment. The Court stated: “The double jeopardy clause is not confined to cases where the same statute is violated, but extends to cases where the same offense is charged under different statutes.” Id. at 103.

In this case, the prior conviction allegation was essential to the imposition of a longer sentence under California’s Three Strikes law. The trial court’s finding that defendant had suffered a prior serious felony conviction directly affected the length of his sentence. The reversal of that finding based on insufficient evidence constitutes an acquittal of the enhancement allegation. Under *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), such an acquittal bars retrial. The fact that the proceeding is labeled a “sentencing” hearing does not alter the constitutional protection. The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense, regardless of the label attached to the proceeding.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding, which permits retrial of a prior conviction allegation after an acquittal, is inconsistent with the settled principles of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court’s decisions in *United States v. Ball*, *United States v. Wilson*, *United States v. Lanza*, *United States v. Kahriger*, and *United States v. Jenkins* establish that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial after an acquittal, even in the context of sentencing. The fact that the proceeding is noncapital does not diminish the constitutional protection. The Clause is not limited to capital cases, nor is it confined to the trial on guilt or innocence.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to vacate the sentence enhancement based on the prior conviction allegation, in accordance with the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution.
