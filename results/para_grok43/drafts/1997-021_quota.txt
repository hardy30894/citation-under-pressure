The National Labor Relations Act does not prescribe any evidentiary threshold for employer polling of incumbent-union support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987). The Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” before polling is therefore a creature of administrative policy, not statutory command. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). That policy cannot be sustained when it renders the very device it purports to authorize a nullity.

Three courts of appeals have recognized the anomaly. An employer may lawfully poll only when it already possesses evidence that would justify outright withdrawal of recognition, yet the Board simultaneously insists that polling serves a distinct informational function. Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982); A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that an employer meeting the reasonable-doubt standard may still confront “inherent uncertainty” about actual majority support. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). Requiring the same quantum of proof for an unsupervised poll as for withdrawal of recognition therefore defeats the Board’s stated rationale for permitting polls at all.

The Board’s choice is also internally inconsistent with its preference for Board-conducted elections. Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978). If RM elections and polls serve the same statutory purpose, the evidentiary bar for the less formal procedure should be no higher; yet the Board applies identical standards while conceding that polls lack the procedural safeguards of elections. Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). This asymmetry cannot be reconciled with the Act’s text or with the Board’s duty to balance employer and employee interests evenhandedly.

Even under the Board’s standard, the record contains substantial evidence of reasonable doubt. Seven unit employees expressly repudiated the union. Three others—Ridgick, Marsh, and Bloch—made statements that, under controlling precedent, independently support an objective basis for doubt. Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989); Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). The Board’s refusal to credit these statements rested on nothing more than its own policy preferences rather than record evidence, and therefore fails substantial-evidence review. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951).

The Board’s order should be reversed.
