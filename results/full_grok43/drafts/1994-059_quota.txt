The Qualifications Clauses fix the exclusive criteria for congressional service. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; art. I, § 3, cl. 3. States lack authority to supplement them. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 547 (1969) (“the Constitution fixes the qualifications”). Amendment 73’s ballot-access bar for multi-term members therefore adds an impermissible qualification. See id. at 522.

The Framers rejected state-by-state variation to ensure national uniformity. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 431 (1819) (states may not “retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control” federal operations). Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1964), confirms that congressional qualifications derive solely from the federal charter. Allowing Arkansas to impose term limits would produce the “hodge-podge” of qualifications the Constitution was designed to prevent.

Ballot-access measures that effectively bar candidates are subject to the same scrutiny. Williams v. Rhodes, 393 U.S. 23, 30 (1968); Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, 788 (1983). Amendment 73’s restriction “places a substantial burden on the right to vote” and on associational freedoms without advancing a compelling state interest tied to the enumerated qualifications. Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724, 736 (1974); Tashjian v. Republican Party of Connecticut, 479 U.S. 208, 217 (1986).

Even under the more flexible Burdick framework, the state’s interest in “entrenched incumbency” cannot justify altering federal qualifications. Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428, 434 (1992). Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 555 (1964), underscores that state electoral rules must yield to federal constitutional commands. Because Section 3 is nonseverable from the remainder of Amendment 73, the entire measure fails. See Powell, 395 U.S. at 550.

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
