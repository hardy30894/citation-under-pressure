ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews de novo a district court's dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, including a determination that the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") bars suit. See Aquamar, S.A. v. Del Monte Fresh Produce N.A., Inc., 179 F.3d 1279, 1290 (11th Cir. 1999) (subject matter jurisdiction dismissals reviewed de novo). Because the applicability of an FSIA exception presents a question of law, no deference is owed to the district court's ruling.

II. The Commercial Activity Exception to Sovereign Immunity Applies

The FSIA embodies the "restrictive" theory of sovereign immunity, under which a foreign state is immune from suit for its public, sovereign acts but not for the acts it undertakes as a market participant. Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 461 U.S. 480, 486-88 (1983) (describing adoption of the restrictive theory). Congress codified that distinction in 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), which withdraws immunity for any action "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state," or upon an act performed elsewhere in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state that "causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

A. Punjab's Land-Exchange Negotiation Was Commercial Activity Carried On in the United States

A "commercial activity" is "either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act," and "[t]he commercial character of an activity shall be determined by reference to the nature of the course of conduct or particular transaction or act, rather than by reference to its purpose." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The Supreme Court has instructed that "the question is not whether the foreign government is acting with a profit motive or instead with the aim of fulfilling uniquely sovereign objectives; rather, the issue is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in 'trade and traffic or commerce.'" Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992).

The record establishes that the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States who negotiated and offered Beg a substitute parcel of land in exchange for the property taken from him. Negotiating an exchange of real property is precisely the sort of transaction "by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce." Id. A private landowner routinely offers, negotiates, and conveys substitute parcels to settle a property dispute; nothing about that conduct is uniquely sovereign. Because this negotiation took place on American soil, it constitutes "commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" within the first clause of § 1605(a)(2). No showing of "direct effect" is required where, as here, the commercial activity itself occurred within the United States. See 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2); Weltover, 504 U.S. at 611.

B. In the Alternative, the Deprivation of Beg's Property Caused a Direct Effect in the United States

Even if this Court concludes that the relevant commercial activity occurred abroad, jurisdiction remains proper because Pakistan's disposition of the seized parcel—transferring it for military housing rather than compensating Beg—caused a direct effect within the United States. An effect is "direct" if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's ... activity." Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618 (quoting Texas Trading & Milling Corp. v. Federal Republic of Nigeria, 647 F.2d 300, 312 (2d Cir. 1981)). In Weltover, the Court found a direct effect where Argentina's unilateral refinancing of bonds meant that money "which was supposed to have been delivered" to a New York bank "was not forthcoming." 504 U.S. at 619. The same logic applies here: because Beg is owed compensation that has never been paid, and because the immediate consequence of Pakistan's conduct is a financial loss suffered by Beg, the effect of the foreign state's conduct is felt directly in the United States, satisfying the third clause of § 1605(a)(2).

III. The District Court's Contrary Analysis Cannot Be Reconciled with the Nature-of-the-Act Test

The district court's conclusion that "Pakistan had not engaged in any commercial activity" overlooks that immunity turns on the nature, not the sovereign purpose, of the challenged conduct. Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614. Whatever governmental motive underlay the original taking, Punjab's subsequent offer of substitute land—negotiated in this country—was conduct "of the same character as private trade" and thus commercial for FSIA purposes. See id.; 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The district court's immunity ruling should therefore be reversed and the case remanded for adjudication on the merits.
