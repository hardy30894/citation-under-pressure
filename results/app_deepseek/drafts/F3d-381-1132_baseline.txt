**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Federal Claims committed reversible error by granting summary judgment in favor of the government. The court incorrectly held that the scope of an easement is irrelevant to a physical takings analysis and that no physical taking occurred because the government’s fiber optic cables caused no additional physical intrusion to Tuthill Ranch, Inc.’s (“Tuthill”) land. This decision conflicts with bedrock principles of Fifth Amendment takings jurisprudence and must be reversed.

**I. The Lower Court Erred in Holding That the Scope of the Easement Is Irrelevant to Whether a Physical Taking Occurred**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits the government from taking private property for public use without just compensation. A permanent physical occupation of property is a *per se* taking, regardless of the public benefit served or the economic impact on the owner. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 434-35 (1982). When the government holds an easement, its rights are strictly limited to the terms and purposes of that easement. *See Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 176-80 (1979) (holding that the government’s use of a pond beyond the scope of a navigational servitude constituted a taking because it interfered with the owner’s right to exclude). An easement does not grant the government a blank check to use the land in any manner it chooses; rather, the “scope of the easement” *defines* the limits of authorized physical presence. Any use that exceeds those limits is a new, unauthorized invasion of the owner’s property—a physical taking.

The lower court incorrectly severed the scope inquiry from the takings analysis, stating that “the scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking.” That statement is legally erroneous. When the government installs physical structures that exceed the scope of its easement, the resulting physical occupation is a taking. *See, e.g., Hendler v. United States*, 952 F.2d 1364, 1376-77 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (holding that the government’s installation of monitoring wells beyond the scope of an access agreement constituted a physical taking). Here, the 1953 easement authorized “signal lines” necessary for the operation of power transmission. The government’s installation of fiber optic cables with capacity far exceeding its own needs, and its subsequent leasing of unused fibers to third-party telecommunications companies, is a use plainly outside that scope.

**II. The Government’s Installation of Excess Capacity Fiber Optic Cables and Leasing of Unused Fibers Constitutes a Permanent Physical Occupation**

It is undisputed that the government installed fiber optic cables with 36 or 72 fibers, while its current needs require only a fraction. It is also undisputed that the government leases the excess capacity to a for-profit telecommunications company. This is not a permissible “natural upgrade” of signal lines; it is a commercial exploitation of Tuthill’s property for purposes wholly unrelated to the original easement. The physical presence of the cables, combined with the government’s commercial use, constitutes a permanent physical occupation beyond the easement’s grant.

In *Loretto*, the Supreme Court held that even a minor physical occupation—a cable box affixed to an apartment building—was a *per se* taking. 458 U.S. at 434-35. The Court reasoned that “the character of the invasion is such that, without compensation, the owner’s right to exclude others from the property has been ‘taken in a most essential and fundamental respect.’” *Id.* at 435 (quoting *Kaiser Aetna*, 444 U.S. at 176). Here, the fiber optic cables are permanently affixed to Tuthill’s land. The government’s leasing of fibers to third parties is an exercise of a right—the right to use the property for profit—that the easement never granted. This use is a physical invasion of Tuthill’s property interest because it interferes with Tuthill’s right to exclude others from its land. *Kaiser Aetna*, 444 U.S. at 176 (the right to exclude is “one of the most essential sticks in the bundle of rights that are commonly characterized as property”).

**III. The Lower Court’s “No Additional Physical Intrusion” Analysis Is Legally Inadequate**

The lower court reasoned that “no additional physical intrusion to [Tuthill’s] land has resulted from the lease to third parties.” This analysis conflates *physical intrusion* with *legal scope*. A physical intrusion that is authorized by an easement is not a taking; a physical intrusion that exceeds the easement’s scope is a taking. The court’s focus on whether the cables physically occupied *more* land is misplaced. The relevant question is whether the government’s current use is authorized by the easement. If not, then the government’s physical presence on Tuthill’s land is uncompensated and constitutes a taking. The fact that the cables are the same size as those installed originally is irrelevant; the *use* has changed from an authorized signal line to an unauthorized commercial telecommunications facility.

The government overbuilt the system to accommodate projected future needs and then leased the excess. This is a deliberate decision to use Tuthill’s property for a purpose not contemplated by the original easement. As this Court recognized in *Adams v. United States*, 391 F.3d 1212, 1221 (Fed. Cir. 2004), a physical taking occurs when the government “occupies the land in a manner that is not consistent with the easement’s terms.” Here, the government’s use—commercial leasing of excess capacity—is manifestly inconsistent with the easement’s terms, which limit signal lines to those “necessary” for power transmission.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court of Federal Claims erred as a matter of law. The government’s installation of excess capacity fiber optic cables and its leasing of unused fibers to third parties constitute a use beyond the scope of the 1953 easement, resulting in a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s property that is a *per se* taking under the Fifth Amendment. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of just compensation.
