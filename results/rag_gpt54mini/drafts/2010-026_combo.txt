The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 cannot be squared with FOIA’s text or this Court’s instruction that exemptions be “narrowly construed.” Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976); U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). Exemption 2 covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). Congress used a limiting phrase, not a broad safety exception. Under the statute, the Navy had to show that the requested ESQD materials are in fact internal personnel rules or practices. They are not.

This Court has never held that agency planning or safety data may be withheld merely because disclosure might make agency operations easier to thwart. In Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, the Court explained that Exemption 2 reaches routine internal matters such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave,” 425 U.S. at 363, 369, but stressed that FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” Id. at 360-61. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” rule impermissibly detaches the exemption from the statutory words “personnel rules and practices” and turns Exemption 2 into a free-floating operational-security exemption. That is not what Congress enacted.

Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), does not support the judgment below. Hardy held that law-enforcement materials, if disclosure may risk circumvention, may fall within Exemption 2. Id. at 656. But Hardy did not hold that all sensitive agency records are exempt, nor did it authorize courts to dispense with the threshold requirement that the records concern internal personnel rules and practices. The D.C. Circuit later expanded the doctrine in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), but that construction is not compelled by the statute or by this Court’s precedent. See Rose, 425 U.S. at 369. The Ninth Circuit erred by converting Crooker’s gloss into a rule that displaces the statute’s own language.

The better reading is confirmed by this Court’s insistence that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. at 151 (quoting Adm’r FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). If the Navy believes disclosure would endanger security, FOIA already contains other provisions that may apply in proper cases, including Exemption 1 for classified material and Exemption 7 where law-enforcement requirements are met. 5 U.S.C. §§ 552(b)(1), (7). Courts may not enlarge Exemption 2 to do work that Congress assigned elsewhere. See Rose, 425 U.S. at 361.

The Navy’s reliance on “risk of circumvention” is especially misplaced here because the ESQD documents are not operational instructions or personnel rules at all. They are planning and safety materials describing the effects of explosions and the distances at which explosives may be stored. Information of that kind is not “personnel rules and practices” within the ordinary meaning of the statute. Compare United States v. MacDaniel, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 1 (1833), with United States v. Mouat, 124 U.S. 303 (1888), and United States v. Hendee, 124 U.S. 309 (1888), which reflect that statutory terms governing naval administration are to be applied according to their own limits, not expanded by analogy to whatever a department deems useful. See also Hannum v. United States, 226 U.S. 436 (1913); Fogarty v. United States, 340 U.S. 8 (1950); United States v. Alger, 152 U.S. 384 (1894). The same principle applies here: a court may not rewrite “internal personnel rules and practices” into “any record that helps the Navy plan safely.”

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the well-settled presumption that agency disclosure obligations are controlled by statute, not by internal preference. United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen, 340 U.S. 462 (1951), and Cole v. Young, 351 U.S. 536 (1956), confirm that agency regulations and personnel measures must operate within the bounds Congress set. Here, Congress set a narrow Exemption 2. The Navy’s desire to withhold ESQD maps because disclosure might help outsiders infer sensitive information does not satisfy that narrow text.

Finally, Northern Pacific Railway Co. v. United States, 330 U.S. 248 (1947), underscores that courts look to the actual statutory basis for governmental action, not to generalized claims of convenience or sensitivity. The Navy had to identify a valid FOIA exemption, and Exemption 2 is not one for non-personnel safety data. The judgment below should be reversed.
