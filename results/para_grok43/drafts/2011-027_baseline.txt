The equal footing doctrine vests a State with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of statehood. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 228-29 (1845). Navigability for title is determined by whether the river "was used, or was susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce" at that historic moment. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). This inquiry focuses on the river's condition and commercial utility on the date the State entered the Union. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894).

The same standard applies to discrete segments of a river. When evidence shows that a particular stretch was navigable at statehood, title to its bed passes to the State; stretches that were not navigable remain subject to federal disposition. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 77, 82-83 (1931). The trial court therefore properly directed its attention to the stretches underlying PPL's facilities and to the historical record of their use or susceptibility for trade at the moment Montana became a State in 1889. That segment-specific, statehood-date analysis satisfies the constitutional test.

PPL's contrary contention—that navigability may be assessed without regard to the precise reaches occupied or to conditions existing in 1889—finds no support in the governing decisions. The Daniel Ball and Utah both require contemporaneous evidence tied to the relevant waterway and the date of statehood. Because the District Court applied precisely that standard, its conclusion that the beds in question were navigable at statehood is consistent with controlling precedent.
