**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erroneously Admitted Highly Prejudicial Opinion Testimony That Vouched for Appellees’ Credibility, Expressed Legal Conclusions, and Interpreted Evidence, Requiring Reversal.**

A witness may not opine on the credibility of another witness, as such vouching “invades the province of the jury.” *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18 (1985); *see also United States v. Perez*, 144 F.3d 204, 210 (2d Cir. 1998). At trial, two Assistant District Attorneys and Lieutenant Gorman repeatedly testified that Officers Ramos and Rivera were truthful and had no motive to lie. Trial Tr. at 245–48, 312–14. This directly bolstered Appellees’ account, depriving Appellants of a fair assessment by the jury.

Moreover, witnesses may not testify to legal conclusions such as the existence of probable cause. *United States v. Bilzerian*, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991). The ADAs explicitly stated that probable cause existed to arrest Appellants and to charge Cameron. Tr. at 267–70. Such testimony usurps the jury’s role to decide ultimate issues. *See Hynes v. Coughlin*, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996) (holding legal conclusions inadmissible).

Finally, lay witnesses may not interpret evidence for the jury when jurors can draw their own inferences. *United States v. Kaplan*, 490 F.3d 171, 179 (2d Cir. 2007). Here, the lieutenant and ADAs commented that photographs corroborated Appellees’ version and undercut Appellants’ defense. Tr. at 290–93. This narrative testimony impermissibly guided the jury’s analysis.

These errors were not harmless. Under *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946), an error is harmful if it had substantial influence on the verdict. The admitted testimony directly addressed the two core disputes: Appellees’ credibility and the existence of probable cause. Without this improper bolstering, the jury could have rejected Appellees’ account. A new trial is required.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Judgment as a Matter of Law.**

Appellants are entitled to judgment as a matter of law under Fed. R. Civ. P. 50 because the security-camera photographs “blatantly contradict” Appellees’ version, leaving no reasonable jury to accept it. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000). The photographs show Cameron’s hand never entered the squad car, directly refuting the sole basis for arrest. Appellees offered no contrary evidence. The court’s denial was error.

**III. The District Court Erred in Its Jury Instructions.**

**A. The Court Should Have Instructed That Obstructing Governmental Administration Requires a Lawful Arrest.**

Under N.Y. Penal Law § 195.05, an officer must be engaged in an “authorized” act. *People v. Lupinacci*, 191 A.D.2d 579, 580 (2d Dep’t 1993). Because Appellants’ defense was that the arrest was unlawful, the jury required an instruction that a lawful arrest is an element. The court’s refusal was error. *See United States v. Nersesian*, 824 F.2d 1294, 1323 (2d Cir. 1987).

**B. The Court Should Have Given a Punitive Damages Instruction.**

Punitive damages are available under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for malicious conduct. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 51 (1983). Appellants presented evidence that Appellees fabricated the story, permitting an inference of malice. *See Hudson v. New York City*, 195 A.D.2d 319, 320 (1st Dep’t 1993). The court’s refusal to instruct on punitive damages was therefore erroneous.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial with proper instructions.
