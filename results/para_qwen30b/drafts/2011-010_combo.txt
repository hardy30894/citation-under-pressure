**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1331 et seq., extends the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA) to employees injured in connection with operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The question before this Court is whether such coverage is limited to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf itself, or whether it extends to injuries occurring onshore when those injuries are the result of operations conducted on the Shelf. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that OCSLA coverage applies regardless of the situs of injury. The correct interpretation of § 1333(b) is that it requires both a *situs of operations* on the outer Continental Shelf and a *situs of injury* within the maritime domain—specifically, on the outer Continental Shelf or on navigable waters above it. This interpretation is compelled by the plain language of the statute, the settled construction of the LHWCA, and the well-established principle that maritime jurisdiction is governed by *situs*, not merely *status* or *causation*.

**I. The OCSLA’s extension of the LHWCA is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf or on navigable waters above it.**

The LHWCA provides compensation for injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The OCSLA does not expand this coverage beyond the maritime situs; rather, it *incorporates* the LHWCA’s provisions *for injuries occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf*. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not eliminate the requirement that the injury itself occur in a maritime location. To hold otherwise would transform the LHWCA’s situs requirement into a mere causation test, which would eviscerate the foundational principle that maritime law is governed by *where* the injury occurs, not merely *why*.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), where the Court held that the LHWCA applies only to injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” Id. at 304. The Court emphasized that the statute’s coverage is “limited to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States,” and that “the situs of the injury is the controlling factor.” Id. at 305. The Court further rejected the argument that an injury could be deemed maritime simply because it was caused by a maritime operation. Id. at 306. The same principle applies here: an injury occurring on land, even if caused by offshore operations, does not fall within the LHWCA’s maritime situs unless the injury itself occurs on navigable waters or on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which permits coverage for injuries occurring onshore, contradicts this settled principle. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, the Court rejected a similar argument that an injury to a worker on a dock was covered because it was caused by a vessel’s operation. Id. at 305–306. The Court held that the injury must occur *on* the navigable waters, not merely *because of* a maritime activity. The same logic applies to the outer Continental Shelf. The OCSLA does not create a new, expansive maritime jurisdiction based on causation alone.

**II. The OCSLA’s situs requirement is not altered by the phrase “occurring as the result of.”**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. That decision, rendered after 1970, is not binding and is contrary to the pre-1970 precedent. More importantly, the Third Circuit’s interpretation of § 1333(b) as a “but for” test ignores the statutory language and the well-established maritime doctrine that *situs* governs jurisdiction.

The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not eliminate the need for a maritime situs. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, the Court held that the LHWCA’s coverage is limited to injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States,” and that the “situs of the injury is the controlling factor.” 332 U.S. at 305. The Court rejected the argument that an injury could be deemed maritime simply because it was caused by a maritime operation. Id. at 306. The same principle applies here: an injury occurring on land, even if caused by offshore operations, does not fall within the LHWCA’s maritime situs unless the injury itself occurs on navigable waters or on the outer Continental Shelf.

**III. The Outer Continental Shelf is a maritime domain, and injuries on land are not within its jurisdiction.**

The outer Continental Shelf is defined as “all submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters.” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Supreme Court has held that the outer Continental Shelf is part of the maritime domain. In *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), the Court stated that “the outer Continental Shelf is a part of the seabed and subsoil of the United States, and is subject to the jurisdiction of the United States.” Id. at 25. The Court further held that the outer Continental Shelf is “not within the territorial limits of any State,” and that “the United States has exclusive jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf.” Id. at 26. This jurisdiction includes the right to regulate activities on the Shelf, including the employment of workers.

However, the Court has also held that the outer Continental Shelf is not coextensive with the entire maritime domain. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, the Court held that the LHWCA applies only to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States. 332 U.S. at 305. The Court emphasized that the situs of the injury is the controlling factor. Id. at 305. The same principle applies to the outer Continental Shelf: injuries occurring on land are not within the jurisdiction of the outer Continental Shelf, even if they are caused by operations on the Shelf.
