**ARGUMENT**

**I. Arkansas may not add qualifications for Members of Congress beyond those fixed in the Constitution.**

The Constitution sets the exclusive qualifications for service in Congress. Article I provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state elected from at the time of election, U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2, while a Senator must be at least thirty, nine years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the state elected from at the time of election, U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. The question is whether a State may supplement those qualifications with term limits. It may not.

In *Powell v. McCormack*, the Court held that the textual qualifications in Article I are exclusive and that the House could not exclude a duly elected Member who met them. 395 U.S. 486, 522-48 (1969). The Court explained that the Framers “set the qualifications for membership in the House” and that the House lacked authority to “add to the qualifications set forth in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 522, 550. That principle controls here. If the House itself cannot add to Article I’s qualifications, a State certainly cannot do so indirectly by barring otherwise qualified candidates from the ballot. See *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 827-35 (1995).

The State’s ballot-access label does not change the constitutional character of the restriction. In *Cook v. Gralike*, the Court invalidated Missouri’s attempt to mark ballot labels for candidates who declined to support term limits, holding that a State may not use its election machinery to “dictate electoral outcomes” or “disfavor a class of candidates.” 531 U.S. 510, 523-24 (2001). The Court emphasized that the Elections Clause does not authorize measures that regulate the “manner” of elections only incidentally but instead operate to influence the people’s choice among candidates. *Id.* at 523. Arkansas’s Amendment 73 likewise functions to disqualify a class of candidates based on prior service and therefore exceeds any state power over election mechanics.

This Court’s precedents also confirm that the qualifications for federal office are fixed by the Constitution and are not subject to state alteration. In *Sturgis v. Crowley*, the Court recognized that states may regulate the time, place, and manner of elections, but not impose substantive qualifications on federal officeholders. 279 U.S. 368, 372-73 (1929). And in *Smiley v. Holm*, the Court distinguished permissible procedural regulation from measures that trench on substantive constitutional structure. 285 U.S. 355, 366-67 (1932). Amendment 73 does precisely what those decisions forbid: it makes prior service a disqualification for future congressional candidacy.

The State cannot avoid this result by arguing that the Amendment merely limits ballot access rather than eligibility. A law that prevents a person who satisfies Article I from being elected is a qualification in functional effect. In *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, the Court held that even ostensibly procedural election laws burden First and Fourteenth Amendment rights when they restrict voters’ and candidates’ political participation. 460 U.S. 780, 788-94 (1983). Here, the burden is far more direct: the Amendment categorically bars certain otherwise eligible candidates from appearing on the ballot.

**II. The Arkansas term-limit provisions for state offices are severable and should be sustained.**

Even if Section 3 is unconstitutional, that does not require invalidating the Amendment’s separate limits on state officials. Severability depends on whether the valid portions can stand independently and whether the voters would have adopted them without the invalid part. *Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock*, 480 U.S. 678, 684-86 (1987); *Dorchy v. Kansas*, 264 U.S. 286, 290 (1924). A severability clause creates a presumption that the valid portions are intended to survive. *Alaska Airlines*, 480 U.S. at 686.

That presumption is especially strong here because Sections 1 and 2 regulate Arkansas executive and legislative offices only, while Section 3 concerns federal offices. These are distinct subjects, and nothing in the text makes state term limits dependent on the federal candidacy restrictions. The Arkansas Constitution’s severability clause confirms that the voters intended the valid portions to remain operative. See *Ayotte v. Planned Parenthood of Northern New England*, 546 U.S. 320, 328-30 (2006). When a constitutional or statutory provision is partially invalid, courts should invalidate no more than necessary. *Id.* at 328.

The respondent’s position is also consistent with *New York v. United States*, where the Court emphasized that courts should preserve the valid portions of a law when they are fully operative standing alone. 505 U.S. 144, 186-87 (1992). Because the state-office term limits are complete, independent, and constitutionally unobjectionable, they should be sustained even if Section 3 falls.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court should hold that Arkansas may not impose additional qualifications for Congress beyond those in Article I, but it should also hold that the invalidity of Section 3 does not undermine the independent term limits for Arkansas state offices.
