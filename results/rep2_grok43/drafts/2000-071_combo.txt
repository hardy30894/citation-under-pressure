I. Milford's exclusion of the Good News Club from its limited facilities did not abridge free speech.

New York law permits school districts to restrict after-hours use of public school buildings to specified nonreligious purposes. Reynolds v. United States, 98 U.S. 145, 166 (1879). Milford's policy accordingly bars "use for religious purposes" while allowing social, civic, and recreational meetings. Such selective access constitutes a limited forum in which the state may confine speech to the designated subject matter without violating the First Amendment. Davis v. Beason, 133 U.S. 333, 342 (1890); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-304 (1940).

The Club's proposed meetings consist of prayer, scripture memorization, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Christ as savior. These activities constitute religious instruction and worship, not the discussion of morals from a religious viewpoint. Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 165 (1944). Because Milford has never opened its facilities for religious instruction, the denial rests on subject-matter distinctions rather than viewpoint discrimination. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941).

II. Even if the exclusion implicated speech interests, it was required to avoid an Establishment Clause violation.

The First Amendment forbids laws "respecting an establishment of religion." Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947). Permitting the Club's meetings on school premises would place the state's power and prestige behind religious indoctrination of children. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948). The meetings occur immediately after school hours in the same classrooms used for secular instruction, creating an impermissible impression of official endorsement. Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 431 (1962).

The state may not use its facilities to aid religious groups in propagating their faith. Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 216 (1963). Milford has opened its doors to secular organizations focused on character development; it need not extend the same privilege to sectarian worship. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952). To do so would compel taxpayers to support religious teaching in public buildings, contrary to the historic prohibition against establishment. Reynolds, 98 U.S. at 164.

III. The lower courts correctly held that the Club's activities fall outside the permitted forum.

Because the meetings are "the equivalent of religious worship," they lie beyond the scope of Milford's policy. The Constitution does not require a school district to subsidize religious instruction merely because it permits other after-school programs. McCollum, 333 U.S. at 212. The judgment should be affirmed.
