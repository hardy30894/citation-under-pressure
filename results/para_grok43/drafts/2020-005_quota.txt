**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits specific personal jurisdiction over Ford because the claims “arise out of or relate to” Ford’s purposeful contacts with Montana. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945). Ford has continuously advertised, sold, and serviced vehicles and replacement parts in Montana for decades, thereby availing itself of the privilege of conducting business there. World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980); Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985).

Specific jurisdiction requires only that the suit “relate to” the defendant’s in-state activities; causation is not required. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this standard when it held that Ford’s extensive Montana marketing and parts distribution created the necessary relationship with the design-defect and failure-to-warn claims arising from a vehicle crash on a Montana highway.

Ford’s reliance on cases requiring strict causation misreads precedent. Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984), addressed only general jurisdiction. Daimler AG v. Bauman, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014), and Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011), likewise concerned general jurisdiction and did not disturb the “relate to” formulation for specific jurisdiction. Calder v. Jones, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984), and Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770, 776 (1984), confirm that a defendant’s in-state conduct need not be the proximate cause of the plaintiff’s injury so long as the conduct gives rise to the litigation.

Ford’s additional contacts—maintaining dealerships, supplying warranty parts, and advertising in Montana—supply the requisite affiliation. Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987). These activities make it reasonable for Ford to anticipate being haled into Montana courts when one of its vehicles injures a Montana resident. The judgment below should be affirmed.
