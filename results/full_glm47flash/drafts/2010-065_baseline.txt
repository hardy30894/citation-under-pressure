**Argument**

**I. A Private Citizen Lacks Standing to Assert a Tenth Amendment Challenge to a Federal Statute**

The Tenth Amendment reserves powers to the states and the people, but it does not create individual rights against the federal government. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 168 (1992). Consequently, a private citizen lacks standing to challenge a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Tenth Amendment is a structural limitation on federal power designed to protect state sovereignty, not a source of individual rights that allows a private party to assert a defense to a federal prosecution.

The Supreme Court has explicitly held that private parties lack standing to assert Tenth Amendment claims. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, the Court rejected a challenge to the federal government’s operation of the Tennessee Valley Authority on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Court held that the utility companies, as private entities, had no standing to raise the issue. The Court stated that "the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). This holding is grounded in the understanding that the Tenth Amendment is a shield for state sovereignty, not a sword for private citizens to use against the federal government.

This principle is well established in the lower courts. The First Circuit has explained that "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state’s policy of acquiescence." *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 35 (1st Cir. 2005). Here, the State of Pennsylvania has not refused to prosecute; it has allowed the federal government to act under its treaty obligations. Bond cannot claim a right to be prosecuted under state law that the state has not asserted. Because the Tenth Amendment is a limitation on federal power, not a grant of power to the individual, Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise this challenge.
