**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) commands that after coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings conclude, the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” remand each transferred action to the district from which it originated. That mandatory directive admits no exception. A transferee district court conducting pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a) may not invoke § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial, because doing so nullifies Congress’s explicit remand requirement and exceeds the limited authority conferred on transferee courts.

**I. Section 1407(a) Unambiguously Requires Remand After Pretrial Proceedings**

Congress chose the word “shall” to describe the Panel’s duty to remand. “The word ‘shall’ is ordinarily ‘the language of command.’” *Alabama v. Bozeman*, 533 U.S. 146, 153 (2001) (quoting *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947)). Section 1407(a) provides that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings to the district from which it was transferred.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This language leaves no room for a transferee court to circumvent the Panel’s exclusive remand authority by using § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial. “When the statute’s language is plain, the sole function of the courts—at least where the disposition required by the text is not absurd—is to enforce it according to its terms.” *Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A.*, 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000) (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, the statutory command is clear, and nothing in § 1407 suggests that a transferee court may short-circuit the remand process.

**II. The Transferee Court Lacks Authority Under § 1404(a) to Retain the Case for Trial**

Section 1404(a) is a general venue statute that permits a district court to transfer a civil action “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” But § 1407 is a specific statute governing multidistrict litigation. “Where there is no clear intention otherwise, a specific statute will not be controlled or nullified by a general one.” *Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 550-51 (1974). The specific remand requirement of § 1407(a) controls over the general transfer authority of § 1404(a). A transferee court’s use of § 1404(a) to assign a case to itself for trial effectively amends § 1407 by adding an exception Congress did not provide.

Moreover, the transferee court’s authority under § 1407 is strictly limited to “pretrial proceedings.” That term encompasses discovery and other matters preliminary to trial, but not the trial itself. *See, e.g., In re Patents*, 395 U.S. 199, 203 (1969) (per curiam) (noting that § 1407 “does not authorize the transfer of actions for trial”). A motion under § 1404(a) to change venue for trial is not a “pretrial proceeding” within the meaning of § 1407. The statute’s structure confirms this: the Panel alone decides whether to transfer for pretrial purposes, and it alone decides when to remand. Allowing a transferee judge to override that scheme by invoking § 1404(a) usurps the Panel’s statutory role.

**III. The Practice Sanctioned by Some Circuits and Panel Rule 14(b) Conflicts with Congressional Intent**

A handful of lower courts have permitted transferee courts to retain cases under § 1404(a), but those decisions rest on policy preferences rather than statutory text. In *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971), the court acknowledged that its holding was not supported by the language of § 1407 but justified it as a matter of convenience. Similarly, *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), relied on the same flawed reasoning. As Judge Kozinski observed in dissent below, “Seldom have so many been so consistently wrong.” *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, No. 95-70380, slip op. at 17 (9th Cir. July 21, 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). The Judicial Panel’s Rule 14(b), which purports to authorize this practice, cannot override an act of Congress. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 2072(b) (rules of procedure “shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right”).

Congress enacted § 1407 to balance the efficiency of consolidated pretrial proceedings against plaintiffs’ traditional right to choose the trial forum. *See H.R. Rep. No. 90-1130, at 3 (1968)*. The practice of self-transfer undermines that balance by forcing parties to trial in a distant district long after the common pretrial proceedings have ended. Here, Lexecon was haled from Illinois to Arizona for pretrial proceedings that never involved coordinated discovery with other parties. The district court then used § 1404(a) to keep the case for trial, effectively punishing Lexecon for complying with the Panel’s transfer order. That result is precisely what § 1407’s remand requirement was designed to prevent.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to order the district court to remand the action to the Northern District of Illinois pursuant to § 1407(a).
