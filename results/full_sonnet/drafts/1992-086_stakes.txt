# ARGUMENT

## I. THE SCHOOL DISTRICT CREATED A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM BY OPENING ITS FACILITIES TO SOCIAL, CIVIC, AND RECREATIONAL USES

The extent of a State's authority to regulate expressive activity on public property "depend[s] on whether the property is a public forum, a designated public forum, or a nonpublic forum." *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985). A State need not open its property to all speech, but when it "opens a nontraditional forum for public discourse," even on a limited basis, the property becomes a forum "created for a limited purpose such as use by certain groups . . . or for the discussion of certain subjects." *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 n.7 (1983).

Here, the School District permitted outside organizations to use its facilities after hours for a broad range of social, civic, and recreational purposes—including a lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics and a musical performance with religious themes. By its own policy and practice, the District thus created a limited public forum for community discourse on subjects including family life, values, and child-rearing. Having done so, the District "must respect the lawful boundaries it has itself set," *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267–68 (1981), and may not thereafter regulate access in a manner that offends the First Amendment.

## II. WITHIN A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM, THE GOVERNMENT MAY NOT DISCRIMINATE ON THE BASIS OF VIEWPOINT

Even where government reserves a forum for particular subjects, it may not thereafter discriminate among speakers based on the viewpoint they express on those subjects. This Court has repeatedly held that "the government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses on an otherwise includible subject." *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. Such viewpoint discrimination is forbidden not only in designated public fora but even in nonpublic fora, where governmental discretion is otherwise at its height. *Id.* This restriction reflects the bedrock principle that "above all else, the First Amendment means that government has no power to restrict expression because of its message, its ideas, its subject matter, or its content." *Police Dep't of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95 (1972).

*Widmar* is directly on point. There, a state university opened its facilities to student groups for meetings but excluded a religious student organization from using the same facilities for worship and religious discussion. This Court held that once the University created a forum generally open to student groups, its exclusion of religious speech was a content-based discrimination that could be sustained only if "necessary to serve a compelling state interest" and "narrowly drawn to achieve that end." 454 U.S. at 270 (quoting *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45). The University's proffered justification—the Establishment Clause—did not suffice, because "an open forum in a public university does not confer any imprimatur of state approval on religious sects or practices." *Id.* at 274.

## III. THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL'S FILM SERIES WAS VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION, NOT A PERMISSIBLE SUBJECT-MATTER LIMITATION

The District did not exclude Lamb's Chapel because the subject of family values and child-rearing fell outside the boundaries of its forum; to the contrary, the District permitted other groups to address such matters, and civic and community groups regularly used school facilities for programs on comparable topics. The District excluded Lamb's Chapel's film series solely because the *perspective* from which the subject was to be discussed was religious rather than secular. That is the precise evil this Court's forum doctrine forbids: "[e]xclusions from a limited public forum . . . must be viewpoint neutral," and a State may not permit discussion of a subject from every perspective except a religious one. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

The District's own correspondence confirms the point. Pastor Steigerwald was told that the film "does appear to be church related and therefore your request must be refused"—not because the subject matter of family life was foreclosed to community groups, but because the proposed presentation would address that subject from a religious point of view. A rule excluding only religious viewpoints on an otherwise permissible topic is viewpoint discrimination in its clearest form, no different in kind from a rule permitting discussion of labor issues only from a management perspective, or discussion of abortion only from one side. See *Mosley*, 408 U.S. at 95–96.

## IV. THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE DOES NOT JUSTIFY THE DISTRICT'S VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION

The District cannot invoke the Establishment Clause to excuse its discrimination. As this Court explained in *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), "there is a crucial difference between government speech endorsing religion, which the Establishment Clause forbids, and private speech endorsing religion, which the Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses protect." *Id.* at 250. Permitting Lamb's Chapel to use school facilities on the same terms as other community organizations, after school hours and open to the public, would not signal District endorsement of religion; it would simply treat religious speech neutrally alongside other speech on family and social issues, exactly as *Widmar* required with respect to student religious groups. 454 U.S. at 274–75.

## CONCLUSION

Because the District's Rule 7 discriminates against religious viewpoints within a forum otherwise open to discussion of family and social issues, it violates the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment. The judgment below should be reversed.
