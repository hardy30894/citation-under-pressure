**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 18 U.S.C. § 1951 IS DOUBTFUL, AND THE COURT SHOULD RECONSIDER ITS PRECEDENT IN LIGHT OF THE LIMITS ON CONGRESSIONAL POWER UNDER THE COMMERCE CLAUSE**

The government’s reliance on 18 U.S.C. § 1951—the Hobbs Act—as a basis for Mr. Pearson’s conviction rests upon a foundation that has long been eroding under the Supreme Court’s evolving interpretation of Congress’s authority under the Commerce Clause. While this Court has previously upheld the Hobbs Act as a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate interstate commerce, *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), and *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598 (2000), have made clear that Congress’s power is not plenary. The Hobbs Act, which criminalizes robbery or extortion affecting commerce, must be evaluated in light of these precedents, which demand a meaningful connection between the criminal conduct and interstate commerce.

The Hobbs Act, as applied here, criminalizes a robbery that occurred in Wichita, Kansas, and resulted in the death of a local employee. The government’s sole justification for jurisdiction is that Mr. Goodcents, a local restaurant, purchases goods from out-of-state suppliers. This tenuous link—based on the fact that the business “suffers” after a robbery and that some goods were produced outside Kansas—does not satisfy the constitutional threshold established in *Lopez*. There, the Court held that “the possession of a firearm in a local school zone” was not a commercial activity and did not substantially affect interstate commerce. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561. The Court emphasized that “Congress may regulate only those activities having a substantial relation to interstate commerce,” id. at 559, and that “the mere fact that an activity is economic in nature does not automatically bring it within Congress’s regulatory power,” id. at 560.

Here, the robbery was a local, violent crime with no direct connection to the movement of goods across state lines. The government does not allege that the stolen money was used to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers, nor that the robbery disrupted any interstate supply chain. The mere fact that Mr. Goodcents buys some goods from outside Kansas does not transform a local robbery into a matter of interstate commerce. As the Court stated in *Morrison*, “Congress may not convert a noncommercial activity into a commercial one simply by labeling it as such.” *Morrison*, 529 U.S. at 613. The government’s argument reduces the Commerce Clause to a “general police power,” which the Constitution does not grant to Congress. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561.

Moreover, the Hobbs Act’s application in this case—where the only interstate nexus is the purchase of goods by a local business—creates a risk of federal overreach that the Court has repeatedly warned against. In *Lopez*, the Court cautioned that “if we were to accept the Government’s argument, we are hard pressed to posit any activity that Congress could not regulate under the Commerce Clause.” *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 564. The same danger exists here: if Congress may regulate a local robbery based on the remote possibility that the victim business purchases goods from out of state, then virtually any local crime could be federalized. This is not the limited federal power envisioned by the Constitution.

The Tenth Circuit has previously upheld the Hobbs Act in cases involving robberies of businesses with interstate connections. See *United States v. Hines*, 107 F.3d 1478 (10th Cir. 1997). However, those cases involved more direct links to interstate commerce—such as the use of stolen funds to purchase goods shipped across state lines or the operation of a business that regularly engaged in interstate transactions. This case lacks such a connection. The government’s theory rests on speculation and a strained interpretation of “affecting commerce.” That is insufficient.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “the Constitution limits the scope of federal power,” and “Congress may not regulate non-economic, local activity simply because it is part of a larger regulatory scheme.” *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561. The robbery of a local restaurant, even if it results in a death, is not a commercial activity and does not substantially affect interstate commerce. The Hobbs Act, as applied here, exceeds Congress’s authority under the Commerce Clause.

Accordingly, the conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1951 must be reversed. The Court should not permit the federal government to criminalize local, non-commercial crimes under the guise of regulating commerce. To do so would undermine the constitutional balance between federal and state authority and violate the principle that “the powers of the federal government are limited.” *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561.

For the foregoing reasons, the conviction under § 1951 must be vacated.
