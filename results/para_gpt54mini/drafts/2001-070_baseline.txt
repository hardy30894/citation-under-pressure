## ARGUMENT

### The Eighth Amendment does not forbid capital punishment for all offenders with intellectual disability.

Atkins asks this Court to do what the Eighth Amendment does not require: impose a categorical national exemption from the death penalty for a class of offenders that this Court has repeatedly left to the democratic process unless and until objective evidence shows a clear constitutional consensus. The governing inquiry is whether there is a national consensus against the practice and whether independent judgment confirms that capital punishment is inconsistent with the Eighth Amendment’s “evolving standards of decency.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1958); Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 331-35 (1989), abrogated on other grounds by Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002).

Under Penry, there was no basis for a categorical rule. The Court held that intellectual disability, standing alone, did not create an Eighth Amendment bar because the States had not reached a consensus and because individualized sentencing could account for diminished culpability. 492 U.S. at 335. That conclusion remains the proper starting point here unless the Court is persuaded that the relevant legal landscape has changed in a constitutionally dispositive way. Capital punishment is not unconstitutional merely because some offenders are less blameworthy than others; the Eighth Amendment leaves room for the States to calibrate culpability through aggravation, mitigation, and individualized sentencing. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 189-95 (1976); Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862, 879-80 (1983).

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly adhered to that framework. It relied on Penry and rejected the claim that intellectual disability itself categorically bars execution. That decision was faithful to the principle that “the clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values is the legislation enacted by the country’s legislatures.” Penry, 492 U.S. at 331. Here, the relevant evidence is, at most, mixed. The fact that some States have chosen to limit capital punishment for certain offenders does not establish a constitutional command binding on all States. See Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 373-79 (1989), overruled on other grounds by Roper v. Simmons, 543 U.S. 551 (2005). Nor does a developing legislative trend by itself compel a categorical constitutional rule where substantial state disagreement remains. Id. at 377-78.

The Court should also be wary of displacing the jury’s traditional role in capital sentencing. The Constitution has long trusted juries to consider mitigation, including mental impairment, and to determine whether death is warranted in a particular case. Gregg, 428 U.S. at 189-95; Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604-05 (1978). That individualized approach is especially appropriate where the record does not show that the offender lacks all understanding, but only that he claims diminished intellectual functioning. In such cases, the sentencer may consider intelligence deficits, developmental history, and adaptive behavior without erecting a rigid constitutional exemption. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 328-30.

Moreover, the State has compelling interests that Penry recognized but did not eliminate: retribution and deterrence. See Gregg, 428 U.S. at 183-87. Those interests are not categorically absent whenever an offender has intellectual disability. The Constitution permits the States to conclude that, in some cases, such offenders may still possess sufficient moral culpability to justify capital punishment, especially when the crime is deliberate, brutal, or calculated. See Tison v. Arizona, 481 U.S. 137, 157-58 (1987). The Eighth Amendment does not mandate a per se rule that all such offenders are beyond the reach of the death penalty.

Atkins’ best argument is that intellectual disability may diminish blameworthiness and the reliability of sentencing. But that is precisely why individualized capital sentencing exists. Lockett, 438 U.S. at 604-05. A defendant may present expert testimony, family history, and evidence of adaptive deficits; the sentencer may weigh that evidence against the facts of the crime. The Constitution requires consideration of mitigation, not categorical immunity. Id.; Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 113-15 (1982). Because Virginia’s law permits that individualized assessment, it comports with the Eighth Amendment.

Finally, this Court should resist converting a debatable policy judgment into constitutional doctrine. The Eighth Amendment is not a license for the judiciary to impose its own moral view absent a clear national consensus and a convincing constitutional rationale. Penry, 492 U.S. at 331-35. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly concluded that no such categorical rule existed. Its judgment should be affirmed.
