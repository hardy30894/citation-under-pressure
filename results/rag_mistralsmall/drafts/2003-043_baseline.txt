**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS Must Assess Each Individual Partner Separately to Extend the Statute of Limitations Against Them**

The plain text of the Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess tax liabilities against each individual taxpayer within three years of the return’s filing to extend the limitations period for collection. *See* 26 U.S.C. §§ 6501(a), 6502(a). A "taxpayer" is defined to include "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not constitute an assessment against the individual partners, as they are distinct taxpayers under the Code. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (holding that an assessment must identify the taxpayer to be valid); *see also* 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (requiring identification of the taxpayer in an assessment).

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the court merely held that a partner who paid his share of the partnership’s tax liability could not later seek a refund because he was liable under state law. *Id.* at 910. The court did not address whether the IRS could collect the tax from the partner without an individual assessment or judgment. *Id.* at 910. The IRS’s argument thus conflates substantive liability under state law with the procedural requirement of an individual assessment under the Code.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Against the Individual Partners Expired Absent Individual Assessments**

Because the IRS did not assess the individual partners within three years of their returns, the statute of limitations for collection against them expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The IRS’s argument that the partnership assessment extended the limitations period against the partners is unsupported by the Code. Section 6502(a) extends the limitations period only for the taxpayer against whom an assessment is made. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) ("Where the assessment of any tax ... has been made within the period of limitation ... such tax may be collected ... but only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun ... within 10 years after the assessment of the tax."). The IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period against the individual partners, as they were not the taxpayers assessed. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

**III. California Partnership Law Does Not Permit Collection Without a Judgment Against the Partners**

Under California law, a creditor may not collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); *see also* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001). The IRS has not obtained such a judgment against the individual partners, and the limitations period for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Thus, the IRS’s claims against the partners are unenforceable.

**IV. The Bankruptcy Code Does Not Permit the IRS’s Claims**

The Bankruptcy Code permits a creditor to file a claim only if the creditor has a "right to payment" that is not "unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor." 11 U.S.C. §§ 101(5)(A), 502(b)(1). The IRS’s claims against the individual partners are unenforceable because the IRS failed to assess the partners individually and did not obtain a judgment against them. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458. The bankruptcy court therefore properly disallowed the IRS’s claims.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period against the individual partners, and the IRS’s claims against the partners are unenforceable. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
