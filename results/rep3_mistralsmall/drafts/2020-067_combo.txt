**ARGUMENT**

**I. PLAIN ERROR REVIEW IS LIMITED TO THE RECORD BEFORE THE TRIAL COURT.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its plain-error analysis to the trial record. This Court has long held that appellate courts “cannot receive new evidence, but must decide on the record as it comes to them.” *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 256 (1940). The plain-error doctrine does not expand the scope of review beyond the record. *See United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (explaining that plain-error review applies to errors “affecting substantial rights” that were “plain” at the time of trial, which necessarily requires examination of the trial record).

This principle is consistent with the Court’s recognition that appellate courts are not fact-finding tribunals. *See Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) (“The settled rule of this court is that it will not consider any alleged error not distinctly raised and fairly presented for review in the court below.”). The Eleventh Circuit adhered to this rule by declining to consider matters outside the record—such as Greer’s prior convictions—to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982) (noting that plain-error review is “circumscribed” and does not permit supplementation of the record).

**II. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT PROPERLY APPLIED THE PLAIN-ERROR FRAMEWORK.**

Under *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), plain error requires a defendant to show (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) affecting substantial rights, and (4) seriously affecting the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this test. It first determined that the government’s failure to prove *Rehaif*’s knowledge requirement was error and plain. *See Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734 (“At a minimum, the error must be clear under current law.”).

The court then examined whether the error affected Greer’s substantial rights by considering whether it “had an unfair prejudicial impact on the jury’s deliberations.” *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 511 (1995) (quoting *United States v. Olry*, 43 F.3d 704, 707 (5th Cir. 1995)). The Eleventh Circuit did not err by looking to the trial record—including the jury instructions and evidence presented—to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 16 (1985) (“The plain-error doctrine is to be used sparingly, only in those circumstances in which a miscarriage of justice would otherwise result.”).

**III. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT DID NOT ABUSE ITS DISCRETION IN CONCLUDING THAT THE ERROR DID NOT AFFECT FAIRNESS, INTEGRITY, OR PUBLIC REPUTATION.**

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that the error did not undermine the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial is supported by this Court’s precedents. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62–63 (2002), the Court emphasized that the fourth prong of plain error requires a showing that the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” The Eleventh Circuit did not abuse its discretion in determining that Greer failed to meet this standard. *See United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 n.9 (2004) (explaining that appellate courts defer to the lower court’s assessment of the fourth prong).

The court’s reliance on the trial record to assess the impact of the error was consistent with this deferential standard. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (noting that plain-error review is “narrow” and “requires a case-specific inquiry into the particular error and its effect on the fairness of the proceeding”). The Eleventh Circuit did not err by declining to consider matters outside the record—such as Greer’s prior convictions—to assess the fourth prong. *See United States v. Turman*, 104 F.3d 1421, 1424 (9th Cir. 1997) (holding that the fourth prong requires an examination of the record).

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied plain-error review by confining its analysis to the trial record and assessing prejudice and the fourth prong based on the evidence and proceedings below. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
