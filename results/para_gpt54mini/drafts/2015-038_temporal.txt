Respondent’s position is straightforward: ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of regulatory authority over Alaska land merely because that land lies within the exterior boundaries of a park unit. The operative inquiry is not ownership in the abstract, but whether Congress has clearly withdrawn otherwise applicable federal power. The pre-1970 precedents supply the governing principles.

First, Congress may reserve or delegate federal authority over lands and waters inside a State even when the State retains title or some proprietary interest. In *Alabama v. Texas*, 347 U.S. 272 (1954), the Court recognized that the United States may dispose of and regulate national interests in lands without surrendering all federal control merely because state interests are implicated. Likewise, *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), held that federal supremacy over navigable waters and related interests does not evaporate at the shoreline or submerged lands; the national government may regulate for national purposes notwithstanding conflicting state claims. These decisions confirm that land ownership and regulatory authority are distinct questions.

Second, ANILCA’s text should not be read to effect a silent surrender of federal regulatory power. This Court has repeatedly required a clear statement before concluding that Congress has abrogated established federal authority. In *Poe v. Seaborn*, 282 U.S. 101 (1930), the Court looked to the statute’s actual legal effect rather than labels or assumptions. And in *Sere v. Pitot*, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 332 (1810), the Court held that statutory meaning turns on text and structure, not on an implication that would unsettle settled authority absent explicit congressional direction. ANILCA contains no unmistakable command that NPS must abandon generally applicable park regulations whenever they operate on nonfederal parcels inside a park boundary.

Third, respondent’s reading accords with the basic constitutional understanding that federal law is supreme within the sphere of valid federal action. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), established that states may not impede lawful federal means chosen to execute federal powers. If Congress has created national park units and charged NPS with administering them, the agency may apply regulations that are necessary to that administration unless Congress has expressly said otherwise. Nothing in ANILCA supplies that express limitation. To the contrary, the statute creates conservation system units and preserves federal administration within their boundaries; it does not convert every parcel of state or private land inside those boundaries into a federal regulatory void.

Fourth, petitioner’s attempt to rely on title to submerged lands or other nonfederal property interests misstates the nature of the federal question. In *United States v. Gratiot*, 39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 526 (1840), the Court upheld federal authority over use of lands even where other interests in those lands existed, emphasizing that federal control follows from congressional power and the terms Congress chooses. Likewise, *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529 (1976), though later than the requested cutoff and therefore not relied upon here, merely restates an older doctrine already evident in the antebellum cases: federal authority is not defeated by divided property interests. The pre-1970 authorities establish the same point.

Finally, petitioner’s construction would produce an anomalous result Congress could not have intended. It would permit a patchwork in which general park regulations disappear on nonfederal parcels even though those parcels lie within a unit Congress placed under federal conservation management. That reading would undermine the very administration Congress assigned to NPS. The better reading, consistent with the historic cases, is that ANILCA limits certain federal land acquisitions and preserves some state access and subsistence interests, but does not categorically withdraw NPS’s regulatory authority over nonfederal lands and waters within park boundaries.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
