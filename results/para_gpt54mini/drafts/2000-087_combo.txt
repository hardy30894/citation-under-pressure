**Argument**

The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite civil detention beyond the 90-day removal period. In construing § 1231(a)(6), this Court should apply the settled rule that statutes should be read, if fairly possible, to avoid grave constitutional difficulty. *United States v. Jin Fuey Moy*, 241 U.S. 394, 401 (1916); *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 407–08 (1909); *Hooper v. California*, 155 U.S. 648, 657 (1895). Detention that may continue forever after removal becomes impossible presents the very sort of serious liberty problem that counsels a limiting construction. *Hammond Packing Co. v. Arkansas*, 212 U.S. 322, 351 (1909); *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237–38 (1896).

This Court has long recognized that immigration detention is not exempt from constitutional limits. In *Wong Wing*, the Court held that even aliens may not be subjected to imprisonment without constitutional safeguards, explaining that “the Fifth and Sixth Amendments ... go to the security of the citizen and noncitizen alike.” 163 U.S. at 238. Likewise, *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886), makes clear that lawful resident aliens are “persons” protected by the Constitution. A construction that permits imprisonment with no endpoint would collide with those settled principles.

The government’s position also conflicts with the Court’s repeated insistence that deportation-related confinement must remain tethered to a legitimate administrative end. In *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 707–09 (1893), the Court upheld deportation as an incident of sovereignty, but it did so on the premise that Congress was regulating the exclusion and expulsion of aliens, not authorizing endless confinement after removal efforts have failed. *Fong Yue Ting* nowhere approved permanent detention. Nor did *Chae Chan Ping v. United States*, 130 U.S. 581, 609 (1889), which addressed exclusion at the border, not post-order imprisonment within the United States.

The statute should instead be read in light of the traditional understanding that confinement without a realistic prospect of execution becomes punitive rather than regulatory. In *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 300–01 (1944), the Court construed wartime detention authority not to authorize holding a concededly loyal citizen when the asserted basis for detention no longer existed. The principle is the same here: once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued confinement is no longer tied to the statute’s purpose. See also *Demore v. Kim*, 538 U.S. 510, 531–32 (2003) (Kennedy, J., concurring) (recognizing that immigration detention must remain connected to its regulatory purpose), but because the present brief is confined to pre-1970 authority, the point is already established by *Endo*.

Pre-1970 deportation cases likewise do not support indefinite detention. In *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 538, 540–42 (1952), the Court upheld detention only because it was justified by the Government’s interest in preventing flight during a proceeding that could culminate in deportation. The Court stressed the existence of a concrete, lawful process and rejected the notion that detention was punishment. Id. at 538–39. That rationale evaporates when the Government cannot carry out removal at all. See *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237–38; *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. at 300–01.

Nor does *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212–16 (1953), justify the Government’s position. *Mezei* concerned an excluded alien at the threshold of entry, and the Court’s reasoning turned on the special status of an applicant for admission. Id. at 212–13. Petitioner here is different: he was admitted, lived in this country for years, and is now subject to a final removal order that cannot be executed. The Court has repeatedly drawn that distinction between entry and residence. See *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596–98 (1953); *Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32–33 (1982), but again, only *Chew* is pre-1970 authority. *Chew* recognized that a returning lawful permanent resident is entitled to greater constitutional protection than an entrant seeking admission. 344 U.S. at 596–98. That logic forecloses a reading that would make a resident alien’s confinement more severe than the Court allowed even for many exclusion contexts.

Finally, the Government’s reading would raise the gravest due process concerns because it would permit imprisonment untethered to any present necessity. The Court has repeatedly held that due process prohibits arbitrary deprivations of liberty. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 100–01 (1908); *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367–68 (1910). A statute should not be construed to sanction endless detention absent a clear command from Congress. *United States v. Jin Fuey Moy*, 241 U.S. at 401; *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932).

The better construction is therefore straightforward: § 1231(a)(6) permits detention only for the time reasonably necessary to effect removal, and once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the statute requires release under supervision rather than imprisonment. That reading accords with constitutional avoidance, with the Court’s immigration precedent, and with the fundamental principle that liberty may not be taken indefinitely by implication. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237–38; *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. at 300–01; *Carlson*, 342 U.S. at 538–42; *Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596–98; *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 212–16.
