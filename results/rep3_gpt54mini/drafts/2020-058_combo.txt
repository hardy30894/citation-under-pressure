Petitioners are eligible to adjust status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255 because Congress has deemed TPS recipients to be “in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4), and that statutory command must be read in light of the settled rule that Congress does not use surplus words. E.g., Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115 (1879); Montclair v. Ramsdell, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883). The government’s contrary reading collapses the distinction between “admission” and “lawful status,” but this Court has long insisted that statutory terms must be given independent effect. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1869); Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 195 (1857).

Section 1255(a) requires that an alien have been “inspected and admitted or paroled,” while § 1254a(f)(4) says TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” Those are not the same thing. This Court has repeatedly recognized that words of entry and authorization denote a distinct event from a later conferral of legal condition. See Wright v. Roseberry, 121 U.S. 488, 509 (1887) (“the language used is to have effect, if possible, according to the plain and ordinary meaning”); Gould v. Gould, 245 U.S. 151, 153 (1917) (statutes are not enlarged beyond their text by “implication”); Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). If Congress had meant TPS to count as admission, it could have said so expressly, as it has in other contexts. Its decision not to do so is dispositive under the familiar canon that omissions are intentional. See United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820); Thompson v. Railroad Cos., 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 134, 137 (1867).

The structure of the statute confirms that reading. Section 1255 uses admission as a threshold event; lawful status appears separately as a continuing requirement or exception. Courts have long refused to treat separate statutory requirements as interchangeable. See United States v. Isham, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 496, 504 (1873); In re Babcock, 3 F. Cas. 924, 926 (C.C.D. Mass. 1812) (No. 692) (Story, J.) (rejecting constructions that “make one part of a statute contradict another”). Reading TPS as an admission would also nullify Congress’s careful choice of language in § 1254a(f)(4) and § 1255(a), contrary to the settled rule that courts must avoid interpretations that render statutory terms idle. See United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805); Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 174 (1803).

Nor does the remedial character of TPS authorize rewriting the statute. Even remedial enactments must be applied as written. See United States v. Hartwell, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 396 (1868); Schlemmer v. Buffalo, Rochester & Pittsburgh Ry. Co., 205 U.S. 1, 11 (1907). The Court should not infer from TPS’s humanitarian purpose a permanent change in immigration status that Congress did not enact. See Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459–60 (1892) (purpose cannot override text); Wisconsin Cent. R.R. v. United States, 164 U.S. 190, 211 (1896) (statutes are construed by “the language employed”).

Finally, the government’s position disregards the settled rule that jurisdictional or eligibility prerequisites must be met as written, not by equitable approximation. See R.S. v. Barber, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 102, 105 (1869); The Genesee Chief, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 443, 457 (1852). Petitioners were present in the United States without inspection and without admission. TPS gave them lawful status; it did not retroactively transform their unlawful entry into an “admission.” Because the statute says otherwise, petitioners satisfy § 1255 only if admission is read as Congress wrote it, not as the government wishes it had.
