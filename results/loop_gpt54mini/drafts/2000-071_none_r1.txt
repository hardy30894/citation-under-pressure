Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club violated the First Amendment because the school opened its facilities after hours to district residents for community use, yet singled out the Club for exclusion because its moral and character lessons were taught from a Christian viewpoint. When the government creates a forum for private speech, it may not “shut off access” to speakers based on the ideas they express. Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939). Nor may it suppress one side of a debate while permitting others to speak. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940).

The Club’s proposed meetings fell squarely within the subject matter Milford had already opened to others: instruction directed to the welfare of the community, including youth development, values, and character. The school permitted secular groups to meet for comparable purposes, but excluded the Club because its approach was religious. That is viewpoint discrimination, not legitimate subject-matter control. The Constitution does not allow government officials to decide that a topic such as morality may be discussed only from secular premises. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937). The State may not condition access to a forum on the abandonment of a speaker’s religious perspective. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04; Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943); Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944).

Milford’s characterization of the Club’s activities as “religious worship” does not save the exclusion. This Court has long protected the public expression of religious beliefs and the dissemination of religious literature as speech. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 110-11; Follett, 321 U.S. at 577-78. And the Court has held that discriminatory treatment of religious meetings is constitutionally suspect. Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951). Milford’s policy did not bar all expressive use of school property; it barred only religious expression on a subject otherwise open to secular speakers. That is precisely the kind of discrimination the First Amendment forbids. Hague, 307 U.S. at 515-16; Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642.

Nor can Milford justify its exclusion by invoking the Establishment Clause. That Clause prohibits the government from composing or endorsing religious exercises, but it does not authorize hostility toward private religious speech. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), made clear that the State must remain neutral between religion and nonreligion. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), reaffirmed that public authorities need not show “callous indifference to religious groups.” Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 425-32 (1962), and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), invalidated government-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools, but those cases do not hold that private religious speech may be excluded from an otherwise open forum simply because it is religious. To the contrary, the Court’s Establishment Clause cases require neutrality, not censorship. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314; McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212-13 (1948).

Milford’s reliance on a perceived need to avoid Establishment Clause concerns also fails because the danger identified by those cases is official sponsorship, not equal access for private speakers. McCollum, 333 U.S. at 212-13; Engel, 370 U.S. at 425-26; Schempp, 374 U.S. at 222-23. Allowing the Club to meet on the same terms as other community groups would not establish religion; excluding it because its viewpoint is religious does the opposite by burdening religious speech alone. The First Amendment protects against that disparity. Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 400 (1923); Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510, 534-35 (1925); Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 166-67 (1944).

Accordingly, Milford violated the Good News Club’s free speech rights, and its Establishment Clause defense cannot stand. The judgment should be reversed.
