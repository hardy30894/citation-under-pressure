ARGUMENT

The Civil Service Reform Act provides that “cases of discrimination” shall be filed in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). Such cases exist when an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination” prohibited by enumerated statutes. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). These “mixed cases” are routed to district court “whether the Board decided [them] ‘on the merits’ or ‘procedurally.’” Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 50 (2012).

Kloeckner’s reasoning controls here. The Court held that an employee bringing a mixed case need not “show that the MSPB’s decision ‘agrees with’ her allegation” of an appealable action; it suffices that she “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB” and alleges discrimination. Id. at 53 (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A)). The Board’s subsequent determination that the action was voluntary—and thus outside its jurisdiction—does not alter the statutory classification. “No one here contests that [Perry] brought a mixed case—that [he] was affected by an action … appealable to the MSPB and that [he] alleged discrimination.” Id. at 51. The same language applies when the Board dismisses for lack of jurisdiction rather than untimeliness.

Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is incompatible with this holding. Powell treated jurisdictional dismissals as categorically distinct, directing them to the Federal Circuit. Id. at 599-600. Kloeckner rejected any such merits-procedure divide for mixed cases, emphasizing that “the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction … is exclusive only when the MSPB’s decision was not a ‘mixed case.’” 568 U.S. at 47. The voluntariness inquiry that determines MSPB jurisdiction is frequently “bound up with the merits of [the] discrimination claim,” rendering uniform Federal Circuit review inconsistent with the statutory scheme. Id. at 52.

The statute’s text supplies no basis for distinguishing jurisdictional from procedural dismissals. Both turn on whether the employee “may appeal to the MSPB,” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), a question the Board resolves before reaching discrimination. Kloeckner places review of that determination in district court when discrimination is alleged. 568 U.S. at 56. Perry’s petition therefore belongs in district court.
