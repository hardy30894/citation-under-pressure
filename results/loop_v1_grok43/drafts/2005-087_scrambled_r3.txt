The Fourteenth Amendment imposes no requirement that Arizona allow expert evidence of mental condition to negate mens rea when the defendant may also assert insanity. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952), upheld a state rule placing the burden of proving insanity on the accused and recognized that the Due Process Clause leaves the states free to allocate the burden of proof on that issue. The same principle permits Arizona to confine the use of such evidence to the insanity defense.

States possess wide latitude to define the elements of offenses and the scope of available defenses. Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 88 (1934), affirmed that the prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, yet left the states free to decide whether evidence of mental unsoundness bears on those elements or only on a separate defense. Matheson v. United States, 227 U.S. 540, 543 (1913), and Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 326 (1915), likewise treated the admissibility of mental-condition evidence as a matter of local procedural regulation rather than constitutional command.

Due process requires only that a criminal proceeding be fundamentally fair. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Rules that exclude particular categories of evidence do not violate this standard unless they offend “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Arizona’s limitation on expert testimony satisfies that test; it channels mental-condition evidence into the insanity defense without denying the accused any historically guaranteed opportunity to contest the prosecution’s case.

The Clause does not compel the admission of every item of relevant evidence. Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923), and Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 235 (1940), protect against procedures that render a trial a mere sham, but neither decision requires states to adopt any particular rule governing expert testimony on mens rea. Arizona’s statute therefore comports with the Fourteenth Amendment.
