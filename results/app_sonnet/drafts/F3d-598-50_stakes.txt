# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Testimony From Two ADAs and a Police Lieutenant Vouching for Ramos and Rivera and Opining on Probable Cause

This Court reviews evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion, but a district court abuses its discretion when it admits testimony that violates settled rules governing witness competence. Three categories of testimony here were plainly improper.

**A. Vouching for credibility.** It is bedrock law that a witness may not tell the jury which of two competing witnesses to believe; that determination is "for the jury, and the jury alone." In *Nimely v. City of New York*, 414 F.3d 381 (2d Cir. 2005), this Court held that testimony evaluating whether one witness's account was more credible than another's invades the province of the jury, regardless of the witness's official position or expertise. The same is true here: the ADAs' and the lieutenant's assessments of Ramos's and Rivera's honesty did nothing but substitute their judgment for the jury's on the single most contested question at trial.

**B. Legal conclusions.** A witness likewise may not testify in terms that track the legal standard the jury must apply. In *Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 364 (2d Cir. 1992), this Court held it was error to permit a witness to characterize an officer's conduct using language mirroring the legal standard governing the case, because doing so communicates a legal conclusion to the jury rather than assisting it in evaluating facts. *See also United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135 (2d Cir. 1988) (witness may not frame testimony in terms that effectively decide the ultimate legal question for the jury). Testimony that "probable cause existed" for the arrests and prosecution is exactly this kind of conclusion. Probable cause is a legal standard applied to facts; whether it existed here was for the jury to decide once it heard the facts, not for a witness to announce.

**C. Interpreting evidence jurors can evaluate themselves.** Finally, a lay or quasi-expert witness may not tell the jury how to weigh evidence that is within the jury's own competence to assess. Allowing the ADAs to opine on whether the security-camera photographs "strengthened" or "weakened" Appellants' case did precisely that: it substituted the witnesses' interpretive gloss for the jury's independent evaluation of photographic evidence any layperson can examine.

## II. The Errors Were Not Harmless

An evidentiary error requires reversal unless the court can say "with fair assurance ... that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946). That assurance is impossible here. The case turned on two disputed issues — whether Ramos and Rivera told the truth, and whether they had probable cause — and the erroneously admitted testimony spoke directly and repeatedly to both. Allowing three government witnesses, cloaked in the appearance of neutrality, to tell the jury that the officers were credible and that probable cause existed effectively resolved the case for Appellees before the jury ever deliberated. The verdict cannot stand.

## III. Appellants Were Not Entitled to Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Photographs

Under *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000), a court ruling on a motion for judgment as a matter of law "must draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party" and "may not make credibility determinations or weigh the evidence." Appellants argued below that the security-camera photographs so "blatantly contradicted" Appellees' account that, under *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007), no reasonable jury could credit it. But unlike the videotape in *Scott*, which left "no reasonable jury" able to accept the plaintiff's version, the photographs here are consistent with either side's narrative and do not foreclose the officers' account. The District Court therefore correctly denied judgment as a matter of law on this ground.

## IV. On Remand, the Jury Should Be Correctly Instructed

**A. Obstructing governmental administration.** New York courts have held that a defendant cannot be guilty of obstructing governmental administration by interfering with police conduct that is not itself authorized or lawful. *See People v. Lupinacci*, 191 A.D.2d 589 (2d Dep't 1993). Because Higgenbottom's arrest for obstruction depended on the lawfulness of Cameron's arrest, the jury should have been instructed that it must find Cameron's arrest lawful before convicting Higgenbottom's conduct as obstructive.

**B. Punitive damages.** Punitive damages are available under § 1983 "when the defendant's conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Appellants' version of events — that Ramos and Rivera fabricated a story to justify baseless arrests and prosecution — would permit a jury to find exactly this kind of malice. A punitive damages instruction should therefore be given on remand.

For these reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
