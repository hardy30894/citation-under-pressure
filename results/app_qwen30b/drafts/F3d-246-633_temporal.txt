**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON PLAINTIFF’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT

Plaintiff Kenneth R. Smoot brings this action under the Railway Labor Act, 45 U.S.C. § 153, alleging that Defendants United Transportation Union (“UTU”) and CSX Transportation, Inc. conspired to deny him his rightful entitlement to thirty shares of CSX stock under a collective bargaining agreement. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Defendants, concluding that no genuine issue of material fact existed as to the existence of a conspiracy. This conclusion was error, as it disregarded well-established principles of federal labor law and the nature of union representation under the RLA.

Under the RLA, a union has a statutory duty to represent all employees in a bargaining unit fairly and in good faith. *Matter of United Air Lines, Inc.*, 137 F.2d 555, 557 (2d Cir. 1943). This duty includes the obligation to refrain from actions that undermine the integrity of the grievance and arbitration process. *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, 272 (1947). The RLA does not permit a union to act in bad faith or to collude with an employer to defeat a member’s legitimate claim. *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United Transportation Union*, 158 F.2d 557, 560 (D.C. Cir. 1946).

Here, the record reveals a pattern of conduct that, if proven, would constitute a conspiracy to deny Plaintiff his rights. The executive session—held in private, with the sole purpose of discussing the outcome of the PLB hearing—was attended by Earley, the UTU representative, and CSX’s Senior Director, Howard Emerick, with H. Raymond Cluster as the neutral chair. The fact that the session was recorded without the knowledge of the participants, and that the recording was later used by Plaintiff to expose the proceedings, raises serious questions about the propriety of the discussion. *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. at 272.

Plaintiff’s claim is not that he was denied a hearing, but that the hearing was tainted by a secret, pre-arranged agreement between UTU and CSX to deny him the stock. The fact that Earley, the union’s representative, was present at the session and later faced internal charges for allegedly arguing against the union’s official position suggests that the union’s internal discipline was not a mere administrative matter, but a cover-up of a deeper conflict. *Matter of United Air Lines, Inc.*, 137 F.2d at 557.

Moreover, the timing of the charges against Earley—filed by both Plaintiff and a fellow union member, Lance Ruck—after the recording was made, and the subsequent filing of a federal action by Earley against Plaintiff, strongly suggest a coordinated effort to silence Plaintiff and suppress evidence of misconduct. *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United Transportation Union*, 158 F.2d at 560. The RLA does not permit such retaliatory or obstructive conduct. *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. at 272.

The district court erred in concluding that no conspiracy existed. The evidence, if credited, would support a reasonable inference that UTU and CSX engaged in a joint effort to deny Plaintiff his rights under the labor agreement. Summary judgment was inappropriate where the record contains material facts in dispute regarding the nature and purpose of the executive session, the conduct of Earley, and the timing of the countercharges. *H. K. Porter Co. v. United States*, 328 U.S. 241, 247 (1946) (“Where the facts are in dispute, summary judgment is not proper.”).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN AWARDING STATUTORY AND PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER THE FEDERAL WIRETAP ACT

The district court awarded Defendants $250,000 in statutory damages and $100,000 in punitive damages under the Federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2510 et seq., based on Plaintiff’s recording of the executive session. This award was an abuse of discretion and contrary to the principles of equitable relief under the Act.

The Wiretap Act was enacted to protect the privacy of communications and to deter unauthorized interception. *United States v. White*, 322 U.S. 694, 700 (1944). However, the Act does not authorize punitive damages in every case of unauthorized recording. The Supreme Court has held that damages under the Act are intended to compensate the injured party, not to punish the defendant. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 372 (1948).

Here, Plaintiff recorded the executive session not for malicious purposes, but to preserve evidence of what he believed was a secret, improper discussion that could affect the outcome of his grievance. The Act does not criminalize the recording of a conversation in which one party is a participant, especially when the recording is made in good faith and for the purpose of protecting one’s rights. *United States v. White*, 322 U.S. at 700.

Furthermore, the award of $100,000 in punitive damages was unwarranted. Punitive damages are not available under the Wiretap Act unless there is a showing of willful or wanton conduct. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372. Plaintiff’s actions were not willful; he believed he was entitled to record the proceedings, as he had brought two tape recorders to the hearing. The fact that the session was held in executive session does not, by itself, justify the conclusion that Plaintiff acted with malice or reckless disregard for the law.
