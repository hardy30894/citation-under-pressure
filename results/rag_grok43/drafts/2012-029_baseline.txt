**ARGUMENT**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) confers exclusive federal jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. Under *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988), a case arises under federal patent law only if “federal patent law creates the cause of action or … patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims.” Minton’s malpractice claim is created by Texas common law; the experimental-use exception to the on-sale bar, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), is merely one element of the “case within a case” that must be proved. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004).

Even when a federal issue is embedded, *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005), requires that the issue be “actually disputed and substantial” and that federal jurisdiction not “disturb[] any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” The patent question here fails both prongs. First, it is not substantial in the *Grable* sense: resolution affects only the parties’ private damages and creates no precedent binding on the PTO or future patentees. *See also Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) (suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute … respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends”).

Second, exercising exclusive federal jurisdiction would upset the balance Congress struck. State courts routinely adjudicate malpractice claims that incidentally require application of federal law, including patent law. *See, e.g., New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005). Congress has never indicated that patent-related malpractice belongs exclusively in federal court. *Cf. Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), applied an incomplete test that omitted *Grable*’s federalism inquiry. This Court is not bound by those decisions. *See also Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 334, 340 (5th Cir. 2008) (declining to follow *Air Measurement* for trademark malpractice).

Because the federal issue is neither substantial nor jurisdictionally disruptive, exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
