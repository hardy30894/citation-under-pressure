# ARGUMENT

## I. The Eighth Amendment Has Never Been Understood to Impose a Categorical Bar Tied to an Offender's Personal Characteristics; It Prohibits Only Punishments That Are Inherently Barbaric or Grossly Disproportionate to the Offense.

The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause developed as a check on the *method* and *severity* of punishment, not as a vehicle for excusing categories of offenders from an otherwise lawful sentence. In *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878), this Court explained that the Clause forbids only punishments involving "torture or a lingering death," and it upheld execution by firing squad because that method inflicted no such cruelty. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), reaffirmed that "[p]unishments are cruel when they involve torture or a lingering death," but that "the punishment of death is not cruel, within the meaning of that word as used in the constitution." *O'Neil v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323, 332 (1892), likewise confined the Clause's historical office to preventing "torture[] and other barbarous methods of punishment."

Even where this Court has recognized a proportionality component, that inquiry has focused on the relationship between the *offense* and the *sentence*, not on offender characteristics divorced from the crime. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910), stated only that "it is a precept of justice that punishment for crime should be graduated and proportioned to the offense." Atkins does not contend that capital punishment is disproportionate to the crime of premeditated capital murder; he asks this Court to create an entirely new class of persons who are constitutionally immune from a punishment that is otherwise proportionate to their crime. Nothing in *Weems*, *Wilkerson*, or *Kemmler* supports that extension.

## II. Any "Evolving Standards of Decency" Inquiry Requires Objective Evidence of a Settled National Consensus, Which Petitioner Cannot Show.

This Court's modern proportionality analysis derives from *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), which held that denaturalization for wartime desertion was unconstitutional because it inflicted "the total destruction of the individual's status in organized society." *Id.* at 101. In reaching that conclusion, the plurality observed that "[t]he Amendment must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Id.* at 101. That inquiry is objective: it asks whether legislative enactments and jury verdicts across the country demonstrate a settled societal judgment condemning a practice, not whether a litigant or an advocacy group believes the practice unwise.

*Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), illustrates the narrow circumstances in which this Court has invoked the Clause to strike down a punishment altogether. There, California criminalized the mere *status* of narcotics addiction, without any act, and this Court held that punishing an involuntary status "as a crime" was cruel and unusual. *Id.* at 666. Atkins was not punished for a status; he was convicted, after full adversarial process, of abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder — volitional criminal acts committed with the intent required for capital murder. *Robinson* is inapposite where, as here, the defendant's mental condition was placed before the jury as mitigating evidence, weighed against the aggravating circumstances of a deliberate, violent crime, and rejected by two separate juries.

## III. Determinations of Mental Capacity Have Traditionally Been Left to Individualized Assessment Under State Law, Not Categorical Constitutional Exemption.

This Court has consistently respected the states' primary authority to define the relationship between mental capacity and criminal responsibility, reserving mental-capacity questions for individualized adjudication rather than blanket constitutional rules. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), this Court upheld Oregon's insanity standard against due process challenge, recognizing that formulating the legal test for the relationship between mental impairment and culpability is a matter "resting with the States." *Id.* at 800–01. Similarly, in *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9 (1950), the Court upheld a state procedure leaving the question whether a condemned prisoner was too mentally incapacitated to be executed to executive determination, without judicial hearing, explaining that such questions of degree have "always been" committed to flexible state procedures rather than fixed constitutional rules. *Id.* at 11–13.

*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), confirms that this Court has declined to constitutionalize the relationship between a defendant's mental or physical condition and his criminal responsibility even where sympathetic evidence of diminished capacity is present. There, the plurality refused to hold that chronic alcoholism categorically excused a defendant from punishment for public drunkenness, reasoning that such judgments about capacity and blameworthiness are properly the province of legislatures and juries applying the ordinary machinery of the criminal law, not a matter for imposition of a new constitutional status by this Court. *Id.* at 532–33 (plurality opinion). The same reasoning controls here: Atkins's intellectual limitations were fully presented to the sentencing jury as mitigation, exactly as *Leland* and *Powell* contemplate, and two juries — including one that heard the Commonwealth's rebuttal evidence — found that his crime nonetheless warranted death.

## Conclusion

The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause, as construed in *Wilkerson*, *Kemmler*, *O'Neil*, *Weems*, *Trop*, *Robinson*, *Leland*, *Solesbee*, and *Powell*, prohibits torturous methods and grossly disproportionate sentences; it does not compel this Court to fashion a new categorical exemption for a class of offenders whose mental capacity was already presented to, and weighed by, the sentencing jury. The judgment below should be affirmed.
