The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency process, as applied to Eugene Woodard, violates his Fourteenth Amendment due process right and his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that Woodard had no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency procedure.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment applies to clemency proceedings because they are an integral part of the overall adjudicatory process. As the Court held in Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 113 (1935), the state courts have an obligation to "guard and enforce every right secured by (the) Constitution." Clemency proceedings, as a critical component of the state's decision-making process, are subject to due process scrutiny. See Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9, 14 (1950) (Douglas, J., dissenting) ("The power to reprieve or pardon is an act of grace, but it is also a duty, and when exercised it should be done so in accordance with the law.").

The Court of Appeals' reliance on Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), is misplaced because Dumschat involved a challenge to the Connecticut Board of Pardons' denial of commutation without explanation, whereas this case involves a challenge to the clemency process itself. Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that clemency is an important aspect of the criminal justice system, and its exercise is not entirely discretionary. See United States v. Murray, 275 U.S. 347, 356 (1928) ("The power to grant probation ... is not unlimited."); Affronti v. United States, 350 U.S. 79, 83 (1955) (probation is a "correctional device" that is "part of the sentencing process").

Furthermore, the clemency process in Ohio is not merely a matter of executive grace, but rather an integral part of the state's system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant. As such, it is subject to the same type of due process scrutiny applicable to every other stage of the adjudicatory process. See Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), as quoted in Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985). The Ohio clemency process, which includes a pre-hearing interview, is a critical component of this system.

The option of voluntarily participating in an interview as part of the clemency process also violates Woodard's Fifth Amendment rights. The interview process presents a "Hobson's choice" between participating in the clemency process and asserting his Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. See James L. Bradford, Appt. v. United States, 228 U.S. 446, 461 (1913) (a pardon conditioned on the relinquishment of property rights is not necessarily voluntary). The State's interest in conducting clemency interviews does not outweigh Woodard's constitutional rights. See United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914) (the executive's clemency power is not unlimited).

The Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that Ohio's clemency process violates Woodard's due process and Fifth Amendment rights. The clemency process is an integral part of the state's adjudicatory process, and as such, it is subject to due process scrutiny. The interview process, which presents a "Hobson's choice" to death row inmates, also violates the Fifth Amendment. See United States v. Fletcher, 148 U.S. 84, 91 (1893) (the President's approval of a court-martial sentence is required for its validity); Zerbst v. Kidwell, 304 U.S. 359, 362 (1938) (parole is a "privilege" that can be revoked for misconduct). The Court should also consider the historical context of clemency and its role in the criminal justice system, as discussed in Johnson v. Massachusetts, 390 U.S. 511, 521 (1968) (Marshall, J., dissenting).

In conclusion, the Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency process, as applied to Eugene Woodard, violates his constitutional rights. The Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that the clemency process is subject to due process scrutiny and that the interview process violates the Fifth Amendment.
