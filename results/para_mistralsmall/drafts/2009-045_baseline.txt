**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s award of attorneys’ fees, including a $4.5 million enhancement, because the lodestar method does not foreclose consideration of superior performance and exceptional results. *See Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). This Court has long recognized that fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988 permit enhancements in “rare” and “exceptional” cases where the lodestar fails to reflect the true value of the services rendered. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I")*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986).

The district court’s enhancement was grounded in specific, detailed findings that counsel’s performance was “superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and that the results obtained were “exceptional.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899. The court observed that plaintiffs’ counsel achieved comprehensive systemic reforms benefiting thousands of foster children, secured a groundbreaking settlement, and demonstrated extraordinary dedication over a prolonged litigation. These findings are amply supported by the record and fall squarely within the discretion afforded district courts under *Delaware Valley I*. *See id.* at 565-66 (upholding enhancements where “specific evidence” demonstrates superior performance and exceptional results).

Contrary to Petitioners’ contention, *Blum* does not categorically bar enhancements for quality of representation. Rather, it requires that such enhancements be supported by specific evidence in the record—precisely what the district court provided here. *Id.* at 899. The Eleventh Circuit has consistently followed this approach, affirming enhancements where, as here, the district court’s findings are detailed and evidence-based. *See Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1242; *NAACP v. City of Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332, 1336-37 (11th Cir. 1987) (per curiam).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Delaware Valley I* is misplaced. While the Court cautioned that the lodestar “is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” it explicitly left open the possibility of enhancements in exceptional cases. 478 U.S. at 565. The Court reiterated that upward adjustments are permissible where “specific evidence” supports a finding of superior performance and exceptional results. *Id.* at 565-66. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision comports with this guidance, as the district court’s enhancement was not based on speculation but on concrete observations of counsel’s extraordinary efforts and the transformative nature of the relief obtained.

**II. The District Court’s Findings Were Sufficiently Specific and Supported by the Record.**

Petitioners argue that the district court’s findings were insufficiently specific or amounted to improper judicial testimony. This contention ignores the deferential standard of review and the district court’s unparalleled ability to assess counsel’s performance. *See Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1242 (deferring to district court’s factual findings). The court’s order detailed its 58 years of experience observing advocacy, its familiarity with prevailing rates, and its firsthand observations of counsel’s skill, commitment, and dedication. These observations are not “testimony” but reasoned judicial assessments entitled to deference. *See id.* at 1242-43.

Moreover, the record contains ample evidence of exceptional results: a landmark settlement reforming Georgia’s foster care system, court-supervised monitoring to ensure compliance, and systemic improvements benefiting thousands of children. Such outcomes are precisely the type of “exceptional success” that justifies an enhancement under *Delaware Valley I* and *Blum*. 478 U.S. at 565; 465 U.S. at 899.

**III. No Circuit Split Exists, and the Eleventh Circuit’s Precedent Is Consistent with Supreme Court Guidance.**

Petitioners mischaracterize the state of the law. Every circuit to address this issue—including the Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh—permits enhancements for superior performance and exceptional results in rare cases. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Kenny A.* aligns with this consensus and does not conflict with any controlling authority.

**Conclusion**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on specific evidence of superior performance and exceptional results. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed this award, and this Court should deny review.
