**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination case on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a conflict between the D.C. Circuit’s rigid adherence to *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and the clear statutory text and precedent governing judicial review of administrative decisions. The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to recognize the distinction between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals, and its insistence that *Powell* remains binding despite the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), is incompatible with this Court’s settled jurisprudence. The proper forum for review of the MSPB’s jurisdictional dismissal in this case is the federal district court, not the Federal Circuit.

The statutory framework governing judicial review of MSPB decisions is clear. Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), decisions of the MSPB are generally reviewable in the Federal Circuit. However, § 7703(b)(2) creates a well-defined exception: “In the case of a mixed case, the petition for review shall be filed in the district court.” A “mixed case” is defined in § 7702(a)(1) as one in which an employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” certain federal statutes. This statutory language makes plain that the forum for review turns on whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB”—a question that must be answered *before* the Board reaches the merits of the discrimination claim.

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that *Powell* remains binding despite *Kloeckner* rests on a false dichotomy between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” grounds for dismissal. But this Court has long rejected such a distinction when it comes to the finality and scope of administrative decisions. In *Hussey v. United States*, 222 U.S. 88, 94 (1911), this Court held that “the jurisdiction of the Court of Claims is conferred by statute,” and that “the question of jurisdiction is one of law, and not of fact.” When a court determines that it lacks jurisdiction over a claim, it is not merely applying a procedural rule—it is making a legal determination that the claim is not within the court’s power to hear. The same principle applies to the MSPB. When the Board concludes that an employee’s retirement was voluntary and thus not appealable, it is not merely applying a procedural rule; it is determining that the action is not one “which [the employee] may appeal to the MSPB,” as required by § 7702(a)(1)(A). That determination is a jurisdictional one, and it triggers the mixed-case exception.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. In *Powell*, the Board dismissed the appeal because the employee’s transfer was voluntary. The court held that such a jurisdictional dismissal must be reviewed in the Federal Circuit. But *Powell* was decided before this Court’s clarifying opinion in *Levers v. Anderson*, 326 U.S. 219 (1945), which established that a failure to exhaust administrative remedies is not a jurisdictional bar unless the statute explicitly so provides. In *Levers*, the Court held that the failure to file a motion for reconsideration under Treasury Regulations did not deprive the court of jurisdiction, because the statute did not make exhaustion mandatory. Similarly, the MSPB’s jurisdiction over a case is not contingent on the employee’s compliance with procedural rules; it depends on whether the action is one that may be appealed under § 7512. When the Board concludes that the action is not appealable—because it was voluntary—it is not applying a procedural rule; it is making a jurisdictional determination.

The D.C. Circuit’s argument that *Kloeckner* did not overrule *Powell* because the former involved a “procedural” dismissal is legally unsound. In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court did not distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional grounds; it held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on any ground that prevents it from reaching the merits—whether untimeliness or otherwise—review lies in district court. The Court emphasized that “the question is not whether the Board’s decision was correct, but whether the employee was ‘affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.’” *Kloeckner*, 133 S.Ct. at 604. That language applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals. If the Board determines that the action was not appealable because it was voluntary, then the employee was not “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” and thus the case is not a mixed case under § 7702(a)(1)(A). But if the Board had found that the action *was* appealable, and then dismissed the case on procedural grounds, the employee would still be entitled to district court review. The distinction is not between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” grounds, but between whether the action was appealable at all.

This Court has long recognized that the finality of administrative decisions depends on the nature of the underlying claim. In *Beadles v. Smyser*, 209 U.S. 393, 397 (1908), this Court held that “the jurisdiction of the court is a question of law, and not of fact,” and that “the court cannot proceed to hear a case unless it has jurisdiction.” Similarly, in *Glaspell v. Northern Pacific Railroad*, 144 U.S. 211, 215 (1892), the Court stated that “the court has no power to act unless the jurisdiction is properly invoked.” These principles apply with equal force to the MSPB. When the Board determines that it lacks jurisdiction because the action was voluntary, it is not merely applying a procedural rule—it is concluding that the case is not within its authority to hear. That conclusion is final and binding, and it triggers the mixed-case exception.
