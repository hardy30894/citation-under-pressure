**ARGUMENT**

**I. Bryant, as the prevailing party in arbitration, has standing to seek judicial enforcement of the award.**

The district court erred in concluding that Bryant could not seek enforcement of the arbitration award entered in his favor. A party to a grievance arbitration may invoke judicial process to enforce an award when the award confers an enforceable right upon that party. See *United Steelworkers of America v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 596–97 (1960) (“An arbitrator is confined to interpretation and application of the collective bargaining agreement” but the award is nonetheless the means by which the parties’ contractual rights are realized). Federal labor policy strongly favors final and binding arbitration as the agreed method for resolving disputes arising under a collective bargaining agreement. *Textile Workers Union of America v. Lincoln Mills of Alabama*, 353 U.S. 448, 455 (1957); *United Steelworkers of America v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 578 (1960).

Where, as here, an individual employee is the beneficiary of an arbitration award and seeks its enforcement against the employer, he is not a stranger to the award; he is the person whose contractual rights were adjudicated. Nothing in the Labor Management Relations Act limits enforcement actions to unions alone. To the contrary, § 301 of the Act authorizes suits for violation of contracts between an employer and a labor organization, 29 U.S.C. § 185(a), and the Supreme Court has recognized that “the courts have jurisdiction over suits for violation of contracts” under that section. *Textile Workers Union of America v. Lincoln Mills of Alabama*, 353 U.S. at 451. Because Bryant seeks only to enforce the award he won, the district court’s standing ruling should be reversed.

**II. The court applied an overly rigid proof framework and failed to recognize that Bryant’s evidence permits a finding of discrimination and retaliation under Title VII and § 1981.**

Title VII and § 1981 discrimination claims are governed by familiar burden-shifting principles. Under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973), a plaintiff may establish an inference of discrimination by showing that he is a member of a protected class, suffered an adverse employment action, was performing his job at a level that met legitimate expectations, and that similarly situated employees outside the protected class were treated more favorably. Once that prima facie showing is made, the employer must articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for the challenged action, and the plaintiff may then prove pretext. *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 252–56 (1981).

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the prima facie case is not intended to be applied mechanically. It is simply “a flexible evidentiary standard” designed to eliminate the most common nondiscriminatory reasons for the employer’s action. *Furnco Construction Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567, 577–78 (1978). Thus, the inquiry must focus on whether the evidence, taken as a whole, permits a reasonable inference of discrimination. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 148–49 (2000). *Reeves* further held that a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with evidence that the employer’s stated reason is false, may be sufficient for a trier of fact to infer intentional discrimination. *Id.* at 147–49.

Here, Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic repeatedly disciplined and ultimately discharged him after he protested the Red Letter Day policy, despite his long tenure and repeated efforts to comply through swaps, child-care arrangements, and proposed accommodations. He also presented evidence that Bell Atlantic refused to permit co-workers to cover his assignments, notwithstanding the union president’s request. A reasonable jury could find that Bell Atlantic’s asserted reason—insubordination—was pretextual and that the true motive was unlawful discrimination based on race, color, or gender. See *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 148; *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993).

The district court also erred in evaluating retaliation. Title VII prohibits discrimination against an employee because he “has opposed any practice made an unlawful employment practice” by Title VII. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a). To establish retaliation, a plaintiff must show protected activity, adverse action, and a causal connection. *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). Bryant’s grievance and complaints about the Red Letter Day policy were protected opposition activity, and the escalating discipline culminating in discharge is plainly adverse. Temporal sequence and the record of repeated discipline following Bryant’s protests support causation. See *Clark County School District v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 273 (2001) (per curiam).

**III. Summary judgment was improper because genuine disputes of material fact remain.**

Summary judgment is inappropriate where the record would permit a reasonable jury to return a verdict for the nonmovant. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248, 255 (1986). Courts may not weigh the evidence or make credibility determinations at summary judgment. *Id.* at 249; *Tolan v. Cotton*, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57 (2014) (per curiam). Here, disputes exist over whether Bell Atlantic treated Bryant differently because of his protected characteristics, whether its asserted nondiscriminatory explanation was genuine, and whether its discipline and discharge were retaliatory. Because the record contains ample evidence from which a jury could infer unlawful motive, the grant of summary judgment should be reversed. See *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150–51.
