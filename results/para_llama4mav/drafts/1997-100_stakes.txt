The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its decisions in Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), provide guidance.

In Bullington, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they share "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence." Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439. The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding involved a trial-like process, with the prosecution presenting evidence and the jury making a determination based on that evidence. Id. Similarly, in Rumsey, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a capital sentencing proceeding in Arizona, noting that the sentencing hearing "resembled a trial." Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209-210.

The same principles that led the Court to apply the Double Jeopardy Clause in capital sentencing proceedings should apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings, such as the one at issue here. Under California law, a defendant has a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations, and the prosecution must prove the allegations beyond a reasonable doubt. People v. Tenner, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993). The trial of prior conviction allegations involves many of the same procedural protections as a trial on guilt or innocence, including the right to confront witnesses and the application of the rules of evidence. People v. Reed, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224, 228, fn. 6 (1996).

The Supreme Court's decision in Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), does not undermine the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to noncapital sentencing proceedings. In Caspari, the Court noted that Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases, but it did not hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause is limited to capital cases. Id. at 392. Instead, the Court was addressing the question of whether the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to noncapital sentencing proceedings was a "new rule" for purposes of retroactivity. Id. at 393.

The California Supreme Court's attempt to distinguish Bullington and Rumsey is unpersuasive. The fact that California's noncapital sentencing proceedings may not involve the same level of formality as capital sentencing proceedings does not mean that they are not subject to the Double Jeopardy Clause. The key factor is whether the sentencing proceeding involves a trial-like process, with the prosecution presenting evidence and the trier of fact making a determination based on that evidence. Here, the trial of prior conviction allegations meets this standard.

In conclusion, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The trial of prior conviction allegations under California law involves many of the same procedural protections as a trial on guilt or innocence, and the Supreme Court's decisions in Bullington and Rumsey support the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to such proceedings. Therefore, the judgment of the California Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
