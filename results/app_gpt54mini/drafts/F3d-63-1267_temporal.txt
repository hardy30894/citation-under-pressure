### ARGUMENT

#### I. Stelwagon failed to prove actual injury and recoverable damages under the Clayton Act.

A private plaintiff may recover treble damages under Section 4 of the Clayton Act only upon proof of “injury” to its business or property caused by the antitrust violation. 15 U.S.C. § 15(a); see *Chicago, B. & Q. R.R. v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R.*, 166 U.S. 226 (1897). The Supreme Court has long required that damages not rest on speculation. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931), permits approximation of damages where the defendant’s wrongdoing makes exact proof impossible, but only after the plaintiff establishes a fact of injury and offers a reasonable basis for computation. Likewise, *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251 (1946), allows a jury to estimate damages, but does not dispense with proof that the plaintiff actually suffered antitrust loss.

Here, Stelwagon proved at most that Tarmac sold MAPs at lower prices to Standard and Celotex. That is evidence of price discrimination, but not proof that Stelwagon lost sales, profits, or goodwill because of it. Section 4 does not authorize a windfall. The plaintiff must show that its injury was “the result of” the violation and not some other cause. *Keogh v. Chicago & Nw. Ry. Co.*, 260 U.S. 156 (1922); *Brunswick-Balke-Collender Co. v. Sherman*, 277 U.S. 159 (1928). Under *Brunswick-Balke*, recovery is limited to injury of the type the antitrust laws were designed to prevent. 277 U.S. at 164. Stelwagon’s proof did not satisfy that standard.

The record instead shows a conjectural theory of damages built on the assumption that, because competitors obtained lower prices, Stelwagon must have lost business. But *Middleton v. Kaw Valley R.R. Co.*, 236 U.S. 318 (1915), and *Beckman v. National Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 8 F.2d 286 (2d Cir. 1925), recognize that antitrust damages cannot be founded on mere possibility or surmise. A plaintiff must connect the unlawful pricing to specific lost transactions or demonstrable diminution in value. Stelwagon did not do so.

The district court’s remittitur underscores the point: the jury’s award was found “unsupported by the evidence and grossly excessive.” Yet even the reduced antitrust figure remained unsupported by the record because the evidentiary gap went to liability for damages, not simply the amount. When the proof does not furnish a rational basis for segregating injury caused by the unlawful conduct from ordinary business loss, judgment for damages cannot stand. *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264–65; *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 562–63.

#### II. The Robinson-Patman Act requires proof of competitive injury, which Stelwagon did not establish.

Section 2(a) of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 13(a), prohibits discrimination in price where the effect “may be substantially to lessen competition.” The Supreme Court has made clear that the statute is concerned with competitive injury, not simply disparate prices. *FTC v. Morton Salt Co.*, 334 U.S. 37 (1948), permits an inference of injury from substantial and sustained price differences, but that inference is not irrebuttable and does not itself establish the plaintiff’s damages. *Standard Oil Co. v. FTC*, 340 U.S. 231 (1951), likewise focuses on the effect on competition.

Even assuming Stelwagon proved discriminatory pricing, it did not prove that those prices caused it actual competitive harm of the kind compensable under Section 4. *Bruce’s Juices, Inc. v. American Can Co.*, 330 U.S. 743 (1947), confirms that a private plaintiff must still demonstrate injury and a causal connection. Here, Stelwagon’s own evidence showed it maintained a relationship with Tarmac, sold Tarmac MAPs, and continued serving the same customer base. It offered no non-speculative proof that any customer defected because of Tarmac’s lower prices to Standard or Celotex, or that Stelwagon was unable to compete because of those prices.

That failure is fatal under *Shaw Goodyear Co. v. Cleveland Rubber Co.*, 333 U.S. 114 (1948), where the Court rejected liability based on a discriminatory practice unaccompanied by proof of actual harm. The Robinson-Patman Act does not make every price differential actionable in damages. The plaintiff must show that the discrimination injured its business in fact. Stelwagon did not.

#### III. The contract verdict cannot sustain the trebled federal judgment.

The federal judgment below improperly fused the contract award and the antitrust remedy. Trebling is available only for damages “by reason of” an antitrust violation. 15 U.S.C. § 15(a); *Brunswick-Balke-Collender*, 277 U.S. 159. A state-law contract injury is not transformed into antitrust damages merely because the parties also had a commercial relationship. *Keogh*, 260 U.S. 156. To the extent Stelwagon suffered loss from breach of exclusivity, that loss was contractual, not antitrust, and could not be trebled.

Because Stelwagon failed to prove actual antitrust injury and recoverable damages with the requisite certainty, the judgment awarding treble damages under the Clayton Act must be vacated. At most, the contract verdict may stand on its own terms; the antitrust award may not. See *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. 251; *Brunswick-Balke-Collender*, 277 U.S. 159; *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. 555.
