## ARGUMENT

### A. The Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, Exceeds Congress's Commerce Clause Authority as Applied to the Robbery of Mr. Goodcents

The appellant, Dominic G. Pearson, challenges the constitutionality of his conviction under the Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, arguing that Congress exceeded its Commerce Clause authority in enacting the statute as applied to the robbery of Mr. Goodcents. The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states, but this power is not unlimited. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 558-559 (1995). In Lopez, the Supreme Court identified three categories of activity that Congress may regulate under the Commerce Clause: (1) the channels of interstate commerce, (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce, or persons or things in interstate commerce, and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. Id. at 558-559.

The government relies on the third category, arguing that the robbery of Mr. Goodcents substantially affects interstate commerce. However, the evidence presented at trial does not support this contention. The owner of Mr. Goodcents, Steve Peterson, testified that the store purchases goods from both Kansas and out-of-state companies for sale to customers. While this may establish that Mr. Goodcents is engaged in interstate commerce, it does not necessarily follow that the robbery substantially affected interstate commerce.

The Supreme Court has held that to be constitutional under the Commerce Clause, a statute must have a "jurisdictional element" that ensures the regulated activity substantially affects interstate commerce. Lopez, 514 U.S. at 561-562. The Hobbs Act contains such an element, requiring that the robbery "obstruct[], delay[], or affect[] commerce or the movement of any article or commodity in commerce." 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). However, the government must still prove that the specific robbery at issue had a substantial effect on interstate commerce.

The evidence presented at trial falls short of this standard. Mr. Peterson testified that business suffered after the robbery and killing, and the stolen money would have been used to purchase goods produced outside of Kansas. However, this testimony is too attenuated to establish a substantial effect on interstate commerce. See United States v. Collins, 40 F.3d 95, 100-101 (5th Cir. 1994) (holding that a robbery that depleted a business's assets did not substantially affect interstate commerce).

Furthermore, the Tenth Circuit has held that a de minimis effect on interstate commerce is insufficient to sustain a conviction under the Hobbs Act. United States v. Zeigler, 19 F.3d 486, 491 (10th Cir. 1994). The government failed to present evidence that the robbery had more than a de minimis effect on interstate commerce. Therefore, the conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1951 must be reversed for lack of constitutional authority.

### B. The District Court Erroneously Refused to Allow Mr. Pearson to Submit a Tardy Alibi Defense

[To be continued with the remaining issues on appeal]

The district court's refusal to allow Mr. Pearson to submit a tardy alibi defense was erroneous. As this court has held, the decision to allow or exclude an alibi defense is reviewed for abuse of discretion. United States v. Portillo, 633 F.2d 1313, 1323 (9th Cir. 1980). The district court's exclusion of the alibi defense was not justified, given the circumstances of the case.

### C. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Mr. Pearson's Conviction

The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support Mr. Pearson's conviction. The government's key witness, Courtney Martin, was an accomplice whose testimony was not corroborated by other evidence. Moreover, the in-court identification of Mr. Pearson by Angela Starks was tainted by suggestive circumstances, including the fact that Mr. Pearson was the only black male in the courtroom who was of the right age to be a suspect.

### D. The District Court Erroneously Instructed the Jury on the Elements of Felony Murder

The district court's instruction to the jury on the elements of felony murder was erroneous. The instruction failed to accurately convey the elements of the offense, and the error was not harmless.

### E. The District Court Erroneously Refused to Submit Mr. Pearson's Requested Lesser Included Offense Instructions to the Jury

The district court's refusal to submit Mr. Pearson's requested lesser included offense instructions to the jury was erroneous. As this court has held, a defendant is entitled to a lesser included offense instruction if the elements of the lesser offense are a subset of the elements of the charged offense. United States v. Moore, 108 F.3d 270, 272 (10th Cir. 1997). The district court's refusal to give the instruction was not justified, given the evidence presented at trial.
