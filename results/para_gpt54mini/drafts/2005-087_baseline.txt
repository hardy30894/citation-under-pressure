## Argument

The Fourteenth Amendment does not give a criminal defendant a freestanding right to introduce expert psychiatric testimony to negate mens rea when a State has chosen to channel mental-disease evidence into an insanity defense. Arizona’s rule is constitutionally permissible because due process protects a defendant’s right to a fair opportunity to defend, not a right to present any evidence in any posture he prefers. See *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798–99 (1952); *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210–11 (1977).

### I. Due process does not require States to recognize diminished-capacity evidence as a separate mens rea defense.

This Court has repeatedly held that the Constitution leaves room for States to define criminal responsibility and the allocation of proof concerning insanity and related mental-state defenses. In *Leland v. Oregon*, the Court upheld a state rule placing the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, explaining that due process does not forbid a State from requiring the accused to carry that burden. 343 U.S. at 798–99. In *Patterson v. New York*, the Court reaffirmed that a State may define the elements of its offenses and may require the defendant to prove an affirmative defense so long as the prosecution remains obligated to prove the elements it has chosen to define. 432 U.S. at 210–11.

Arizona did exactly that. It required the State to prove the intent element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt, while separately confining psychiatric testimony to the defendant’s insanity defense. That allocation does not relieve the State of proving intent; it simply determines what kinds of evidence may be used to litigate insanity rather than mens rea. The Constitution does not require a State to recognize a partial-responsibility or diminished-capacity doctrine. See *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 779–80 (2006) (holding that Arizona’s rule limiting mental-disease evidence to insanity did not violate due process).

### II. The exclusionary rule is a permissible evidentiary limitation, not an unconstitutional barrier to defense.

This Court has long recognized that the States have broad authority to exclude evidence under rational evidentiary rules, even where the excluded evidence might bear on an element of the offense, so long as the rule is not arbitrary or disproportionate. See *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 56–57 (1996) (plurality opinion); *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 324–25 (2006). Arizona’s rule is not arbitrary. It reflects a judgment that psychiatric opinion evidence offered to negate mens rea is often unreliable and risks confusing juries by collapsing the distinction between capacity and actual intent. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 770–72.

The danger is especially acute where the defendant seeks to use expert testimony about mental illness to invite a jury to infer lack of intent from symptoms of disorder. As *Clark* explained, “mental-disease evidence” may “mislead” a jury into treating a diagnosis as proof of incapacity or into substituting psychiatric conclusions for the legal standard of intent. 548 U.S. at 773–74. Due process permits a State to conclude that these risks outweigh the marginal probative value of such testimony outside the insanity context. Id. at 770–78.

### III. The Constitution guarantees access to a defense, not unrestricted use of expert evidence.

The cases on which petitioner relies concern the wholesale exclusion of defense evidence central to the defense theory, not a State’s decision to regulate the manner in which mental-disease evidence may be used. In *Chambers v. Mississippi*, the Court invalidated the combined effect of rules that barred reliable third-party confessions and cross-examination essential to the defense. 410 U.S. 284, 302–03 (1973). In *Washington v. Texas*, the Court invalidated a statute that barred a defendant from calling an accomplice witness at all. 388 U.S. 14, 22–23 (1967). And in *Crane v. Kentucky*, the Court held that a defendant had to be allowed to present evidence about the circumstances of his confession because that evidence bore directly on reliability. 476 U.S. 683, 690–91 (1986).

Arizona did not bar Clark from presenting a defense. It allowed him to introduce psychiatric evidence to support insanity, the defense the State had chosen to recognize. See *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 756–59. What Arizona prohibited was a separate, expert-driven attempt to use the same mental-disease evidence as a negation-of-intent defense. That is a narrower evidentiary restriction than the exclusions condemned in *Chambers*, *Washington*, and *Crane*, and it falls comfortably within state authority under *Patterson* and *Egelhoff*.

### IV. The judgment should be affirmed.

Due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the elements the State defines, but it does not compel Arizona to permit expert testimony on mental illness to serve double duty as both insanity evidence and a separate mens rea defense. *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 210–11; *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798–99; *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 779–80. Because Arizona’s rule was a valid evidentiary and substantive choice within the State’s prerogative, the conviction should be affirmed.
