**ARGUMENT**

**I. A live controversy remains when a plaintiff seeks nominal damages for a past constitutional violation, even if the challenged policy has been repealed.**

The Court has long recognized that a request for nominal damages preserves a live controversy where the challenged conduct has ceased. In *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 292 U.S. 59 (1934), the Court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past violation of his constitutional rights was sufficient to sustain jurisdiction, even though the allegedly unconstitutional ordinance had been repealed. The Court explained that the plaintiff’s right to nominal damages for the past violation was a “substantial controversy” sufficient to keep the case alive. *Id.* at 60. Similarly, in *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court permitted a suit for nominal damages to proceed despite the repeal of the challenged ordinance, emphasizing that the plaintiff’s right to nominal damages for the past violation was a live controversy. *Id.* at 501.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017), is inapposite. That case relied on modern precedent and misapplied the Court’s longstanding rule. The Court has never required a plaintiff to demonstrate ongoing harm to recover nominal damages for a past constitutional violation. *See, e.g., Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 (1946) (holding that nominal damages are a permissible form of relief for a constitutional violation). The fact that the challenged policy has been repealed does not moot a claim for nominal damages where the plaintiff alleges a past violation. *See Fowler*, 292 U.S. at 60.

**II. The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages is not rendered speculative by the repeal of the challenged policy.**

The Court has repeatedly held that a plaintiff need not show that the challenged policy will be reenacted to maintain a claim for nominal damages. In *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283 (1944), the Court permitted a habeas corpus action to proceed despite the government’s representation that the challenged detention policy had been abandoned, emphasizing that the plaintiff’s right to nominal damages for the past violation was sufficient to keep the controversy alive. *Id.* at 285. Similarly, in *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416 (2d Cir. 1945), the court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past antitrust violation was sufficient to sustain jurisdiction, even though the challenged conduct had ceased. *Id.* at 444.

The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages is not rendered speculative by the repeal of the challenged policy. The Court has long held that a plaintiff may recover nominal damages for a past constitutional violation, regardless of whether the challenged policy remains in effect. *See, e.g., Bell*, 327 U.S. at 678. The fact that the challenged policy has been repealed does not moot the plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages.

**III. The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages is not rendered moot by their failure to allege ongoing harm.**

The Court has never required a plaintiff to demonstrate ongoing harm to recover nominal damages for a past constitutional violation. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court permitted a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages to proceed despite the fact that the challenged conduct had ceased, emphasizing that the plaintiff’s right to nominal damages for the past violation was sufficient to keep the controversy alive. *Id.* at 101. Similarly, in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages for a past constitutional violation was sufficient to sustain jurisdiction, even though the challenged conduct had ceased. *Id.* at 326.

The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages is not rendered moot by their failure to allege ongoing harm. The Court has long held that a plaintiff may recover nominal damages for a past constitutional violation, regardless of whether the plaintiff alleges ongoing harm. *See, e.g., Bell*, 327 U.S. at 678. The fact that the plaintiffs have not alleged ongoing harm does not moot their claim for nominal damages.
