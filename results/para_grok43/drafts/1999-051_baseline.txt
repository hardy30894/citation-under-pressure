ARGUMENT

A state prisoner may rely on a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel to establish cause for the default of an underlying constitutional claim. In Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), this Court held that “the exhaustion doctrine generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” Id. at 488. The Court did not, however, require that the ineffective-assistance claim itself be free of procedural default. To the contrary, Carrier recognized that “the right to effective assistance of counsel at trial is a fundamental right,” id. at 488, and that a prisoner who has been denied that right may demonstrate cause even when state procedural rules have barred review of the ineffectiveness claim.

Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), confirms the point. There the Court reiterated that “a prisoner may obtain federal review of a defaulted claim by showing cause for the default and prejudice from a violation of federal law.” Id. at 750. Cause exists when “some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” Id. at 753 (quoting Carrier, 477 U.S. at 488). Ineffective assistance of appellate counsel is precisely such an external factor. Nothing in Coleman suggests that the cause inquiry collapses if the ineffectiveness claim is itself defaulted; the inquiry remains whether counsel’s deficient performance actually impeded compliance with state procedure.

The Sixth Circuit correctly applied these principles. It first determined that Carpenter’s appellate counsel rendered constitutionally ineffective assistance by failing to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence on direct appeal. That finding established cause for the default of the sufficiency claim. The court then examined whether the default of the ineffectiveness claim itself could be excused, and concluded that the same deficient performance supplied the requisite cause. This two-step analysis is faithful to Carrier and Coleman: each default is assessed independently, yet the underlying constitutional violation—here, the denial of effective appellate counsel—remains available to satisfy the cause requirement for both.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would insulate state-court defaults of ineffectiveness claims from federal review even when those defaults are themselves the product of the same constitutional violation. Such a rule would undermine the “cause and prejudice” standard’s purpose of ensuring that “a prisoner who has been denied a fundamental constitutional right may obtain federal review.” Carrier, 477 U.S. at 488. Because the record establishes both cause and prejudice, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
