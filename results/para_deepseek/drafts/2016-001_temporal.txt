**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text and History of the Design Patent Act Limit Damages to the Profits Attributable to the Infringing Component.**

The governing statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that a design patent infringer “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit.” This Court has long held that the word “total” does not authorize recovery of profits from an entire product when only a component infringes. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court held that a design patent covering only the “carpet pattern” could not support an award of all profits from the sale of the entire carpet. The Court explained that the patentee “must show that the profits are attributable to the patented design, and not to the other parts of the carpet.” *Id.* at 444. The same principle applies here: Samsung’s design patents cover only components of a smartphone—such as the shape of the device or the arrangement of icons—not the entire phone. Apple cannot recover profits from the sale of the whole phone without proving that those profits are attributable to the patented components.

The 1887 revision of the design patent statute, which became § 289, did not alter this rule. In *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), the Court reaffirmed that a design patentee must “separate the profits due to the design from those due to the other parts of the article.” *Id.* at 17. The Court rejected the argument that the statute’s reference to “total profit” allowed recovery of all profits from the entire article, holding that “the profit for which the defendant is liable is only that which is attributable to the use of the patented design.” *Id.* at 18. This Court’s decision in *Dobson v. Bigelow Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), similarly required the patentee to “show what part of the profit is due to the use of the patented design.” *Id.* at 444.

**II. The “Article of Manufacture” Must Be the Component, Not the Entire Product.**

Section 289 imposes liability for profits from the “article of manufacture” to which the design is applied. This Court has defined “article of manufacture” as the “thing made” by the infringer. *See American Wood-Paper Co. v. Fibre Disintegrating Co.*, 90 U.S. 566, 593 (1874). When a design is applied only to a component, the “article of manufacture” is that component, not the larger product. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. at 444, the Court treated the carpet pattern as the relevant article, not the entire carpet. Similarly, in *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. at 17, the Court focused on the “design for a carpet” rather than the carpet itself.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach—treating the entire smartphone as the article of manufacture—conflicts with this Court’s precedent. In *Dobson v. Bigelow Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. at 444, the Court rejected the argument that the “article of manufacture” is always the entire product sold to consumers. Instead, the Court held that the relevant article is the “thing to which the design is applied.” *Id.* Here, Apple’s design patents are applied to specific components—such as the bezel, the screen, or the icon layout—not to the entire phone. The “article of manufacture” for each patent is therefore that component, and damages must be limited to profits from that component.

**III. The Apportionment Requirement Is Consistent with General Damages Principles.**

This Court has consistently required apportionment of profits in patent cases. In *Westinghouse v. Wagner Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604 (1912), the Court held that a patentee must “separate the profits due to the patented invention from those due to other parts of the machine.” *Id.* at 614. The same rule applies to design patents. *See Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. at 17. The Federal Circuit’s decision to award all profits from the entire smartphone violates this fundamental principle.

The rule of apportionment is not a new invention. In *Livingston v. Woodworth*, 56 U.S. 546 (1853), this Court held that a patentee could recover only “the profits which the defendant has made by the use of the plaintiff’s invention.” *Id.* at 560. The Court explained that “the defendant is not to be charged with the profits which he has made from other sources.” *Id.* at 561. This principle applies with equal force to design patents. Apple cannot recover profits from Samsung’s sale of cameras, processors, or other non-infringing components.

**IV. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Would Produce Absurd Results.**

If the Federal Circuit’s rule were correct, a design patent on a single button could entitle the patentee to all profits from the sale of an entire car. This Court has never endorsed such a result. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. at 444, the Court expressly rejected the argument that a design patent on a pattern could support recovery of all profits from the sale of the carpet. The Court noted that “the carpet is not the design, but the design is applied to the carpet.” *Id.* The same logic applies here: the smartphone is not the design; the design is applied to a component of the smartphone.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of damages limited to the profits attributable to the infringing components.
