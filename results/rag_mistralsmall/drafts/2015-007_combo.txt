**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not run afoul of the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). The Supreme Court in *Ring* held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury—not a judge—to find the facts that elevate a homicide to a capital offense. *Id.* at 609. Florida’s scheme complies with this principle because it requires the jury to find the existence of at least one statutory aggravating factor before a death sentence may be imposed. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2012); *see also* *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984) (upholding Florida’s scheme against Sixth Amendment challenge). The jury’s role in finding aggravating factors satisfies *Ring*’s core holding.

Moreover, this Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment does not mandate jury unanimity in sentencing determinations. *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 752 (1948) (upholding a federal sentencing scheme where the jury’s role was advisory and the judge made the final sentencing decision). Florida’s scheme similarly entrusts the jury with an advisory recommendation, while reserving the ultimate sentencing authority to the trial judge. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465. This division of labor is consistent with historical practice and does not offend the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 455 (citing *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 244 (1949)).

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme also withstands Eighth Amendment scrutiny. The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in sentencing recommendations, nor does it mandate that juries determine mental retardation as an absolute bar to execution. *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 585 (1959) (upholding a death sentence where the jury’s role was advisory and the judge made the final determination). The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment does not extend to procedural rules governing sentencing recommendations. *Id.* at 584.

Furthermore, Florida’s scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s heightened reliability requirement for death sentences. *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510, 522 n.20 (1968) (upholding a death sentence where the jury was not required to be unanimous in its recommendation). The Supreme Court has repeatedly deferred to state legislatures in crafting sentencing procedures, so long as the scheme provides sufficient safeguards against arbitrary and capricious imposition of the death penalty. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. at 244 (upholding a sentencing scheme that allowed the judge to consider broad information, including unadjudicated conduct).

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY DELEGATE SENTENCING AUTHORITY TO THE JUDGE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not impermissibly delegate sentencing authority to the judge. The jury’s role in finding aggravating factors and making a sentencing recommendation ensures that the judge’s ultimate sentencing decision is informed by the jury’s deliberations. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465. This division of labor is consistent with historical practice and does not offend the Constitution. *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 583 (1968) (upholding a federal sentencing scheme where the jury’s role was advisory).

**IV. FLORIDA’S SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT BY FAILING TO REQUIRE UNANIMOUS JURY RECOMMENDATIONS.**

Florida’s scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment by allowing non-unanimous jury recommendations. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld sentencing schemes that permit non-unanimous jury recommendations. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1879) (upholding a death sentence where the jury’s role was advisory). The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in sentencing recommendations. *Id.* at 136.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
