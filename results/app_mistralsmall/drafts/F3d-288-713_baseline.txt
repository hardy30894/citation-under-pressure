**ARGUMENT**

I. **The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Petition Without an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct**

Riddle contends that the prosecution violated his due process rights under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), by failing to disclose material impeachment evidence regarding District Attorney Danny Hill’s prior misconduct. The Fifth Circuit has held that a *Brady* violation occurs when (1) the prosecution suppresses evidence favorable to the defense, (2) the evidence is material, and (3) the defendant was prejudiced as a result. *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 674–75 (1985); *Moore v. Johnson*, 225 F.3d 460, 466 (5th Cir. 2000).

Here, Riddle testified that Hill threatened him with the death penalty after his arraignment, which the prosecution rebutted by calling Hill to the stand. However, Hill had a history of misconduct, including an ethics complaint filed against him for making improper statements to a defendant. This evidence was never disclosed to Riddle despite its clear relevance to Hill’s credibility. The district court erred in failing to hold an evidentiary hearing to determine whether this suppression prejudiced Riddle’s defense. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312–13 (1963) (holding that an evidentiary hearing is required when factual disputes exist regarding a *Brady* claim).

II. **The Prosecution’s Improper Vouching for Its Witnesses Warranted Reversal Under *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1 (1985)**

During closing argument, the assistant prosecutor argued that Riddle should be viewed as a liar because Hill had demonstrated Riddle’s dishonesty through his testimony. This amounted to improper vouching for the credibility of Hill, a key witness. The Supreme Court has held that such arguments violate due process by undermining the jury’s role as the sole arbiter of witness credibility. *Young*, 470 U.S. at 11–12. The Fifth Circuit has consistently reversed convictions where prosecutors improperly vouched for witnesses. *See, e.g., United States v. Iredia*, 866 F.2d 114, 117 (5th Cir. 1989).

III. **The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Riddle’s Request for an Evidentiary Hearing**

Riddle sought an evidentiary hearing to develop his *Brady* claim and to challenge the prosecution’s misconduct. The Fifth Circuit has held that a district court must hold an evidentiary hearing when a habeas petitioner presents a colorable claim that, if proven, would entitle him to relief. *See, e.g., Martinez v. Cockrell*, 333 F.3d 523, 528 (5th Cir. 2003). The district court’s denial of this hearing was an abuse of discretion, as Riddle’s claims were not conclusively refuted by the record.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Riddle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for an evidentiary hearing on his *Brady* claim and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
