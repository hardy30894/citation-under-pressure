\title{Citation Under Pressure: What Deployment Constraints Do to Legal Citation Integrity, and What Deterministic Verification Can Repair}

\author{\fnms{Harsh} \snm{Dubey}%
\thanks{Corresponding Author: Harsh Dubey, New York University; harshdubeyjp@gmail.com.}}
\runningauthor{H. Dubey}
\address{New York University, New York, USA}

\begin{abstract}
Sanctions over fabricated legal citations have made language-model drafting a problem for courts, and most research on the problem has produced detection benchmarks that score errors in finished briefs. This paper measures how the errors are produced. Six models from six vendors, from a 30B open-weight model to two frontier systems, drafted argument sections for 48 U.S. Supreme Court matters under five prompting conditions: baseline, a citation quota, a restriction to pre-1970 authority, a sanctions warning, and all three combined. Every citation in the \nDrafts{} drafts was checked deterministically, with no language-model judge in any primary measurement: existence against an 18-million-row reporter index, quotations against a verbatim checker validated on seeded faults, and pinpoint pages against official reporter pagination from public archives. Under combined pressure the smallest model's citation existence fell from \existQwenBase{} to \existQwenCombo{}, and verbatim quotation accuracy fell significantly for three of six models; only the strongest model was unaffected. In a second experiment the checker fed its findings back into revision. The strongest model reached \loopSonnetTrueFinal{} verbatim accuracy, and a per-quotation trace shows how: of its \trFlagRZeroSonnet{} flagged quotations, \trTrueFlagToAccSonnet{} were corrected and the rest were deleted or stripped of their quotation marks. False feedback produced the same deletions aimed at correct quotations, and five of six models ended less accurate than they started. We release the instruments, including a page-level pinpoint verifier built from public data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Since 2023, courts have repeatedly sanctioned lawyers who filed briefs containing citations that a language model invented, beginning with \textit{Mata v.~Avianca}~\cite{mata2023} and continuing in a growing docket that a public tracker now counts in the hundreds~\cite{charlotin2025tracker}. Most published work frames the problem as detection: given a brief that already contains errors, how many can a model or agent find~\cite{liu2026whochecks,chen2026legalcitebench}? Detection accuracy is the relevant measure for review of a filed brief. It does not tell a deployer which prompting conditions produce the errors, which is the question this paper addresses.

This paper asks two production-side questions. First, which prompting conditions cause citation failure, and does the answer depend on the model? Prompts in practice carry constraints of the kind studied by Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2026deployment} for scholarly citations: a minimum count of authorities, a restriction on which authorities may be cited, and a warning about consequences. That study found that such constraints, temporal restrictions in particular, collapse citation existence even in capable models. We are not aware of a comparable study for law. Second, when a checker that can verify citations deterministically feeds its findings back into revision, does the model correct its drafts, fail to, or change the draft so that the checker's tests pass without the quotations being corrected?

Both questions need an instrument the field has said does not exist in public: the best published detection agent misses nearly half of incorrect pinpoint citations (52.8 percent recall; open-weight detectors 19 to 51 percent), and its authors conclude that ``no models can reliably detect wrong pincites'' because page-level information lives behind commercial databases~\cite{liu2026whochecks}. We built the instrument from public sources: citation existence and name pairing against a local index derived from CourtListener bulk data, verbatim quotation checking validated on seeded faults, and page-level pinpoint verification against the official reporter pagination in the Caselaw Access Project's volume archives~\cite{cap2024}. Every primary number reported here is produced by this instrument; no language-model judge is used in any primary measurement.

Our scope is the closed-book use of general-purpose models, the setting of most sanctioned filings documented to date~\cite{charlotin2025tracker,dahl2024fictions}; retrieval-grounded commercial tools are a different setting, and Section~7 says what we expect to transfer.

The paper reports three results. First, pressure degrades citation integrity beginning with the smallest model, and the degradation is rarely accompanied by refusal; in the \nPressured{} drafts under the four non-baseline conditions (the pressured drafts) there were three refusals. Second, feedback from a deterministic checker leads capable models to delete flagged quotations far more often than to correct them, and false feedback leads them to delete correct ones. Third, in the strongest models the remaining errors are mostly genuine legal language attributed to the wrong case or the wrong page, and the released instruments detect both classes.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Legal citation reliability.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2026whochecks} build a detection benchmark from real appellate briefs with injected errors and a taxonomy grounded in sanctioned filings; we adopt their five error categories as outcome coding and measure the naturally occurring analogue their limitations section asks for. LegalCiteBench~\cite{chen2026legalcitebench} shows that models asked closed-book for the citation of a named case almost never produce the right one and almost never abstain; our task asks the easier question of whether the cases a model chooses to cite exist, and the low abstention transfers. Dahl et al.~\cite{dahl2024fictions} measured hallucinated authority in general-purpose chatbots, and Magesh et al.~\cite{magesh2025hallucinationfree} in commercial legal research tools; together they establish the rate of hallucinated authority in deployed systems.

\textbf{Constraints and fabrication.} Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2026deployment} is the closest design: five prompting regimes over scholarly claims, verified by fuzzy bibliographic matching that left 36 to 61 percent of citations unresolved. We transplant their factorial to law with deterministic verification (\unverifiablePct{} percent unresolved) and a formal statistical model; their mechanism, compliance with the temporal window filled with fabrications, transfers to law for some models and not others (Section~4).

\textbf{Revision with execution feedback.} The loop experiment takes its protocol from execution-feedback work in code generation~\cite{gehring2025rlef}, including a no-feedback control that separates feedback content from extra revision attempts. Our version adds a second control in which the feedback is false.

\textbf{AI and law.} Blair-Stanek and Van Durme~\cite{blairstanek2025unstable} found at ICAIL that frontier models flip the winning party across identical zero-temperature runs; we ran each cell once, and Section~8 treats that instability as a limitation of this design. Evaluation methodology in this community has moved toward separating factual truth from reasoning soundness~\cite{gultekin2025twodim} and pairing automated metrics with the checks lawyers perform~\cite{sanchi2024hybrid}; citation integrity is a check for which deterministic ground truth is available, and this paper automates it. Closest to our pinpoint instrument, Garneau et al.~\cite{garneau2024pincites} found that retrieving the paragraph a proposition rests on is far harder than retrieving the case, using network and text signals to propose pincites; our verifier approaches the same gap from the other side, checking a generated pincite against the page text. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025overruled} show models miss overruled precedent, a failure adjacent to the wrong-authority errors we measure, and ConsRAG~\cite{nguyen2024consrag} mitigates hallucination through constrained retrieval, the strategy complementary to the post-hoc feedback our loop experiment tests. Two studies of model-drafted litigation documents frame the trust question: Trozze et al.~\cite{trozze2025crypto} found mock jurors' decisions unrelated to whether a complaint was written by ChatGPT or a lawyer, and Harasta et al.~\cite{harasta2026cannotberight} found lawyers rate identical documents lower when told a model wrote them; machine verification of citation integrity offers a basis for trust that depends on neither perception. Gray et al.~\cite{gray2025magnitudes} generate legal arguments whose quality is judged by lawyers, and Pires et al.~\cite{pires2025legalwriting} evaluate legal writing automatically; neither checks the authority the text cites, which is the layer this paper adds. Citation-worthiness detection~\cite{khatri2023citecaselaw} asks where a citation is needed; the citation index behind our checker is the same kind of network studied in~\cite{sadeghian2018edgelabeling,dadgostari2021lawsearch,mumford2025citationnetworks}, used here to test whether a cited case exists.

\section{Tasks, Conditions, and Instruments}

\textbf{Terms.} A citation such as \textit{Brown v.~Board of Education}, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) names the parties, the volume (347), the reporter (U.S., the official United States Reports), and the first page (483); the optional second page (495) is the \textit{pincite}, which for a quotation must be the page where the quoted language appears. Throughout, \textit{citation existence} is the share of adjudicable citations that resolve to a real case, and \textit{strict quotation accuracy} is the share of scored quotations that reproduce the opinion verbatim. The five prompting conditions are called baseline, quota, temporal, stakes, and combined.

\textbf{Matters.} The matters are forty-eight Supreme Court cases from 1990 to 2020, drawn from a 500-case pool built from the Supreme Court Database~\cite{spaeth2025scdb}. Each packet holds the question presented, party names, a factual summary, and a lower-court opinion excerpt, with the Supreme Court's decision excluded and every packet screened for outcome leakage. The model argues an assigned side (petitioner for even-numbered matters, respondent for odd) in an argument section of under 900 words; nothing in the design predicts outcomes, so the memorization confound of Supreme Court prediction tasks~\cite{ortega2025scotusmem} is out of scope.

\textbf{Conditions.} The five conditions are baseline; quota (``cite at least eight distinct case authorities''); temporal (``every authority you cite MUST have been decided before 1970''); stakes (``this section will be filed with the court; citing authority that does not exist, or misquoting authority, risks sanctions under Rule~11''); and combined, which concatenates the three clauses. Each condition has one fixed template, run at temperature 0. The stakes clause names Rule~11 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure because it is the sanctions rule invoked in the sanctioned-filing cases~\cite{mata2023}. Rule~11 does not govern Supreme Court filings, which fall under the Court's own rules and inherent authority; the clause is a stakes signal, not a statement of the governing law. The conditions isolate mechanisms rather than simulate practice. A robustness check re-ran twelve matters under alternative phrasings of every condition.

\textbf{Models.} The models are Qwen3-30B-A3B, Mistral Small, Llama-4 Maverick, DeepSeek V4 Flash, GPT-5.4-mini, and Claude Sonnet~5, one per vendor, all accessed through one API gateway. The hypotheses concern how effects change with capability, so the set spans open-weight 30B to frontier with no vendor contributing two models; we do not assume a total ordering, and tables are ordered by baseline strict quotation accuracy. The gateway identifiers (\texttt{qwen3-30b-a3b-instruct-2507}, \texttt{mistral-small-2603}, \texttt{llama-4-maverick}, \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash}, \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, \texttt{claude-sonnet-5}) were run in August 2026 through OpenRouter with completion limits of 1,400 to 12,000 tokens. A seventh model, GLM-4.7-flash, completed \glmDrafts{} of 240 drafts and is reported in Section~4.

\textbf{Adjudication.} Citations are extracted with eyecite~\cite{cushman2021eyecite} and given one of three labels: exists, not found, or unverifiable (vendor citations and coverage gaps), so that index coverage is never counted as fabrication; \unverifiablePct{} percent of the \nCitationRecords{} citation records in the full run are unverifiable. Existence and name pairing resolve against an 18-million-row citation table. Quotations are located by balanced quotation marks and attributed to the case named in the same sentence, else to the nearest citation within 260 characters after the quotation, else to the nearest preceding one. Each is checked verbatim against the cited opinion's text from a local-first chain of public sources; a quotation is \textit{near miss} when every fragment reaches token coverage of at least 0.85 without matching exactly, and \textit{inaccurate} otherwise. The strict rate for a draft $d$ is
\begin{equation}
S_d = \frac{n_{\text{accurate}}}{n_{\text{accurate}} + n_{\text{near miss}} + n_{\text{inaccurate}}},
\end{equation}
and a lenient rate counting near misses as correct is reported alongside it. Before any measurement the quotation checker scored 100 of 100 on twenty synthetic drafts with planted genuine quotations, wrong attributions, single-word corruptions, fabrications, and formatting artifacts; because that is a self-authored test, Section~6 also audits its verdicts on real drafts. Pinpoint pages are verified against the official reporter pagination parsed from the Caselaw Access Project's HTML volumes, which mark every page break; a quotation counts as on the cited page when its longest fragment lies within that page's text, and adjacent-page matches are reported separately. Vendor citations (Westlaw and Lexis) are excluded from fabrication counts.

\textbf{Human reference.} The same instrument scored \humanExcerpts{} clean, pre-ChatGPT federal appellate brief excerpts from the benchmark of Liu et al.~\cite{liu2026whochecks}. Human lawyers reach \humanStrict{} strict and \humanLenient{} lenient quotation accuracy (\humanScored{} scored quotations; the near misses are chiefly bracketed alterations and ellipses) and \humanExist{} citation existence, the shortfall being index coverage of state and unpublished reporters rather than fabrication. Human briefs cite more non-federal authority and quote statutes and records more often than the models do, so the human figures serve only as a reference for the severity of the verbatim standard.

\textbf{Inference.} The primary analysis is a citation-level logistic generalized estimating equation per model, $\text{logit}\,P(y_{imk}=1)=\beta_0+\beta_k$ for citation $i$ in matter $m$ under condition $k$, with exchangeable working correlation within matters~\cite{liang1986gee}, so that the 48 matters rather than the citations are the independent units. Each model has \nHolmTests{} contrasts (four conditions, two outcomes); we report Holm-corrected $p$ values within model and call an effect significant only when it survives correction. Correction is within model, so statements that count models (``three of six'') are descriptive. Odds ratios, 95 percent confidence intervals, and raw and corrected $p$ values for all 48 contrasts are in the released results. Existence cells with few failures are reported as raw counts. A matter-level cluster bootstrap over draft rates agreed in sign with every corrected result. For the revision experiment, which was pre-registered descriptively, we add matter-paired comparisons of final strict accuracy between arms (Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a bootstrap interval on the mean difference); these are the only tests in Section~5 and were chosen after the data were seen. Hypotheses and decision rules were frozen before the full run; every later analysis decision, including two findings withdrawn after our own verification passes, is recorded in a dated ledger released with the code.

\textbf{Citation existence.} Only the 30B model's existence moves under pressure: from \existQwenBase{} at baseline to \existQwenCombo{} under the combined condition, with the quota, temporal, and combined contrasts all significant after correction (combined OR \orCiteQwenCombo, 95 percent CI \ciCiteQwenCombo, corrected $p\,\holmCiteQwenCombo$). Mistral Small's existence sits between 0.86 and 0.93 across conditions and falls under the temporal and combined conditions without reaching significance; the four other models stay between 0.93 and 1.00 in every condition.

\textbf{Quotation accuracy.} The temporal restriction is the condition that damages quoting. After correction, strict quotation accuracy falls significantly under temporal and combined pressure for Qwen3-30B (\strictQwenBase{} to \strictQwenCombo) and Llama-4 (\strictLlamaBase{} to \strictLlamaCombo), and under the combined condition for DeepSeek (\strictDeepseekBase{} to \strictDeepseekCombo); GPT-5.4-mini and Mistral Small fall in the same direction without surviving correction, and Sonnet~5 does not fall at all. The Llama-4 row is the most consequential for practice: at baseline it quotes at the human reference level, \strictLlamaBase{} against \humanStrict, and under the temporal restriction its accuracy drops to \strictLlamaTemporal{} (OR \orQuoteLlamaTemporal, CI \ciQuoteLlamaTemporal, corrected $p\,\holmQuoteLlamaTemporal$). The lenient rates in Table~1 move in the same direction as the strict rates for every model, so the result does not depend on how bracketed alterations are scored.

\textbf{A pre-registered contrast that did not survive correction.} Sonnet~5's strict quotation accuracy is numerically highest under the combined condition (\strictSonnetCombo{} against \strictSonnetBase{} at baseline; uncorrected $p=\pQuoteSonnetCombo$, corrected $p=\holmQuoteSonnetCombo$), and its citations not found went from \nfSonnetBase{} of \exSonnetBase{} at baseline to \nfSonnetCombo{} of \exSonnetCombo{}. Neither survives correction, the stakes condition alone moves neither outcome, and the temporal restriction shifts the cited cases toward older, better-memorized opinions (median decision year 1943 under the combined condition against 1986 at baseline), an untested alternative to any reading in terms of more conservative citing. We report it as a null result and draw no conclusion from it.

\textbf{Compliance with the temporal rule.} Violations of the pre-1970 restriction (Table~1) run from \violMistralTemporal{} percent of adjudicable citations for Mistral Small to \sonnetTempViol{} in \sonnetTempChecked{} for Sonnet~5 under the temporal and combined conditions. Mistral Small violates the rule most while its existence and quotation figures sit mid-table, so compliance does not track the other outcomes. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2026deployment} found near-zero violations with fabrication filling the window; here the smaller models both fabricate and violate.

\textbf{Refusals and one model that stalls.} Across the \nPressured{} pressured drafts there were three refusals (two from DeepSeek, one from GPT-5.4-mini). GLM-4.7-flash produced no output on \glmMissing{} of its 240 drafts, \glmMissingCombo{} of them in the combined condition, reasoning until a 30,000-token budget was exhausted in two escalating attempts; because its missing drafts concentrate in the condition under comparison it is excluded from Table~1 and reported as a third failure mode, distinct from fabrication and from refusal.

\section{Experiment 2: The Checker Inside the Revision Loop}

Combined-condition drafts entered a revision loop. Each round, the checker returned one line per failure (a citation that does not resolve; a quotation that does not appear verbatim in the cited opinion), the model revised, and the loop ended on a clean draft or after three revisions. A control with \textit{false feedback} gave the same number of identically formatted lines pointed at items that were correct, and a \textit{no-feedback} control, added after the pre-registered analysis, gave the same number of revision rounds with an instruction to revise as the model saw fit. The loop used the first 24 matters in the pre-registered order (twelve per side). Loop rates are means over drafts on 24 matters, so round-0 figures differ from the pooled citation-level rates over 48 matters in Table~1. Citation counts below include unverifiable citations. \textbf{Satisfying the checker by deletion.} Under true feedback the final strict rate ranges from \loopQwenTrueFinal{} for Qwen3-30B to \loopSonnetTrueFinal{} for Sonnet~5 (Table~2; the order is not monotone in the table's baseline ordering), and the rate alone is consistent with repair. The trace shows that the rise comes from deletion. Sonnet~5 had \trFlagRZeroSonnet{} flagged quotations at round 0: \trTrueFlagToAccSonnet{} were corrected, \trTrueFlagDequotedSonnet{} kept their words and lost their quotation marks, and \trTrueFlagDeletedSonnet{} were deleted; \trTrueAccRemovedSonnet{} of its \trAccRZeroSonnet{} accurate quotations were removed as well. Its scored quotations fell from \lqScoredSonnetTrueStart{} to \lqScoredSonnetTrueFinal{} while its accurate ones went from \lqAccSonnetTrueStart{} to \lqAccSonnetTrueFinal{} (\trTrueAddedAccSonnet{} newly added). DeepSeek and GPT-5.4-mini corrected \trTrueFlagToAccDeepseek{} and \trTrueFlagToAccGpt{} of theirs; Qwen3-30B corrected none and kept most of its flagged quotations in place. Citation existence, by contrast, was repaired: the loop existence rate rose from \lExistQwenTrueStart{} to \lExistQwenTrueFinal{} for Qwen3-30B and from \lExistLlamaTrueStart{} to \lExistLlamaTrueFinal{} for Llama-4 while citation counts fell by at most a tenth, so unresolvable citations were replaced with real ones rather than dropped. At every capability tested, checker feedback led models to remove flagged quotations or convert them to unmarked paraphrase far more often than to correct them; neither outcome is flagged by the checker, and the revised draft does not indicate which claims were originally quoted.

\textbf{The two controls.} Table~3 separates feedback content from revision itself. Without feedback, Sonnet~5's strict rate still rose (\loopSonnetNoneStart{} to \loopSonnetNoneFinal), and true feedback added \ldiffSonnetNone{} to the final rate over revision alone (interval \lciSonnetNone, $p$=\lpSonnetNone); the same holds for DeepSeek (\ldiffDeepseekNone, $p$=\lpDeepseekNone) and Llama-4 (\ldiffLlamaNone, $p$=\lpLlamaNone). For GPT-5.4-mini the difference between true feedback and no feedback is \ldiffGptNone{} with an interval that includes zero, so its apparent repair is not distinguishable from what revision alone does; for Mistral Small and Qwen3-30B neither arm moves far. Under false feedback the flagged items were correct, and the models treated them as they treated true flags: Sonnet~5 removed \trScrAccRemovedSonnet{} of its \trAccRZeroSonnet{} accurate quotations (\trNoneAccRemovedSonnet{} under no feedback) and its strict rate fell from \loopSonnetScrStart{} to \loopSonnetScrFinal; GPT-5.4-mini removed \trScrAccRemovedGpt{} of \trAccRZeroGpt{} and fell to \loopGptScrFinal. Five of six models ended below their starting rate (Qwen3-30B by under a point); Llama-4, the exception, and Qwen3-30B are also the two that changed least under true feedback. The mechanism is deletion of correct quotations, not rewriting: inaccurate counts fell in every model under false feedback. Our checker's own false-alarm rate, measured by the provenance audit of Section~6, was \falseAlarmPctSonnet{} percent of Sonnet~5's inaccurate verdicts (\falseAlarmSonnet{} of \inaccTotalSonnet), low enough not to produce this effect; a detector with a false-positive rate in the tens of percent would be expected to produce it, and intermediate rates were not tested.

\section{Residual Errors in the Strongest Models}

Three error classes remain when fabrication is rare, each measured deterministically on all six models.

\textbf{Paraphrase inside quotation marks.} Decomposing every inaccurate quotation against the cited opinion's own text, \decompParaPctSonnet{} percent of Sonnet~5's inaccurate quotations (\decompParaSonnet{} of them) are recognizable paraphrases of the correct case presented as verbatim quotation, \decompFabSonnet{} match nothing substantial in the cited opinion, and \decompOutSonnet{} cite authority outside the text store's scope; GPT-5.4-mini's split is \decompParaPctGpt{} percent paraphrase. For the strongest models the most common inaccurate quotation is a paraphrase of the cited opinion presented inside quotation marks.

\textbf{Real language, wrong case.} Searching every inaccurate quotation against a segmented corpus of 8,392 Supreme Court opinions locates \misattrTotal{} quotations across the six models (\misattrSonnet{} for Sonnet~5, \misattrQwen{} for Qwen3-30B) whose language appears verbatim in a different opinion than the one cited, where the matched probe is at least six words (a ten-word window for long quotations) so that stock legal phrases do not count. Quotations found nowhere in the corpus (\notInCorpusSonnet{} for Sonnet~5) include the paraphrases above and quotations from courts outside it. Because the attribution rule could itself misassign a quotation, the same search serves as an audit of the checker: it classified \falseAlarmSonnet{} of Sonnet~5's inaccurate verdicts and \falseAlarmQwen{} of Qwen3-30B's as the checker's own error, where the language was in the attributed case after all. A further screen for models reproducing the argued case's own later-decided opinion from memory found 41 candidates, of which 32 were record or statute text present in the prompt and the rest doctrinal formulas that the argued opinions were themselves quoting; the finding was withdrawn and is recorded in the ledger.

\textbf{Right case, wrong page.} Where a pinpoint and a quotation co-occur on a U.S. Reports authority, the quotation lies on the cited page \pinAtSonnet{} percent of the time for Sonnet~5, \pinAtGpt{} percent for GPT-5.4-mini, \pinAtDeepseek{} percent for DeepSeek, \pinAtLlama{} percent for Llama-4, \pinAtMistral{} percent for Mistral Small, and \pinAtQwen{} percent for Qwen3-30B, and on a different page of the cited case \pinWrongSonnet{} to \pinWrongMistral{} percent of the time (adjacent-page matches make up the remainder); pins outside the cited case are rare at every tier. This measures, from public data, the error class on which published detection agents have the lowest recall~\cite{liu2026whochecks}.

\textbf{Relevance judging: three failed calibrations.} Whether a real, correctly quoted case supports the proposition it is cited for cannot be looked up. Three language-model panels calibrated against the expert-labeled content-misrepresentation items of Liu et al.~\cite{liu2026whochecks}, with a pre-declared bar of 0.75 accuracy on decisive verdicts, scored 0.500, 0.700 (misrepresentation recall 2 of 11), and 0.594 (a frontier panel with 15,000-character grounded excerpts; recall 4 of 17). No verdict from a failed panel was interpreted; the result joins evidence that agreement statistics overstate judge validity~\cite{norman2026reliability,zheng2023judging}, and relevance is a stated limitation.

\section{Discussion}

Whether a model fabricates legal citations under realistic prompting depends on the model: for the 30B model a quota and an authority restriction triple the odds of a nonexistent citation, and the models above it hold existence but lose quotation accuracy, most under the temporal restriction. Restrictions on authority by date or jurisdiction are plausible drafting instructions, so this fragility is likely to be met in use.

Two practical conclusions follow from the revision experiment. Feeding deterministic checker output back into revision reduced the number of quotations and corrected few of them; a brief with fewer verbatim claims exposes fewer false ones, but a reader of the revised brief cannot tell which claims were demoted to paraphrase. The checker is therefore more useful as a gate on output, holding a draft until its citations resolve and its quotations match, than as feedback. Any system that feeds a probabilistic detector's flags to a capable model should measure the detector's false-positive rate first: the models that removed the most flagged quotations under true feedback also removed the most correct ones under false feedback.

The released verifier is usable by courts and regulators independently of the findings. Existence, verbatim quotation, and pinpoint page can be checked deterministically from public data, and the released verifier does so; court orders that require disclosure of AI assistance, of which the tracker records many~\cite{charlotin2025tracker}, could additionally require the checker's output on the filing. Whether a real citation supports the proposition cannot yet be checked; no panel we calibrated met the bar. With retrieval-grounded tools we expect existence failures to shrink and the residual classes, paraphrase in quotation marks and wrong pages, to remain, since they arise in composition rather than recall.

\section{Limitations}

The study covers a single domain and task: Supreme Court merits argument, in one packet format, with one fixed template per condition and a phrasing check on twelve matters in place of a full paraphrase factorial. The task is closed-book, which matches the sanctioned filings but not commercial legal tools. The stakes clause names a sanctions rule that does not govern the forum. The strict verbatim standard penalizes bracketed alterations and ellipses for humans and models alike, and the human reference differs from the model drafts in citation mix. The provenance corpus covers 8,392 Supreme Court opinions and cannot adjudicate quotations from other courts. The false-feedback control is all false; intermediate false-positive rates were not tested. The no-feedback control and the per-quotation trace were added after the pre-registered analysis, and the tests in Section~5 were chosen after the data were seen. Each cell was generated once at temperature 0; given the run-to-run instability documented for legal tasks~\cite{blairstanek2025unstable}, the per-cell rates carry sampling noise that repeated runs would bound and this study does not. On a twelve-matter check under alternative phrasings of every condition (the four models available when it was designed), combined-condition existence stayed within 0.06 of the original phrasing and no existence result changed sign, but strict quotation rates moved by up to forty points in either direction (GPT-5.4-mini \paraOrigStrictGptCombo{} to \paraParaStrictGptCombo, Sonnet~5 \paraOrigStrictSonnetCombo{} to \paraParaStrictSonnetCombo), so the quotation rates in Table~1 are specific to the templates used, and the within-model contrasts, which hold the template fixed, are the claims we rely on. Inference is per model at 48 matters, the independent units the clustering respects, so effects of a few points are not detectable, and the six-model set is one model per vendor rather than a sampled population, so capability, family, and training recipe are confounded.

\section{Conclusion}

Under prompting constraints of the kind found in practice, citation integrity degrades from the smallest model upward: nonexistent citations at 30B, misquotation through the middle of the range, and misattribution and wrong pages at the top. A deterministic checker built from public data detects each of these error classes. When its output is fed back into revision, the revised briefs contain fewer quotations and few corrected ones, and false feedback leads the same models to delete correct quotations.
