**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATUTE’S DISCLOSURE REQUIREMENTS ARE OVERBROAD AND UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE, VIOLATING THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

The Florida statute’s disclosure provisions sweep far too broadly, chilling protected First Amendment activity. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (holding that compelled disclosure of membership lists "may constitute as effective a restraint on freedom of association as the forms of governmental action in the cases above cited"). The requirement that lobbyists disclose "all lobbying expenditures" and categorize them—including vague terms like "research" and "communication"—lacks sufficient limiting principles. *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 253 (1957) (invalidating a statute requiring disclosure of lecture content as "too broad and indefinite").

The statute’s lack of clarity also renders it unconstitutionally vague. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (striking down a statute as void for vagueness where it failed to provide "sufficient warning" of prohibited conduct). The categories of disclosure are not clearly defined, leaving lobbyists to guess whether their activities fall within the statute’s reach. *United States v. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (holding that a statute must "define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited").

**II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN IMPOSES AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL RESTRAINT ON PROFESSIONAL SPEECH AND ASSOCIATION.**

The Florida statute’s ban on contingency-fee lobbying directly infringes on protected First Amendment rights. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945) (holding that a statute restricting solicitation of union membership violated the First Amendment). The ban restricts how lobbyists may contract for their services, effectively penalizing the exercise of professional speech. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 512 (1939) (invalidating a permit requirement for public assembly as an unconstitutional prior restraint).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that restrictions on professional speech must be narrowly tailored. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939) (holding that a city ordinance banning leafleting was unconstitutional because it was not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling interest). The Florida statute’s ban is not narrowly tailored; it sweeps broadly without justification. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (invalidating a statute requiring permits for religious solicitation as an unconstitutional prior restraint).

**III. THE STATUTE IS FACIALLY INVALID BECAUSE IT CHILLS PROTECTED SPEECH.**

The League’s facial challenge is proper because the statute’s overbreadth and vagueness deter protected activity. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 377 U.S. 288, 307 (1964) (holding that a facial challenge was appropriate where the statute "may have deterred" protected association). The statute forces lobbyists to choose between compliance and risking civil penalties, creating a classic chilling effect. *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 486 (1965) (recognizing that statutes with "a potentially inhibiting effect on speech" may be challenged facially).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of facial challenges in protecting First Amendment rights. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 432-33 (1963) (holding that a facial challenge was appropriate where the statute "may have operated to inhibit" protected speech). The Florida statute’s overbreadth and vagueness similarly justify a facial challenge.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in upholding the Florida statute. The disclosure requirements are unconstitutionally overbroad and vague, and the contingency-fee ban violates the First Amendment. The judgment below should be reversed.
