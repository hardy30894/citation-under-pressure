ARGUMENT

A district court's failure to comply with former Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not furnish an independent ground for relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 when the defendant knew of his right to appeal and deliberately forwent it. Collateral attack is available only when the asserted error constitutes "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A technical violation of the notice requirement does not meet that standard. United States v. Timmreck, 441 U.S. 780, 783-784 (1979); United States v. Addonizio, 442 U.S. 178, 185 (1979).

Rule 32 violations are therefore subject to harmless-error analysis on collateral review. Brecht v. Abrahamson, 507 U.S. 619, 637-638 (1993). The defendant must show that the omission "had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining" the outcome of the proceeding. Id. at 623 (quoting Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946)). Where, as here, the record establishes that the defendant was already aware of his appellate rights, the purpose of the rule has been fulfilled and no prejudice can be shown. See Rodriquez v. United States, 395 U.S. 327, 330 (1969) (recognizing that the right to appeal may be waived when the defendant makes a knowing and intelligent decision not to pursue it).

This Court has repeatedly declined to treat every departure from a federal rule as automatically cognizable on habeas. Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974). Constitutional claims themselves are subject to harmless-error review when the error is not structural. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967); Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 306-307 (1991). A Rule 32 omission is not structural; it does not affect the framework within which the trial proceeds. Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 468-469 (1997).

The court of appeals therefore correctly required a showing of prejudice and, having found none, properly affirmed the denial of relief.
