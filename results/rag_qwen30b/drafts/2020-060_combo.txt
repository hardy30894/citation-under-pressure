**ARGUMENT**

The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover cleanup costs from the United States. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) constituted a judicially approved settlement resolving Guam’s liability for response actions at the Ordot Dump, thereby triggering CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing Guam’s right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The Court of Appeals’ contrary holding misreads the statutory framework and ignores binding precedent governing the effect of consent decrees in environmental enforcement.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam’s Liability for Response Actions, Triggering Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has resolved its liability to the United States “for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties. The key inquiry is whether the agreement “resolved” liability. The Supreme Court has long held that a consent decree that imposes binding obligations and extinguishes liability constitutes a settlement within the meaning of CERCLA. In *Pearcy v. Stranahan*, 205 U.S. 257, 264 (1907), the Court emphasized that “the court takes judicial cognizance whether or not a given territory is within the boundaries of the United States, and is bound to take the fact as it really exists, however it may be averred to be.” This principle applies with equal force to the legal effect of judicially approved agreements. The 2004 Consent Decree was entered by the District Court of Guam, approved by the court, and is binding on both parties. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system—obligations that directly address the response actions mandated by the Clean Water Act and CERCLA. The decree’s terms were not merely procedural; they imposed substantive, enforceable duties that fulfilled the core requirements of a response action.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that consent decrees in environmental cases are not mere procedural devices but substantive settlements that resolve liability. In *Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock*, 347 U.S. 110, 113 (1954), the Court stated that “the power of the agency head to make the determinations or decisions specified in paragraphs (12), (13), (14), (15), and (16) of section 2 (c) and in section 5 (a) shall not be delegable,” underscoring the finality and legal weight of administrative and judicially approved agreements. The 2004 Consent Decree, approved by a federal court, carries the same legal force. It was not a mere agreement to negotiate; it was a final adjudication of obligations. The fact that the decree disclaimed liability does not alter its legal effect. As the Court held in *Vilas v. City of Manila*, 220 U.S. 345, 351 (1911, “it is equally settled in the same public law that that great body of municipal law which regulates private and domestic rights continues in force until abrogated or changed by the new ruler.” The legal effect of a consent decree is not negated by a disclaimer of liability; rather, the substance of the agreement governs. Here, the substance is clear: Guam agreed to perform response actions and pay penalties, thereby resolving its liability for those actions.

**II. Section 107(a) Is Inapplicable Once a Settlement Triggers Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

The Court of Appeals erred in treating § 107(a) and § 113(f)(3)(B) as alternative remedies. They are not. Once a party has settled its liability under § 113(f)(3)(B), it may no longer pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The Supreme Court has long recognized that settlements in federal enforcement actions extinguish the right to pursue further claims. In *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 220 (1845), the Court held that “nothing remained to the United States, according to the terms of the agreement, but the public lands,” and that “an express stipulation had been inserted in the agreement, granting the municipal right of sovereignty and eminent domain to the United States, such stipulation would have been void and inoperative.” This principle applies here: once the court approved the Consent Decree, the United States’ claim against Guam was resolved, and Guam’s liability was extinguished. The Court of Appeals’ conclusion that Guam could still pursue § 107(a) is inconsistent with the statutory structure and the finality of judicially approved settlements.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that consent decrees are binding and enforceable. In *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad*, 118 U.S. 394, 400 (1886), the Court stated that “the right of removal was recognized by the State court, and the action proceeded in the Circuit Court,” emphasizing the binding nature of judicial decisions. The 2004 Consent Decree was not a mere administrative agreement; it was a judicially approved settlement with the full force of law. The fact that the decree was entered in a civil action under the Clean Water Act does not diminish its effect under CERCLA. The Supreme Court has held that “the collection of leachate and runoff” is a “remedial action” under CERCLA, § 101(24), and the decree required exactly that. Thus, the decree resolved liability for a response action within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B).

**III. The Statute of Limitations Bars Guam’s Claim**

The Court of Appeals’ conclusion that Guam may proceed under § 107(a) is untenable because it ignores the statutory framework. Section 113(g)(3) imposes a three-year statute of limitations on contribution claims, which began to run when the Consent Decree was entered in 2004. The Court of Appeals’ decision effectively allows Guam to bypass this limitation
