ARGUMENT

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. The Third Circuit’s objective “reasonable speaker” standard, drawn from United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), cannot be reconciled with controlling precedent.

“True threats” are statements “where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). This definition incorporates the speaker’s purpose; an objective test that ignores whether the speaker “means” the words as a threat therefore fails to identify unprotected speech. The same subjective element appears in the incitement doctrine, which protects advocacy unless “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action.” Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (per curiam). Because threats and incitement are adjacent categories of unprotected speech, both demand proof of the speaker’s intent. See also Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam) (distinguishing “political hyperbole” from true threats by reference to speaker’s purpose).

First Amendment limitations on other categories of speech confirm the point. Defamation liability requires “actual malice”—knowledge that the statement was false or reckless disregard of its falsity. New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 279-80 (1964). Child-pornography prohibitions likewise turn on the defendant’s knowledge of the victim’s age. United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 78 (1994). A negligence standard for threats would be anomalous.

The Court has repeatedly rejected efforts to dilute mens rea in speech cases. See, e.g., R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 388 (1992) (content-based restrictions must be narrowly tailored to the harm); United States v. Alvarez, 567 U.S. 709, 717-18 (2012) (plurality opinion) (false statements protected absent specific intent to defraud). These authorities establish that the government may not criminalize speech based solely on how a reasonable listener would perceive it.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on an objective test also conflicts with decisions of other circuits that have read Black to require subjective intent. See, e.g., United States v. Cassel, 408 F.3d 622, 631-33 (9th Cir. 2005); United States v. Heineman, 767 F.3d 970, 978 (10th Cir. 2014); United States v. Bagdasarian, 652 F.3d 1113, 1117-18 (9th Cir. 2011). Because the jury was instructed only on objective foreseeability, the convictions rest on an impermissible standard and must be reversed.
