**ARGUMENT**

I. Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule for state judges does not violate the ADEA because appointed judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” within 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office … or any person … [who is] an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In **Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d 598, 602–06 (8th Cir. 1990)**, the court correctly held that Missouri judges selected under the Missouri Plan fall within that exclusion. That conclusion is compelled by the statutory text, structure, and the federalism-sensitive principle that Congress must speak clearly when it intrudes on a State’s authority over its judiciary. See **United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971)**.

First, judges necessarily exercise policymaking authority. As the Eighth Circuit recognized, judging is not merely the mechanical application of fixed rules; it often requires resolving ambiguities, filling gaps in constitutional and statutory law, and creating rules that guide future conduct. **Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d at 604–05**. That understanding accords with the First Circuit’s decision in **EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988)**, which held that “policymaking” is part of the judicial function because judging involves lawmaking “to fill the interstices of authority.” See also **EEOC v. Massachusetts, 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)**.

Second, the ADEA’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended a narrow exemption for “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” **H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 950, 92d Cong., 2d Sess. 9–10 (1972), reprinted in 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179, 2180**. State judges fit comfortably within that language. As the Eighth Circuit explained, state courts are among the most powerful agencies of state government, and judges, especially those on a state’s highest courts, exercise substantial supervisory and rulemaking authority. **Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d at 605–06**.

Third, interpreting the ADEA to reach appointed state judges would create an implausible anomaly: elected judges would be excluded by the statute, yet appointed judges performing the same functions would be covered. **Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d at 605–06**. Congress is not presumed to have enacted such a federalism-disrupting distinction without clear indication. **Bass, 404 U.S. at 349**.

Accordingly, the state judges are not “employees” covered by the ADEA, and Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not conflict with federal law. See **Orzel v. City of Wauwatosa Fire Dep’t, 697 F.2d 743, 752 (7th Cir. 1983)**.

II. Missouri’s age-70 retirement requirement does not violate the Equal Protection Clause because age is not a suspect classification and the rule is rationally related to legitimate state interests.

Age-based classifications receive rational basis review. **Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312–14 (1976) (per curiam)**. Under that deferential standard, a classification must be upheld if any reasonably conceivable state of facts could provide a rational basis for it. **FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307, 313–15 (1993)**.

Missouri has several legitimate interests supporting mandatory retirement for judges. The Eighth Circuit identified the State’s interest in avoiding the difficulty of individualized competence evaluations, ensuring judicial vigor and mental acuity, reducing the risk of delay or incapacity in the administration of justice, and promoting a bench that remains responsive to the experiences of the community. **Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d at 606–07**. These are rational considerations. Other courts have reached the same conclusion. See **Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979)**; **Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980)**; **Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988)**.

Petitioners’ disagreement with the empirical wisdom of Missouri’s policy cannot establish an equal protection violation. Under **Murgia, 427 U.S. at 314–15**, the Constitution does not require the State to adopt the least restrictive or most scientifically precise method of regulating judicial tenure. It requires only a rational basis, and Missouri plainly has one. See **Gregory v. Ashcroft, 898 F.2d at 607**.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
