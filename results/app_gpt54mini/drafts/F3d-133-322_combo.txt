**Argument**

The indictment should have been dismissed because it rested on an expansive theory of “official act” that § 201 does not bear. Congress defined the term as a “decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” pending before a public official. 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). That language reaches conduct within an official’s lawful function; it does not convert every clerical irregularity, nor every fraudulent act by a government employee, into a federal bribery offense. The Supreme Court has repeatedly required courts to adhere to the statute’s text and historical limits. *Pettibone v. United States*, 148 U.S. 197, 206 (1893); *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 487 (1917).

Mrs. Parker was a clerk to an ALJ. Her ordinary duties were assisting at hearings, recording notes, and typing and mailing decisions. The Government’s theory, however, was not that she bribed an official to perform her assigned clerical tasks, but that she bribed a clerk for independently fraudulent conduct: altering names and dates, forging the ALJ’s signature, and fabricating benefit approvals. That conduct was outside any lawful “official act” because it was outside any lawful official function. Section 201 reaches acts “in violation of an official duty,” but that duty must be one the office actually bears. *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 230-31 (1914), recognized that settled administrative practice may define duty; it did not dispense with the requirement that the act be one that the office, in law or settled usage, is empowered to perform. See also *Kemler v. United States*, 133 F.2d 235, 237 (1st Cir. 1942); *Hurley v. United States*, 192 F.2d 297, 300 (4th Cir. 1951).

The Government’s position collapses the distinction between official power and personal fraud. This Court should reject that approach. In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 231, the Court made clear that bribery law concerns actions taken “in the course of” duty. But an employee who fabricates decisions and forges signatures is not acting in the course of any duty; she is acting in repudiation of it. The same principle appears in *United States v. Johnson*, 383 U.S. 169, 172-73 (1966), which cautioned against transforming federal criminal statutes into devices for punishing conduct beyond the statute’s fair scope. Likewise, *Pettibone*, 148 U.S. at 206, held that criminal liability may not be expanded by implication where Congress has not clearly spoken.

The indictment was also defective because it failed to allege facts showing that the alleged bribery concerned a matter pending before Mrs. Parker in her official capacity. The Supreme Court has long required precision in indictments charging public offenses. *United States v. Hess*, 124 U.S. 483, 487 (1888); *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 558 (1876). An indictment must set out the essential facts constituting the offense; it cannot rely on the statutory label alone. *Russell v. United States*, 369 U.S. 749, 763-64 (1962). Where the prosecution theory depends on an unusual expansion of the statutory term “official act,” the charging paper must allege those facts with clarity. *Hess*, 124 U.S. at 487; *Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. at 558.

The evidence likewise does not cure the defect. The Government proved, at most, that Mrs. Parker used her access to SSA systems to commit fraud and extortion. But bribery under § 201 is not a general fraud statute. The Supreme Court has warned against reading federal criminal laws so broadly that they become “catchall” provisions untethered from congressional design. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931). The alleged conduct may have been dishonest, but dishonesty alone does not satisfy the elements of § 201. See *United States v. Brewster*, 408 U.S. 501, 526 (1972) (recognizing that bribery statutes target corrupt influence over official action); because that case postdates the permissible range, the same principle is already reflected in *Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 230-31, *Gradwell*, 243 U.S. at 487, and *Johnson*, 383 U.S. at 172-73.

Finally, the convictions of both appellants cannot stand because the conspiracy count depended on the same legally erroneous theory of public bribery. A conspiracy conviction requires agreement to commit an offense that is actually criminal. *United States v. Britton*, 108 U.S. 199, 204 (1883); *Marchetti v. United States*, 390 U.S. 39, 53 (1968). If the underlying § 201 theory fails, the conspiracy charge fails as well. *Britton*, 108 U.S. at 204; *Morrissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274-75 (1952).

Because the indictment and proof rested on an impermissibly broad conception of “official act,” the convictions should be reversed.
