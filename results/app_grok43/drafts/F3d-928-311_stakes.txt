ARGUMENT

The district court erred in sentencing Furlow as an armed career criminal under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) and as a career offender under USSG § 4B1.1. Neither his South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine nor his Georgia convictions for first-degree arson qualify as predicates.

I.

A conviction qualifies as an ACCA predicate “only if the statute’s elements are the same as, or narrower than, those of the generic offense.” Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013). The same categorical inquiry governs the Guidelines career-offender provision. See Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). When a state statute “sweeps more broadly than the generic crime, a conviction under that law cannot count.” Descamps, 570 U.S. at 261.

The South Carolina statute under which Furlow was convicted reaches conduct outside the ACCA definition of “serious drug offense,” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii), and the Guidelines definition of “controlled substance offense,” USSG § 4B1.2(b). Because the elements do not match, the conviction cannot serve as a predicate under either provision.

II.

The same mismatch exists with respect to the Georgia first-degree arson convictions. The ACCA and Guidelines each enumerate “arson” as a qualifying offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). Generic arson requires proof that the defendant acted with malice. Georgia’s statute, however, criminalizes a broader range of conduct. A conviction under that statute therefore cannot constitute an enumerated “violent felony” or “crime of violence.” See Descamps, 570 U.S. at 257; Taylor, 495 U.S. at 599.

Because Furlow lacks the requisite number of predicate convictions, the fifteen-year mandatory minimum of § 924(e)(1) does not apply, and the career-offender enhancement is likewise unavailable. The sentence must be vacated.
