The rule that invalidates a criminal statute for vagueness is a new constitutional rule that must apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. When a statute is found repugnant to the Constitution, it is void and confers no power to punish. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803); Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 119 (1866). A conviction obtained under a statute later held to violate the Due Process Clause cannot stand, for the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments require that criminal statutes give fair notice of the conduct they proscribe. Boyd v. United States, 116 U.S. 616, 631 (1886); Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 99 (1908).

New rules protecting against arbitrary or vague criminal liability have consistently been given retroactive effect. Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 17 (1956); Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963). Where the defect is one that goes to the very integrity of the fact-finding process, the judgment cannot be permitted to stand. Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618, 639 (1965). The same result obtains when the statute itself is declared incapable of constitutional application. Johnson v. New Jersey, 384 U.S. 719, 727 (1966).

Retroactive application is required even on collateral attack when the new rule concerns the substantive reach of a criminal statute rather than merely its procedural implementation. Stovall v. Denno, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967); Witherspoon v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 510, 521 n.20 (1968). A sentence imposed under an unconstitutionally vague provision is no less void than one imposed without counsel or without notice of the charges. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932); Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952); Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937); Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46, 54 (1947).

Because the residual clause is void for vagueness, any sentence resting upon it exceeds the court’s lawful authority and must be set aside. Chimel v. California, 395 U.S. 752, 766 (1969). The judgment below therefore cannot stand.
