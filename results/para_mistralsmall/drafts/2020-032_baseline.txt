**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Court Should Affirm the Eleventh Circuit’s Decision Because Petitioners’ Claims Are Moot.**

A case is moot when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). This Court has long held that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice” unless “it is absolutely clear the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000) (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College (GGC) revised its challenged policies to permit expressive activities anywhere on campus without a permit, rendering Petitioners’ claims for prospective relief moot. *See* 18-12676, ECF No. 38-1 at 5 (GGC’s revised “Freedom of Expression Policy”); *id.* at 6 (GGC’s revised “Student Code of Conduct”). Because GGC’s policy changes are not “transitory or evanescent,” *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. at 189, and Petitioners do not allege a reasonable expectation of recurrence, their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot.

Petitioners argue that their request for nominal damages preserves a live controversy. However, this Court has repeatedly held that a request for nominal damages does not save a moot claim. *See Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 480 (1990) (“[A] request for nominal damages does not ordinarily suffice to prevent mootness.”); *Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc) (“[A] prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.”). The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this precedent in affirming the district court’s dismissal. *See* 18-12677, ECF No. 38 at 17-18.

Petitioners’ reliance on *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95 (1983), is misplaced. In *Lyons*, the Court held that a plaintiff seeking injunctive relief must show a “real and immediate threat” of future injury. *Id.* at 105. Here, Petitioners do not allege any such threat; GGC’s policy changes mooted their claims entirely. *See id.* at 111 (“[T]he federal judiciary may not entertain claims for injunctive relief unless there is a real and immediate threat that the plaintiff will again be wronged.”).

**II. Petitioners’ Claims for Nominal Damages Do Not Preserve a Live Controversy.**

Petitioners contend that their complaint sought compensatory damages, not merely nominal damages. However, the Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that Petitioners’ complaint did not plausibly allege compensable harm. *See Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (“Compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the ‘abstract value’ of the constitutional rights that may have been violated.”). Petitioners’ complaint alleged only “irreparable harm” without specifying any concrete injury. *See* 18-12676, ECF No. 1 at ¶¶ 54, 60, 66, 72. This is insufficient to state a claim for compensatory damages. *See Slicker*, 215 F.3d at 1231.

Petitioners’ attempt to amend their complaint after the district court’s ruling does not revive their claims. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend, as Petitioners failed to follow proper procedural rules. *See Santiago v. Wood*, 904 F.2d 673, 675 (11th Cir. 1990) (reviewing denial of leave to amend for abuse of discretion). Even if Petitioners had properly moved to amend, their belated attempt to add a claim for compensatory damages would not retroactively moot their claims. *See Czeremcha v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers*, 724 F.2d 1552, 1556 (11th Cir. 1984) (noting that amendments should be granted liberally but do not retroactively cure mootness).

**III. The Court Should Affirm the Judgment Below.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Petitioners’ claims are moot and that their request for nominal damages does not preserve a live controversy. This Court should affirm the judgment below, as it aligns with well-established precedent on mootness and the limited role of nominal damages in preserving constitutional challenges. *See Lewis*, 494 U.S. at 480; *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255.
