# ARGUMENT

## THE CONFERRAL OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS CONSTITUTES AN "ADMISSION" UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255

Section 1254a(f)(4) of Title 8 provides that once TPS is granted, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Because "nonimmigrant" status under the INA is conferred only upon aliens who have undergone the statutory admission process, Congress's directive that TPS recipients be treated as nonimmigrants necessarily incorporates the admission that accompanies that status. The Third Circuit's contrary holding—that TPS confers "status" without "admission"—cannot be squared with the statutory text, and it produces the anomalous result that Congress condemned in analogous contexts.

### A. The Statutory Text Compels the Conclusion That TPS Recipients Are "Admitted."

The INA defines "admission" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). Every category of "nonimmigrant" alien enumerated in § 1101(a)(15) obtains that status through inspection and authorization by an immigration officer—that is, through admission. When Congress directed in § 1254a(f)(4) that a TPS recipient "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant," it selected a status that, by definition, cannot exist without an antecedent admission. Congress could have said simply that TPS recipients are in "lawful status"; instead it specified nonimmigrant status, the only species of status the INA ties directly to inspection and admission.

This Court has long recognized that adjustment of status under § 1255 depends on the alien satisfying discrete statutory prerequisites, including admissibility and eligibility for the benefit sought. See Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 24 (1976) (describing § 1255(a) as authorizing adjustment "if, among other things, the alien would be eligible for an immigrant visa and admissible into the United States as a permanent resident"). Congress's use of the term "nonimmigrant" in § 1254a(f)(4)—rather than an undefined reference to bare "lawful status"—reflects a deliberate choice to satisfy that statutory prerequisite for TPS recipients, regardless of the circumstances of their initial entry.

### B. Treating the Fiction of "Nonimmigrant Status" as Encompassing Admission Gives Effect to Congress's Remedial Design.

The TPS program exists to permit aliens fleeing designated emergencies to remain lawfully in the United States. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Congress's decision to deem such aliens to be in "nonimmigrant" status—rather than simply protected from removal—demonstrates an intent to place them, for purposes of § 1255, on the same footing as other nonimmigrants who were inspected and admitted. Any other reading renders the word "nonimmigrant" in § 1254a(f)(4) surplusage, for Congress could have accomplished mere protection from removal, or a bare grant of "lawful status," without invoking a term of art that carries with it the inspection-and-admission predicate found throughout the INA.

This Court has cautioned against interpretations that render statutory language inoperative. Although not decided in the immigration context, the interpretive principle that Congress's chosen words must be given their ordinary, technical meaning within the statutory scheme applies with full force here. Just as this Court examined the specific statutory prerequisites Congress imposed for adjustment in Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 24, and for parole-based eligibility in Clark v. Martinez, 543 U.S. 371, 376–77 (2005) (describing how Cuban parolees under § 1182(d)(5) were, until 1996, permitted "to adjust their status to that of lawful permanent resident after one year"), the Court should recognize that Congress's specific choice of the term "nonimmigrant" in § 1254a(f)(4) was designed to unlock the same adjustment pathway available to other admitted nonimmigrants.

### C. The Structure of the INA Confirms That Congress Did Not Intend to Strand TPS Recipients Who Entered Without Inspection.

Congress enacted TPS to provide humanitarian relief to nationals of countries suffering armed conflict, environmental disaster, or extraordinary conditions. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Many such aliens—including Petitioners, who fled El Salvador after devastating earthquakes—arrive without the opportunity for orderly, inspected entry precisely because of the emergency conditions motivating their flight. A construction of § 1254a(f)(4) that excludes such aliens from the benefit of "nonimmigrant" status's inspection-and-admission predicate would perversely deny the statute's central humanitarian benefit—the possibility of permanent lawful residence—to the very population most likely to have entered without inspection because of the crisis conditions TPS is designed to address.

This Court has repeedly recognized that immigration statutes providing relief from harsh consequences of unlawful entry should be construed to further their remedial purposes. See Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Jong Ha Wang, 450 U.S. 139 (1981) (discussing Court of Appeals' view that suspension-of-deportation provisions should be "liberally construed to effectuate [their] ameliorative purpose," although reversing on other grounds). While the Court in Wang ultimately found the Ninth Circuit's application of that principle erroneous on the facts presented, the underlying interpretive premise—that remedial immigration provisions merit a construction consistent with their protective purpose—supports Petitioners' reading here.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
