**Argument**

The Constitution does not give Clark a separate due process right to introduce psychiatric testimony to negate mens rea apart from the State’s insanity rules. The Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the charged offense, *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), but States retain broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the defenses available to them. Long before 1970, this Court recognized that “the practice of psychiatry is not yet a science,” *Nagel v. State*, 83 N.J.L. 321, 324, 84 A. 110, 111 (1912), and that a State may confine mental-disease evidence to the legal issue of insanity without violating due process.

Arizona’s rule is exactly that sort of permissible limitation. A defendant may present an insanity defense and offer expert proof on that issue, but the State may refuse to let psychiatric opinion be used to carve out a free-floating, partial defense that would undercut the legislature’s definition of criminal responsibility. This Court has repeatedly upheld state authority to define insanity and to regulate the use of evidence bearing on mental disease. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952), sustained a state law placing the burden of proving insanity on the defendant; if a State may require the defendant to establish insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, it necessarily follows that the State may also channel mental-illness evidence to that affirmative defense. Likewise, *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1895), and *Smith v. United States*, 237 U.S. 1, 10-11 (1915), recognized that the insanity inquiry is distinct from the elements of the offense and is governed by legal rules the States may structure.

The cases cited by petitioner do not establish a constitutional right to present expert “diminished capacity” evidence whenever mental illness is relevant to intent. To the contrary, the Court has emphasized that the admissibility of evidence is ordinarily a matter for the States, so long as the defendant is not arbitrarily deprived of a meaningful opportunity to defend. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973), is not available to petitioner in any event because it postdates 1970; but even the earlier cases on which such arguments rest show only that a defendant must be allowed to present relevant, competent evidence under ordinary rules. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), invalidated a statute that barred an accomplice’s testimony for the defense, but it did not hold that the Constitution requires admission of all expert testimony bearing on mental state. And *Hinton v. Alabama*, 271 Ala. 44, 51, 122 So. 2d 737, 742 (1960), reflects the traditional rule that expert testimony is admissible only when it assists rather than supplants the jury’s determination of a fact issue; Arizona’s rule merely keeps psychiatric opinion within the insanity framework the State has chosen.

That distinction is constitutionally sound because intent remains a jury question proved from conduct and circumstances, not from post hoc expert conclusions about a defendant’s internal psychology. In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 584-85 (1884), the Court upheld broad exclusionary authority over witnesses and evidence where the State adopted a general rule of competency. Similarly, *Turner v. American Security & Trust Co.*, 213 U.S. 257, 267-68 (1909), reaffirmed that States may regulate the form and scope of proof. Arizona did not prevent Clark from contesting intent through lay testimony, cross-examination, or proof of the surrounding events; it only prevented him from converting psychiatric diagnosis into a second, judicially created mens rea defense.

Finally, Clark’s theory would constitutionalize a doctrine this Court has never required and that many States have rejected. No pre-1970 decision holds that due process compels admission of expert testimony to negate mens rea where the State has provided an insanity defense. The most that can be said is that mental disease evidence may be relevant to culpability in some jurisdictions. But relevance is not constitutional compulsion. As *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 799, makes clear, “we cannot say that the Oregon rule ... is so lacking in reason as to deprive petitioner of due process of law.” Arizona’s rule is likewise rational: it preserves a clear line between insanity, which excuses criminal responsibility, and intent, which defines the offense. The Constitution does not require States to erase that line merely because a defendant has introduced expert psychiatric testimony.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed.
