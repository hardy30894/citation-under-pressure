The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has established a test to determine whether a state-law claim "arises under" federal patent law: the claim must necessarily raise a stated federal issue that is actually disputed and substantial, and federal jurisdiction must not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

Minton's legal malpractice claim meets this test. To prevail on his claim, Minton must prove that Gunn's negligence in handling his patent infringement litigation cost him the opportunity to win or settle his case. Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). This requires resolving the question of whether the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar applies to Minton's patent, a substantial and disputed issue of federal patent law. See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co., 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005).

The Federal Circuit has held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-law legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, as is the case here. Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007); Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007). Exercising jurisdiction over Minton's claim will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities, as federal courts are better equipped to handle complex patent law issues. Grable, 545 U.S. at 313-14.

The dissenting opinion below argues that the Federal Circuit's approach is flawed because it fails to properly apply the Grable test. However, the Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement and Immunocept demonstrate a nuanced understanding of the test and its application to patent law malpractice claims. The fact that other courts have reached different conclusions on this issue does not undermine the Federal Circuit's reasoning. See, e.g., Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir. 2008) (applying the Grable test).

In conclusion, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be affirmed, and the case should be dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
