The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and is inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). In Tennessee Electric Power Co., the Court held that private parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim because the Amendment is a limitation on federal power that protects the states, not individual citizens. Id. at 143-144. The Court stated that "the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144.

The majority of circuit courts have followed this holding, denying private parties standing to bring Tenth Amendment challenges. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir.2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir.2009); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir.2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir.2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir.2004). This Court should join these circuits and hold that Bond lacks standing to challenge § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds.

Bond's argument that Tennessee Electric Power Co. is dicta is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court's holding on standing is an alternative ground for its decision, rather than obiter dictum. See Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 34. Moreover, the standing question concerns the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, and its resolution predominates any discussion of the merits. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998).

In any event, even if Bond had standing, her challenge to § 229 would fail on the merits. The statute is a valid exercise of Congress's power under the Necessary and Proper Clause to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention, a treaty ratified by the United States. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). The Treaty Power grants Congress the authority to enact laws necessary to effectuate the treaty's provisions, even if those laws might otherwise be beyond Congress's enumerated powers. Id. at 433.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
