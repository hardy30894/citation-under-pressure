The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s minimal regulation of uninspected vessels categorically preempts OSHA under § 4(b)(1). The Occupational Safety and Health Act withdraws OSHA authority only where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” as to the working conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court has repeatedly described that provision as turning on actual, overlapping regulation of the relevant workplace conditions, not on a broad inference of agency dominance over an industry. See Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Assn., 505 U.S. 88 (1992); Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall, 445 U.S. 1 (1980).

That principle controls here. OSHA’s mission is to assure “safe and healthful working conditions,” and Congress authorized nationwide standards for workplace hazards. American Textile Manufacturers Institute, Inc. v. Donovan, 452 U.S. 490 (1981); Atlas Roofing Co. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Comm’n, 430 U.S. 442 (1977). Section 4(b)(1) is an exception to that general rule and should not be enlarged beyond its terms. Gade, 505 U.S. 88. The statutory question is therefore not whether the Coast Guard has some authority over vessels, but whether it has exercised authority over the specific occupational hazards OSHA cited. See Gade, 505 U.S. 88; Whirlpool, 445 U.S. 1.

On this record, it has not. The Coast Guard’s regulation of uninspected vessels is “minimal” and limited to discrete items such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and similar equipment. That is not the kind of comprehensive safety regime that displaces OSHA across the board. Congress certainly knew how to create specific maritime regulatory schemes when it wanted to; but § 4(b)(1) requires an agency’s exercise of authority over the “working conditions” in question, not merely its possession of some maritime safety powers. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); Gade, 505 U.S. 88. As this Court has explained in a different OSHA context, the Act’s standards are meant to operate unless another federal regime actually governs the issue, and OSHA may not be excluded by speculation or general agency background authority. Whirlpool, 445 U.S. 1.

The Fifth Circuit relied heavily on Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). But those cases do not answer the question presented here. Neither decision held that every uninspected vessel is outside OSHA whenever the Coast Guard exercises any authority at all. Rather, they reflected the Fifth Circuit’s view that seamen on vessels in navigation were subject to Coast Guard regulation in the circumstances before it. Clary, 609 F.2d 1120; Donovan, 720 F.2d 825. That reading cannot be squared with the text of § 4(b)(1), which asks whether another agency “exercise[s] statutory authority” over the relevant conditions, not whether it has some general maritime jurisdiction. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); Gade, 505 U.S. 88.

Nor does the fact that Rig 52 was an uninspected vessel make OSHA’s jurisdiction vanish. The Fifth Circuit treated the Coast Guard’s limited authority over uninspected vessels as enough to displace OSHA, but the cases and statutes cited by that court show only that the Coast Guard may regulate selected safety items on such vessels, not that it has occupied the field of occupational safety and health. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. pts. 25-26. Congress’ purpose was to avoid duplicative regulation of the same hazard, not to create immunity from OSHA whenever another agency regulates anything on the same platform. Gade, 505 U.S. 88; Whirlpool, 445 U.S. 1.

This Court’s maritime decisions also counsel against the Fifth Circuit’s sweeping rule. In Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Court emphasized that offshore work presents distinct hazards and that Congress and the Coast Guard have allocated regulatory responsibility with attention to the particular workplace. The same logic defeats a blanket preemption rule here: the relevant inquiry is hazard-specific regulation, not vessel status in the abstract. Herb’s Welding, 470 U.S. 414; Gade, 505 U.S. 88.

Because the Coast Guard had not exercised comprehensive authority over the occupational hazards OSHA cited on Rig 52, § 4(b)(1) did not preempt OSHA. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule would convert a narrow exception into a maritime immunity doctrine, contrary to the Act’s text and this Court’s precedents. Gade, 505 U.S. 88; Atlas Roofing, 430 U.S. 442; Whirlpool, 445 U.S. 1.
