**Argument**

The writ should be denied because a district court’s failure to give the notice of appeal required by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure does not itself warrant habeas relief where, as here, the defendant already knew of the right and elected not to appeal. Habeas relief is reserved for custody that violates the Constitution or laws of the United States, not for every procedural misstep. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947); *Stonebreaker v. Smyth*, 163 F.2d 498, 499 (4th Cir. 1947). A collateral attack cannot be used as a substitute for a direct appeal that the defendant knowingly chose to forego. *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178-79; *United States v. Smith*, 331 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1947).

Rule-based errors are not automatically jurisdictional and do not invariably require reversal. This Court has long distinguished between mandatory procedures and defects that are harmless where they cause no prejudice. In *Brock v. North Carolina*, 344 U.S. 424, 427-28 (1953), the Court rejected a technical challenge where the asserted error did not undermine the fairness of the proceeding. Likewise, *Harrington v. California*, 395 U.S. 250, 254 (1969), held that constitutional error may be harmless where it had no substantial effect on the outcome. The same principle applies here with even greater force: if the defendant already knew of his right to appeal, the district court’s omission did not deprive him of any opportunity to seek review.

The core purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is notice. A defendant cannot show prejudice from the failure to receive notice if he already had actual knowledge of the right. The law does not require the doing of a vain thing. *Norton v. Shelby County*, 118 U.S. 425, 440 (1886); *Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Minneapolis Civic & Commerce Ass’n*, 247 U.S. 490, 501 (1918). Put differently, relief is not justified where the asserted omission did not alter the defendant’s legal position. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 511 (1954), explains that collateral remedies do not exist to redress every irregularity, but to correct fundamental defects.

Nor does the silence of the record automatically help petitioner. Long before the Rules, this Court recognized that not every failure to advise a defendant of a procedural right invalidates a conviction absent actual prejudice. In *Knewel v. Egan*, 268 U.S. 442, 446-47 (1925), the Court denied habeas relief where the asserted irregularity did not amount to a denial of due process. Similarly, *Frank v. Mangum*, 237 U.S. 309, 327-28 (1915), and *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 87-88 (1923), make clear that collateral relief turns on fundamental unfairness, not mere deviations from ideal procedure. Petitioner has shown none.

Petitioner’s position would also collapse the distinction between a Rule violation and a miscarriage of justice. But the Court has never treated every departure from procedural form as a ground for setting aside a conviction. In *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178-79, the Court explained that habeas is not for the correction of ordinary trial errors that could have been raised by appeal. And in *Sunal*’s companion principle, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938), the Court emphasized that waiver turns on an intentional relinquishment of a known right. The District Court found exactly that here: Peguero knew of his right to appeal and chose not to do so. Once that factual finding is accepted, there is no denial of the right to appeal that can support collateral relief.

Petitioner may argue that any Rule 32 violation is per se reversible. But the Court has repeatedly declined to adopt such rigid rules where the error causes no prejudice. *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371-72 (1951), and *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946), both instruct that convictions are not to be upset for technical mistakes that do not affect substantial rights. The same logic controls here. If the defendant already knew he could appeal, the omission of the formal advisement did not affect his substantial rights, because it did not deprive him of an informed choice.

Finally, habeas relief would be especially unwarranted where, as here, petitioner waited years before seeking collateral review. The law disfavors allowing a defendant to remain silent in the trial court, forego appeal, and later invoke habeas to reopen the case. *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178-79; *Stonebreaker*, 163 F.2d at 499. The district court’s factual finding that petitioner knew of his appeal right is therefore dispositive. Because the omitted warning did not mislead him, did not prejudice him, and did not prevent an appeal he otherwise would have taken, the judgment below should be affirmed.
