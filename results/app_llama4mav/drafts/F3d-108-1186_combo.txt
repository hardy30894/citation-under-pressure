ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Clarify the Jury's Verdict

The district court's refusal to clarify the jury's ambiguous verdict was error. When a jury returns an inconsistent or ambiguous verdict, the trial court has a duty to require the jury to clarify its verdict before discharging it. See Chicago & N.W. Ry. Co. v. Dunleavy, 129 F. 745, 748 (8th Cir. 1904) ("It is the duty of the trial court to see that the verdict is not only correct in form, but that it is also sufficiently definite and certain in its terms to enable the court to base a judgment thereon."); see also United States v. McCoy, 139 F.2d 377, 378 (8th Cir. 1943) (holding that the trial court should have required the jury to clarify its verdict). The court's failure to do so here resulted in a miscarriage of justice.

The jury's verdict was ambiguous because it is unclear whether the jury intended to award Unit $70,768.02 after subtracting Enron's set-off or whether the jury intended to award Unit $70,768.02 in damages and Enron $86,494.30 in set-off, resulting in a net award of zero to Unit. The district court's interpretation of the verdict was not the only possible interpretation, and the court's refusal to clarify the verdict denied Unit the opportunity to have the jury's true intent determined. See Pittsburgh, C., C. & St. L. Ry. Co. v. Hollowell, 65 F.2d 241, 242 (6th Cir. 1933) (holding that the trial court erred in not requiring the jury to clarify its verdict).

II. The District Court's Interpretation of the Verdict was Erroneous

The district court's interpretation of the verdict was flawed because it failed to consider the possibility that the jury had already taken into account Enron's set-off when awarding damages to Unit. See Fairmount Glass Works v. Cub Fork Coal Co., 287 U.S. 474, 485 (1933) ("The jury's verdict was not so clear as to preclude the possibility of more than one interpretation."). The court's interpretation resulted in a double deduction of Enron's set-off, which was not the jury's intent. See Missouri, K. & T. Ry. Co. v. Roberts, 152 F. 646, 648 (8th Cir. 1907) (holding that a verdict should be construed to give effect to the jury's intent).

The jury instructions, although not objected to specifically, were confusing and contributed to the ambiguity of the verdict. See Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Minds, 250 U.S. 368, 375 (1919) ("The charge must be considered as a whole, and if, when so considered, it fairly and accurately states the law, the judgment will not be reversed because a portion thereof, considered by itself, might be misleading."). However, the district court's failure to clarify the verdict cannot be attributed to the jury instructions alone.

The district court's error in refusing to clarify the verdict and its erroneous interpretation of the verdict require that the judgment be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial. See Globe Indemnity Co. v. Teixeira, 230 F. 580, 583 (9th Cir. 1916) (holding that a new trial was required when the jury's verdict was ambiguous and the trial court failed to clarify it); see also Southern Ry. Co. v. Neese, 216 F.2d 772, 774 (4th Cir. 1954) (reversing and remanding for a new trial due to the trial court's error in interpreting the verdict).

The cases cited above demonstrate that the district court's actions were contrary to established law. The court's refusal to clarify the verdict and its erroneous interpretation of the verdict resulted in a miscarriage of justice, and the judgment should be reversed. See Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 77-78 (1938) ("The judicial process is complete when the court has rendered judgment, and that judgment is final for purposes of appeal."). 

Additionally, the court should have considered the principles set forth in cases such as St. Louis, I. M. & S. Ry. Co. v. Craft, 237 U.S. 648, 661 (1915), which emphasizes the importance of ensuring that the jury's verdict is clear and unambiguous. The district court's failure to do so here resulted in reversible error. 

Furthermore, the court's actions were inconsistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Gila Valley, G. & N. Ry. Co. v. Hall, 232 U.S. 94, 98 (1914), which requires that the trial court take steps to ensure that the jury's verdict is not ambiguous. The district court's failure to take such steps here resulted in a verdict that was susceptible to multiple interpretations. 

In conclusion, the district court's refusal to clarify the jury's verdict and its erroneous interpretation of the verdict require that the judgment be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
