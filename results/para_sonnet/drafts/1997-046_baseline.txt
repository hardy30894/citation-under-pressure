# ARGUMENT

## I. AN INMATE HAS NO PROTECTED LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS

### A. Clemency Is an Act of Grace, Not an Entitlement Subject to Due Process

This Court has long recognized that executive clemency is "the historic remedy for preventing miscarriages of justice where judicial process has been exhausted." *Herrera v. Collins*, 506 U.S. 390, 411 (1993). Clemency proceedings are categorically different from judicial proceedings because they are not designed to adjudicate guilt or innocence but rather to extend mercy beyond what the law strictly requires. Because clemency is "a matter of grace," *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), no individual has a constitutional entitlement to it, and thus no life or liberty interest attaches to the *procedures* by which clemency is granted or denied.

In *Dumschat*, this Court held that a Connecticut inmate had no due process right to a statement of reasons when the Board of Pardons denied his commutation application, even though the Board had granted clemency in 75% of prior cases. The Court explained that "[t]he power of executive clemency exists, in part, to allow the Executive flexibility." *Id.* at 469. Critically, the Court held that a "constitutional entitlement cannot be created—as if by estoppel—merely because a wholly and expressly discretionary state privilege has been granted generously in the past." *Id.* at 465. The same logic controls here: Ohio's grant of a voluntary interview opportunity as part of its clemency process does not transform an act of grace into a constitutionally protected interest.

This Court reaffirmed the "unfettered discretion" inherent in the clemency power in *Woodard*'s companion reasoning, and consistently in *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272 (1998), where the plurality confirmed that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts," quoting *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. Because "the executive's clemency authority" is rooted in "the historic role of executive clemency to redress possible miscarriages of justice," *Herrera*, 506 U.S. at 415, judicial oversight of the *procedures* attending that authority would improperly convert a discretionary grace into a judicially enforceable right.

### B. Respondent's "Second Strand" Theory Conflates Judicial Process with Executive Grace

The Sixth Circuit majority erred in holding that because Ohio's clemency procedure is temporally connected to the "system for finally adjudicating guilt," it must satisfy the same due process demanded of trial and appellate proceedings, relying on *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), and *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985). That reliance is misplaced. *Evitts* addressed the right to *effective assistance of counsel on a first appeal as of right*—a procedure integral to determining guilt or innocence. Clemency, by contrast, operates *after* guilt has been conclusively established through full appellate review; it is not a stage of adjudication but "a matter of grace." *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464.

Indeed, this Court has already declined to extend even the right to counsel—firmly rooted in *Evitts*—beyond the first appeal, holding in *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974), that no such right attaches to discretionary review or habeas proceedings. If the right to counsel does not extend to habeas corpus—a proceeding far more "integral" to the adjudicative system than clemency—it cannot logically extend to executive clemency, which exists wholly outside Article III's judicial framework.

## II. THE VOLUNTARY OPTION TO PARTICIPATE IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

### A. The Regulation Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination

The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination; it does not create a right to avoid all consequences flowing from a voluntary choice to speak. Ohio's Death Penalty Clemency Procedure imposes no compulsion whatsoever—it merely *offers* the inmate an interview, which he may accept, decline, or limit as he sees fit. As Judge Nelson correctly observed below, "a prisoner who requests a clemency interview remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes."

This voluntary structure is squarely governed by *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), where this Court held that permitting an adverse inference from a prison inmate's silence at a disciplinary hearing did not violate the Fifth Amendment, because the inmate's choice to remain silent or speak remained his own. The Court cautioned against granting relief for a "purely speculative" risk of self-incrimination, deeming such relief "premature" where no concrete criminal jeopardy had yet materialized. *Id.* at 324. The same reasoning forecloses Respondent's claim: any risk that a voluntary clemency interview might later yield incriminating evidence is entirely hypothetical, particularly here, where Respondent has already been convicted and sentenced and does not allege that this action could affect his existing convictions.

### B. The "Unconstitutional Conditions" Doctrine Does Not Apply Where No Government Benefit Is Conditioned on Waiver

The Sixth Circuit's suggestion that the interview option creates a "Hobson's choice" akin to an unconstitutional condition is unsupported by the regulation's text. Unlike cases involving true compulsion—such as *Lefkowitz v. Cunningham*, 431 U.S. 801 (1977), where public employment was conditioned on waiver of the privilege—Ohio's procedure conditions nothing on Respondent's waiver. He may request an interview, decline one, or answer selectively, all without penalty to his clemency application, which remains supported by counsel's submissions regardless of his participation.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Sixth Circuit and hold that Respondent possesses no protected liberty interest in Ohio's clemency procedures, and that the voluntary interview option does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
