**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Content-Neutral, Reasonable Time-Place-Manner Regulation**

The First Amendment does not invalidate every regulation of speech in public places. *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 86 (1949) (“[T]he streets are proper places for the exercise of the freedom of communicating information and disseminating opinion.”). Rather, as this Court has long recognized, “[t]he privilege of a citizen of the United States to use the streets and parks for communication of views on national questions may be regulated in the interest of all.” *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (Roberts, J., concurring). The Massachusetts statute at issue here fits squarely within this tradition. It is a content-neutral regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech in a public forum, narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests, and leaving open ample alternative channels for communication.

The First Circuit correctly held that the statute is content-neutral. *See McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F.Supp.2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010), aff’d, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009), aff’d, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009). The law does not distinguish among speakers or messages; it applies equally to all who seek to engage in expressive activity within the buffer zone. *See Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness*, 452 U.S. 640, 649 (1981) (upholding content-neutral regulation of speech in a public forum). The statute’s purpose—ensuring safe and unobstructed access to healthcare facilities—is unrelated to the content of any speech. *See Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 558 (1965) (upholding a breach-of-the-peace statute applied neutrally to all demonstrators, regardless of message).

**II. The Statute Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve Significant Government Interests**

The Commonwealth has a substantial and compelling interest in protecting the safety and privacy of patients and clinic staff. *See Rowan v. Post Office Dep’t*, 397 U.S. 728, 736 (1970) (“The ancient concept that ‘a man’s home is his castle’ into which ‘not even the king may enter’ has lost none of its vitality.”). This Court has repeatedly recognized that buffer zones around polling places serve similar interests. *See Burson v. Freeman*, 504 U.S. 191, 199 (1992) (upholding a 100-foot buffer zone around polling places to prevent voter intimidation and preserve order). The same logic applies here. *See id.* at 204 (plurality opinion) (“The State’s compelling interests in preventing voter intimidation and election fraud are directly implicated by the proximity of the prohibited activity to the polling place.”).

The Massachusetts statute is narrowly tailored to achieve these interests. It does not ban all speech near clinics; it merely restricts speech within a 35-foot radius of entrances, exits, and driveways. *See Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 481 (1988) (upholding a ban on targeted residential picketing because it was narrowly tailored to protect residential privacy). The statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *See Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (“The government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, or manner of protected speech, provided the restrictions are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.”). Petitioners can still speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive activity outside the buffer zone. *See id.* at 46.

**III. The Statute Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels for Communication**

The First Circuit correctly held that the statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *See McCullen*, 759 F.Supp.2d at 145. Petitioners can still speak to individuals approaching the clinics from outside the buffer zone. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 723 (2000) (upholding a similar statute because it left open “ample alternative channels for communication”). They can also use signs, symbols, and other non-verbal means of expression. *See Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 87 (upholding a ban on sound trucks because it left open other means of communication).

The statute’s exemptions further demonstrate that it is narrowly tailored. It exempts clinic employees, patients, and law enforcement officers, ensuring that the buffer zone does not unduly burden essential activities. *See Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966) (upholding a trespass conviction for protestors blocking a jail entrance because the state could regulate the time, place, and manner of speech to protect public property).

**IV. Hill v. Colorado Is Controlling and Should Not Be Overruled**

The First Circuit correctly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), in upholding the Massachusetts statute. *See McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. *Hill* squarely addressed a similar buffer-zone statute and upheld it under the First Amendment. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723. The Court held that the statute was content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and left open ample alternative channels for communication. *See id.* at 715-16. The Massachusetts statute is indistinguishable from the Colorado statute in *Hill*. *See id.* at 707-08.

Petitioners’ argument that *Hill* should be overruled is unavailing. *Hill* was decided by a majority of this Court and remains binding precedent. *See Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/Am. Express, Inc.*, 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989) (“[A]n earlier decision of this Court should not be followed if subsequent developments in the law have undermined its theoretical underpinnings.”). No such developments have occurred. *See id.* Petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. *Sorrell* addressed a content-based restriction on commercial speech, not a content-neutral time-place-manner regulation. *See id.* at 566.

**V. The Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment**

For the foregoing reasons, the Massachusetts buffer-zone statute does not violate the First Amendment. It is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech in a public forum. It serves significant government interests and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. The First Circuit correctly upheld the statute, and this Court should affirm its judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
