**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim as “cause” to excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was fairly presented to the state courts, or the petitioner independently establishes cause and prejudice for that default. That rule follows from the Court’s long-settled habeas doctrine, which insists that state courts receive the first opportunity to correct alleged constitutional errors. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 447-48, 485-86 (1953); *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950); *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944); *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977).

The exhaustion requirement is central. A federal claim must be “fairly presented” to the state courts before it may be pursued in federal habeas. *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971); *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447-48 (1965). That rule reflects comity and respect for state procedural systems, not mere formality. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 447-48. If a petitioner could rely on a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse a separate default, he would evade exhaustion by indirection and deprive the state courts of their first chance to address the asserted attorney error.

That is precisely what *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), forbids. The Court held that attorney error may constitute cause only when it amounts to constitutionally ineffective assistance under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). But the Court also said that “the exhaustion doctrine” requires an ineffective-assistance claim to be presented to the state courts “as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause.” *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489. That sentence resolves this case. A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause unless the petitioner first complied with the state procedures governing that claim, or otherwise excused the default of the ineffectiveness claim itself.

Petitioner’s contrary position would collapse the distinction between cause and the underlying default. On petitioner’s theory, a habeas applicant could default a trial claim, default the ineffectiveness claim that allegedly explains the first default, and then ask a federal court to overlook both defaults at once. The Court has repeatedly refused to convert habeas into a device for bypassing state procedural rules. *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 87-91; *Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 493-94 (1976); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947).

The older right-to-counsel cases do not suggest a different result. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938), and *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516-17 (1962), concern waiver and the constitutional entitlement to counsel. They do not hold that an unexhausted or defaulted ineffectiveness claim may automatically excuse another default. The governing framework was supplied later by *Strickland* and *Carrier*: ineffective assistance is a constitutional claim that must be judged on its own terms, 466 U.S. at 687, 694, and, before it can serve as cause, it must itself have been properly presented to state court, 477 U.S. at 489.

Finality and regularity support the same result. Habeas review is not a substitute for direct review, and procedural default rules protect the States’ interest in orderly adjudication. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 485-86; *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 88-91. Allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse another default would make state filing rules largely optional: a petitioner would need only recast his failure to comply as counsel’s ineffectiveness, even when that ineffectiveness theory was never fairly presented below. That would substantially undermine *Picard*, *Darr*, *Hawk*, and *Carrier*.

The correct rule is therefore simple and consistent with this Court’s precedents. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another default unless the ineffectiveness claim itself has been fairly presented to the state courts, or the petitioner independently establishes cause and prejudice for that default. Because respondent’s contrary rule would defeat exhaustion, comity, and procedural-default doctrine, the judgment below should be affirmed.
