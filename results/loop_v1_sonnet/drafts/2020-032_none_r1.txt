# ARGUMENT

## I. Article III Requires a "Definite and Concrete" Controversy, Which a Bare Request for Nominal Damages Cannot Supply Once the Challenged Policy Is Withdrawn.

The judicial power of the federal courts extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has long refused to adjudicate disputes that have lost their live, adversarial character. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911). In *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227 (1937), this Court explained that "[t]he controversy must be definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests," and it "must be a real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts." *Id.* at 240-41.

Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged provision of the Student Code of Conduct, no live dispute remained over the validity of rules that no longer existed. Petitioners face no threat of enforcement, no continuing relationship with the defendants arising from those provisions, and no prospect of "specific relief" beyond a symbolic label attached to a policy already gone. A demand for a token sum cannot resurrect that extinguished controversy. As this Court held in *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), "[t]he duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is limited to determining rights of persons or of property, which are actually controverted in the particular case before it." *Id.* at 653. Where the policy giving rise to the suit has been repealed and superseded, and where petitioners allege no actual, continuing injury flowing from that repealed policy, there is no "right actually controverted" for a court to decide.

## II. This Court Has Repeatedly Held That Insubstantial or Residual Claims Cannot Preserve Jurisdiction Once the Underlying Dispute Has Been Resolved.

This Court's mootness jurisprudence has never permitted a merely technical or residual claim to keep alive a controversy that has otherwise been resolved. In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the State sued for a modest sum of back taxes; while the case was pending, the legislature released the underlying claim. This Court held the case moot because the dispute no longer presented any matter of genuine consequence between the parties, notwithstanding the technically surviving demand. *Id.* at 313-14. The same logic governs here: once GGC rescinded the challenged provisions, the only thing petitioners retained was a request for nominal damages untethered to any pleaded actual injury—the very type of vestigial claim *San Pablo* held insufficient to sustain continuing federal jurisdiction.

*United States v. Alaska Steamship Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920), confirms the point from another angle. There, this Court recognized that where intervening events destroy the practical significance of a dispute, the case becomes moot even though some formal remnant of the original controversy remains on the docket. *Id.* at 115-16. GGC's repeal of the Prior Policies is precisely such an intervening event. It eliminated any occasion for equitable relief and left behind only a nominal-damages request incapable of restoring a live controversy over provisions no longer in force.

## III. Voluntary Cessation of the Challenged Policy, Combined With No Reasonable Expectation of Recurrence, Moots the Case Notwithstanding the Residual Damages Request.

Where a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct and the plaintiff shows no personal stake tied to concrete, continuing injury, the controversy is moot absent a reasonable expectation that the dispute will recur. *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969). In *Golden*, this Court dismissed a challenge to an anonymous-handbill statute as moot where the legislator whose past campaign had prompted the suit no longer held office, leaving "no reasonable expectation" that the same controversy would recur. *Id.* at 109. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held—and petitioners do not dispute—that GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct mooted the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief. That same voluntary and complete abandonment of the challenged provisions likewise deprives the residual nominal-damages request of any "definite and concrete" controversy to which it might attach. *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 240.

*St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), illustrates why a technical remnant of a claim cannot rescue an otherwise moot case. This Court dismissed a contempt appeal as moot once the underlying sentence had been fully served, notwithstanding lingering collateral consequences, because no genuine, concrete stake in the outcome remained. *Id.* at 43. By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court declined to find mootness because a substantial, quantifiable dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction project—a real economic stake directly bearing on the parties' rights. *Id.* at 306 & n.3. Petitioners' claim for nominal damages, unmoored from any pleaded actual injury, is the mirror image of the substantial cost dispute preserved in *Liner*; it is instead precisely the sort of empty formalism this Court has never allowed to substitute for a live controversy.

## Conclusion

Because the Prior Policies no longer exist, because petitioners allege no actual injury flowing from them, and because a request for a token sum cannot supply the "real and substantial controversy" that Article III demands, *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 240, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held this case moot, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
