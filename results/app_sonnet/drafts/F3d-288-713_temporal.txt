## ARGUMENT

### I. Riddle Was Denied Due Process When the Chief Prosecutor Testified Against His Credibility and Then Personally Argued That Same Credibility Issue to the Jury.

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a capital defendant "[a] fair trial in a fair tribunal," which is "a basic requirement of due process." *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955). That guarantee is not satisfied merely by the appearance of neutrality on the bench; it extends to the integrity of the prosecutorial function itself, for "justice must satisfy the appearance of justice." *Offutt v. United States*, 348 U.S. 11, 14 (1954). A prosecutor is not "an ordinary party to a controversy," but the representative of a sovereign "whose interest ... in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done." *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935).

Here, District Attorney Danny Hill did not merely supervise the prosecution—he became a witness in it. When Riddle testified that Hill had threatened to "do his damnest to make sure [he] got the death penalty," Hill took the stand to rebut that testimony and then resumed his role as chief prosecutor, permitting his assistant to argue in closing that Riddle was a liar precisely because Hill's own testimony had discredited him. This blending of advocate and witness offends the structural safeguards this Court has long protected. In *Turner v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 466 (1965), the Supreme Court held that a jury's continuous exposure to deputies who were also key prosecution witnesses violated due process, because the fundamental guarantee of a fair trial requires that "the 'evidence developed' against a defendant shall come from the witness stand in a public courtroom" untainted by extrajudicial influence from persons whose institutional loyalty runs to the prosecution. *Id.* at 473. The same infirmity exists—indeed is aggravated—where the very officer directing the prosecution testifies against the accused and then argues, through his own subordinate, that the jury should credit his testimony over the defendant's. No juror could realistically separate Hill's testimonial credibility from his authority as the elected prosecutor overseeing the case. The result is precisely the kind of "constitutionally intolerable" commingling of roles condemned in *Turner*. *Id.*

This is not a case of harmless overlap. Riddle's entire defense turned on the jury's assessment of his credibility—his account of consent, self-defense, and panic versus the State's theory of burglary and premeditated killing. By allowing the case's chief architect to become a witness on the ultimate issue of Riddle's truthfulness, and then to continue prosecuting the very charge his testimony was designed to secure, the trial denied Riddle the "fair tribunal" that due process demands. *Murchison*, 349 U.S. at 136. Just as due process forbids a tribunal in which the decision-maker has a personal stake in the outcome, *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 532 (1927) (a defendant "had a right to have an impartial judge"), it likewise forbids a prosecution in which the State's chief advocate injects himself as a witness whose credibility becomes indistinguishable from the prosecution's institutional interest in conviction. The conviction and sentence obtained through this structural conflict cannot stand.

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle an Evidentiary Hearing.

Federal habeas corpus exists to test the fundamental fairness of a state conviction, and a hearing is required whenever the state court's fact-finding process was inadequate or the facts material to the constitutional claim were not developed. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312–13 (1963). The Supreme Court there catalogued the circumstances compelling a hearing, including where "the fact-finding procedure employed by the state court was not adequate to afford a full and fair hearing" and where "the applicant did not receive a full and fair evidentiary hearing in a state court, either at the time of trial or in a collateral proceeding." *Id.* at 313.

Riddle's claim regarding the prosecutor's dual role as witness and advocate was never fully developed at the state level, and the district court granted summary judgment without permitting Riddle to build a factual record concerning the extent of Hill's continued participation in strategy, argument, and case management after he took the stand. Because "the material facts were not adequately developed at the state-court hearing," *id.*, and because the constitutional violation here goes to the heart of trial fairness rather than a matter capable of resolution on the paper record alone, the district court's summary denial of a hearing violated the standard this Court must apply. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (due process forbids proceedings that offend "the rudimentary requirements of fair play"). Riddle respectfully requests that this Court vacate the judgment below and remand for an evidentiary hearing, or, in the alternative, grant the writ outright.
