**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford's Community Use Policy Excludes an Entire Category of Activity—Religious Worship and Instruction—Not a Viewpoint on a Permitted Subject.**

Because Milford's facilities were opened only for enumerated "social, civic and recreational" purposes under N.Y. Educ. Law § 414, and not for use by the public generally, the forum here is, at most, a limited public forum subject to reasonableness review rather than strict scrutiny. *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). This Court has explained that "[i]mplicit in the concept of the nonpublic forum is the right to make distinctions in access on the basis of subject matter," id., and that a restriction on access need only be "reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum," *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806, 809 (1985). A restriction survives even if it is "not the most reasonable or the only reasonable limitation." Id. at 808.

Milford's line—permitting discussion of moral and character development, including from a religious perspective, while excluding "religious instruction" and "religious worship"—is precisely such a subject-matter distinction. It is not, as the Second Circuit correctly recognized, the viewpoint discrimination this Court condemned in *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), or *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995). In both of those cases the government had opened the forum to a subject—family values in *Lamb's Chapel*, student journalism in *Rosenberger*—and then excluded only the religious perspective on that otherwise-permitted subject. Here, by contrast, Milford excludes the categories of "religious instruction" and "religious worship" regardless of who seeks to engage in them; no group, religious or secular, may use Milford's facilities to conduct devotional exercises, proselytize, or lead attendees in prayer and conversion. The Club's proposed meetings—opening and closing prayer, recitation of scripture, an "invitation" inviting children to "ask the Lord Jesus to come into your heart" and to "pray this prayer" of salvation—are not a religious perspective on the secular subject of character; they are the devotional exercise itself. As the Second Circuit found, the Club's activities are the "equivalent of religious worship" rather than the teaching of secular values from a religious viewpoint. 202 F.3d at 510. Excluding worship and proselytization from a forum limited to civic and recreational activities is the kind of reasonable, content-based—but not viewpoint-based—boundary that *Cornelius* and *Perry* permit a government body to draw when it has not thrown open its property to all comers on all subjects.

**II. In Any Event, Milford's Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation Justifies Its Denial.**

Even if this Court concludes that Milford's policy burdens the Club's speech, the denial is justified by Milford's interest in not sponsoring or appearing to sponsor religious worship on public school property immediately after the school day, using the same classrooms, teachers' oversight, and compulsory-attendance infrastructure that define the school day for the elementary-age children involved. This Court has long recognized that "the interest of the State in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation may be characterized as compelling." *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981). That interest has particular force in the elementary school setting, where this Court has repeatedly acknowledged the heightened susceptibility of young children to perceived school endorsement of religion. In *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992), the Court emphasized that schoolchildren are subject to subtle pressures toward religious conformity that adults are not, and that the "State may not, consistent with the Establishment Clause, place primary and secondary school children in this position." Id. at 592. This Court has since reaffirmed that concern for the youngest students in *Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290 (2000), recognizing that school sponsorship of religious activity risks being perceived by students as an endorsement of religion by the school itself. Milford's building is not a generic community hall; it is the elementary school these children attend daily, staffed by the same personnel, and the Club's meetings—held in classrooms immediately after dismissal for children as young as six—present precisely the risk of perceived school endorsement this Court has guarded against.

Milford was entitled to conclude, consistent with *Widmar*'s recognition of a compelling Establishment Clause interest, that permitting a proselytizing religious club to conduct devotional exercises and issue an "invitation" to accept Christ as personal savior, in classrooms immediately following the school day and attended predominantly by the school's own young students, created a substantial risk that its youngest, most impressionable pupils would perceive the school as endorsing the Club's religious message. That risk is categorically different from the risk presented by an evening film screening for a general adult audience, as in *Lamb's Chapel*, or by a university's disbursement of a religion-neutral printing subsidy to an adult publication, as in *Rosenberger*. The unique vulnerability of elementary schoolchildren, combined with the temporal and physical proximity of the Club's activities to the school day, gave Milford ample constitutional justification—independent of any forum analysis—for declining to permit the Club's worship and proselytizing activities within its walls.

For these reasons, Milford's denial did not violate the Free Speech Clause, and, even if it burdened the Club's speech, that burden was justified by Milford's compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.
