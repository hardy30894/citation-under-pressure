ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), protects a criminal defendant from being retried after an appellate court has found the evidence insufficient to support a conviction. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 18 (1978). This protection extends to sentencing proceedings that “resemble a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). California’s proceeding to determine the truth of a prior conviction allegation under Penal Code § 1025 is such a trial-like proceeding. Because the California Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to prove the prior conviction allegation, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars the State from retrying that allegation.

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Applies to Trial-Like Sentencing Proceedings**

In *Bullington*, this Court held that double jeopardy protections attach to a capital sentencing proceeding where the State must prove aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, the defendant may present mitigating evidence, and the sentencer must choose between life imprisonment and death. *Id.* at 438–439. The Court reasoned that such a proceeding “in all relevant respects was like the immediately preceding trial on the issue of guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 438. The same reasoning applies here.

California’s prior conviction determination is a separate, trial-like proceeding. The State must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993). The defendant has a statutory right to a jury trial, § 1025, and the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996). The rules of evidence apply. *Id.* at 224. These hallmarks mirror those of a guilt-phase trial. Thus, under *Bullington*, the Double Jeopardy Clause governs this proceeding.

This Court has never limited *Bullington* to capital cases. In *Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33 (1988), the Court applied double jeopardy principles to a noncapital habitual-offender sentencing hearing without questioning their applicability. The Court assumed that a finding of insufficient evidence at such a hearing would bar retrial, holding only that retrial was permitted because the evidence, including erroneously admitted evidence, was sufficient. *Id.* at 40–42. *Lockhart* thus confirms that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings that are trial-like.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held only that the Due Process Clause does not require a trial-like sentencing procedure. It did not hold that a State that *chooses* to provide such a procedure may then deny double jeopardy protections. *Bullington* itself distinguished *Williams* on that ground. 451 U.S. at 438 n.11. California has elected to provide a full trial on prior conviction allegations; it cannot now strip that trial of its constitutional consequences.

**II. The Court of Appeal’s Insufficiency Finding Bars Retrial**

The California Court of Appeal held that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon in the prior assault—a necessary element for the “serious felony” enhancement. That determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal. *Burks*, 437 U.S. at 10–11. Just as a defendant may not be retried for a crime after an acquittal, he may not be subjected to a second sentencing proceeding after a finding that the State failed to prove a sentence-enhancing fact. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 445–446.

The State’s interest in obtaining a correct sentence does not outweigh the defendant’s interest in finality once the evidence has been found insufficient. *See Burks*, 437 U.S. at 16–17. Allowing retrial would subject Monge to the “embarrassment, expense, and ordeal” that the Double Jeopardy Clause was designed to prevent. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957).

**III. The California Supreme Court’s Decision Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents**

The California Supreme Court held that double jeopardy does not apply because sentencing is not a “jeopardy of life or limb.” But this Court has long held that the Clause applies to all criminal penalties. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1873). Moreover, the court’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* based on the capital nature of that case is unpersuasive. *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), merely noted that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases; it did not hold that their reasoning is limited to that context. Indeed, *Lockhart* applied double jeopardy to a noncapital sentencing without any such limitation. The California court’s decision thus creates a conflict with *Lockhart* and *Bullington*.

**Conclusion**

The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of a prior conviction allegation when an appellate court has found the evidence insufficient. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
