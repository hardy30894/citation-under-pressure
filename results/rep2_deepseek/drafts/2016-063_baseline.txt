**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of the governing statute, the structure of the Civil Service Reform Act, and this Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), all compel the conclusion that when the Merit Systems Protection Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal on jurisdictional grounds, judicial review lies in federal district court, not the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

**I. The Statutory Definition of a Mixed Case Does Not Turn on the Ground of the Board’s Dismissal**

Under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), a mixed case exists when an employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” The definition focuses on the nature of the employee’s claim—whether the employee was affected by an action appealable to the Board *and* alleges discrimination. Nothing in the statute conditions the mixed-case designation on the Board’s ultimate decision on the appealability of the action.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision below, relying on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), erroneously held that a jurisdictional dismissal strips the case of its mixed-case character because the employee was never “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Board.” But that reading conflates the employee’s initial burden to state a claim *within* the Board’s jurisdiction with the Board’s final determination that it lacks jurisdiction. When an employee like Perry brings a colorable claim that her retirement was involuntary—and coerced by discrimination—she has alleged an action that is “appealable” within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1)(A). The Board’s subsequent finding that the action was voluntary does not retroactively erase the appealable nature of the claim. *Kloeckner* made clear that the relevant inquiry is “whether the employee's claim falls within the Board's jurisdiction,” not whether the Board ultimately agrees. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 54.

**II. *Kloeckner* Rejected the Very Distinction on Which the D.C. Circuit Relied**

In *Kloeckner*, this Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case on a procedural ground—there, untimeliness—the employee must seek review in district court, not the Federal Circuit. *Id.* at 52. The Court expressly rejected the argument that the distinction between “jurisdictional” and “procedural” dismissals should control the forum for judicial review. *Id.* at 49–50. The Court emphasized that the statute’s “detailed procedural scheme” for mixed cases “would be thrown into disarray if the forum for judicial review depended on the ground on which the Board dismissed the case.” *Id.* at 50.

The D.C. Circuit below tried to distinguish *Kloeckner* by asserting that jurisdictional dismissals are different because the Board lacks authority to excuse a jurisdictional defect, whereas it can waive procedural defects. But *Kloeckner* itself noted that the Board’s discretion to waive procedural rules does not alter the statutory definition of a mixed case. 568 U.S. at 49. The Court’s reasoning did not hinge on the Board’s power to cure defects; it hinged on the employee’s initial allegation of an appealable action and discrimination. *Id.* at 46–47. Indeed, the Court observed that the employee’s right to go to district court “is not defeated by the Board’s decision to dismiss the appeal on the ground that it is not appealable.” *Id.* at 54.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is untenable after *Kloeckner*. *Powell* held that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit because “the boundaries of the Board’s jurisdiction should be subject to uniform interpretation in a single forum.” 158 F.3d at 600. But *Kloeckner* rejected that very rationale, holding that the statute’s text and purpose require that discrimination claims be reviewed in district court regardless of the ground for dismissal. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50–51. The uniform interpretation argument is no more persuasive for jurisdictional dismissals than for procedural ones; Congress chose to centralize discrimination adjudication in district courts, not the Federal Circuit.

**III. The Practical Consequences of the D.C. Circuit’s Rule Undermine the Statutory Scheme**

If the D.C. Circuit’s rule stands, an employee who brings a mixed case and loses on the threshold question of jurisdiction will be forced to litigate the discrimination claim in the Federal Circuit—a court that lacks expertise in employment discrimination law. That result directly contradicts Congress’s intent to ensure that discrimination claims are heard in district courts, which have “specialized experience in handling such claims.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50. Moreover, the rule creates an anomaly: an employee who alleges discrimination and loses on the merits of the Board’s jurisdiction goes to district court, while an employee who loses on the same jurisdictional issue but without a merits determination goes to the Federal Circuit. That makes no sense.

**IV. The Board’s Jurisdictional Determination Here Was Inextricably Intertwined with the Merits of the Discrimination Claim**

Perry’s claim of discrimination centered on the voluntariness of his settlement and retirement. The Board’s jurisdictional ruling—that the action was voluntary—necessarily resolved the factual predicate of his discrimination claim. When the Board’s jurisdictional decision overlaps with the merits of the discrimination allegation, the proper forum for review is district court. *See Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 54 (noting that the “intertwined” nature of jurisdictional and merits issues supports district court review). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Powell* was based on a flawed premise that the Board’s jurisdictional ruling is independent of the discrimination claim. *Powell*, 158 F.3d at 599–600. *Kloeckner* abrogated that premise.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to transfer the petition for review to the appropriate federal district court.
