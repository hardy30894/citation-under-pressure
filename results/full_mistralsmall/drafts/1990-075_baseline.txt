**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT’S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The Constitution vests “[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted . . . in a Congress of the United States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. This Court has long recognized, however, that Congress may delegate authority to the executive branch so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s exercise of that authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision of the Controlled Substances Act (CSA), 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies this standard.

Section 811(h) authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance as a controlled drug if he finds that doing so is “necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The statute further directs the Attorney General to consider specific factors—including the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health—before exercising this authority. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). These statutory criteria are sufficiently definite to cabin the Attorney General’s discretion and to prevent arbitrary enforcement. *SeeMistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 (upholding delegation where Congress provided “considerable specificity” in directing the Sentencing Commission’s work).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In those cases, the Court invalidated delegations because the statutes failed to articulate any policy or standard whatsoever. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542. Here, by contrast, Congress has provided clear and detailed guidance. The temporary scheduling provision is not a blank check; it is a limited grant of authority circumscribed by specific statutory criteria. *SeeMistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 (distinguishing *Panama Refining* and *Schechter Poultry* as “extreme cases” where Congress provided “no guidance at all”).

Moreover, the CSA’s structure ensures that the Attorney General’s exercise of delegated authority is subject to meaningful constraints. Before temporarily scheduling a substance, the Attorney General must consult with the Secretary of Health and Human Services and publish notice in the Federal Register. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). The temporary scheduling order is also time-limited—initially for one year, with a possible six-month extension—and is subject to judicial review in the context of a criminal prosecution. *Id.* § 811(h)(2), (6). These procedural safeguards further ensure that the delegation does not exceed constitutional bounds. *SeeYakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 430 (1944) (upholding delegation where “[t]he procedure prescribed for the determination of prices is appropriate and adapted to the end sought to be attained”).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY DELEGATED HIS TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.**

The CSA grants the Attorney General broad authority to delegate his functions under the statute. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) (authorizing the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice”). Pursuant to this authority, the Attorney General has delegated his scheduling powers—including the temporary scheduling authority under § 811(h)—to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b).

The Toubys argue that this subdelegation is improper because § 811(h) does not expressly authorize the Attorney General to delegate his temporary scheduling power. This contention ignores the well-established principle that “[a] statutory grant of authority to an executive officer includes, by implication, the power to delegate that authority to subordinates.” *United States v. Ehrlichman*, 546 F.2d 910, 927 (D.C. Cir. 1976) (citing *United States v. Gibert*, 253 F.2d 583, 586 (2d Cir. 1958)). The CSA’s delegation provision, § 871(a), is broad enough to encompass the temporary scheduling authority. *SeeUnited States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987) (upholding subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority under § 811(h)).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), is unavailing. In *Field*, the Court invalidated a delegation to the President to suspend tariff laws because the statute provided no standards whatsoever. *Id.* at 692. Here, by contrast, the Attorney General’s subdelegation is authorized by a statute that itself contains intelligible principles. The subdelegation is thus a permissible exercise of the Attorney General’s delegated authority. *SeeMistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372 (upholding delegation where Congress provided “an intelligible principle”).

**CONCLUSION**

The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, and the Attorney General lawfully delegated his authority to the DEA Administrator. The judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
