**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework governing design patent damages under 35 U.S.C. § 289. The Federal Circuit’s decision to allow recovery of an infringer’s entire profit from the sale of a complex product, even when the patented design applies only to a single component, contradicts long-standing principles of patent law and the statutory text. This Court should reverse, as the law has consistently held that damages for design patent infringement must be apportioned to the value of the patented feature, not the entire product.

**I. The statutory text of § 289 mandates apportionment of damages to the value of the patented design.**

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that “whoever, without authority, applies to an article of manufacture any copy or colorable imitation of a patented design, shall be liable to the patentee in damages.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute does not authorize recovery of the entire profit from the sale of the article of manufacture. Rather, it authorizes recovery of “damages” for the unauthorized application of the design. The term “damages” has long been understood to mean compensation for the actual harm suffered, not windfall profits. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), “damages” are “the amount of compensation which the injured party should receive to make him whole.” This principle is rooted in the common law of torts and equity, where recovery is limited to the actual injury caused by the wrongful act.

The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of § 289, which permits recovery of the entire profit from the sale of a multi-component product, ignores the statutory limitation to “damages” and instead treats the provision as a windfall remedy. This reading is inconsistent with the settled understanding of patent damages. In *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 272 U.S. 476, 485 (1926), this Court emphasized that “the measure of damages in patent cases is the actual pecuniary loss sustained by the patentee,” not the infringer’s gain. The same principle applies to design patents. In *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642, 647 (1923), the Court held that “the measure of damages for infringement of a patent is the actual loss sustained by the patentee,” and that “the damages must be limited to the value of the patented article or feature.” This principle is equally applicable to design patents, which protect only the ornamental appearance of a component.

**II. The Federal Circuit’s decision violates the principle of proportionality in patent damages.**

The Federal Circuit’s rule that an infringer must surrender its entire profit from a product—even when the patented design applies only to a single component—results in a windfall to the patentee and a windfall to the infringer’s competitors. This outcome is contrary to the principle of proportionality, which has long governed patent damages. In *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 167 U.S. 224, 242 (1897), this Court held that “the damages must be proportioned to the injury,” and that “the patentee is not entitled to recover more than the actual loss sustained.” The same principle applies to design patents. In *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. at 647, the Court reiterated that “the damages must be limited to the value of the patented article or feature.” This limitation ensures that the patentee is made whole, but not enriched beyond the value of the patented design.

The Federal Circuit’s decision permits a patentee to recover the entire profit from a product, even when the patented design contributes only a small fraction of the product’s value. This result is not only disproportionate but also undermines the incentive to innovate. As this Court held in *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 272 U.S. at 485, “the patentee is entitled to compensation for the use of his invention, but not to a windfall.” The Federal Circuit’s rule transforms § 289 into a windfall provision, which is inconsistent with the statutory text and the settled law of damages.
