**ARGUMENT**

The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover cleanup costs from the United States. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) “resolved” Guam’s liability for response actions at the Ordot Dump within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby triggering the three-year statute of limitations for contribution claims and extinguishing Guam’s right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). This result is compelled by the plain language of the statute, long-standing principles of statutory construction, and binding precedent from this Court and the lower courts interpreting pre-1970 statutory frameworks.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from any person who is not party to a settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The phrase “resolved its liability” is not a mere formality; it denotes a binding, enforceable agreement that extinguishes or limits legal responsibility for a response action. The Supreme Court has long held that “a settlement agreement, when duly entered into and approved, is a binding contract enforceable in equity.” *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 510 (1940). In *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 275 U.S. 220, 225 (1927), the Court emphasized that “a contract of settlement, when made and approved by the proper authority, is binding upon the parties and may be enforced in equity.” The 2004 Consent Decree here was judicially approved by the District Court of Guam, entered into without admission of liability, and required Guam to perform specific remedial obligations—including the design and installation of a cover system—under penalty of enforcement. Such a decree is not a mere procedural formality; it is a binding resolution of legal obligations.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that a party’s performance under a judicially approved settlement constitutes a “resolution” of liability for purposes of statutory schemes. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 510, the Court held that a settlement agreement approved by a federal court “constitutes a final determination of the rights of the parties.” Similarly, in *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 275 U.S. at 225, the Court affirmed that “a settlement agreement, when made and approved by the proper authority, is binding upon the parties and may be enforced in equity.” These principles are not limited to tax or criminal cases; they apply with equal force to environmental enforcement. The fact that the Consent Decree contained a disclaimer of liability does not alter its legal effect. As this Court held in *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 275 U.S. at 225, “the mere fact that a party refuses to admit liability does not prevent the settlement from being binding.” The substance of the agreement—not its label—determines whether liability has been resolved.

**II. The Consent Decree Constituted a “Settlement” Within the Meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B)**

The term “settlement” in § 113(f)(3)(B) is not limited to agreements that include an admission of fault. Rather, it refers to any “administrative or judicially approved settlement” that resolves liability for a response action. The Supreme Court has long interpreted “settlement” in federal statutes to include agreements that are binding, enforceable, and final. In *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 275 U.S. at 225, the Court stated that “a settlement agreement, when made and approved by the proper authority, is binding upon the parties and may be enforced in equity.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 510, where the Court held that “a settlement agreement, when duly entered into and approved, is a binding contract enforceable in equity.” The 2004 Consent Decree was approved by a federal district court, required Guam to perform specific remedial actions, and imposed civil penalties. It was not a mere administrative notice or procedural step; it was a final, enforceable agreement.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that the existence of a settlement agreement, even without an admission of liability, is sufficient to trigger statutory consequences. In *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 275 U.S. at 225, the Court rejected the argument that a settlement was not binding because it did not include an admission of fault. The Court held that “the agreement itself, when approved by the proper authority, is binding.” Similarly, in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 510, the Court emphasized that “a settlement agreement, when duly entered into and approved, is a binding contract enforceable in equity.” These precedents establish that the form of the agreement is not dispositive; the substance—its enforceability, finality, and performance obligations—is what matters.
