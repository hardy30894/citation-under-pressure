**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text and Structure of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act Require That the Injury Occur on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits only for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies the injury. The natural reading is that the injury must bear a geographic connection to the shelf—the operations are *conducted on* the shelf, and the injury must occur *as the result of* those operations. But the statute does not say the injury itself must occur on the shelf. However, the overall structure of OCSLA demonstrates that Congress intended coverage to be determined “principally by locale.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). In *Tallentire*, this Court explained that “the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Id.* The Court then noted that only § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. That footnote—though dictum—reflects the Court’s considered view that a situs requirement exists under § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit erred in dismissing it as “unconsidered.” *Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1126, 1134 (9th Cir. 2010). This Court’s dictum is entitled to “greater weight and deference” as a prophecy of what the Court might hold. *United States v. Montero-Camargo*, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc). The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, correctly recognized that *Tallentire* supports a situs-of-injury requirement. *Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 361 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc).

Moreover, in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), this Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* at 423-24. That “explicit geographic limitation” refers to the requirement that operations be conducted on the outer Continental Shelf. The Court’s reasoning confirms that OCSLA coverage is tied to the shelf itself. The Fifth Circuit in *Mills* relied on *Herb’s Welding* to hold that an injury must occur on the shelf or on the waters above it. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361-62. That holding is consistent with the statute’s text and this Court’s guidance.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Conflicts with the Statute and Creates Uncertainty.**

The Ninth Circuit adopted a “substantial nexus” test, requiring only a causal connection between the injury and operations on the shelf. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1138. That test reads the geographic limitation out of the statute. Under the Ninth Circuit’s approach, any injury—no matter how far from the shelf—could be covered if it bears some relationship to offshore operations. That result is inconsistent with Congress’s intent to limit OCSLA coverage to the shelf. The Third Circuit in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), adopted a similar “but for” test, but that decision predates *Mills* and has been rejected by the only other circuit to consider the issue en banc. The Fifth Circuit’s approach in *Mills* is more faithful to the statute: the injury must occur on the shelf or on the waters above it. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 362; *see also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009). That bright-line rule provides certainty and respects the statutory text.

Here, Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore processing facility 250-300 feet from the coast. That facility is not on the outer Continental Shelf. The injury did not occur on the shelf or on the waters above it. Therefore, OCSLA does not extend LHWCA coverage to his death. The Benefits Review Board correctly denied benefits, and the Ninth Circuit erred in reversing.

**III. The LHWCA’s Situs Requirement Independently Bars Coverage.**

Even if OCSLA applied, Valladolid’s injury would not satisfy the LHWCA’s situs requirement. The LHWCA covers injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States . . . if disability or death results from an injury occurring upon the navigable waters, including any adjoining pier, wharf, dock, or area of land customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The onshore facility here is not a maritime situs. It is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, has no direct access to a pier, and its primary purpose is processing crude oil—a non-maritime activity. *Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 422-24. The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the denial of LHWCA benefits on this ground. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1140-41. Thus, even under the Ninth Circuit’s OCSLA test, Valladolid’s widow cannot recover under the LHWCA directly.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
