# ARGUMENT

**Amendment 73 Unconstitutionally Adds to the Qualifications for Congress Fixed by Article I**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment cannot stand because Article I of the United States Constitution establishes the sole qualifications for membership in the House of Representatives and Senate, and a State may not supplement them, however that supplementation is styled. This Court so held in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), where it examined at length the historical record of the Constitutional Convention and concluded that the Framers deliberately enumerated the qualifications of age, citizenship, and inhabitancy in Article I, §2, cl. 2, and Article I, §3, cl. 3, and rejected proposals to allow Congress to impose additional restrictions such as property ownership. The Court explained that the Convention's rejection of open-ended qualification power reflected the Framers' considered judgment that the qualifications for Congress "were to be fixed" by the Constitution itself and were not to be left to the discretion of any other body. *Id.* at 539-41. If Congress itself lacks the power to add qualifications beyond those the Constitution specifies, it follows *a fortiori* that an individual State — one of fifty constituencies whose delegations together compose a national legislature — cannot claim a power the Framers denied to Congress as a whole.

The structural logic of *Powell* rests on this Court's longstanding recognition that the National Government's officers, once created by the Constitution, are not creatures of state law. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), the Court held that the right to elect Representatives is a right that "has its foundation in the Constitution of the United States," not in the will of any individual State, because Congress is an institution belonging to the whole Union. That principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), where the Court recognized that the right to choose federal representatives is one "secured by the Constitution" and that the machinery of a congressional election, though administered by the States, exists to serve a specifically federal end. A State that bars its most experienced citizens from congressional service is not merely regulating the mechanics of an election; it is redefining who may hold a federal office — a power the structure of Article I withholds from it.

**The Ballot-Access Label Cannot Rescue What Is in Substance a Qualification**

Petitioners' characterization of Section 3 as a "ballot access" restriction rather than a formal disqualification is a distinction without constitutional significance. The practical effect of Amendment 73 is identical to an outright bar: an incumbent who has served three House terms or two Senate terms may not appear on the ballot in the only forum through which Arkansans elect their federal representatives. This Court has never permitted the States to accomplish through electoral mechanics what they may not accomplish directly. In *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), the Court confirmed that whatever authority the States possess over the "Times, Places and Manner" of congressional elections under Article I, §4, is procedural in character — it concerns the how of an election, not the who may be elected. Nothing in that residual procedural authority permits a State to use its control over the ballot to accomplish a substantive exclusion the Qualifications Clauses forbid. As the Arkansas Supreme Court itself recognized, "the distinction between outright bar and ballot access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp," and it is no less too fine a point to escape the constitutional prohibition established in *Powell*.

**A State May Not Use Its Reserved Powers to Control an Institution of the National Government**

Petitioners' reliance on the Tenth Amendment and the States' reserved powers cannot supply what Article I withholds. This Court settled long ago that the National Government, "though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action," McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819), and that a State may not employ its own constitutional processes to obstruct or reshape the institutions the Constitution itself creates. In *Prigg v. Pennsylvania*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 539 (1842), the Court held that where the Constitution vests a subject in the national scheme, state law that conflicts with that scheme must yield, regardless of the state interest asserted. Congress is such a subject. It is not a state institution on loan to the federal government; it is, as *Yarbrough* and *Classic* confirm, a body whose composition and qualifications the People of the United States fixed through the Constitution's ratification, not through the reserved powers of any single State. Arkansas's Term Limitation Amendment, however popular with its electorate, cannot alter the qualifications for an office that belongs to the Nation as a whole.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, Section 3 of Amendment 73 is unconstitutional under *Powell v. McCormack* and the structural principles confirmed in *McCulloch v. Maryland*, *Ex parte Yarbrough*, *United States v. Classic*, and *Prigg v. Pennsylvania*. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court holding Section 3 void should be affirmed.
