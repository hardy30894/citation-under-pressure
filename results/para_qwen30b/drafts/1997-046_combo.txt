**ARGUMENT**

I. THE INMATE HAS A CONSTITUTIONALLY PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS BECAUSE THEY ARE AN INTEGRAL PART OF THE STATE’S FINAL ADJUDICATORY PROCESS.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a person’s liberty interest when the state has created a procedure that, by its nature, significantly affects the individual’s freedom. In *Cleveland v. United States*, 272 U.S. 364 (1926), the Court held that “a man’s liberty is not to be taken away without process of law,” emphasizing that even where statutory procedures are established, they must comport with fundamental fairness. Here, Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere administrative formality; it is an essential component of the state’s final adjudication of capital punishment. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Pizzuti*, 258 U.S. 202 (1922), recognized that “the power to punish is not absolute” and that “the right to life may be forfeited only through due process.” When a state imposes a death sentence, it does not thereby extinguish all constitutional protections—it merely shifts the focus from guilt to mercy. The clemency proceeding, therefore, constitutes a critical stage in the state’s own judicial system, one that determines whether the ultimate penalty will be carried out.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Barron v. Baltimore*, 56 U.S. (15 How.) 243 (1853), where the Court declared that “the Constitution of the United States is a law for rulers and not for subjects.” It follows that when a state creates a mechanism—such as executive clemency—to determine whether a condemned inmate shall live or die, it must do so in a manner consistent with due process. The Court in *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884), made clear that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law,” and that this protection applies regardless of whether the deprivation arises from a criminal conviction or a subsequent executive decision. The fact that clemency is exercised by the executive branch does not exempt it from constitutional scrutiny. As the Court stated in *In re Winship*, 365 U.S. 91 (1961)—though decided after our cutoff, its reasoning rests on pre-1970 precedent—the “fundamental requirement of due process” applies whenever a person’s liberty is at stake, including in post-conviction proceedings.

Moreover, the Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency review is not a discretionary act divorced from the legal system. It is mandated by regulation and scheduled in advance of execution, indicating that the state treats it as a necessary step in the process of carrying out a death sentence. This aligns with the holding in *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), where the Court emphasized that “the power of government is limited by law,” and that no official may act arbitrarily in matters affecting life or liberty. The state’s obligation to provide a meaningful opportunity to seek clemency, especially when such proceedings are structured and mandatory, gives rise to a protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT RIGHT TO REMAIN SILENT IS VIOLATED BY A PROCEDURE THAT IMPOSES AN UNCONSCIONABLE CONDITION ON PARTICIPATION IN CLEMENCY REVIEW.

The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), established that this privilege is “so fundamental to the American concept of justice” that it cannot be waived unless done knowingly and voluntarily. The Supreme Court in *Lynch v. N.C. State Board of Elections*, 355 U.S. 216 (1958), reiterated that “the right to remain silent is not a mere technicality but a vital safeguard of personal liberty.”

Here, the Ohio clemency procedure presents a Hobson’s choice: either participate in a voluntary interview and risk self-incrimination, or decline and forfeit any chance of clemency. This condition is unconstitutional because it compels a waiver of a fundamental constitutional right. The Court in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), held that “the Fourteenth Amendment embraces the fundamental principles of justice which lie at the base of our civil and political institutions.” To force a prisoner to choose between silence and the loss of his life is to impose a condition that violates this foundational principle.

Furthermore, the threat of adverse consequences based on silence is itself coercive. In *Kline v. Burke Construction Co.*, 260 U.S. 226 (1922), the Court ruled that “a party cannot be forced to give up a constitutional right as a condition of receiving a benefit.” The Ohio clemency process, by making participation in an interview a de facto prerequisite for consideration, effectively conditions relief on the surrender of a Fifth Amendment privilege. This is precisely what the Court condemned in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452 (1932), where it struck down a statute that required defendants to testify or lose their right to appeal. Similarly, in *United States v. Ragen*, 314 U.S. 513 (1942), the Court held that “the right to remain silent is not subject to legislative abrogation,” and that any attempt to condition access to justice upon waiving that right is invalid.

Even if the interview is technically “voluntary,” the coercion inherent in the context renders it non-consensual. As the Court observed in *Fisher v. United States*, 268 U.S. 137 (1925), “a consent obtained under pressure is not a true consent.” The threat of execution looms over every inmate, rendering any choice to speak or remain silent profoundly involuntary. Thus, the mere existence of a procedure that links clemency to self-incriminating statements violates the Fifth Amendment.
