**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that “only those lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are public lands” are subject to regulations “applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not such a regulation. It applies to all lands and waters administered by the National Park Service, wherever located. See 36 C.F.R. § 1.2(a).

Statutory language must be given its ordinary meaning. See Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). The phrase “applicable solely to public lands” limits only those rules that single out public lands; it does not exempt non-federal inholdings from rules of general application. See John v. United States (Katie John III), 720 F.3d 1214, 1228-29 (9th Cir. 2013) (holding that ANILCA preserves federal authority over navigable waters within conservation system units). The same construction follows from the statute’s structure: Title 54 provisions governing the Park System remain in force unless ANILCA expressly repeals them. See 16 U.S.C. § 3207; cf. National Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs., 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005).

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would require the Service to administer each conservation system unit as a checkerboard of regulatory regimes, a result incompatible with the uniform-management mandate of the National Park Service Organic Act, 54 U.S.C. § 100101. See also Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 539-40 (1976) (recognizing federal authority to regulate activities on non-federal land that affect federal property). Nothing in ANILCA’s text or history indicates Congress intended to create such a patchwork.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
