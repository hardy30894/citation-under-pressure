ARGUMENT

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien beyond the removal period. Where a statute admits of two constructions, one raising grave constitutional questions and the other avoiding them, the latter must be adopted. United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366, 407 (1909); Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932).

Indefinite detention of an alien who has effected entry into the United States implicates the Due Process Clause. Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896) (“all persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection guaranteed by” the Fifth Amendment). Resident aliens enjoy the same protections as citizens against arbitrary restraint. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886); Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 596 n.5 (1953). Prolonged confinement without a foreseeable end exceeds any legitimate governmental interest in effectuating removal and therefore violates substantive due process. See Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 544 (1952) (detention must bear a reasonable relation to its purpose).

The statute’s text permits detention only “pending removal.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). This language presupposes that removal remains possible within a reasonable time. When removal becomes impossible, continued detention ceases to serve the statutory purpose and must end. See United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957) (construing immigration supervision provisions narrowly to avoid constitutional doubt). A construction authorizing permanent incarceration would render the statute vulnerable under the Fifth Amendment; the narrower reading limiting detention to the period reasonably necessary for removal therefore controls.

Petitioner’s release is accordingly required once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. The judgment below should be affirmed.
