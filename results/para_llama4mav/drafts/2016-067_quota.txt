The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The distinction between these two terms is crucial in determining whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander) is subject to the FDCPA. As the Fourth Circuit correctly held, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting debts on its own behalf, not on behalf of another.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The plain language of this definition indicates that a debt collector is someone who collects debts for others, not for themselves. See Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)); Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015) (holding that a person must regularly collect debts for others to qualify as a debt collector).

The petitioners argue that Santander is a debt collector because it acquired defaulted debts from CitiFinancial Auto. However, this argument is unpersuasive. The FDCPA's definition of "creditor" excludes "any person to the extent that he receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). This exclusion does not apply to Santander because it purchased the debts for its own account, not solely for the purpose of collecting for CitiFinancial Auto. See Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (holding that a debt collector is someone who collects debts for others, not for themselves).

The petitioners also rely on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the definition of debt collector "any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt ... owed or due another to the extent such activity ... concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person." However, this exclusion is inapplicable because Santander was collecting debts it had purchased, not on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698 (explaining that this exclusion was intended to protect loan servicers who service debts not in default).

The Fourth Circuit's decision is consistent with the holdings of other circuits. In Davidson, the Eleventh Circuit held that a bank was not a debt collector when it collected debts on its own behalf, even though it had previously serviced debts for others. 797 F.3d at 1315-16. Similarly, in Schlegel, the Ninth Circuit held that a bank was not a debt collector when it collected debts it had purchased, even though the debts were originally owed to another. 720 F.3d at 1209.

The petitioners' argument that Santander's status as a debt collector before purchasing the debts somehow makes it a debt collector after the purchase is also unpersuasive. As the Fourth Circuit held, Santander's status as a creditor or debt collector is determined by its actions at the time of the alleged FDCPA violation, not by its previous actions. See Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 362 (6th Cir. 2012) (holding that a loan servicer's status as a debt collector is determined by its actions at the time of the alleged FDCPA violation).

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting debts on its own behalf, not on behalf of another. The Fourth Circuit's decision is consistent with the plain language of the FDCPA and the holdings of other circuits. See FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007); Ruth v. Triumph P'ships, 577 F.3d 790, 796-97 (7th Cir. 2009); Tepper v. Amos Fin. LLC, 898 F.3d 364, 367 (3d Cir. 2018). Therefore, the judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
