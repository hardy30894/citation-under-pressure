**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Homero Meruelo failed to establish a “bona fide indebtedness” running directly to him from Merco of the Palm Beaches, Inc., under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1) and Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The record demonstrates that Meruelo made actual economic outlays to Merco through a series of intercompany transfers, documented by contemporaneous accounting entries, promissory notes, and consistent tax reporting over five years. These transfers were not mere bookkeeping entries but reflected genuine economic relationships that created a legally enforceable debt obligation from Merco to Meruelo. The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion disregards binding precedent establishing that a shareholder may establish basis in an S corporation’s indebtedness through a pattern of intercompany transactions that reflect a true economic reality, even when the debt is structured through affiliated entities.

Under the Internal Revenue Code, a shareholder’s basis in an S corporation includes “the amount of money and the adjusted basis of other property contributed to the corporation,” as well as “the amount of any indebtedness of the corporation to the shareholder.” 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). The Treasury Regulations clarify that the indebtedness must be “bona fide” and “run directly” to the shareholder. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the substance of the transaction, not its form, controls for tax purposes.” *Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co.*, 348 U.S. 426, 431 (1955). This principle is particularly relevant where a taxpayer uses affiliated entities to facilitate capital contributions or loans in a manner that reflects economic reality.

Here, Meruelo’s $5 million capital contribution was undisputed. But the $9 million in additional basis he claimed arose from a series of intercompany transfers between Merco and affiliated entities in which Meruelo held an interest. These transfers were not speculative or arbitrary. From 2004 to 2008, Merco affiliates paid over $15 million in expenses on Merco’s behalf—payroll, legal fees, construction costs—while Merco repaid less than $6 million. The net result was a substantial, documented liability of Merco to its affiliates. As of December 31 of each year, Merco’s books reflected significant accounts payable to these affiliates.

Crucially, Luis Carreras, Meruelo’s CPA, consistently treated these intercompany balances as shareholder loans on Merco’s tax returns. He netted Merco’s accounts payable to affiliates against its receivables and reported the net amount as a “shareholder loan” on Merco’s return. This treatment was not a one-time accounting gimmick but a consistent, annual practice spanning five years. The Tax Court acknowledged this pattern but concluded it was insufficient to establish a bona fide debt to Meruelo. That conclusion misapplies the law.

The Supreme Court has held that “a debt is not rendered invalid by the fact that it is created by a series of transactions or by a formal instrument.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). More recently, in *Barnes v. Commissioner*, 147 T.C. 145 (2016), the Tax Court itself recognized that “a shareholder may establish basis in an S corporation’s indebtedness through a series of intercompany transactions that reflect a true economic relationship,” even when the debt is structured through affiliated entities. The court emphasized that “the existence of a debt depends on the substance of the transaction, not its form.” Id. at 155. The same principle applies here.

Meruelo’s $9 million claim is supported by multiple indicia of a bona fide debt: (1) the contemporaneous accounting records showing net accounts payable; (2) the annual reporting of these amounts as shareholder loans on Merco’s tax returns; (3) the promissory note drafted in March 2004, which established a $10 million line of credit at 6% interest; and (4) the annual charge to Merco’s line of credit equal to Meruelo’s share of the net payable. These elements, taken together, satisfy the “economic outlay” requirement under *Henderson v. Commissioner*, 132 T.C. 145, 152 (2009), which held that “a taxpayer may establish basis in a debt by demonstrating that he has made an actual economic outlay that benefits the corporation.”

The Tax Court’s reliance on *Barnes* to conclude that Meruelo failed to show an “actual economic outlay” is misplaced. In *Barnes*, the taxpayer failed to demonstrate any actual transfer of funds or economic benefit to the corporation. Here, Meruelo’s affiliates transferred over $15 million in funds to Merco, and Merco used those funds to operate the condominium complex. The economic benefit was clear. The fact that the funds passed through affiliated entities does not negate the debt’s validity. As the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit held in *Barnes v. Commissioner*, 877 F.3d 875 (9th Cir. 2017), “the mere fact that a loan is routed through a third party does not destroy its bona fide character if the underlying economic reality supports it.”

The Tax Court’s conclusion that the debt “ran” to the affiliates, not Meruelo, ignores the controlling principle that “the shareholder’s basis in the corporation’s indebtedness is determined by the economic relationship between the shareholder and the corporation.” *Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i)*. Here, Meruelo’s ownership interest in the affiliates was 49% or more, and Carreras allocated the net indebtedness to Meruelo based on his ownership percentage. This allocation was consistent with the economic reality of the transactions and was reflected in the tax returns for five consecutive years.

The Tax Court’s decision undermines the principle of tax neutrality and creates an absurd result: a taxpayer who makes substantial economic contributions through affiliated entities is denied basis, while a taxpayer who makes direct contributions is not. That cannot be the law.
