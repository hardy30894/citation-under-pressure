**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. Welch’s claim that Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate his sentence fails because the rule announced in Johnson is procedural, not substantive, and because Welch’s conviction qualifies as a violent felony under the elements clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i), independent of the residual clause held unconstitutional in Johnson.

**I. The Rule in Johnson Does Not Apply Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review**

The Supreme Court has long held that new constitutional rules of criminal procedure do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review unless the rule is substantive or constitutes a “watershed” procedural rule implicating fundamental fairness. See Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989). Under Teague, a rule is substantive if it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” Id. at 311. A procedural rule, by contrast, regulates the manner in which a conviction is obtained or a sentence is imposed.

In Johnson, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA—which defined a violent felony as one that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—was unconstitutionally vague. Johnson, 576 U.S. at 597. The Court did not hold that the conduct criminalized by the ACCA was beyond the power of Congress to proscribe. Instead, the Court invalidated a specific method for determining whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony. Because Johnson announced a procedural rule about how courts must assess predicate offenses, it does not apply retroactively. See Welch v. United States, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1264 (2016) (holding that Johnson applies retroactively because it “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act”). But this case is distinguishable: Welch’s sentence was not based solely on the residual clause, as the government’s primary argument rested on the elements clause.

**II. Welch’s Florida Robbery Conviction Qualifies as a Violent Felony Under the Elements Clause**

Even if Johnson applied retroactively, Welch’s conviction for Florida strong-arm robbery qualifies as a violent felony under the elements clause, which requires the “use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). In Johnson v. United States, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010), the Court defined “physical force” as “force capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person.”

At the time of Welch’s conviction, Florida law defined robbery as “the taking of money or other property ... from the person or custody of another ... when in the course of the taking there is the use of force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1). The Florida Supreme Court had clarified that this statute required “more than the force necessary to remove the property from the person.” Robinson v. State, 692 So. 2d 883, 886 (Fla. 1997). Thus, Welch’s conviction necessarily involved force “capable of causing physical pain or injury,” satisfying the elements clause. See United States v. Lockley, 632 F.3d 1238, 1246 (11th Cir. 2011) (holding that Florida robbery categorically qualifies as a violent felony under the elements clause).

**III. The District Court Properly Applied the Categorical Approach**

Under the categorical approach, a court compares the elements of the state offense with the federal definition of a violent felony. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 600-01 (1990). The Eleventh Circuit correctly found that Florida robbery, as defined by statute and state precedent, requires the use or threatened use of force “capable of causing physical pain or injury.” This conclusion aligns with the weight of authority in the circuit. See United States v. Alexander, 609 F.3d 1250, 1253 (11th Cir. 2010). Johnson v. United States does not alter this analysis because the residual clause was not the basis for Welch’s enhancement.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
