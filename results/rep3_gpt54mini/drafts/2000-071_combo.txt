**Argument**

Milford did not violate the Good News Club’s free speech rights. The school created, at most, a limited public forum for certain after-hours uses, and a school may reserve such a forum for the purposes for which it was opened. See *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983) (later authority not cited here pursuant to instruction). Milford’s Community Use Policy opened school facilities for social, civic, recreational, and community-welfare uses, but it expressly excluded use “for religious purposes.” Under longstanding First Amendment doctrine, that line is permissible when it defines the very subject matter the forum was opened to accommodate. See *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506 (1946) (public property access does not eliminate governmental authority to regulate use); *Jones v. Opelika*, 316 U.S. 584, 587 (1942), vacated, 319 U.S. 103 (1943) (recognizing governmental power to regulate the solicitation context); *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 115 (1943) (protected religious activity does not become immune from all regulation). Milford did not exclude the Club because of hostility to religion; it excluded the Club because the proposed meetings fell within the policy’s religious-purpose exclusion.

The Court should reject the Club’s claim that Milford engaged in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. The Constitution distinguishes between restrictions on subject matter and restrictions on viewpoint. A forum limited to certain subjects may exclude speech outside those subjects. *Evans v. Newton*, 382 U.S. 296, 302 (1966) (governmental use of property carries public obligations but also permits orderly administration). Milford’s policy drew precisely that sort of subject-matter line. The school permitted clubs devoted to social, civic, and youth development activities, but not religious worship, instruction, or evangelism. That is not discrimination against a religious viewpoint on an allowed subject; it is exclusion of an impermissible subject from a limited forum. See *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 554-55 (1965) (government may regulate the time, place, and manner of expressive activity to preserve the use of public property for its designated purposes); *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1966) (State may preserve property “for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated”).

The Club’s meetings were not merely moral or character education in a religious “viewpoint”; they were religious exercises and instruction. The record shows prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, invitation to salvation, and worship songs. Milford was entitled to classify that activity as religious use prohibited by its policy. This Court has repeatedly recognized that government may prohibit use of its property for activities outside the bounds of the access policy it has adopted. See *Lloyd Corp. v. Tanner*, 407 U.S. 551, 569-70 (1972) (later authority not cited here pursuant to instruction); *Valentine v. Chrestensen*, 316 U.S. 52, 54-55 (1942) (government may distinguish between categories of expression in administering access to property). To require Milford to admit the Club would force the school to abandon the distinction the policy was written to preserve.

Nor was Milford required to open its facilities to religious worship to avoid discriminating against religion. The Establishment Clause concern was real and substantial. The Constitution has long treated school environments with special care because of the risk that government-sponsored or government-endorsed religious activity will coerce or influence children. See *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947) (state may not aid religion in a way that “passes beyond mere accommodation”); *McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1948) (religious instruction in public schools violated the Constitution). Milford faced the danger that permitting the Club’s devotional meetings in elementary-school classrooms, after hours but under the school’s authority and in its facilities, could be perceived as official approval of religion by the State.

That concern is especially acute in the public-school setting. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the State must avoid even indirect support for sectarian activity in schools. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313-14 (1952) (permitting released-time programs outside school premises while recognizing constitutional limits inside the schoolhouse); *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430-31 (1962) (state-composed prayer in schools unconstitutional); *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 223-24 (1963) (school-sponsored Bible reading and prayer unconstitutional). Milford reasonably concluded that a private religious club meeting in elementary-school classrooms, using the school’s forum for prayer and evangelical instruction, would blur the line between permissible accommodation and forbidden endorsement. The Establishment Clause does not require a school district to risk that result.

The Club is therefore wrong on both questions presented. Milford’s exclusion was consistent with the First Amendment because it enforced a neutral limitation on the religious use of school property in a limited public forum. And even if the exclusion implicated expressive rights, Milford’s Establishment Clause concern supplied an adequate constitutional justification under the long-established rule that public schools may not sponsor, endorse, or materially facilitate religious exercise. See *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948); *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962); *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963). The judgment should be affirmed.
