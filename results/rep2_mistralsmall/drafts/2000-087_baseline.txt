**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699 (2001). The Supreme Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 690. The Court held that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." *Id.* at 701.

The Fifth Circuit erred in upholding Zadvydas’s detention under § 1231(a)(6) because it failed to apply this "reasonable time" limitation. The court instead deferred to the government’s assertion that removal remained possible, even though no country had agreed to accept Zadvydas. *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 291 (5th Cir. 1999). This approach ignored the Supreme Court’s clear directive that indefinite detention is impermissible. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 699.

The Court established a rebuttable presumption that detention beyond six months is unreasonable unless the government demonstrates a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. *Id.* at 705. Here, Zadvydas had been detained for years beyond the removal period with no prospect of removal. The government’s efforts to locate a country willing to accept him were unsuccessful, and no such prospect existed. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding misapplied the statute and violated Zadvydas’s due process rights.

II. INDEFINITE DETENTION VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

The Due Process Clause prohibits indefinite civil detention absent compelling justification. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690 (citing *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 77 (1992)). The Court recognized that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention only to effectuate removal, not as punishment. *Id.* at 697. Once removal is no longer possible, continued detention lacks rational justification and violates due process.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), was misplaced. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien at the border, not a resident alien with a final removal order. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700. The Court distinguished *Mezei* on this basis, emphasizing that Zadvydas’s detention was not punitive but aimed at effectuating removal. *Id.* However, when removal becomes impossible, detention serves no legitimate purpose and violates due process.

The government’s argument that Zadvydas’s detention was justified by his criminal history is unavailing. The Court rejected the notion that dangerousness alone justifies indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6). *Id.* at 699. The statute’s text and purpose limit detention to the removal period, and any further confinement must be justified by a legitimate government interest. *Id.* at 697. Here, no such interest exists.

III. THE HABEAS CORPUS STATUTE PROVIDES A REMEDY FOR UNLAWFUL DETENTION

The district court properly exercised habeas jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 to review Zadvydas’s detention. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 687. The Court held that habeas review is available to challenge post-removal-period detention under § 1231(a)(6). *Id.* at 693. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding in *Zadvydas v. Underdown* was rejected. *Id.*

The government’s assertion that Zadvydas’s detention was authorized by former § 1252(a) is unpersuasive. The Court clarified that § 1231(a)(6) is the governing statute for post-removal-period detention. *Id.* at 693. The government’s reliance on mandatory detention provisions is misplaced, as those provisions apply only during the removal period. *Id.* at 697.

**CONCLUSION**

The Fifth Circuit erred in upholding Zadvydas’s indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6). The statute does not authorize detention beyond a reasonable time, and once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates due process. The district court properly granted habeas relief, and its judgment should be affirmed.
