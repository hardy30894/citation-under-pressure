**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from the arbitrary deprivation of life or liberty without due process. *Santosky v. Kramer*, 455 U.S. 745, 753 (1982). While the Supreme Court has long recognized that “the Constitution does not require the State to create any liberty interest at all,” *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972), it has also held that when a state *does* create a liberty interest—whether through statute, regulation, or established practice—due process protections attach. *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974). In this case, Ohio has not only created a clemency process but has structured it as a mandatory, pre-execution review with significant consequences for the inmate’s fate.

The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s (APA) Death Penalty Clemency Procedure, codified at Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07 and implemented through a regulation effective July 21, 1994, mandates that a clemency review be scheduled 45 days before any scheduled execution date—unless stayed. This is not a discretionary or advisory process; it is a statutorily required administrative step. The APA’s own regulation explicitly states that “the parole board must schedule a clemency review” under these circumstances. *See* Ohio Admin. Code § 5120-1-07. This mandatory nature transforms what might otherwise be a mere formality into a process that creates a protected liberty interest.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when a state establishes a procedure that “has a significant impact on an individual’s freedom,” a liberty interest arises. *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 484 (1995). In *Sandin*, the Court emphasized that “the existence of a liberty interest depends on whether the deprivation of the interest is so significant that it implicates due process.” Id. at 484. Here, the deprivation is not merely significant—it is existential. The clemency process is the final opportunity for a death row inmate to seek mercy before execution. To deny that this process creates a liberty interest is to ignore the very real consequences of its operation.

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that no such interest exists is inconsistent with *Dumschat*, which the Court itself has cited as controlling. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that a prisoner had no protected liberty interest in a pardon process. But the Court’s reasoning rested on the fact that the Connecticut Board of Pardons had no established procedure and operated with no transparency or consistency. *Id.* at 464. In contrast, Ohio has a codified, mandatory, and predictable clemency process. The APA’s regulation is not a mere “suggestion” or “invitation”; it is a binding administrative requirement. The distinction is critical. As the Court noted in *Wolff*, “the nature of the interest and the procedures by which it is affected are central to the due process inquiry.” 418 U.S. at 557.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that even in the context of executive clemency, due process may apply when the state creates a structured process. *See* *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985) (holding that due process applies to the first appeal as of right because the state has made it “an integral part of the adjudicatory process”). Ohio has done the same with clemency: it has made the process mandatory, time-bound, and institutionally embedded. To treat it as a mere “grace” is to ignore the reality of its operation. The state’s own regulation confirms that the clemency review is not optional—it is required by law. That alone is sufficient to trigger due process protections.

II. The APA’s clemency interview procedure violates the Fifth Amendment by coercing a waiver of the right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be a witness against themselves. *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 460 (1966). This right is not forfeited by participation in a state-administered process unless the waiver is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Colorado v. Spring*, 479 U.S. 564, 574 (1987). Here, the APA’s clemency interview procedure creates a coercive environment that effectively compels a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right.

The regulation provides that if a prisoner requests a personal interview, the APA will appoint one or more members to conduct it. The interview is conducted in private, and the prisoner is told that any statement made will be considered at the clemency hearing. *See* Ohio Admin. Code § 5120-1-07(C)(2). While the regulation does not *require* the prisoner to speak, the structure of the process creates a “Hobson’s choice”: either participate and risk self-incrimination, or remain silent and forfeit any opportunity to present mitigating evidence.

This is not a hypothetical concern. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the threat of adverse consequences” can render a waiver involuntary. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976). In *Baxter*, the Court held that a prisoner’s right against self-incrimination was violated when prison officials threatened to deny him good-time credits if he refused to answer questions. The Court emphasized that “the risk of adverse consequences” could “coerce a waiver” even if no formal compulsion existed. Id. at 324.

Here, the adverse consequence is not a loss of good time, but the loss of a final opportunity to seek mercy. The APA’s own regulation acknowledges that statements made during the interview “shall be considered at the clemency hearing.” This creates a direct link between silence and disadvantage.
