ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Holding that Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. However, this holding is contrary to the Supreme Court's decision in O'Neal v. McAninch, 513 U.S. 432 (1995). In O'Neal, the Court emphasized that a habeas petitioner's failure to exhaust state remedies does not necessarily constitute a waiver of their claims. Id. at 444. Rather, the Court held that a petitioner's failure to exhaust may be excused if the petitioner can demonstrate cause and prejudice. Id.

Here, Selam's failure to raise his Confrontation Clause claim in the tribal court of appeals does not necessarily constitute a waiver. Under the Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA"), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), tribal courts are required to provide defendants with certain fundamental rights, including the right to confrontation. The ICRA does not explicitly require that defendants exhaust their tribal remedies before seeking habeas relief in federal court. See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 59 (1978) (noting that the ICRA "does not explicitly require exhaustion of tribal remedies").

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit has held that a petitioner's failure to exhaust tribal remedies may be excused if the tribal court has not provided an adequate opportunity for review. See, e.g., Poodry v. Tonawanda Band of Seneca Indians, 85 F.3d 874, 894 (2d Cir. 1996) (cited with approval in Miranda v. Anchondo, 684 F.3d 844, 853 (9th Cir. 2012)). In this case, Selam's tribal court appeal was denied without a hearing, and he was not provided with an opportunity to raise his Confrontation Clause claim. Under these circumstances, the district court erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim.

II. The Tribal Court Denied Selam His Right to Compulsory Process

The district court also erred in holding that Selam was not denied his right to compulsory process. Under the ICRA, tribal courts are required to provide defendants with the right to compulsory process, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). The Supreme Court has held that this right is essential to a defendant's ability to present a defense. See Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) (holding that the right to compulsory process is "essential to a fair trial").

Here, Selam provided his attorney, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of five potential character witnesses. Although Leno-Baker located three of these witnesses, two of them informed her that their testimony would be damaging to Selam. The tribal judge and Leno-Baker both advised Selam of his right to compel the appearance of witnesses on his behalf, but Selam did not ask the judge to subpoena anyone. However, this does not necessarily mean that Selam waived his right to compulsory process.

The Ninth Circuit has held that a defendant's failure to request a subpoena does not constitute a waiver of their right to compulsory process if the defendant has not been adequately informed of their rights. See United States v. Westerdahl, 945 F.2d 1083, 1086 (9th Cir. 1991). In this case, the record suggests that Selam was not adequately informed of his rights, as he relied on Leno-Baker to contact his witnesses and did not understand the need to request a subpoena. Under these circumstances, the tribal court denied Selam his right to compulsory process.

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's denial of Selam's habeas petition should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
