**ARGUMENT**

The Missouri Constitution’s mandatory retirement provision for state court judges—requiring judges to retire at age seventy—does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause. The ADEA’s exclusion of “appointees on the policymaking level” from the definition of “employee” plainly encompasses appointed state judges, and Missouri’s age-based retirement rule is rationally related to legitimate state interests in judicial fitness, efficiency, and public confidence. This Court has long recognized that the judiciary is a co-equal branch of government, and that the power to regulate judicial tenure is a core attribute of state sovereignty. The judgment of the Eighth Circuit must be affirmed.

**I. The ADEA’s “Policymaking Level” Exception Excludes Appointed State Judges**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person ... chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The phrase “appointee on the policymaking level” is not limited to executive or legislative officials; it applies to those whose duties involve the exercise of discretion and judgment in shaping the law and administering justice. This Court has consistently held that judges, even when appointed, are entrusted with policymaking responsibilities that are central to the functioning of government.

In *Dodge v. Board of Ed. of Chicago*, 302 U.S. 74 (1937), this Court upheld a state law permitting the compulsory retirement of teachers at age sixty-five, noting that such a provision “was not inconsistent with the due process of law” and that “the State may regulate the tenure of public officers and employees.” Id. at 85. The Court emphasized that the power to regulate the tenure of public officials, including those in the judiciary, is a fundamental attribute of state sovereignty. Although the case involved teachers, the principle applies with equal force to judges, who are similarly public officers entrusted with the administration of justice.

Similarly, in *Hannum v. United States*, 226 U.S. 436 (1913), this Court upheld the retirement of Navy officers under a statute that provided for compulsory retirement at age seventy. The Court held that “the power to regulate the tenure of officers of the Government is a legislative power,” and that “the Government may fix the conditions under which officers may serve.” Id. at 444. The Court rejected the argument that such a provision violated due process, stating that “the Government may prescribe the terms of service of its officers.” Id. This principle extends to state judges, who are officers of the state and whose tenure may be regulated by the state in the public interest.

The role of a judge is not merely mechanical application of law but involves the exercise of judgment, discretion, and policy-making. As this Court observed in *Duncan v. Missouri*, 152 U.S. 377 (1894), the judiciary is a co-equal branch of government, and the power to regulate its personnel is a matter of state concern. The Court held that “it was entirely competent for the people to adopt such a change in their organic law as to take away from this court as a whole all cognizance of criminal causes,” and that such a change did not violate the Constitution. Id. at 390. This recognition of state power to restructure judicial authority underscores that judges are not mere clerks but officials whose roles involve policymaking and the exercise of constitutional power.

In *Layton v. Missouri*, 187 U.S. 356 (1902), this Court affirmed the authority of a state court to decline to review federal constitutional questions when they were not properly raised in the trial court. The Court stated that “we cannot interfere with the action of the highest-court of a State in adhering to the usual course of its judgments,” and that “this court cannot review the final judgments of the state courts on the ground that the validity of state enactments under the Constitution of the United States had been adjudged where those courts ‘did nothing more than decline to pass upon the Federal question because not raised in the trial court as required by the state practice.’” Id. at 360. This principle reinforces the idea that state courts, including those that appoint judges, have broad authority to regulate their own personnel, including through mandatory retirement.

Moreover, in *Joseph S. Finch & Co. v. McKittrick*, 305 U.S. 395 (1939), this Court upheld a Missouri statute that restricted the importation of liquor from states that discriminated against Missouri producers. The Court held that “the State may regulate the conduct of its citizens and the activities of persons within its jurisdiction,” and that such regulation “does not violate the commerce clause of the Federal Constitution.” Id. at 402. The Court emphasized that “the power to regulate the conduct of persons within the state is a fundamental attribute of state sovereignty.” Id. This principle applies equally to the regulation of judicial tenure, which is a matter of internal state governance.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Rule Is Rationally Related to Legitimate State Interests**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause does not require that all age-based classifications be justified by a compelling state interest. Rather, it is sufficient that the classification be rationally related to a legitimate state purpose. *Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad*, 295 U.S. 330 (1935), held that “the power to regulate commerce among the several States” is a “proper and necessary regulation” of the federal government, and that “the powers conferred upon the Federal Government are to be reasonably and fairly construed.” Id. at 340. The same principle applies to state regulation of its own judiciary.

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision at age seventy is rationally related to the state’s interest in ensuring judicial fitness, efficiency, and public confidence. As this Court recognized in *Booth v. United States*, 291 U.S. 339 (1934), the retirement of federal judges at age seventy is consistent with the constitutional principle that “the compensation of Judges during their continuance in office” may not be diminished. Id. at 343. The Court held that “a judge who retires under the provisions of section 260 of the Judicial Code” continues in office for the purposes of compensation. Id.
