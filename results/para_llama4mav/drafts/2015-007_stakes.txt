The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously sentence a defendant to death.

First, regarding the determination of mental retardation, the Supreme Court in Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction on execution would be enforced. Florida law sets forth a three-pronged test to determine mental retardation as a bar to the death penalty, which is to be determined by the trial court. See Nixon v. State, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla.2009); § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). The burden is on the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. See Nixon, 2 So.3d at 145; § 921.137(4), Fla. Stat. (2012). As the determination of mental retardation is not a fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the statutory maximum, it is not subject to the Sixth Amendment jury trial guarantee as interpreted in Ring.

Second, regarding the requirement of a unanimous jury verdict, Florida's capital sentencing scheme is not constitutionally infirm. While the Supreme Court in Ring held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find aggravating factors that increase the penalty for a crime beyond the statutory maximum, it did not require a unanimous jury verdict. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida's scheme, which allows a jury to recommend death by a majority vote, is consistent with the Sixth Amendment.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of "heightened reliability" in death penalty cases, see Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), but it has not mandated jury unanimity. Florida's outlier status among death penalty states is a matter for the legislature to address, not the courts. See Steele v. State, 921 So.2d 538, 548-49 (Fla.2005).

In conclusion, the Florida death sentencing scheme complies with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The determination of mental retardation is a matter for the trial court, and the jury's non-unanimous recommendation of death is permissible under the Constitution.
