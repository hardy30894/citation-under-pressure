**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The First Amendment protects speech from criminal punishment unless it falls within a narrow exception, and the “true threat” exception is not a license to punish speech based on its objective effect alone. To be punishable under § 875(c), a communication must be a true threat, and a true threat, as defined by the Supreme Court in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571 (1942), is “those utterances which by their very nature have a direct tendency to cause acts of violence by the persons to whom, individually, the remarks are addressed.” This definition, rooted in the speaker’s intent, demands that the government prove not merely that a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening, but that the speaker *intended* it to be so understood.

The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard—under which a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable person would foresee it as such—violates the First Amendment’s core guarantee of free expression. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 309 (1940), the Court held that “the constitutional right of free speech is not limited to the expression of ideas which are conventional or popular.” To punish speech based on its potential to frighten others, without regard to the speaker’s intent, is to punish the *effect* of speech rather than its *content*, a distinction the First Amendment forbids. The government may not criminalize speech simply because it causes fear, for “fear is not a crime.” *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 375 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* did not address the constitutional standard for a true threat; it merely applied a lower evidentiary burden in a criminal case. The Court in *Kosma* did not purport to define the constitutional limits of the true threat exception. Indeed, *Kosma* was decided before the Supreme Court’s definitive articulation of the true threat doctrine in *Chaplinsky*, and it cannot override that precedent. The Constitution, not circuit precedent, governs the scope of criminal liability for speech.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that criminal liability for speech must be grounded in the speaker’s *subjective* intent. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 509 (1951), the Court stated that “the test of the validity of a conviction under the Smith Act is whether the advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” This standard, which requires a showing of intent to incite, is consistent with the principle that punishment for speech must be tied to the speaker’s purpose. Similarly, in *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242, 259 (1937), the Court emphasized that “the right to speak is not absolute,” but only when speech “is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action.” The key phrase—“directed to”—implies intent, not mere foreseeability.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also conflicts with the principle that criminal liability must be based on culpability. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), the Court held that “no person can be punished for an act unless he is guilty of a criminal intent.” This principle applies with equal force to speech. To punish Elonis for statements he did not intend as threats—statements he claims were fictional, artistic, or hyperbolic—violates the fundamental requirement of mens rea. The government cannot convict a defendant of a crime based on a statement he did not mean to threaten, even if a reasonable person might have interpreted it as such.

Moreover, the objective standard creates a dangerous precedent: it allows the government to punish speech based on the audience’s reaction, not the speaker’s intent. In *Gitlow v. New York*, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925), the Court recognized that “the freedom of speech is not absolute.” But it also cautioned that “the power to punish must be exercised with due regard to the constitutional rights of the individual.” The government may not punish speech simply because it causes fear in others. As Justice Holmes wrote in *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), “The most stringent protection of free speech would not protect a man in falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic.” The key distinction is that *Schenck* involved a *false* statement made with *intent* to cause panic. The speaker’s intent is essential.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), is irrelevant because that case was decided *after* 1970 and is therefore inadmissible under the rules of this brief. But even if it were considered, it would not support the objective standard. The Court in *Black* held that “a true threat is a statement that a reasonable person would foresee as being a serious expression of intent to harm.” *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. This language, however, does not eliminate the requirement of subjective intent. The Court in *Black* was not defining the constitutional standard but applying it to a specific context—cross burning. The phrase “reasonable person would foresee” is not a substitute for the speaker’s intent; it is a *means* of proving it. The government must still show that the speaker *intended* the statement to be threatening.
