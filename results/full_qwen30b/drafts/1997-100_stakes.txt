**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing enhancements—contradicts settled precedent and undermines a fundamental protection against governmental overreach. The Court must reverse.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This protection is not limited to trials on the underlying substantive offense. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a capital offense could not be retried on the same charge after the state appealed the acquittal and the appellate court reversed. The Court emphasized that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” and that “the State’s interest in obtaining a conviction is not sufficient to justify a second trial after an acquittal.” *Id.* at 439, 440. The Court did not limit this principle to capital cases. Rather, it grounded its holding in the constitutional text and the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” including the risk of multiple prosecutions, the burden on the defendant, and the potential for abuse. *Id.* at 439.

The same principles apply with equal force in noncapital sentencing proceedings. When a defendant is charged with a prior conviction to trigger a sentencing enhancement—such as under California’s Three Strikes law—the determination of that prior conviction is not a mere administrative formality. It is a factfinding proceeding that directly affects the severity of punishment. As the Court recognized in *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 155 (1997), “the sentencing judge may consider any fact that increases the penalty for a crime, so long as the fact is not itself a crime.” But when the government seeks to increase punishment based on a prior conviction, it is not merely assessing a fact—it is prosecuting a new issue: whether the defendant has a prior felony conviction that qualifies for enhancement. That is, in substance, a second prosecution for a sentencing-related offense.

The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it involved capital sentencing is legally unsound. The Court in *Bullington* did not limit its reasoning to capital cases. In fact, the Court explicitly stated that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” without qualification. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. The Court’s concern was not the severity of the penalty, but the *nature* of the proceeding: whether it bore the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence. *Id.* at 439–440. The Court in *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203, 209 (1984), reaffirmed this principle, holding that “the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings when the proceeding involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 209. The Court in *Rumsey* applied the Clause to a federal sentencing hearing where the defendant had been acquitted of a prior conviction, and the government sought to retry the prior conviction to enhance the sentence. *Id.* at 205–206.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection in sentencing is misplaced. *Williams* held that due process does not require a jury trial or formal evidentiary procedures at sentencing. *Id.* at 245–246. But *Williams* did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. Indeed, the Court in *Williams* explicitly noted that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251. That statement reflects a recognition that sentencing is not a trial on guilt or innocence, but it does not mean that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable. The Clause is not a procedural rule; it is a substantive constitutional protection. Its purpose is to prevent the government from subjecting a defendant to multiple prosecutions for the same offense, regardless of whether the proceeding is labeled a “trial” or a “sentencing hearing.”

The California Supreme Court’s assertion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because the prior conviction is “not a crime” is a non sequitur. The Clause does not require the prior conviction to be a crime; it protects against being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *U.S. Const. amend. V*. The offense in question is not the prior crime itself, but the *prosecution* of the prior conviction allegation to enhance punishment. When the government seeks to retry a prior conviction allegation after a reversal based on insufficient evidence, it is prosecuting the same issue—whether the defendant has a prior felony conviction—under a different label. That is precisely the kind of double jeopardy the Clause was designed to prevent.

The Court has consistently held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when the government seeks to retry a fact that was previously litigated and resolved. In *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 11 (1978), the Court held that “a defendant who has been acquitted of a criminal charge may not be retried on that charge, even if the acquittal was based on insufficient evidence.” *Id.* at 11. The Court reasoned that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects the defendant’s right to have the prosecution’s case tested by a jury and to have the jury’s verdict be final.” *Id.* at 10. The same logic applies here: when a jury or judge finds that the prior conviction allegation is not proved beyond a reasonable doubt, the government should not be allowed to retry the same issue to enhance punishment.
