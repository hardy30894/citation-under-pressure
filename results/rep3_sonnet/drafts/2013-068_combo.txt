ARGUMENT

I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION THAT SATISFIES THE FIRST AMENDMENT.

This Court has long recognized that "a State may by general and non-discriminatory legislation regulate the times, the places, and the manner of soliciting upon its streets, and of holding meetings thereon." Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 306 (1940). The Massachusetts Legislature did precisely that. Section 120E½ says nothing about what may be said near a reproductive health care facility; it regulates only where speakers of every viewpoint may stand. Petitioners, clinic escorts, commuters, and clergy alike are subject to the identical thirty-five-foot limitation. A law that applies "without regard to content" is the paradigmatic content-neutral regulation this Court has repeatedly sustained. Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949) (upholding a ban on sound trucks because "[t]he right of free speech" does not guarantee "the best" means of reaching an audience where adequate alternatives remain).

Public streets and sidewalks are indeed "quintessential" forums for expression, but that status has never rendered them immune from reasonable regulation. As this Court explained in Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939), the privilege to use the streets for communication "is not absolute, but relative, and must be exercised in subordination to the general comfort and convenience, and in consonance with peace and good order." The government's "power to regulate the use of its own streets and parks in the interest of all" is undeniable when regulation is even-handed and directed at legitimate ends. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). Massachusetts's interest here—ensuring that patients and staff can safely traverse a fixed, clearly marked zone at clinic entrances—is exactly the kind of "public convenience" interest this Court sanctioned in Cox and Kovacs.

II. FIXED BUFFER ZONES AROUND SENSITIVE FACILITIES HAVE LONG BEEN HELD CONSTITUTIONAL.

Petitioners' complaint is not truly that Massachusetts singled out their message, but that it drew a line around a location. This Court has consistently approved exactly that kind of place-based regulation when tied to an important government interest. In Cameron v. Johnson, 390 U.S. 611 (1968), the Court sustained a Mississippi statute forbidding picketing that obstructed ingress and egress to courthouses, because the statute was directed at conduct that interfered with orderly access to a building, not at any viewpoint. The same logic controls here: the Act does not silence petitioners' message; it merely ensures that the doorway itself remains unobstructed.

Likewise, in Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 559, 562 (1965), this Court recognized that "[a] State may adopt safeguards necessary and appropriate to assure that the administration of justice at all stages is free from outside control and pressure," and upheld the State's authority to keep courthouse environs clear of picketing. If a State may protect the undisturbed function of a courthouse, it may surely protect a woman's undisturbed access to a health facility. And where the government's own property or a facility's approach is concerned, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). Massachusetts's thirty-five-foot markers do nothing more than preserve clinic entrances for their intended, lawful use—patient access—without touching what may be said a few feet away.

III. THE ACT LEAVES OPEN AMPLE ALTERNATIVE CHANNELS OF COMMUNICATION.

A valid time, place, and manner regulation need not maximize a speaker's preferred method of contact; it need only leave open adequate substitute channels. Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), upheld a municipal licensing scheme for park meetings against a First Amendment challenge because orderly, non-discriminatory regulation of the "manner" of assembly did not foreclose the underlying right to speak. The record below shows that petitioners retain every meaningful channel: they may speak, sing, pray aloud, display signs, wear expressive clothing, and hand literature to those who approach or pause outside the thirty-five-foot line. McCullen herself reported dozens of successful conversions even after the buffer zones took effect. That petitioners might prefer closer physical proximity does not establish a constitutional violation; Kovacs squarely rejected the notion that the First Amendment guarantees a speaker's optimal method of persuasion. 336 U.S. at 87.

IV. THIS COURT'S PRE-EXISTING FRAMEWORK, INDEPENDENT OF ANY LATER DECISION, RESOLVES THIS CASE.

The Act can be sustained without resort to any single case; it follows directly from the settled principles of Cantwell, Hague, Cox v. New Hampshire, Kovacs, Poulos, Adderley, Cox v. Louisiana, and Cameron. Each confirms that a State may draw reasonable, content-neutral lines around sensitive locations to protect public safety and orderly access, so long as speech itself remains free to occur nearby. Massachusetts's thirty-five-foot buffer does exactly that—no more, no less.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
