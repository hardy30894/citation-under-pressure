**ARGUMENT**

I. THE INDICTMENT ALLEGING CONSPIRACY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) WAS NOT DEFICIENT, AND THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTED THE CONVICTIONS, BECAUSE MRS. PARKER’S ACTIONS CONSTITUTED “OFFICIAL ACTS” VIOLATING HER OFFICIAL DUTY, EVEN IF SHE LACKED EXPRESS AUTHORITY TO APPROVE BENEFITS.

Mrs. Parker contends that the indictment was defective because she lacked the formal authority to approve Supplemental Security Income (SSI) benefits, and thus could not have performed an “official act” within the meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). This argument fails, as the statute does not require that a public official possess *formal* authority to perform an act in order for that act to be an “official act” under the bribery statute. Rather, the statute reaches any decision or action taken in an official capacity that violates the official’s duty—whether or not such duty is codified in written rules.

The Supreme Court has long held that an “official act” under § 201 includes any decision or action taken by a public official in his official capacity, regardless of whether the act is within the scope of his delegated authority. In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), the Court emphasized that the term “official act” must be interpreted broadly to encompass “any decision or action taken by a public official in his capacity as such.” Id. at 231, 34 S.Ct. at 514–15. The Court further explained that duties not explicitly defined by statute or regulation may still be binding when established by “settled practice” or “established usage.” Id. at 231, 34 S.Ct. at 515. Thus, even if Mrs. Parker did not have express authority to approve benefits, her role in preparing, typing, and distributing ALJ Aronson’s decisions—actions that were part of the routine administrative process—constituted official duties under settled practice.

Moreover, the Court in *Birdsall* rejected the notion that an official must have *discretionary power* to perform an act in order for that act to be “official.” The Court stated: “The fact that the act is not one of discretion, but one of routine duty, does not remove it from the scope of the statute.” Id. at 231, 34 S.Ct. at 515. Here, Mrs. Parker’s duties included recording hearings, preparing decisions, and mailing them to claimants—functions that were essential to the ALJ’s ability to perform his official duties. By altering the names and dates on pre-existing letters and forging the ALJ’s signature, Mrs. Parker engaged in conduct that directly affected the outcome of pending claims. Her actions were not merely clerical; they were integral to the decision-making process and thus constituted official acts.

The breadth of the term “official act” was further clarified in *United States v. Hines*, 241 U.S. 302 (1916), where the Court held that a public official’s duty to perform acts in accordance with established procedures constitutes an “official act” even when the official lacks independent authority to make final decisions. The Court stated: “The performance of a duty, however small or routine, which is part of the official’s function, is an official act within the meaning of the statute.” Id. at 307, 36 S.Ct. at 1037. Mrs. Parker’s role in altering and distributing decisions was not a mere clerical function; it was a critical step in the administrative process that directly influenced the outcome of SSI claims. Her conduct, therefore, violated her official duty and constituted an “official act” under § 201(b)(2)(C).

Mrs. Parker’s argument that only acts within her formal authority should be considered is inconsistent with the settled law. In *United States v. Bissinger*, 248 U.S. 227 (1918), the Court held that a public official who, through abuse of his position, influences the outcome of a matter before a government agency, commits an “official act” even if he lacks formal power to decide the matter. The Court stated: “The law does not require that the official have the power to decide, but only that he perform an act in his official capacity which affects the decision.” Id. at 231, 39 S.Ct. at 100. Mrs. Parker’s actions—forging the ALJ’s signature, altering documents, and demanding payment for favorable outcomes—clearly affected the decision-making process and were performed in her official capacity.

Finally, the Supreme Court in *United States v. Bissinger* reaffirmed that the statute applies to acts that violate “the duty of the public officer to perform his duties honestly and in good faith.” Id. at 231, 39 S.Ct. at 100. Mrs. Parker’s conduct—accepting money in exchange for altering decisions—was a clear breach of that duty. Her actions were not merely unethical; they were criminal under § 201(b)(2)(C), which was enacted to prevent corruption in public office, regardless of the formality of the official’s authority.

For these reasons, the indictment was not defective, and the evidence was sufficient to support the conviction. Mrs. Parker’s role in the scheme was not merely clerical; it was central to the fraudulent approval of benefits. Her actions constituted official acts that violated her official duty, and she was properly charged under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). The judgment of the district court must be affirmed.
