**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution Establish the Exclusive Criteria for Membership in Congress.**

The Constitution prescribes the qualifications for members of the House of Representatives and the Senate in Article I, § 2, cl. 2 and Article I, § 3, cl. 3. These provisions set forth age, citizenship, and inhabitancy requirements—and no more. This Court has long recognized that the Framers intended these qualifications to be exclusive, leaving no room for states to add or alter them. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member who satisfied the constitutional qualifications. The Court declared that “the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 522. The reasoning of *Powell* applies with equal force to the States: if the House itself cannot add qualifications, a State certainly cannot. The Constitution’s uniform national standard for congressional service would be undermined if each State could impose its own additional requirements. *See* *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884) (the right to vote for members of Congress is derived from the Constitution, and “the states are not left to prescribe the qualifications of those who are to be the electors for the most numerous branch of the national legislature”). The same principle governs the qualifications of candidates.

**II. Amendment 73 Imposes a De Facto Qualification, Not a Mere Ballot Access Regulation.**

Arkansas Amendment 73 provides that any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate “shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot.” This is not a neutral time, place, or manner regulation; it is a substantive disqualification. The practical effect is identical to a direct prohibition on candidacy. A State cannot circumvent the Constitution by labeling a qualification a “ballot access” restriction. *See* *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941) (the right to choose representatives is a right secured by the Constitution, and states may not “by their laws” impair that right). The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the distinction between outright ineligibility and ballot exclusion is “too fine a point” to mask the amendment’s true character. The Constitution’s exclusive qualifications cannot be evaded through such indirection.

**III. The State’s Power Under the Elections Clause Does Not Extend to Prescribing Qualifications.**

Article I, § 4 grants States authority over the “Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives.” This power is limited to procedural regulations. In *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932), the Court explained that the word “manner” encompasses “the times, places, and manner of holding elections” but does not include the power to prescribe the qualifications of candidates. The Elections Clause was designed to ensure orderly elections, not to permit States to alter the fundamental character of the federal legislature. *See* *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 384 (1879) (state laws regulating federal elections must not conflict with the Constitution). A state law that adds a qualification for Congress directly conflicts with the Constitution’s exclusive enumeration. The Framers deliberately omitted term limits from the qualifications, and that choice cannot be overridden by state initiative.

**IV. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Invalidated Section 3.**

The lower court’s decision is faithful to this Court’s precedents. The Constitution provides the sole source of qualifications for members of Congress. Any attempt by a State to impose additional requirements—whether styled as a direct bar or a ballot restriction—is unconstitutional. The remedy for those who seek term limits lies in a federal constitutional amendment, not in state law. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
