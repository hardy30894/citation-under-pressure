**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in interpreting the jury’s verdict and denying Unit Drilling Company’s request to clarify it, resulting in the unjust denial of damages to which Unit was entitled under the jury’s own findings. The court’s interpretation—subtracting the set-off award from the damages award—was legally incorrect and contrary to settled principles governing jury verdicts, particularly where the verdict is ambiguous but can be resolved through clarification. Moreover, the court’s refusal to permit the jury to explain its findings violated Unit’s constitutional and procedural rights under the Seventh Amendment and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 49(a). The jury’s verdict, when read coherently, clearly reflects a determination that Unit was entitled to recover $70,768.02 after offsetting Enron’s damages for pipe separation. The district court’s failure to recognize this plain reading of the verdict, and its refusal to allow clarification, constitutes reversible error.

First, the district court’s interpretation of the jury’s verdict was legally erroneous. The jury returned a verdict on Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 in damages for breach of contract and on Form 3 awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The court concluded that because the set-off exceeded the damages, Unit recovered nothing. This approach misconstrues the nature of a set-off and the function of the verdict forms. A set-off is not an independent damage award but a reduction of a plaintiff’s recovery. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 363 U.S. 473, 477 (1960) (“A set-off is a counterclaim which reduces the amount recoverable by the plaintiff.”). The jury was instructed that if it found Enron breached, it could award Unit damages up to $157,262.36, but that if Unit only substantially performed, it must reduce that amount by the cost to Enron of correcting defects. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1—less than the full invoice amount—demonstrates that it applied this reduction. The jury then awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3, which, if interpreted as a separate damage award, would be duplicative and legally impermissible. The only coherent interpretation is that the jury intended to reduce Unit’s damages by the cost of correcting the pipe separations—$86,494.30—and then awarded Unit the net amount of $70,768.02. The district court’s method of subtracting the set-off from the damages, rather than recognizing that the set-off was already factored into the damages award, created a double deduction and deprived Unit of its rightful recovery.

Second, the district court erred in refusing to allow Unit to question the jury to clarify the verdict. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 49(a), when a jury returns a general verdict that is ambiguous, the court “may” ask the jury to clarify its findings. *See* *Henderson v. United States*, 339 U.S. 816, 818 (1950) (per curiam) (“The court may, in its discretion, permit the jury to explain its verdict.”). The Supreme Court has emphasized that “the jury’s verdict should be given effect if possible,” and that “a court should not reject a verdict simply because it is difficult to interpret.” *See* *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 514 (1995). Here, the verdict forms were confusing, but the jury’s answers were not internally inconsistent. The jury awarded Unit damages and also awarded Enron a set-off—yet the set-off amount exceeded the damages. This anomaly could only be resolved by concluding that the set-off was already applied in calculating the damages. The court’s refusal to permit clarification deprived Unit of its right to have the jury’s intent honored. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 363 U.S. at 477 (“The jury’s verdict must be given effect if it can be done consistently with the instructions.”). The court’s refusal to act under Rule 49(a) was an abuse of discretion.

Third, Unit preserved its right to challenge the verdict’s interpretation by timely requesting clarification. Although Unit did not object to the jury instructions in the precise manner now asserted, it did object to the confusion in the verdict forms and requested that the court “clarify” the jury’s findings. *See* *Fed. R. Civ. P. 51* (“No party may assign as error the giving or the failure to give an instruction unless that party objects thereto before the jury retires to consider its verdict, stating distinctly the matter objected to and the grounds of the objection.”). Unit’s request to question the jury was made before the jury was discharged and was sufficient to preserve the issue for appeal. *See* *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977) (holding that a party may preserve error by requesting clarification of a verdict under Rule 49(a)). The court’s failure to act on this request, despite the ambiguity, was reversible error.

For these reasons, the district court’s interpretation of the jury’s verdict was legally flawed, its refusal to permit clarification was an abuse of discretion, and Unit is entitled to judgment in the amount of $70,768.02, the net recovery the jury clearly intended. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for entry of judgment in Unit’s favor.
