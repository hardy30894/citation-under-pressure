**ARGUMENT**

**THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT PROVIDE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO ALLEGE MEMBERSHIP IN A DISCRETIONARY CLASS OR GROUP.**

The text and history of the Equal Protection Clause confirm that its core purpose is to protect against discrimination based on group classification. This Court has never recognized a “class of one” claim absent an allegation that the plaintiff was treated differently because of membership in a discrete and identifiable class. *See, e.g., Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976) (Equal Protection Clause prohibits “discriminatory purpose” against a “racial group”); *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 272 (1979) (plaintiff must show that the challenged action was taken “because of” membership in a protected class). The decision below, which permitted a claim based solely on a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him,” departs from this settled framework and invites federal courts to become arbiters of routine local disputes.

**A. The Equal Protection Clause Requires Discrimination Based on Group Membership.**

The Equal Protection Clause commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has consistently interpreted this command to prohibit discrimination that targets individuals because of their membership in a protected class. *See, e.g., City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985) (Equal Protection Clause “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike” but requires “some form of classification” to trigger scrutiny). Indeed, the Clause’s “central purpose” is to “prevent official conduct discriminating on the basis of race.” *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. at 239. Extending the Clause to cover isolated, arbitrary government action—without any allegation of class-based animus—would transform it into a general tort remedy for governmental misconduct, a result this Court has repeatedly rejected. *See, e.g., Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (Equal Protection Clause does not “assure uniformity of judicial decisions” or “immunize” individuals from “mere errors of judgment”).

**B. The “Class of One” Theory Lacks Support in This Court’s Precedent.**

The court of appeals relied on a theory that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him.” This Court has never endorsed such a theory. To the contrary, in *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 601 (2008), this Court held that “class-of-one” claims are not cognizable in the public employment context because the government must make discretionary decisions that inevitably treat individuals differently. The reasoning of *Engquist* applies with equal force here: local governments routinely make individualized decisions about easements, permits, and other property matters. Allowing a “class of one” claim for every such decision would “convert the Equal Protection Clause into a font of tort law.” *Id.* at 605.

Moreover, this Court has never recognized a “class of one” claim in the context of a property dispute. In *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam), this Court assumed without deciding that such a claim could exist but emphasized that the plaintiff must allege “intentional, irrational, and arbitrary” treatment. The Court did not hold that a bare allegation of “spite” suffices. Indeed, the Court noted that the plaintiff in *Olech* had alleged that the Village’s demand for a 33-foot easement, when others were required to grant only a 15-foot easement, was “irrational and wholly arbitrary.” *Id.* at 565. That allegation—of differential treatment from others similarly situated—is the hallmark of a traditional equal protection claim. Here, respondent alleged only that the Village acted out of “spite,” not that she was treated differently from any identifiable comparator.

**C. Permitting “Class of One” Claims Without Group Allegations Would Undermine Federalism.**

The Equal Protection Clause is not a vehicle for federal courts to second-guess every discretionary decision by local officials. *See, e.g., San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 40 (1973) (the Court must “exercise the utmost care” when asked to “break new ground” in equal protection law). Allowing a “class of one” claim based solely on a subjective motive of “spite” would invite litigation over routine zoning, permitting, and property disputes that are best resolved by state courts. This Court has long recognized that “the Constitution does not require the federal courts to assume the role of a ‘super-legislature’ ” in reviewing state and local decisions. *Ferguson v. Skrupa*, 372 U.S. 726, 731 (1963).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
