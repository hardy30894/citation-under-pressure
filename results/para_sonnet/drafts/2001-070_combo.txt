# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CATEGORICALLY EXEMPT MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS FROM CAPITAL PUNISHMENT.

**A. The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause Targets Barbaric Methods and Grossly Disproportionate Sentences, Not Individualized Culpability Determinations.**

The Clause has historically been understood to condemn the *manner* of punishment, not to forbid a punishment otherwise lawful simply because the offender possesses a particular characteristic. This Court has explained that "[p]unishments are cruel when they involve torture or a lingering death." *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890). Death by a method the State lawfully employs is not inherently cruel. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135–36 (1878). Petitioner does not challenge Virginia's method of execution; he asks this Court to declare an entire class of offenders categorically immune from a punishment concededly constitutional for others who commit identical crimes. That is a proportionality argument, and this Court has cautioned that only sentences "grossly out of proportion to the severity of the crime" offend the Amendment. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). Atkins' crime—abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder—was not altered by his intellectual capacity; his culpability for that crime was a question properly submitted to, and resolved by, the sentencing jury.

**B. No Objective Indicia of a National Consensus Support a Categorical Exemption.**

This Court has held that the Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). That standard requires *objective* evidence of consensus, not the Court's own judgment substituted for the collective judgment of the States. At the time of Atkins' sentencing, the overwhelming majority of capital jurisdictions—including Virginia—declined to adopt a categorical bar, choosing instead to treat retardation as a mitigating factor for the sentencer's individualized consideration. The *Trop* inquiry cannot supply a national consensus where none of "the progress of a maturing society" has yet crystallized into a settled legislative judgment.

**C. Individualized Sentencing, Not a Categorical Rule, Is the Constitutionally Preferred Vehicle for Considering Diminished Capacity.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that capital sentencers must be permitted the widest latitude to consider the whole person before them. A sentencing authority may consider "the fullest information possible concerning the defendant's life and characteristics." *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949). Likewise, the jury's function in capital cases is to render an individualized moral judgment, not to apply a mechanical rule. *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510, 519 (1968). Atkins' jury heard extensive testimony—from the same forensic psychologist relied upon at both sentencing hearings—regarding his intellectual limitations, and the Commonwealth's evidence in rebuttal. The jury weighed that evidence and twice returned a sentence of death. A categorical exemption would strip from the jury precisely the individualized weighing function this Court has said the Constitution demands, replacing it with a bright-line disability classification that Virginia's own sentencer considered and rejected on this record.

**D. States Retain Broad Latitude to Define the Legal Significance of Mental Incapacity in the Criminal Process.**

This Court has long deferred to the States in defining how mental incapacity bears on criminal responsibility. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 800–01 (1952), the Court held that due process does not compel any particular test of criminal responsibility, leaving the States "wide latitude" to formulate their own standards. So too here: Virginia was entitled to treat Atkins' intellectual limitations as a matter for the jury's mitigation calculus rather than as an automatic constitutional bar. Similarly, in *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950), the Court left to state executive procedures—not federal constitutional command—the determination whether an individual's mental condition should stay an execution, holding that such determinations have "been traditionally left to the Executive." The same restraint counsels against constitutionalizing a categorical retardation exemption here.

**E. This Court Has Cautioned Against Enshrining Contested Medical Categories into Eighth Amendment Doctrine.**

In declining to extend *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), to bar punishment of a chronic alcoholic for public intoxication, a plurality of this Court warned against "plung[ing] the law into a new and unchartered area" premised on unsettled medical and scientific classification. *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 531 (1968). *Robinson* itself struck down only punishment for "the mere status" of narcotics addiction, 370 U.S. at 666; Atkins was not punished for a status but for the act of murder, following a jury's individualized weighing of aggravation and mitigation, including his intellectual limitations.

**F. The Court Should Not Extend the Amendment Beyond Its Traditional Scope.**

Finally, this Court has repeatedly resisted expanding the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause beyond its established historical boundaries absent compelling evidence of a settled societal judgment. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (plurality declining to find a second execution attempt unconstitutionally cruel). The same restraint is warranted here.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
