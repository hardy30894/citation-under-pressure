**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT DOES NOT PROHIBIT A COURT FROM ORDERING A REDUCTION IN PRISON POPULATION TO CURE EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS.**

The State argues that the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) bars the district court from ordering a reduction in the prison population. This argument is foreclosed by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493 (2011). In *Plata*, the Court held that the PLRA does not prohibit a court from ordering a population reduction to remedy unconstitutional conditions. The Court explained that the PLRA’s “good faith” requirement applies to prisoners’ lawsuits, not to court-ordered remedial plans. *Id.* at 511. The Court further noted that the PLRA’s “equity” requirements are satisfied when a court orders the “least restrictive means” of achieving the objectives of the decree. *Id.* at 511–12. Here, the district court’s order is precisely that: the least restrictive means of achieving the constitutional objective of providing adequate medical care to inmates.

**II. THE ORDER IS NECESSARY TO COMPLY WITH THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

The district court’s order is grounded in the Supreme Court’s Eighth Amendment jurisprudence. The Court has established that “deliberate indifference to serious medical needs of prisoners” violates the Eighth Amendment. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976). This standard requires prison officials to provide adequate medical care. *Id.* at 104. In *Plata*, the Supreme Court found that California’s prisons were so overcrowded that they were unable to provide constitutionally adequate health care. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 511. The Court noted that the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations was the “severe overcrowding” in the state’s prisons. *Id.* at 511. Therefore, the district court’s order to reduce the population is a direct response to the State’s failure to meet its constitutional obligations.

**III. THE STATE CONSENTED TO THE REMEDIAL PROCESS, AND THE PLRA AUTHORIZES THE ORDER.**

The State’s argument that the PLRA bars the order is undermined by its own consent to the receivership and the specific language of 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b). The State consented to the receivership and the implementation of the Receiver’s plans. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 3589 F.3d 945, 960 (9th Cir. 2009). Under *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), a consent decree is binding and enforceable. *Id.* at 421. The PLRA explicitly authorizes courts to “enter an order... that is the least restrictive means of achieving the objectives” of the decree. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b). The district court’s order is a valid exercise of this statutory authority.

**IV. THE ORDER IS THE “LEAST RESTRICTIVE MEANS” OF ACHIEVING CONSTITUTIONAL COMPLIANCE.**

The State contends that the PLRA’s “equity” requirements prevent the court from ordering population reductions. However, the Supreme Court has held that prison regulations are valid if they are “reasonably related to legitimate penological interests.” *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987). The district court’s order is not a regulation, but a remedial measure. The Court in *Plata* found that the State’s efforts to build new facilities were insufficient to cure the overcrowding. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 511. Therefore, the order to release inmates is the only means of achieving the constitutional objective of providing adequate health care.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court’s order is a necessary and lawful remedy for the State’s Eighth Amendment violations. The PLRA does not prohibit such an order. The State’s argument is without merit.
