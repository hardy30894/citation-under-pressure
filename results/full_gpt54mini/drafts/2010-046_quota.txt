The order should be affirmed because the Prison Litigation Reform Act permits prospective relief that is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary, and is the least intrusive means to correct a proven federal violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The Supreme Court has made clear that when prison overcrowding is the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions, population reduction may be ordered if supported by a record showing no other remedy will suffice. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 503-04, 511-13 (2011). That is exactly what the three-judge court found here after years of failed remedial efforts. Id. at 502-03, 511-14.

The PLRA expressly authorizes population reduction orders in this setting. A three-judge court may enter such relief only after it finds by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the primary cause of the violation and that no other relief will remedy the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). The Court in Brown v. Plata held those findings were satisfied on this record, explaining that California’s overcrowding created systemic delays in medical and mental-health care that violated the Eighth Amendment. 563 U.S. at 503-09, 517-21. Under Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97, 104-05 (1976), and Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 834, 847 (1994), prison officials must provide adequate medical care and may not be deliberately indifferent to substantial risks to inmate health. The record here showed persistent, systemwide noncompliance with those constitutional minima. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 507-10.

California’s attempt to characterize the order as an invasion of state sovereignty fails for the same reason. Federal courts have long had authority to enter effective structural relief when state institutions operate in violation of federal law. Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687-88 (1978). The Eleventh Amendment does not bar prospective relief against state officials to end an ongoing federal violation. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908). Nor does the PLRA displace that core remedial power; it channels it. Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347-48 (2000). So long as the remedy is necessary and tailored to the violation, federal courts may require states to take affirmative steps to cure unconstitutional prison conditions. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-14; Ruiz v. Estelle, 679 F.2d 1115, 1160-61 (5th Cir. 1982).

The order here is also entitled to deference because it followed repeated, unsuccessful efforts at less intrusive relief. Courts are not required to tolerate endless noncompliance while unconstitutional conditions continue. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1971). In prison cases, “[i]n the exercise of their equitable powers,” courts may use the “least intrusive” means that will actually remedy the violation, but they need not accept remedies that have repeatedly failed. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511, 526-27; Lewis v. Casey, 518 U.S. 343, 362-63 (1996).

Finally, the scope of relief satisfies the PLRA. The statute requires the court to make the need-narrowness-intrusiveness findings, but it does not forbid effective relief merely because the order has significant fiscal or administrative consequences. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 531-33. The Court has recognized that “[c]ourts may not allow constitutional violations to continue simply because a remedy would involve intrusion into the realm of prison administration.” Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511. The order here does exactly what the PLRA permits: it addresses an ongoing constitutional violation, it is limited to the amount of population reduction needed to cure that violation, and it leaves California discretion to decide how to comply. Id. at 524-31.

For those reasons, the prison-population order does not violate the PLRA; it is the statute’s intended mechanism for remedying a proven, statewide Eighth Amendment violation. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011).
