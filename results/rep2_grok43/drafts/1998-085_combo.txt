The common law has long required that punitive damages rest on conduct that is outrageous or egregious, beyond the mental state necessary for liability itself. As the Court explained in Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), such awards are proper only when the defendant’s actions exhibit “that entire want of care which would raise the presumption of a conscious indifference to consequences.” See also Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875) (punitive damages demand “gross negligence” amounting to “willful and intentional wrong”); Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858) (recovery turns on “circumstances of aggravation”); Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851) (exemplary damages require “circumstances of fraud, oppression, or gross negligence”).

This independent requirement of outrageous conduct appears throughout the authorities. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 562-63 (1886), held that malice in the legal sense must be accompanied by “such a degree of wantonness or oppression” as to justify the sanction. New York Central Railroad Co. v. Johnson, 279 U.S. 310, 315 (1929), likewise conditioned punitive liability on “flagrant” misconduct. See also Eshelman v. Rawalt, 298 Ill. 192, 198, 131 N.E. 675, 678 (1921) (punitive damages unavailable absent “willful and wanton” conduct evincing “a reckless disregard of the rights of others”); Kurn v. Weaver, 25 Tenn. App. 556, 570, 161 S.W.2d 422, 430 (1942) (same); Topeka City Railway Co. v. Higgs, 138 Kan. 33, 37, 23 P.2d 480, 483 (1933) (requiring “outrageous” acts).

Title VII’s incorporation of these standards confirms that malice or reckless indifference alone cannot sustain punitive damages. The statute’s text and common-law backdrop therefore demand an independent showing of egregious conduct. Because the record contains no such evidence, the judgment denying punitive damages should be affirmed.
