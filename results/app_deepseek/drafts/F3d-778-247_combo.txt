**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Search Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus Between the Alleged Crimes and the Place to Be Searched.**

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant particularly describe the place to be searched and the things to be seized. *See Marron v. United States*, 275 U.S. 192, 196 (1927). Probable cause must establish a nexus between the contraband sought and the location to be searched. *See Dumbra v. United States*, 268 U.S. 435, 441 (1925). Here, the affidavit failed to connect Joubert’s alleged misconduct to his parents’ home. The affiant’s conclusory statement that persons engaged in molestation “often maintain” evidence of their crimes is insufficient. *See Nathanson v. United States*, 290 U.S. 41, 47 (1933) (holding that mere affirmations of suspicion are not enough). The affidavit did not allege that Joubert stored evidence at that address; it only noted that he moved there and discarded a computer tower. *See Byars v. United States*, 273 U.S. 28, 29 (1927) (requiring particularized facts). The warrant therefore violated the Fourth Amendment.

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting Evidence of Uncharged Child Molestation.**

Evidence of other crimes is inadmissible to prove propensity. *See Boyd v. United States*, 142 U.S. 450, 458 (1892). The district court admitted testimony of uncharged molestation under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), but such evidence must be offered for a proper purpose, such as intent or plan. *See United States v. Provoo*, 350 U.S. 857, 858 (1955) (per curiam). Here, the evidence served only to portray Joubert as a pedophile, prejudicing the jury. *See Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948) (warning against character assassination). The probative value was substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. *See United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 839 (2d Cir. 1938) (L. Hand, J.). Reversal is required.

**III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert’s Intrastate Conduct.**

Congress may regulate only activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. *See United States v. E.C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1, 12 (1895). Joubert’s conduct—filming a child in a private home—was purely intrastate. *See Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 548 (1935) (striking down regulation of local activities). The statutes, as applied here, exceed Congress’s commerce power. *See Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 304 (1936) (holding that mining is local). The government cannot rely on a jurisdictional hook that is pretextual. *See United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 68 (1936) (rejecting use of taxing power to regulate local matters). Joubert’s convictions must be vacated.

**IV. The 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable.**

A sentence must be proportional to the offense. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). Joubert’s 40-year sentence is grossly disproportionate to his non-violent conduct. *See Ex parte Wilson*, 114 U.S. 417, 427 (1885) (requiring proportionality). The district court failed to consider mitigating factors, including Joubert’s age and lack of prior convictions. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949) (sentencing must be individualized). The sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. *See Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958) (plurality opinion). This Court should remand for resentencing.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse Joubert’s convictions or vacate his sentence.
