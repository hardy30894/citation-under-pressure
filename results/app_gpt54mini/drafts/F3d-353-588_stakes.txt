## Argument

### I. The district court erred in holding that Epps was not “regarded as” disabled under the ADA and the MHRA.

The ADA protects not only individuals with an actual disability, but also persons whom an employer “regards as having” a substantially limiting impairment. 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C) (2000); Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). An employee may proceed under the “regarded as” prong by showing that the employer mistakenly believed he had an impairment that substantially limited a major life activity, or mistakenly believed an actual impairment was more limiting than it is. Sutton, 527 U.S. at 489; Murphy v. United Parcel Serv., Inc., 527 U.S. 516, 521-22 (1999). In this circuit, the question is whether the employer viewed the employee as unable to perform a class of jobs or a broad range of jobs in various classes, not merely a particular position. Brunko v. Mercy Hosp., Inc., 260 F.3d 939, 942-43 (8th Cir. 2001); Cooper v. Olin Corp., 246 F.3d 1083, 1089-90 (8th Cir. 2001).

The summary-judgment record permits exactly that inference. Chief Hardy reviewed Epps’s personnel file and learned that Epps had filed “quite a few workers’ compensation claims” and had missed “a lot of work.” He then reviewed Dr. Mirkin’s note stating that Epps had degenerative disk disease and would likely need to seek a different occupation. Hardy relied on that material in recommending termination, first on September 29, 1999, and again on March 17, 2000, when he emphasized that officers were required to bend, squat, and run, that no light-duty assignments were available, and that Epps would never be able to function as a regular officer. Those facts support the conclusion that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in working. See Murphy, 527 U.S. at 523; Taylor v. Nimock’s Oil Co., 214 F.3d 957, 959-60 (8th Cir. 2000).

The district court appears to have treated Pine Lawn’s reliance on Epps’s physical limitations as proof that he was not disabled within the meaning of the ADA. That reasoning conflicts with Sutton and Murphy. The Supreme Court has made clear that an employer’s belief that an employee cannot perform a broad range of jobs because of a perceived impairment is enough to satisfy the “regarded as” definition, even if the employee is not actually disabled. Sutton, 527 U.S. at 489; Murphy, 527 U.S. at 523. Here, Hardy did not merely think Epps could not patrol that week; he concluded that Epps could not function as a regular officer and would need a different occupation. That is a classic “regarded as” case. See Cooper, 246 F.3d at 1090; Brunko, 260 F.3d at 943.

The MHRA should be construed consistently with the ADA on this point. See Dorsey v. Pinnacle Automation Co., 278 F.3d 830, 836-37 (8th Cir. 2002). Because the same record supports a finding that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled under the ADA, it likewise supports his MHRA claim. Id.

### II. The district court erred in summarily holding that sovereign immunity barred Epps’s Missouri workers’ compensation retaliation claim.

Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 creates a cause of action against an employer who discharges an employee “because the employee has exercised his or her rights” under the workers’ compensation law. If Pine Lawn is a municipal employer acting in a proprietary capacity, sovereign immunity does not bar the claim. Jungerman v. City of Raytown, 925 S.W.2d 202, 204-05 (Mo. 1996) (en banc); New Liberty Hosp. Dist. v. Pratt, 778 S.W.2d 892, 894-95 (Mo. Ct. App. 1989).

The record does not permit summary judgment on immunity. Pine Lawn employed Epps as a police officer, filed workers’ compensation reports and claims, and made termination decisions based in part on those claims. Missouri courts have recognized that immunity turns on the nature of the function performed, and that a city may be liable when it acts outside a strictly governmental role. Jungerman, 925 S.W.2d at 204-05; Bituminous Cas. Corp. v. City of St. Louis, 450 S.W.2d 462, 465-66 (Mo. 1970). At minimum, the court must examine whether Pine Lawn’s conduct in administering employment and retaliation for compensation claims falls within the protected sphere claimed below.

Moreover, a municipality is not free to use sovereign immunity to defeat a statutory retaliation claim where the legislature has created a specific remedy. See State ex rel. St. Louis-San Francisco Ry. Co. v. Campbell, 326 Mo. 907, 33 S.W.2d 969, 972 (1930) (en banc). Section 287.780 expresses Missouri’s policy against retaliatory discharge, and summary judgment should not have been entered on immunity grounds without careful analysis of whether the statute applies to municipal employers. See Jungerman, 925 S.W.2d at 204-05.

### III. The intentional infliction of emotional distress claim was not properly disposed of on sovereign-immunity grounds.

To the extent the district court rested dismissal of the emotional-distress claim on sovereign immunity, that ruling likewise fails to address whether Pine Lawn acted in a proprietary capacity or whether any recognized exception applies. Missouri has long distinguished governmental and proprietary functions in assessing municipal immunity. Jungerman, 925 S.W.2d at 204-05; Prendergast v. City of St. Joseph, 332 S.W.2d 570, 572-73 (Mo. 1960). Because the court resolved immunity as a matter of law without parsing the function at issue, Epps was denied the opportunity to show that the city’s conduct falls outside immunity’s protection.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for trial on all claims.
