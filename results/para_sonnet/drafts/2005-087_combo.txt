# ARGUMENT

## I. STATES POSSESS BROAD AUTHORITY TO DEFINE AND CHANNEL THE USE OF MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE WITHOUT OFFENDING DUE PROCESS

This Court has long recognized that "it is not the province of this Court to sit as a super-legislature" over the substantive criminal law of the several States. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952). In Leland, this Court upheld Oregon's requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process compelled any particular procedural framework for mental-state defenses. The Court explained that the definition of insanity and its relationship to criminal responsibility "is an issue of fact" for the States to resolve, and that due process "does not put New York or the other States which have similar statutes in this class." Id. at 800-01 (citing the M'Naghten framework as one of several permissible approaches).

This principle traces to Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), and finds its clearest articulation in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), where Justice Cardozo held that due process is violated only when a state procedure "offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." Id. at 105. Arizona's channeling of mental-state evidence to the insanity inquiry—while excluding it from the mens rea determination—reflects a considered legislative judgment about the reliability of psychiatric testimony on the discrete question of specific intent, a judgment well within the tradition Snyder protects.

## II. THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN INSANITY AND DIMINISHED CAPACITY IS A PERMISSIBLE LEGISLATIVE CHOICE

Respondent's rule tracks a distinction this Court has long tolerated: the difference between the affirmative defense of insanity, which excuses conduct, and evidence offered merely to negate an element of the offense. In Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), this Court affirmed a conviction where the District of Columbia courts refused to permit psychiatric evidence of "partial insanity" to reduce murder to manslaughter, holding that "the whole problem has been left by Congress to the courts" and that adoption of a diminished-capacity doctrine was a policy choice for the local jurisdiction, not a constitutional mandate. Id. at 466. Justice Frankfurter's dissent notwithstanding, the majority's holding controls: due process does not require a State to recognize partial or diminished responsibility as distinct from the traditional insanity defense.

Similarly, in Leland, this Court rejected the claim that Oregon was required to allow psychiatric evidence to negate intent outside the insanity framework, noting that "the science of psychiatry has become convincing" to many, but that such convictions "were not required by the Fourteenth Amendment." 343 U.S. at 800-01. Arizona's rule is materially indistinguishable.

## III. STATES RETAIN WIDE LATITUDE TO ESTABLISH RULES OF EVIDENCE AND BURDEN ALLOCATION IN CRIMINAL TRIALS

The power to exclude or channel evidence, so long as a defendant retains some meaningful opportunity to present a defense, has repeatedly survived constitutional challenge. In Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554 (1967)—though this Court has cautioned against constitutionalizing state evidentiary rules—the Court upheld Texas's procedure permitting evidence of prior convictions during trial, reasoning that "the Due Process Clause does not impose upon the States a duty to establish schemes of criminal procedure that will assure perfect symmetry." Id. at 564. Arizona's channeling rule likewise does not deny Clark a defense; it structures how that defense—insanity—must be proved, consistent with Leland.

Moreover, in Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46 (1947), this Court held that the privilege against self-incrimination did not bind the States, emphasizing that "the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment" was not "a shorthand" for federal procedural guarantees but instead protected against fundamental unfairness. Id. at 54. The same restraint counsels against importing a discrete "capacity evidence" right the Constitution's text does not supply and this Court's precedents do not compel.

## IV. THE STATE'S INTEREST IN RELIABLE ADJUDICATION JUSTIFIES THE CHANNELING RULE

Finally, this Court has recognized that psychiatric testimony directed at capacity to form specific intent poses genuine risks of confusing the jury and undermining the reliability of verdicts—concerns squarely within the State's police power. In Patterson v. New York-adjacent reasoning found in Leland, the Court noted that requiring the defendant to prove insanity "beyond a reasonable doubt" was justified by the State's interest in preventing "the danger of simulated defenses." 343 U.S. at 799. Arizona's decision to cabin expert testimony to the insanity determination—where the burden and standards are clear—rather than allow diffuse "mens rea" psychiatric evidence under the more demanding beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard, serves the identical interest in verdict integrity that this Court sanctioned in Leland and Fisher.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Arizona's channeling rule comports with due process, and the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
