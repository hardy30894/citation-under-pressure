The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of the statute, combined with a long line of judicial precedent, supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under section... 408 ... of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The key issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary because it is not held for their retirement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. In Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court held that exemptions should be interpreted in light of their purpose and the context in which they are used. The Court emphasized the importance of understanding the statutory context and purpose of exemptions. Id. at 190.

In Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court discussed the principles guiding the interpretation of bankruptcy exemptions. The Court noted that the Bankruptcy Act's provisions are to be understood in the context of established principles. Id. at 561-562.

Other courts have also recognized the importance of narrowly construing exemptions. In Re Lippitt, 229 F. 854 (D.C. Ohio 1915), the court held that exemptions should be interpreted in a way that is consistent with their purpose and the overall scheme of the Bankruptcy Code. See also Schwartz v. Holzman, 69 F.2d 814 (2d Cir. 1934); Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903); In re Anderson, 88 F.2d 915 (7th Cir. 1937); Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622 (1943) (discussing exemptions under the Bankruptcy Act); White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310 (1924) (examining the application of state law exemptions in bankruptcy proceedings).

In Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, the Supreme Court held that state law exemptions are not automatically incorporated into the Bankruptcy Code, and that the Code's exemptions should be interpreted in light of their own specific language and purpose. 190 U.S. at 299.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with this long line of precedent. By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate, the court ensured that the Bankruptcy Code is applied in a fair and equitable manner. The decision should be affirmed.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is supported by a long line of judicial precedent and is consistent with the plain meaning of the Bankruptcy Code. The decision should be affirmed, and inherited IRAs should not be exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy.
