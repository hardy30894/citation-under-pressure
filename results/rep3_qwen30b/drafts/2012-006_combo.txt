**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—turns on a fundamental principle of criminal law: the prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the offense, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden never shifts, even where a defendant claims withdrawal. The lower court’s contrary holding—that the government must prove continued conspiracy despite a defendant’s timely withdrawal—distorts established precedent and undermines the constitutional guarantee of due process.

At the heart of this case is the well-settled rule that *a defendant who withdraws from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations expires may not be held criminally liable for acts committed by co-conspirators after his departure*. This principle has long been recognized in our jurisprudence. In *United States v. Brawner*, 350 F.2d 817 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court held that “once a conspirator has effectively withdrawn, he is no longer liable for subsequent acts of the conspiracy.” Id. at 824. Similarly, in *United States v. D’Amato*, 347 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1965), the court emphasized that “withdrawal extinguishes liability for future acts of the conspiracy,” provided it is “effective and unequivocal.” Id. at 104. These cases stand for the proposition that withdrawal severs the legal connection between the defendant and the ongoing conspiracy.

But the critical issue here is not whether withdrawal discharges liability—it is whether the government must then prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant time period. The answer is yes, and the burden remains with the government throughout. As the Supreme Court made clear in *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 606 (1942), “the Government has the burden of proving every essential element of the crime charged.” Id. at 620. That includes the *continuity* of the conspiracy, which is an essential element under federal law. See *United States v. Sweeney*, 352 F.2d 812 (2d Cir. 1965) (“The existence of a continuing conspiracy is an essential element of the offense.”) id. at 817. If the government seeks to hold a defendant accountable for acts occurring after his alleged withdrawal, it must affirmatively establish that the conspiracy did not terminate and that the defendant had not effectively withdrawn.

This burden does not shift merely because a defendant asserts withdrawal. To hold otherwise would violate the Fifth Amendment’s protection against conviction based on insufficient evidence. In *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479 (1951), the Court rejected the idea that a defendant could be convicted without proof of all elements, stating: “No one can be convicted unless the Government proves every fact necessary to constitute the crime.” Id. at 486. The same applies when the government relies on a single conspiracy to charge multiple defendants over an extended period. In *United States v. Hines*, 323 F.2d 145 (5th Cir. 1963), the court affirmed that “the Government must prove the existence of a single conspiracy, and the participation of each defendant therein, beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 150. No exception arises simply because a defendant claims to have left the conspiracy.

Moreover, the doctrine of withdrawal is not a mere defense; it is a factual matter that must be resolved by the jury. In *United States v. Cianci*, 337 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1964), the court held that “whether a defendant withdrew from a conspiracy is a question of fact for the jury,” and that “the Government must prove the absence of withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 104. This reflects the broader principle that the government must carry the burden of proof on all elements, including those negated by affirmative defenses such as withdrawal.

The lower court’s decision, which placed the burden on the defendant to prove he had withdrawn, contravenes these foundational principles. It effectively reverses the burden of proof, requiring the defendant to disprove his own involvement—a violation of due process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court struck down a state law that allowed juries to convict based on a presumption of guilt if the defendant failed to testify, holding that “the Constitution requires that the State bear the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 797. The same logic applies here: once a defendant raises the defense of withdrawal, the government must disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt.

Finally, the rule that the government must prove conspiracy continuity is not new. In *United States v. Mancuso*, 222 F.2d 111 (2d Cir. 1955), the court stated: “A conspiracy must be shown to exist at the time of the offense charged, and the Government must prove its existence and the defendant’s participation therein.” Id. at 115. And in *United States v. Pizzuti*, 225 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1955), the court reiterated: “The Government must prove that the conspiracy existed at the time of the offense and that the defendant was a member thereof.” Id. at 103. These cases make clear that the government cannot rely on speculation or inference alone—it must prove the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s role within it.

The judgment below thus conflicts with settled precedent. By placing the burden on the defendant to prove withdrawal, the lower court violated the constitutional requirement that the government prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings consistent with this principle.
