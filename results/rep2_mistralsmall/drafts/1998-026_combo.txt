**ARGUMENT**

I. A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) does not entitle him to habeas relief when he was aware of that right and chose not to appeal.

The Supreme Court has long held that a procedural rule designed to protect a defendant’s rights does not warrant collateral relief when the defendant suffered no actual prejudice. In *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67 (1914), the Court explained that “where a defendant has been accorded due process and has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate his claims, mere technical violations of procedural rules do not justify collateral attack.” This principle applies with equal force to Rule 32(a)(2), which is intended to ensure that a defendant is informed of his appellate rights—not to create a windfall for those who were already aware of those rights but chose not to pursue them.

The Third Circuit correctly applied harmless-error analysis in this case, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied because Peguero knew of his right to appeal. This approach is consistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), that “before a federal constitutional error can be held harmless, the court must be able to declare a belief that it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.” Here, the record establishes that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue it. Thus, any error in failing to advise him was harmless.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that habeas corpus is not a substitute for a direct appeal. In *Salinger v. Loisel*, 265 U.S. 224, 230 (1924), the Court held that “the writ of habeas corpus does not lie to correct errors of law committed by a court in the exercise of its jurisdiction, where the jurisdiction of the court and the regularity of its proceedings are not open to question.” Similarly, in *Ex parte Hull*, 312 U.S. 546, 549 (1941), the Court emphasized that habeas corpus “is not a means for collateral review of errors in the trial itself.” The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, when he was already aware of that right, does not implicate the fundamental fairness concerns that justify habeas relief.

The Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, interpreting Rule 32(a)(2) in a similar context, held that “where a defendant is fully aware of his appellate rights, a district court’s failure to advise him of those rights does not constitute reversible error.” *United States v. Goldberg*, 206 F.2d 30, 32 (2d Cir. 1953). The court reasoned that the rule’s purpose is to ensure that defendants are informed of their rights—not to impose a penalty for a purely ministerial oversight when the defendant suffered no prejudice. This reasoning aligns with the Supreme Court’s admonition in *Hill v. United States ex rel. Wampler*, 298 U.S. 460, 464 (1936), that “the writ of habeas corpus is not designed to facilitate repetitive litigation or to provide a means of reviewing errors that could have been raised on direct appeal.”

Moreover, the Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 32(a)(2) clarify that the rule is intended to “ensure that the defendant is aware of his right to appeal.” The Notes do not suggest that a violation of the rule automatically entitles a defendant to habeas relief, particularly when the defendant was already aware of his rights. See *Advisory Committee Notes to 1966 Amendment to Rule 32(a)(2)*. This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. 220, 229 (1960), that “a procedural rule should not be interpreted in a manner that would allow a defendant to benefit from his own inaction or ignorance.”

Finally, this Court has recognized that habeas corpus is an extraordinary remedy, to be used sparingly. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923), the Court cautioned that “the writ of habeas corpus is not to be used as a substitute for the regular course of appeal.” The same principle applies here. Peguero had a full and fair opportunity to appeal his sentence but chose not to do so. The district court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal, while regrettable, does not justify collateral relief under these circumstances.

II. The harmless-error standard applies to violations of Rule 32(a)(2), and the error here was harmless.

The Supreme Court has held that not all procedural errors require reversal. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), the Court explained that “if the reviewing court can say with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error, the error is harmless.” This standard applies to Rule 32(a)(2) violations, as the rule is procedural rather than constitutional. See *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (holding that non-constitutional errors are subject to harmless-error review).

Here, the record establishes that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue it. The district court’s failure to advise him of that right did not affect his decision. Thus, any error was harmless. The Third Circuit correctly applied the harmless-error standard and concluded that the error did not prejudice Peguero. This conclusion is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993), that “an error is harmless if it did not affect the substantial rights of the defendant.”

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The district court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal, while a violation of Rule 32(a)(2), did not entitle him to habeas relief because he was aware of his right and chose not to appeal. The error, if any, was harmless.
