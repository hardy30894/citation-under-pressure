The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute is beyond the federal government's enumerated powers and inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

As the Third Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held, Bond's challenge to § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds is barred due to her lack of standing. The Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), establishes that private parties, like Bond, do not have standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim. In Tennessee Electric, the Court held that the utility companies, which were chartered in Tennessee, lacked standing to challenge the Tennessee Valley Authority's sale of electrical power as an impermissible federal regulation of a local matter in violation of the Tenth Amendment. Id. at 143-144. The Court reasoned that the sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment and that the utility companies, absent the states or their officers, have no standing to raise any question under the Tenth Amendment. Id. at 144.

The Third Circuit's decision is consistent with the majority of circuit courts that have addressed this issue. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir.2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir.2009); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir.2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir.2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir.2004). These courts have held that private parties lack standing to challenge federal statutes on Tenth Amendment grounds.

Bond's attempt to distinguish Tennessee Electric is unpersuasive. She argues that the language regarding private party Tenth Amendment standing in Tennessee Electric is "textbook dicta" and does not bind this Court. However, as the First Circuit Court explained in Medeiros, the Supreme Court's holding in Tennessee Electric is an alternative ground for the decision, rather than obiter dictum. Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 34.

Furthermore, allowing private parties to challenge federal statutes on Tenth Amendment grounds would undermine the principles of federalism and the separation of powers. The Tenth Amendment is intended to protect the states' rights and interests, and it is for the states, not private parties, to raise claims under the Tenth Amendment. See Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35.

In conclusion, Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Third Circuit's decision should be affirmed, and Bond's challenge to § 229 should be rejected.
