**ARGUMENT**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The jury’s advisory role comports with *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), because the judge—not the jury—makes the ultimate findings of aggravating factors. *Ring* held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find aggravating circumstances that make a defendant eligible for death. *Id.* at 609. But *Ring* expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme, noting that “Florida does not require the jury to find any aggravating factor.” *Id.* at 608 n.4. Instead, Florida’s judge “makes the actual findings of fact.” *Id.* This Court has repeatedly upheld such judicial factfinding. *See Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504, 515 (1995) (upholding Alabama’s similar scheme); *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984) (rejecting Sixth Amendment challenge to Florida’s scheme). Because the jury’s recommendation is advisory, *Ring* does not require a unanimous jury finding of aggravators. *See Bottoson v. Moore*, 833 So. 2d 693, 695 (Fla. 2002) (holding *Ring* inapplicable to Florida).

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. This Court has held that non-unanimous verdicts in state criminal cases do not violate the Sixth Amendment. *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 406 (1972) (plurality opinion). The Eighth Amendment’s heightened reliability requirement is satisfied by Florida’s robust appellate proportionality review. *See Pulley v. Harris*, 465 U.S. 37, 50–51 (1984) (no constitutional requirement for comparative proportionality review). Florida’s scheme ensures reliability through judicial findings, appellate review, and the requirement that the judge weigh aggravating and mitigating circumstances. *See § 921.141, Fla. Stat.* The jury’s non-unanimous recommendation does not undermine reliability; the judge independently evaluates the evidence. *See Harris*, 513 U.S. at 515.

Regarding mental retardation, *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Florida’s procedure—requiring the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence to the judge—is constitutionally adequate. *See § 921.137(4), Fla. Stat.* The Sixth Amendment does not require jury determination of mental retardation because it is a mitigating factor, not an element of the offense. *See Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609 (aggravating factors are “the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense”). Mental retardation is a “circumstance that militates against” the death penalty, not a fact that makes death eligible. *See Kansas v. Marsh*, 548 U.S. 163, 175 (2006). Thus, the judge may decide it. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 648 (1990) (upholding judicial determination of mitigating circumstances), *overruled on other grounds by Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609.

Finally, Florida’s scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The jury’s advisory role and non-unanimity do not create an arbitrary or capricious system. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 195 (1976) (plurality opinion) (approving guided discretion). Florida’s judge must make specific findings of aggravators and mitigators, and the sentence is subject to mandatory appellate review. *See § 921.141(3), Fla. Stat.* This Court has upheld similar schemes. *See Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 252 (1976) (plurality opinion) (approving Florida’s scheme). The judgment below should be affirmed.
