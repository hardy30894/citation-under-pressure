# ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall be subject "for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. As this Court has explained, that guaranty protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, against a second prosecution for the same offense after conviction, and against multiple punishments for the same offense. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This Court held in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), that the prohibition against double jeopardy is enforceable against the States as an element of the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of due process—a guarantee this Court has separately recognized as one "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968). The California Supreme Court's holding that this fundamental guaranty simply does not apply to noncapital sentence-enhancement proceedings cannot be reconciled with this Court's double jeopardy jurisprudence, which has never confined the Clause's protection to the adjudication of guilt on the underlying substantive offense.

## I. A Finding That the Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove the Prior-Conviction Allegation Is the Functional Equivalent of an Acquittal and Forecloses Retrial.

The Court of Appeal held that the evidence introduced against Monge was insufficient to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that he had personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury in his prior offense. That determination is indistinguishable in substance from a judgment of acquittal, and this Court has consistently held that such judgments are final regardless of the correctness of the reasoning underlying them. In Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), this Court held that the government may not obtain appellate correction of an acquittal, because doing so would subject the defendant to a second jeopardy for the same charge. This Court reaffirmed the finality owed to a tribunal's resolution of a factual issue against the government in Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575 (1948), holding that double jeopardy and related principles preclude the government from relitigating facts necessarily resolved against it in a prior proceeding.

There is no principled reason why this rule should evaporate merely because the factual predicate at issue is a prior conviction used to enhance punishment rather than an element of the charged offense. The consequence to the defendant—years added to a term of imprisonment—is no less severe, and the government's incentive to relitigate a losing factual issue is no less real.

## II. The Multiple-Punishment and Repeated-Prosecution Components of the Double Jeopardy Clause Independently Bar Retrial of the Enhancement Allegation.

As Pearce makes clear, the Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. 395 U.S. at 717. This protection has no less force where the "offense" at issue is the enhancement allegation itself—an allegation that, under California's own procedures, must be tried, proven beyond a reasonable doubt, and defended against with the right of confrontation and cross-examination. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967) (due process requires that a defendant subject to a factfinding proceeding materially affecting the length of confinement be afforded the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them). Once the State has attempted to prove the enhancement and failed, permitting a second attempt exposes Monge to the very evil the double jeopardy guarantee was designed to prevent. As this Court explained in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187–88 (1957), "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity, as well as enhancing the possibility that even though innocent he may be found guilty." That reasoning applies with full force to a State's repeated attempts to prove a sentence-enhancing allegation after its first attempt has failed for want of sufficient proof.

By contrast, this Court has permitted retrial only where the original proceeding ended not in a resolution on the merits but in a procedural termination such as a reversal for trial error unconnected to the sufficiency of the proof. See United States v. Tateo, 377 U.S. 463 (1964). No such circumstance is present here: the Court of Appeal's ruling rested squarely on the insufficiency of the State's proof, not on an unrelated procedural defect.

## III. The Proceeding Below Bore the Hallmarks of a Trial and Therefore Falls Within the Core Protection of the Clause.

California itself has treated prior-conviction proceedings as trial-like adjudications, affording the reasonable-doubt standard, the rules of evidence, and confrontation rights consistent with Specht, 386 U.S. at 610. Having voluntarily structured its enhancement proceeding to mirror a trial on guilt or innocence in every respect but the label attached to it, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend such proceedings. This Court's teaching in Kepner and Sealfon does not turn on formal labels; it turns on whether the proceeding functions to determine, through an adversarial, evidence-based process, a fact that exposes the defendant to further jeopardy. The enhancement trial here did precisely that.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Court of Appeal's insufficiency ruling functions as an acquittal, and because the Double Jeopardy Clause's guarantees against successive prosecution and multiple punishment for the same offense are not confined to the adjudication of the substantive charge, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
