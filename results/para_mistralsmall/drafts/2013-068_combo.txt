**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Valid Time, Place, and Manner Regulation**

The First Amendment permits content-neutral restrictions on speech that are narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 86–87 (1949) (“[A] sound level of 88 decibels is not constitutionutional merely because it is lower than 92.”); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (“The fundamental law does not permit a State to regulate the use of its streets and parks in order to prohibit the dissemination of religious views.”). The Massachusetts statute at issue here fits squarely within this framework. It restricts speech only within a 35-foot radius of clinic entrances, exits, and driveways—areas where congestion and obstruction have repeatedly endangered patients and clinic staff. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160–61 (1939) (“The streets are proper places for the exercise of the freedom of communicating information and disseminating opinion.”); *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (“The authority of a municipality to impose regulations in order to assure the safety and convenience of the people in the use of public highways has never been regarded as inconsistent with civil liberties.”).

The statute is content-neutral because it applies equally to all speakers, regardless of viewpoint. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar buffer zone as content-neutral); *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 115 (1971) (invalidating a ban on “noise and diversion” near schools because it was viewpoint-discriminatory). It does not suppress any message; it merely regulates *where* speech may occur to prevent interference with public safety and access to medical care. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574 (“The privilege of a citizen to use the streets and parks for communication of views on national questions may be regulated in the public interest.”).

The statute is narrowly tailored because it does not ban speech entirely but instead confines it to areas where patients and staff can still be reached. *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 87 (“The ordinance does not unduly limit the area of operation of loud speakers.”). The 35-foot buffer is no broader than necessary to prevent the crowding and harassment that previously obstructed clinic entrances. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 161 (“The streets are proper places for the exercise of the freedom of communicating information and disseminating opinion.”). The statute also leaves open ample alternative channels of communication, including sidewalks beyond the buffer zone, where petitioners may speak, distribute literature, and engage in peaceful counseling. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308 (“The fundamental law does not permit a State to regulate the use of its streets and parks in order to prohibit the dissemination of religious views.”).

**II. The Massachusetts Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment’s Public Forum Doctrine**

The sidewalks and streets adjacent to abortion clinics are traditional public fora where speech is constitutionally protected. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (“Wherever the title of streets and parks may rest, they have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions.”). However, the government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions in such fora to serve significant public interests. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574 (“The authority of a municipality to impose regulations in order to assure the safety and convenience of the people in the use of public highways has never been regarded as inconsistent with civil liberties.”).

The Massachusetts statute is a permissible restriction because it is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open alternative channels of communication. *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 86–87. It does not discriminate based on the message being conveyed, nor does it ban speech entirely. Instead, it ensures that patients and staff can enter and exit clinics without obstruction. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160–61. The buffer zone is no broader than necessary to achieve this goal, and petitioners remain free to speak on public sidewalks beyond the restricted area. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308.

**III. The Massachusetts Statute Does Not Constitute an Unlawful Prior Restraint**

A law that restricts speech based on its content is subject to strict scrutiny, but a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation is permissible if it is narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308. The Massachusetts statute is not a prior restraint because it does not require a permit or pre-approval for speech. Instead, it simply designates a limited area where speech may not occur to prevent obstruction and harassment. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574. The statute does not vest officials with discretion to suppress speech based on its content or viewpoint. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515.

**IV. The Massachusetts Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment’s Overbreadth Doctrine**

A statute is unconstitutionally overbroad if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308. The Massachusetts statute does not suffer from this defect because it is narrowly tailored to address the specific problem of congestion and obstruction at clinic entrances. *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 86–87. It does not ban all speech near clinics but only within a 35-foot radius of entrances, exits, and driveways. The statute also exempts patients, employees, and others with legitimate business at the clinics, ensuring that it does not sweep too broadly. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160–61.

**V. The Massachusetts Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment’s Void-for-Vagueness Doctrine**

A statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide fair notice of what conduct is prohibited. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). The Massachusetts statute is not unconstitutionally vague because it clearly defines the restricted area and the conduct that is prohibited. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574. It applies only within 35 feet of clinic entrances, exits, and driveways, and it exempts lawful activities such as entering or leaving the clinic. The statute also provides clear guidance to law enforcement and the public about where the buffer zone begins and ends. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160–61.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation that serves significant government interests without violating the First Amendment.
