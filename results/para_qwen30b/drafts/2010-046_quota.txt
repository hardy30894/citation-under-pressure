**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s dismissal of the appeal and denial of mandamus because the district court’s October 27 Order is not final, and no extraordinary writ is warranted. The order constitutes an interim procedural step in a continuing enforcement proceeding—not a final adjudication—rendering it unappealable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. Moreover, the State’s claims of Eleventh Amendment immunity and violation of the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) are not ripe for review, and the petition for mandamus fails under *Bauman v. United States District Court*, 557 F.2d 650 (9th Cir. 1977), as the State has adequate alternative remedies and the challenged order is not clearly erroneous.

**I. The October 27 Order Is Not Final and Thus Unappealable Under 28 U.S.C. § 1291**

A federal appellate court lacks jurisdiction over non-final orders unless they fall within a narrow exception. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). The October 27 Order plainly does not qualify. It directs California to transfer $250 million by November 5, 2008, but explicitly reserves further proceedings: “If Defendants fail to transfer $250 million… Defendants are hereby ORDERED TO SHOW CAUSE… why they should not be held in contempt.” This language confirms that the order is not a final judgment but rather a preliminary directive leading to a future contempt hearing. As this court has repeatedly held, “an order that merely requires a party to show cause why it should not be held in contempt is not final and thus not appealable.” *SEC v. Hickey*, 322 F.3d 1123, 1127 (9th Cir. 2003); see also *Stone v. City & County of San Francisco*, 968 F.2d 850, 854–55 (9th Cir. 1992) (“An adjudication of civil contempt is not appealable until sanctions have been imposed.”).

The State’s attempt to recharacterize the order as a final injunction fails. The order does not permanently enjoin or modify any prior consent decree; it simply implements a funding mechanism for a receiver already appointed pursuant to those decrees. *Thompson v. Enomoto*, 815 F.2d 1323, 1327 (9th Cir. 1987) (an order is not appealable as an “affirmative injunction” if it is “pursuant to, and not a modification of,” a consent decree). Here, the October 27 Order is rooted in the original stipulated orders and the receivership established in 2006—orders the State never appealed. *See* *Way v. County of Ventura*, 348 F.3d 808, 810 (9th Cir. 2003) (a final order must “clearly evidenc[e] the judge’s intention that it be the court’s final act in the matter”). The October 27 Order contains no such indication. Instead, it contemplates further scheduling, witness lists, and potential modifications—hallmarks of an interlocutory process.

**II. The State’s Claims Are Not Ripe for Review and Cannot Be Separated from the Underlying Litigation**

The State seeks to challenge the constitutionality of the entire receivership framework, including alleged violations of the PLRA and Eleventh Amendment immunity. But these claims are not severable from the underlying litigation and are therefore not properly before this court. *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978) (collateral order doctrine requires that the issue be “completely separate from the merits” and “effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment”). The State’s objections are fully intertwined with the validity of the consent decrees, the appointment of the receiver, and the implementation of the Turnaround Plan—all matters the State previously acquiesced to and failed to appeal.

Moreover, the State’s consent to the original stipulated orders likely waives its Eleventh Amendment immunity. *Hans v. Louisiana*, 192 U.S. 509, 514 (1904) (state may waive immunity by consenting to suit); *Foster v. California*, 309 U.S. 219, 224 (1940) (state’s participation in litigation constitutes waiver). The State participated in the 2002 and 2004 consent decrees, accepted the receiver’s appointment, and even filed statements of non-opposition to the Receiver’s capital plans. These actions constitute clear consent to judicial oversight and preclude later invocation of immunity. *See* *United States v. Mottaz*, 989 F.2d 1056, 1060 (9th Cir. 1993) (waiver arises when state voluntarily submits to federal jurisdiction).

**III. Mandamus Is Inappropriate Because the State Has Adequate Remedies and the Order Is Not Clearly Erroneous**

Even if the order were appealable, mandamus would be improper. *Bauman*, 557 F.2d at 654–55. First, the State has an adequate remedy via appeal after a final contempt finding. *Stone*, 968 F.2d at 854–55. Second, the State will suffer no irreparable harm if forced to pay $250 million pending resolution of the underlying issues. *In re Philippine Nat’l Bank*, 397 F.3d 768, 774 (9th Cir. 2005) (mandamus not appropriate where party can seek relief through normal appellate channels). Third, the order is not “clearly erroneous.” It follows directly from the district court’s prior findings, the consent decrees, and the receiver’s approved plans. *See* *United States v. Ayres*, 166 F.3d 991, 995 (9th Cir. 1999) (litigants cannot raise constitutional challenges to underlying orders during contempt proceedings, but that does not mean the order itself is erroneous).
