**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews *de novo* a district court’s dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. *Gupta v. Thai Airways Int’l*, 487 F.3d 759, 762 (11th Cir. 2007). Because the district court resolved the jurisdictional question solely on the pleadings, the Court must accept the facts alleged in the complaint as true. *Global Village Communications, Inc. v. F.C.C.*, 238 F.3d 126, 129 (11th Cir. 2001).

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding That Pakistan’s Actions Were Not “Commercial Activity” Under the FSIA.**

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) abrogates immunity when “the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States” or “upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity elsewhere” or “upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity elsewhere of the foreign state and that act causes a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The district court characterized Beg’s claim as one for expropriation, a classic sovereign act, and therefore dismissed the case. That was error. The complaint alleges more than a bare taking. It alleges that the Government of Punjab, through a representative in the United States, offered Beg an alternative parcel of land and that the exchange was accepted by the Lahore High Court but later rejected by the Supreme Court. Beg seeks damages for the failure to complete that exchange and to compensate him for the original taking.

The FSIA defines “commercial activity” as “either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act.” 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The Supreme Court has instructed that the inquiry is “whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). In *Weltover*, the Court held that a foreign government’s issuance of bonds was commercial because private parties also issue bonds. *Id.* at 615. Likewise, entering into an agreement to exchange real property—and offering such property through a representative in the United States—is the kind of transaction in which private parties routinely engage. The exchange of land is a quintessential commercial transaction.

The district court likely relied on decisions holding that expropriations are sovereign. *See, e.g., Callejo v. Bancomer, S.A.*, 764 F.2d 1101, 1116 (5th Cir. 1985) (noting that expropriation is “peculiarly sovereign”). But that line of cases is inapposite. Beg’s claim is not based solely on the expropriation; it is based on the subsequent commercial exchange and the Government of Punjab’s failure to honor it. The element of Beg’s claim that must be proved is the existence of an agreement to exchange property and the breach of that agreement. That is commercial, not sovereign. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 617 (holding that “based upon” requires only that the commercial activity be the element of the claim).

Moreover, the sending of a representative to the United States to offer Beg a parcel of land is an “act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity” under § 1605(a)(2). The representative’s presence and offer were integral to the exchange. That act was not a sovereign act of eminent domain; it was a voluntary, private act of negotiation.

**III. The District Court Erred in Finding No Direct Effect in the United States.**

Even if the offer made in the United States were not itself dispositive, the expropriation and the subsequent failure to compensate produced a “direct effect in the United States” within the meaning of § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has held that a direct effect is one that “follows as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s activity.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. In *Weltover*, the Court found a direct effect in the United States because bond repayments were to be made in New York. *Id.* Here, the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States to offer Beg property, and the failure to complete that exchange caused Beg an immediate loss in the United States. That loss is not a remote or speculative consequence; it is the precise result of Pakistan’s failure to honor the exchange agreement.

Beg is before this Court seeking monetary compensation for the taking and for the broken promise. The property at issue was valued at $10 million, and the exchange agreement was a substitute for compensation. The direct effect of the failure to complete that exchange was the deprivation of that value in the United States, where the offer was made and where Beg now seeks relief. *Cf. Harris v. VAO Intourist, Moscow*, 481 F. Supp. 1056, 1061 (E.D.N.Y. 1979) (finding direct effect where the defendant’s actions caused financial loss to a United States entity).

**IV. Conclusion**

The district court viewed the complaint through a single lens of expropriation and ignored the commercial exchange at the heart of Beg’s claim. The actions of Pakistan and the Government of Punjab—offering land, entering an exchange agreement, and failing to compensate—are commercial in nature. Those actions had a direct effect in the United States. This Court should reverse the district court’s dismissal and remand for further proceedings.
