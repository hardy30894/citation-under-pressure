The Ninth Circuit's decision affirming the dismissal of Michael Bryant, Jr.'s indictment should be reversed because it permits the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), violating the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel in criminal proceedings. The Supreme Court has long recognized that this right is essential to a fair trial. In Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), the Court held that the right to counsel is a fundamental right that is essential to a fair trial. The Court has also held that a conviction obtained in violation of this right is invalid. See Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938).

The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) raises significant Sixth Amendment concerns. Although tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment, see Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), the use of their convictions in federal court to enhance a sentence or establish an element of a crime implicates the Sixth Amendment. The Supreme Court has held that a conviction obtained without counsel cannot be used to enhance a sentence. See Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967).

In Burgett, the Court held that a prior conviction obtained without counsel could not be used to enhance a sentence under a recidivist statute. The Court reasoned that the use of such a conviction would erode the constitutional right to counsel. Similarly, in this case, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) erodes the Sixth Amendment right to counsel.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on Nichols v. United States is misplaced. Nichols involved the use of a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. However, Nichols did not involve the use of a tribal court conviction, and the Court's holding was limited to the sentencing context. See Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-47.

The Supreme Court has also held that a defendant has the right to challenge the validity of prior convictions used to enhance a sentence. See Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448 (1962); Chandler v. Fretag, 348 U.S. 3 (1954). In Oyler, the Court held that a defendant has the right to challenge the validity of prior convictions used to enhance a sentence under a recidivist statute. Similarly, Bryant should be allowed to challenge the validity of his tribal court convictions.

Furthermore, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) is inconsistent with the principles of comity and respect for tribal sovereignty. The Supreme Court has held that federal courts should extend comity to tribal court judgments. See Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805 (9th Cir. 1997) (citing Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9 (1987)). However, this comity is not unlimited, and federal courts have a duty to ensure that the use of tribal court convictions does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

In conclusion, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Bryant's indictment should be dismissed. The Supreme Court's precedents in Burgett, Oyler, Chandler, Gideon, Johnson, and Powell support this conclusion.
