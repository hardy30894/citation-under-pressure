## ARGUMENT

### I. The judgment on the sex-discrimination claim should be reversed because the record does not support an inference that Plaza’s actions were taken because of Feltmann’s sex.

Title VII and the MHRA prohibit adverse treatment “because of” sex. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1); *Daugherty v. City of Maryland Heights*, 231 S.W.3d 814, 820 (Mo. 2007) (en banc) (MHRA construed in light of federal law). A plaintiff may prove discrimination either by direct evidence or through the familiar burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). Under that framework, once the employer articulates a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason, the plaintiff must show that the stated reason is pretext for discrimination. *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253-56 (1981); *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 507-08 (1993). “The ultimate burden of persuading the trier of fact that the defendant intentionally discriminated remains at all times with the plaintiff.” *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 253.

That burden was not met here. The evidence showed a lawful, business-driven response to declining sales and profits. Rich’s January memo criticized Feltmann’s sales method, including excessive “guest drives” and poor teamwork. He then placed both Feltmann and male consultant Rick Beutel on probation after low January and February sales. Those are facially neutral management actions tied to performance. Courts do not sit as “super-personnel departments” second-guessing such business judgments absent proof they were a cover for discrimination. *Fuentes v. Perskie*, 32 F.3d 759, 765 (3d Cir. 1994); *Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. 604, 609-10 (1993) (employment decisions motivated by business considerations are not unlawful absent protected-class animus).

The plaintiff’s strongest evidence is that she was the only female consultant and that she did well in some months. But isolated favorable treatment does not establish sex discrimination in the challenged decisions. *Ellison v. City of Bonner Springs*, 63 F.3d 1314, 1321-22 (8th Cir. 1995). Nor does a disagreement with the wisdom of management’s sales expectations create an inference of unlawful motive. *Hutson v. McDonnell Douglas Corp.*, 63 F.3d 771, 778-79 (8th Cir. 1995). Rich’s decision to restrict demo-car use after declining sales was undisputedly based on cost concerns, and Feltmann admitted she continued taking her demo to Marion weekly despite the restriction. That is a classic performance-and-compliance issue, not evidence of sex bias.

The Chicago sales trip likewise does not support liability. Rich offered the opportunity to the top two consultants; when they declined, he did not offer it to several male consultants who wanted to go, and he also did not offer it to Feltmann. A nondiscriminatory mistake or imperfect business decision is not actionable unless it is shown to be a pretext for discrimination. *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 256; *Clark v. Runyon*, 218 F.3d 915, 918-19 (8th Cir. 2000). Here, the same conduct undercuts any claim that the decision singled out women.

Most importantly, there is no evidence of sex-based remarks by Rich, the Capps, or anyone else connected to the challenged employment decisions. The isolated vulgar comment attributed to Anzalone—an employee at another franchise—was promptly reported, investigated, and addressed through a reprimand. Anzalone was not the decisionmaker on Feltmann’s sales evaluations, probation, demo restrictions, or exclusion from the Chicago trip. Discriminatory intent cannot be attributed to Plaza based on stray remarks by a nondecisionmaker. *EEOC v. Liberal R-II School Dist.*, 314 F.3d 920, 924 (8th Cir. 2002); *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 277 (1989) (O’Connor, J., concurring) (remarks must be tied to the decisional process).

At most, the record shows that Feltmann was a successful salesperson who later disagreed with management’s criticisms and restrictions. That is not enough. Because substantial evidence supports Plaza’s legitimate reasons and no reasonable jury could find those reasons were a pretext for sex discrimination, the verdict on the discrimination claim cannot stand. *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 519; *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 253.

### II. The retaliatory-discharge judgment should also be reversed because the challenged actions were not materially adverse retaliation for protected activity.

Title VII’s anti-retaliation provision protects employees who oppose unlawful discrimination, but the plaintiff must prove a causal connection between the protected activity and a materially adverse employment action. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a); *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 67-68 (2006); *Sherman v. Runyon*, 235 F.3d 406, 409-10 (8th Cir. 2000). The employer’s burden is one of production, not persuasion, and the employee must show pretext. *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 254-55; *Gilooly v. Missouri Department of Health & Senior Services*, 421 S.W.3d 394, 401 (Mo. Ct. App. 2013).

Here, the only protected activity was Feltmann’s complaint about Anzalone’s vulgar remark. Plaza responded immediately and appropriately: Rich reported the matter; the owners met with Feltmann; Anzalone was reprimanded and warned that another incident would mean termination. That prompt corrective action negates any inference of retaliatory animus. *Kipp v. Missouri Highway & Transportation Commission*, 280 F.3d 893, 896 (8th Cir. 2002).

The later events do not establish retaliation. The January memo, March probation, demo restrictions, and denial of the Chicago trip all followed documented sales decline or policy concerns. Temporal proximity alone is insufficient where the employer has legitimate reasons for its conduct. *Kipp*, 280 F.3d at 897; *Kobrin v. University of Minnesota*, 34 F.3d 698, 703 (8th Cir. 1994). And the record defeats causation: Feltmann continued to receive the same general opportunities as other consultants, remained a strong seller in several months, and was subject to the same performance-based scrutiny as at least one male coworker.

Because the evidence does not establish that Plaza’s actions were motivated by Feltmann’s sex or by retaliation for her complaint, the judgment should be reversed.
