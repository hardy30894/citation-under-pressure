**ARGUMENT**

**I. The plain-error inquiry has always required appellate courts to test the effect of an unpreserved error against the whole record, not merely the portion of evidence the jury heard.**

The plain-error doctrine that this Court applies today, and that is now codified in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), traces directly to *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936). There, this Court explained that "[i]n exceptional circumstances, especially in criminal cases, appellate courts, in the public interest, may, of their own motion, notice errors to which no exception has been taken, if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* at 160. That standard asks whether the *proceeding as a whole* was fair and reliable—a question that cannot be answered by artificially confining the reviewing court's gaze to the four corners of what the jury happened to be shown. The inquiry Atkinson describes is fundamentally retrospective and holistic: it examines the trial's integrity in light of everything the record discloses.

This Court confirmed that holistic approach a decade later in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), when it articulated the test for whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights. The Court held that a reviewing court must ask "whether the error itself had substantial influence" on the outcome, and that "if one cannot say, with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error, it is impossible to conclude that substantial rights were not affected." *Id.* at 764–65. The words "pondering all that happened" are not a limitation to the jury's evidentiary diet; they are an instruction to consider the entire course of the proceeding—including matters of record that the jury was never asked to evaluate because they were undisputed.

**II. Confining plain-error review to the evidence presented to the jury would contradict the settled practice of testing prejudice against the record as a whole.**

This Court's harmless-error jurisprudence, which supplies the analytical framework later absorbed into the substantial-rights and fourth-prong analysis of plain-error review, likewise instructs courts to consult the entire record. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), this Court reviewed a lengthy antitrust trial record to determine whether evidentiary errors required reversal, examining what the record as a whole revealed about the strength of the government's case rather than treating each erroneous item in isolation. *Id.* at 239–40. The Court's method was to ask whether, given everything properly and improperly before it, the error could have made a difference—an inquiry that is impossible to conduct with blinders limiting the court to only the specific evidence the jury weighed.

Similarly, in *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607 (1946), this Court evaluated whether an erroneous instruction was harmless by considering the full record of the trial, including the undisputed circumstances surrounding the defendant's conduct. *Id.* at 614–15. The Court did not restrict itself to the jury instructions or the evidence the prosecution formally introduced on the element at issue; it asked what the record, taken together, showed about whether the error mattered. That method—assessing prejudice by reference to the record's undisputed contents—is precisely what the Eleventh Circuit did below when it considered Greer's own record of prior felony convictions in assessing whether the *Rehaif* error affected his substantial rights or the fairness of his proceeding.

**III. Petitioner's rule would frustrate the very purpose plain-error review serves.**

Rule 52(b) plain-error review, grounded in *Atkinson*, exists to correct only those errors that undermine confidence in the judicial process, not to grant windfall relief for technical omissions that could not possibly have changed the result. See *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. If reviewing courts were forbidden from consulting undisputed record facts—such as an unbroken history of felony convictions establishing knowledge of felon status—simply because those facts were not placed before the jury on the knowledge element, courts would be forced to ignore the very evidence that *Kotteakos* commands them to weigh in "pondering all that happened." 328 U.S. at 765. That result would sever plain-error review from the "fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings" standard this Court announced in *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160, transforming a doctrine designed to prevent miscarriages of justice into one that manufactures them.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, and consistent with *Atkinson*, *Kotteakos*, *Socony-Vacuum*, and *Bollenbach*, the Eleventh Circuit correctly examined the full record—not merely the evidence submitted to the jury—in determining that the *Rehaif* error did not affect Greer's substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. The judgment below should be affirmed.
