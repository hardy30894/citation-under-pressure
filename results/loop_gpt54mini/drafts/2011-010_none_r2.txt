**Argument**

The Court should affirm because 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) does not reach an onshore forklift accident at a processing facility merely because the employee also worked offshore. Congress extended Longshore Act benefits only for disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” That language requires more than a remote causal chain; it requires an injury sufficiently tied to offshore operations as Congress described them. The statute does not authorize coverage for every shoreside injury connected in some way to offshore production.

Text is the starting point and the stopping point where it is clear. “Where the words of a law are plain, and the meaning clear, we have no right to speculate upon the intentions of the legislature.” **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**. Courts “have no right to add to a statute what Congress has omitted.” **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**. And “words are not to be stretched beyond their plain and obvious import.” **United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820)**. Section 1333(b) is not worded as a general “but for” test; Congress chose the narrower phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That choice matters.

The Supreme Court’s older territorial and statutory cases confirm that Congress’s chosen limits must be respected. In **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**, the Court refused to extend a statute beyond the territory Congress addressed. In **Ex parte McNiel, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 236, 242 (1871)**, the Court treated statutory preconditions as binding, not advisory. In **Morrill v. Jones, 106 U.S. 466, 467 (1883)**, the Court stated that a statutory grant “cannot be enlarged by implication.” Those principles foreclose respondent’s attempt to convert OCSLA into all-purpose coverage for any employee whose job is generally related to offshore drilling.

The compensation cases likewise show that coverage depends on the statute’s own boundaries. In **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932)**, the Court emphasized that compensation jurisdiction turns on facts and limits fixed by Congress. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court again adhered to the statutory line Congress drew. And in **Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 416 (1932)**, the Court recognized that even remedial legislation must be applied as enacted. The same is true here: OCSLA extends Longshore coverage only when the injury is within the scope Congress defined, not whenever the worker’s employment has some upstream connection to offshore operations.

Respondent’s position would erase the statutory phrase “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The injury in this case occurred at La Conchita, an onshore oil-processing facility. However important that facility was to Pacific Operations’s business, it was not on the Shelf. Congress drew a distinction between offshore operations and shoreside facilities, and courts may not collapse that distinction by judicial fiat. **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**, and **Baltimore Mail S.S. Co. v. Fawcett, 269 U.S. 247, 251-52 (1925)** both reflect the same restraint: where Congress has defined the reach of a federal remedy, courts may not extend it to new settings not expressed in the text.

Nor does the humanitarian purpose of compensation law justify expansion. The Court has repeatedly held that statutes with remedial aims still must be enforced according to their terms. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**. The fact that OCSLA is a workers’ compensation statute does not license rewriting its geographic and operational limits. Congress could have covered any injury “related to” offshore work. It did not. It chose instead a more confined formulation, which must be given effect. **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 281 U.S. 574, 580-81 (1930)**.

The proper reading of § 1333(b) therefore bars respondent’s claim. A worker killed while using a forklift at an onshore processing facility was not injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” within the meaning of the statute. The judgment should be affirmed.
