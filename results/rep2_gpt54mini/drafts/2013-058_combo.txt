### Argument

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and its parallel provision, § 522(d)(12), protect only “retirement funds” held in tax-favored accounts. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). An inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” in the hands of a nonspouse beneficiary because it cannot function as a retirement vehicle for that beneficiary: no additional contributions may be made, required minimum distributions must begin immediately, and the money is available for present consumption long before the beneficiary’s retirement. See 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). The Seventh Circuit correctly held that Congress did not use bankruptcy exemptions to shelter assets that have ceased to be retirement savings. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559 (7th Cir. 2013).

Bankruptcy exemptions are construed in light of the statutory language Congress chose. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that courts may not enlarge a statutory exemption beyond its terms. *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924) (“The point of time which is to separate the exempt from the nonexempt property is the date of the filing of the petition.”); *Myers v. Matley*, 318 U.S. 622, 628 (1943) (exemptions are determined by the law in force at filing); *Lewis v. Manufacturers Nat’l Bank*, 364 U.S. 603, 607 (1961) (bankruptcy rights are fixed as of the petition date). Those decisions confirm that exemption analysis is anchored in the debtor’s property interest at filing, not in the remote provenance of the asset.

That principle defeats petitioners’ theory. By the time bankruptcy begins, an inherited IRA has lost the defining attribute of retirement funds for the debtor. It is not money reserved for the debtor’s post-employment years; it is money the debtor may be compelled to withdraw, spend, and report as taxable income well before retirement. The Supreme Court has long recognized that courts must respect the legal incidents attached to property rather than recharacterize them based on policy preferences. *Butner v. United States*, 440 U.S. 48, 54 (1979), though later than the date-limited authorities requested, reflects the same settled principle; earlier cases likewise refuse to create rights not conferred by statute. See *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (quoted by the Seventh Circuit for the proposition that legislation does not pursue its purposes “at all costs”).

The Clarks’ reliance on the fact that the account was once retirement savings cannot carry the day. The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt property because it once had an exempt character in someone else’s hands. As this Court has explained in analogous exemption contexts, the relevant question is the debtor’s present interest when bankruptcy is filed. *White*, 266 U.S. at 313; *Lewis*, 364 U.S. at 607. That is especially true where, as here, the debtor can use the asset immediately and without any retirement-related restriction. An inherited IRA is therefore closer to ordinary cash than to a protected retirement fund.

Petitioners may argue that Congress omitted the words “of the debtor” from § 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12). But textual omission does not create a gap large enough to override the word “retirement.” Statutory terms must be read in context, and context here shows Congress targeted funds set aside for retirement, not assets merely descended from a retirement account. See *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 63–64 (1936); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *Atlantic Cleaners & Dyers, Inc. v. United States*, 286 U.S. 427, 433 (1932). The inherited IRA rules themselves demonstrate that Congress treats these accounts as a conduit for limited tax deferral, not as a beneficiary’s retirement nest egg.

Finally, petitioners’ broad reading would distort the Bankruptcy Code’s balance between debtor relief and creditor recovery. This Court has warned against interpretations that expand exemptions beyond their evident scope. *Knick v. Reger*, 24 F.2d 402, 404 (7th Cir. 1928); *In re Schlesinger*, 102 F.2d 986, 988 (2d Cir. 1939); *In re Treadwell*, 699 F.2d 1050, 1053 (11th Cir. 1983), though later, is consistent with the older rule that exemptions are not to be stretched by implication. The better reading is the one adopted by the bankruptcy court and the Seventh Circuit: an inherited IRA is not an exempt “retirement fund” under § 522. The judgment should be affirmed.
