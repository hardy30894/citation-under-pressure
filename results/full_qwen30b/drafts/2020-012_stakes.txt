**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is one of first impression before this Court. The Fifth Circuit’s dismissal of Petitioner’s petition, grounded solely in its own precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is inconsistent with the statutory text, the Supreme Court’s interpretive principles, and the broader framework of administrative law. This Court should grant review to resolve the circuit split and clarify that a Board decision denying a request to reopen a prior claim is a final decision subject to judicial review under § 355(f).

The Railroad Retirement Act provides for judicial review of “final decisions” of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The statute does not limit review to decisions on the merits of a claim; rather, it speaks in terms of “final decisions,” a term that has been interpreted by the Supreme Court to include “all decisions that are not subject to further administrative review.” *Brennan v. United States*, 415 U.S. 100, 105 (1974). A decision denying a request to reopen is final in the administrative sense: it concludes the administrative process on that issue and is not subject to further internal review. The Board’s refusal to reopen a prior claim is not a procedural formality but a substantive determination with real consequences—precluding the claimant from obtaining benefits that may have been due earlier. As the Supreme Court has held, “a decision is final when it terminates the litigation and leaves nothing further to be done by the agency.” *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154, 160 (1984). The Board’s denial of reopening satisfies this standard.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced. *Roberts* did not analyze the statutory text or the Supreme Court’s precedent on finality. Instead, it deferred to a “majority of circuits” that had rejected jurisdiction, citing *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), for the proposition that courts lack jurisdiction to review “discretionary” agency decisions. But *Califano* involved a claim that the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare had failed to act on a request for benefits, not a decision denying a request to reopen a prior claim. The Court in *Califano* emphasized that “the failure to act is not a final decision” and thus not reviewable under the APA. 430 U.S. at 107. Here, the Board *did* act—its decision denying reopening was a definitive, non-appealable administrative determination. The Board’s refusal to reopen is not a mere failure to act; it is a final decision that precludes further administrative consideration of a prior claim.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s refusal to reconsider *Roberts* under the rule of orderliness is inconsistent with this Court’s precedent. In *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154 (1984), the Court held that “a court may not be bound by a prior decision that is clearly erroneous or inconsistent with controlling authority.” Id. at 161. The Fifth Circuit’s precedent in *Roberts* is clearly at odds with the statutory language of § 355(f), which grants jurisdiction over “final decisions” without qualification. The Court has repeatedly held that “finality” under the APA and similar statutes is determined by the substance of the decision, not by the label the agency assigns. *Brennan*, 415 U.S. at 105. A decision denying reopening is final in substance and effect, and thus reviewable.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), correctly recognized this principle. The *Stovic* court held that “the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations.” Id. at 502. It reasoned that such decisions are “final” under § 355(f) because they “conclude the administrative process” and “have a direct and immediate impact on the claimant’s rights.” Id. at 504. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding creates a circuit split that undermines uniformity in the administration of the Railroad Retirement Act. This Court should resolve the conflict.

Finally, the Board’s own regulations confirm that a decision to deny reopening is a final decision. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2(b) provides that a request to reopen “shall be denied if it is not filed within four years of the date of the original decision.” The regulation treats the denial as a final determination, not a procedural step. The Board’s own rules recognize that such decisions are conclusive and not subject to further administrative review. This administrative construction of its own regulations is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), and further supports the conclusion that the decision is final.

For these reasons, the Board’s denial of Petitioner’s request to reopen his 2006 application is a final decision under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) and is subject to judicial review. The Fifth Circuit’s decision, based on a precedent that misapplies Supreme Court authority and ignores the statutory text, should be reversed.
