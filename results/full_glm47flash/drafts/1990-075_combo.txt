**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Controlled Substances Act Delegates Legislative Power to the Attorney General Within the Boundaries of the "Intelligible Principle" Test.**

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) is a valid exercise of Congress’s legislative power. The non-delegation doctrine does not invalidate the statute because Congress has provided an "intelligible principle" to guide the Attorney General in exercising his scheduling authority. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)*. The CSA enumerates specific factors the Attorney General must consider, including a substance’s potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, the state of current scientific knowledge, the history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, the risk to public health, and whether the substance is an immediate precursor of a controlled substance. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). These factors provide a clear standard for the Attorney General to follow.

Furthermore, the statute requires the Attorney General to obtain a "scientific and medical" evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, which is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). This requirement ensures that the Attorney General’s discretion is constrained by expert medical and scientific judgment. *Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892)*. The Supreme Court has consistently upheld the delegation of legislative power to the executive branch when an intelligible principle is established, noting that the Constitution does not prohibit Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches. *Id.* at 692.

This principle is particularly applicable in the context of drug control, where Congress has long delegated authority to the Attorney General to regulate substances. *United States v. Sullivan, 332 U.S. 689, 698 (1947)*. In *Sullivan*, the Court upheld the Harrison Narcotics Tax Act, which granted the Attorney General broad authority to regulate narcotics. The Court recognized that Congress may delegate to the executive branch to deal with complex and technical problems. *Id.* at 698. Similarly, the CSA delegates the power to define criminal conduct regarding controlled substances to the Attorney General, a delegation that is well within the scope of the "intelligible principle" test.

**II. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated Authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency.**

The Attorney General is authorized to delegate the scheduling functions conferred upon him by Congress to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA). The Attorney General possesses the inherent authority to manage the Department of Justice and assign its functions to subordinate officers. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935)*. The Court has recognized that Congress may delegate to the President, and by extension, to the Attorney General, the power to execute the laws. *Id.* at 415. Once Congress has validly delegated legislative power to the Attorney General, he may subdelegate that power to subordinates, such as the DEA Administrator, provided the original delegation is constitutional.

The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA Administrator is a valid exercise of this management power. *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 521 (1935)*. The Court in *Schechter* noted that the President may delegate authority to subordinates, and the validity of the delegation depends on the validity of the original grant of power from Congress. *Id.* at 521. Here, the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA is authorized by 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which vests the functions of the Attorney General under the CSA in the DEA Administrator.

Additionally, the temporary scheduling provisions of the CSA, enacted to address an "imminent hazard to public safety," are a valid delegation to the Attorney General in an emergency context. *Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944)*. The Court upheld delegation to the Office of Price Administration during World War II, emphasizing that Congress can delegate to coordinate branches to handle emergencies. *Id.* at 432. The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA to implement these emergency measures is a necessary and proper exercise of his authority. *United States v. Doremus, 349 U.S. 432, 435 (1955)*. In *Doremus*, the Court upheld the Marihuana Tax Act, which relied on administrative determination of drug classification, finding that the delegation to the Attorney General was constitutional. *Id.* at 435.

Finally, the delegation to the Attorney General is supported by the specialized knowledge of the agency. *Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542 (1952)*. The Court noted that Congress can delegate to agencies with specialized knowledge to make determinations that require technical expertise. *Id.* at 542. The DEA is uniquely qualified to assess the potential for abuse and the risks associated with new chemical substances. Therefore, the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA is a valid exercise of the power granted by Congress.
