ARGUMENT

I.

Bryant possesses standing to enforce the arbitration award. In United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593 (1960), the Court held that federal courts may enforce awards rendered pursuant to collective bargaining agreements, stating that “the federal policy of settling labor disputes by arbitration” requires judicial enforcement when the award draws its essence from the agreement. Id. at 596. Bryant, as the aggrieved employee and party to the underlying grievance, stands in the position of the union beneficiary and may invoke that enforcement power.

II.

The district court misapplied the proof requirements governing claims under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Long before the enactment of Title VII, the Court recognized that purposeful racial distinctions in employment conditions violate the equal protection component of the Fifth Amendment. See Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954) (“discrimination may be so unjustifiable as to be violative of due process”). Evidence that Bryant, an African-American employee with long service, was subjected to escalating discipline for refusing overtime that conflicted with child-care duties—while the company refused accommodations offered by his union—raises a triable inference of racial motivation. See Shelley v. Kraemer, 334 U.S. 1, 20 (1948) (state enforcement of private discriminatory arrangements denies equal protection). The same facts support Bryant’s retaliation claim, as the final termination followed his repeated protests against the Red Letter Day policy.

III.

Summary judgment was improper because genuine issues of material fact remain. Under Rule 56, a movant must demonstrate the absence of any genuine dispute; “all doubts as to the existence of a genuine issue of material fact must be resolved against the moving party.” Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). The record contains conflicting evidence whether Bell Atlantic’s refusal to permit shift-swapping or union substitution was motivated by Bryant’s race or by legitimate business needs, and whether the company’s stated reason of “insubordination” was pretextual. These factual disputes preclude judgment as a matter of law.

The district court’s orders should therefore be reversed.
