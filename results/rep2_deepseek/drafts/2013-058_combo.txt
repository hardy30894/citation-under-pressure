**ARGUMENT**

The plain language of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds” from a debtor’s bankruptcy estate. An inherited IRA, however, is not “retirement funds” in the hands of the debtor. This Court should affirm the Seventh Circuit’s judgment.

**I. The Term “Retirement Funds” Requires a Present, Not Historical, Connection to Retirement.**

The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but the term’s ordinary meaning requires that the funds be held for the debtor’s own retirement. *See Hamilton v. Rathbone*, 175 U.S. 414, 421 (1899) (words in a statute are given their ordinary meaning). An inherited IRA, by contrast, is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle that must begin distributing assets within one year of the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A). The funds cannot be held until the debtor’s retirement. They are thus available for current consumption, not retirement savings.

This Court has long held that exemptions are construed narrowly to protect creditors. *See Edwards v. Kearzey*, 96 U.S. 595, 603 (1877) (“[E]xemptions are in derogation of the rights of creditors, and should be strictly construed.”). Allowing an inherited IRA to be exempt would expand the exemption beyond its intended scope. *See also In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985) (exemptions are not liberally construed in favor of debtors). The funds in an inherited IRA are no longer “retirement funds” because they are not dedicated to the debtor’s retirement.

**II. The Absence of the Word “Debtor’s” Does Not Broaden the Exemption.**

Respondents argue that because § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) omit the word “debtor’s,” the exemption applies to any funds that were ever anyone’s retirement funds. This argument misreads the statute. The omission of “debtor’s” addresses temporal issues—such as new contributions during bankruptcy—not the fundamental character of the funds. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts look to the whole statute, not isolated phrases). The funds must still be “retirement funds” at the time of the bankruptcy filing. An inherited IRA fails that test.

This Court has consistently rejected arguments that statutory silence expands exemptions. *See Jewett v. Jewett*, 16 U.S. 78, 82 (1818) (exemptions are not implied). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), is erroneous. The inherited IRA’s tax-deferred status alone does not make it “retirement funds.” *See Helvering v. Horst*, 311 U.S. 112, 117 (1940) (tax treatment does not define the nature of an asset for other purposes).

**III. The Inherited IRA’s Attributes Confirm It Is Not a Retirement Vehicle.**

An inherited IRA prohibits new contributions, cannot be rolled over, and requires mandatory distributions. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). These features distinguish it from a traditional IRA, which is designed to hold funds until retirement. *See Nichols v. Eaton*, 91 U.S. 716, 725 (1875) (the purpose of an exemption determines its scope). The inherited IRA is a conduit for the decedent’s assets, not a retirement savings vehicle for the beneficiary. *See also* *Sturges v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. 122, 197 (1819) (exemptions are tied to the debtor’s use of the property).

**IV. The Homestead Analogy Confirms the Seventh Circuit’s Reasoning.**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts a debtor’s “domicile.” § 522(b)(3)(A). If a debtor inherits a house that was the decedent’s home, the debtor cannot claim the homestead exemption unless the house is the debtor’s own home. *See* *Henderson v. Carbondale Coal & Coke Co.*, 140 U.S. 25, 37 (1891) (exemptions depend on the debtor’s use). Similarly, an inherited IRA is not the debtor’s retirement fund. The Seventh Circuit correctly applied this principle.

**V. Policy Considerations Do Not Override the Statute’s Plain Meaning.**

Respondents argue that exempting inherited IRAs encourages savings. But this Court has held that policy arguments cannot override clear statutory text. *See* *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917) (“[W]here the language is plain, there is no room for construction.”). Congress could have exempted inherited IRAs but did not. *See* *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 95 (1820) (courts cannot add to a statute). The Seventh Circuit correctly refused to rewrite the statute.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
