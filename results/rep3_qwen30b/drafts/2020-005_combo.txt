**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when that defendant has established minimum contacts with the forum state such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle is grounded in the requirement that jurisdiction must be based upon the defendant’s own actions—specifically, those activities that are “purposefully directed” toward the forum state and that give rise to the plaintiff’s claims. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1979) (citing *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985)). When a claim arises from events wholly disconnected from the forum state, no amount of incidental or attenuated contact can justify the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction.

Here, Ford Motor Company did not design, manufacture, sell, or distribute the vehicle at issue within Montana. The vehicle was assembled in Kentucky, first sold to a dealership in Washington State, then transferred through Oregon before being brought into Montana by a private purchaser. No Ford sales representative ever entered Montana to solicit business; no Ford advertising targeted Montana residents; no Ford service center operated in Montana at the time of the accident. Indeed, Ford’s only connection to Montana was the sale of replacement parts through its national distribution network—a practice common to all automakers and entirely unrelated to the tire defect that caused Gullett’s death. Such passive, general commercial activity cannot constitute “purposeful availment” of the privileges of conducting business in a state. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958); *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984) (per curiam).

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself” of the privilege of doing business in Montana rests on an impermissible extension of the concept of purposeful availment. That court held that because Ford sells parts in Montana, it has submitted itself to the state’s jurisdiction for any claim arising from those parts—even if the part at issue in the litigation is not the one sold in Montana, and even if the injury occurred entirely outside the state’s reach. This reasoning contradicts settled precedent. In *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987), the Court emphasized that “the foreseeability of harm” alone is insufficient to establish jurisdiction; rather, the defendant must have “purposefully directed” its activities toward the forum. Similarly, in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985), the Court rejected jurisdiction where the defendant had engaged in “generalized marketing efforts” without targeting the forum state specifically.

Moreover, the Montana Supreme Court’s approach effectively transforms every manufacturer’s nationwide distribution system into a basis for personal jurisdiction in every state where its products are sold. This would violate the constitutional limits on state power. As this Court held in *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957), “a single transaction” may suffice for jurisdiction—but only when it is “related to the cause of action.” Here, the tire defect was not caused by any act of Ford in Montana, nor was it foreseeable that a tire failure in Montana would result from a product never sold or serviced there. The mere fact that a defective product ends up in a state does not create jurisdiction unless the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the benefits of that state’s laws.

The Montana Supreme Court also misapplies the doctrine of “arising out of or related to” jurisdiction. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 417 (1984). A claim “arises out of” a defendant’s forum activities only when those activities are the source of the injury. *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 867, 878 (2011). But here, the injury resulted from a tire tread separation during operation on a Montana highway—long after the vehicle left the control of Ford’s distribution chain. The causal link between Ford’s conduct and the injury is severed by the intervening acts of third parties: the dealer in Washington, the Oregon buyer, and the Montana purchaser. This is not a case of “direct causation,” but of remote, indirect consequence. *Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute*, 499 U.S. 585, 592 (1991).

Finally, the Montana Supreme Court’s decision undermines the predictability essential to due process. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1979). If a corporation can be haled into court in any state where its product is used—even if it never marketed or sold there—the risk of litigation becomes arbitrary and unpredictable. This violates the fundamental fairness required by the Constitution. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). The Due Process Clause demands more than a mere possibility of harm; it requires a meaningful connection between the defendant’s conduct and the forum state. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958).

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court erred in finding specific personal jurisdiction over Ford. The Due Process Clause prohibits a state from asserting jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant whose contacts with the forum are too attenuated to satisfy constitutional requirements. The judgment should be reversed.
