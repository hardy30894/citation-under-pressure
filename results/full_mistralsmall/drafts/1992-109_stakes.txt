**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive counsel or plead guilty.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that due process demands a competency inquiry whenever a trial court has or should have a “good faith doubt” about a defendant’s ability to make a reasoned choice among available alternatives. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 270 (9th Cir. 1992) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871 (1988)). This standard reflects the principle that waivers of fundamental constitutional rights must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969). The Court has long recognized that the competency required to waive such rights is greater than the competency needed merely to stand trial. *Id.* at 242 n.5; *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399 (1993) (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)).

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that due process requires a defendant to possess “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” to stand trial. The Court further explained that competency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of functioning, as the defendant must “understand the nature of the rights being abandoned and the consequences of the decision.” *Id.* at 172 n.10. The Ninth Circuit’s articulation of the “reasoned choice” standard comports with this principle. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 270.

II. The trial court erred by failing to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte when substantial evidence raised a good faith doubt about respondent’s ability to make a reasoned choice.

The Ninth Circuit correctly determined that the record contained substantial evidence of respondent’s incompetency at the time he waived counsel and pleaded guilty. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 270. Respondent had attempted suicide only months before the plea hearing, was taking multiple medications with known cognitive side effects, and provided monosyllabic responses to the court’s inquiries. *Id.* at 267-68. Under these circumstances, the trial court had an affirmative obligation to inquire further into respondent’s competency. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946 (1982)).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that trial courts must take care to ensure that waivers of constitutional rights are knowing and intelligent. *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243-44. The failure to conduct a competency hearing when substantial evidence of incompetency exists violates due process. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966) (holding that due process requires a competency hearing whenever a court has reason to doubt a defendant’s competency).

III. The state court’s post-conviction competency determination is not entitled to deference because it applied the wrong legal standard.

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected the state court’s post-conviction competency determination because it applied the standard for competency to stand trial rather than the heightened “reasoned choice” standard required for waivers of constitutional rights. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 272-73. The state court’s reliance on psychiatric evaluations that assessed only competency to stand trial was insufficient to determine respondent’s ability to make a reasoned choice. *Id.* at 274-75.

This Court has held that federal courts must conduct an independent review of a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 116-17 (1985). The Ninth Circuit’s decision to conduct such an independent review was consistent with this principle. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 273.

IV. The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not conflict with this Court’s precedents.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision is fully consistent with this Court’s precedents. The Court has never held that the Due Process Clause permits a trial court to accept a waiver of constitutional rights from a defendant who lacks the capacity for reasoned choice. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of ensuring that such waivers are knowing and intelligent. *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 242-44.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not create a circuit split. The Second, Third, and Seventh Circuits have all held that due process requires a heightened competency standard for waivers of constitutional rights. *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247, 1249 (9th Cir. 1978); *United States v. Gillis*, 773 F.2d 549, 553 (6th Cir. 1985); *United States v. Swanson*, 943 F.2d 1070, 1074 (9th Cir. 1991).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. The trial court erred by failing to conduct a competency hearing when substantial evidence raised a good faith doubt about respondent’s ability to make a reasoned choice. The state court’s post-conviction competency determination is not entitled to deference because it applied the wrong legal standard. The Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents and should be affirmed.
