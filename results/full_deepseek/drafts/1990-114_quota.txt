**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Prohibit Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Because Appointed State Judges Are “Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level.”**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office . . . or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The judges here—initially appointed by the Governor under the Missouri Plan—fall squarely within the “appointee on the policymaking level” exclusion.

Judging is policymaking. As the First Circuit recognized, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). That holding is correct. Judges exercise the same sort of “exercise of discretion” and “exercise of judgment” as policymakers in other branches. *Id.* Moreover, Missouri’s judges exercise supervisory authority over the court system, further confirming their policymaking role.

Any doubt about statutory coverage is resolved by the clear-statement rule. The tenure of state judges is a “traditionally sensitive area[]” affecting the federal balance; thus, courts must apply a clear statement requirement before finding that Congress intended to intrude. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Congress must “expressly and unequivocally” state its intent to preempt state law. *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985). The ADEA’s text does not clearly include appointed state judges; indeed, the statutory exception for “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” demonstrates Congress’s intent to exclude them. *See Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1, 17 (1981) (requiring clear statement before federal statutes upset state interests). Because Congress has not spoken plainly, the ADEA claim fails.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate Equal Protection.**

Age is not a suspect class. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (“This Court’s decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental.”). Mandatory retirement laws are therefore reviewed only for rational basis. *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979). That deferential standard is easily satisfied.

Missouri’s constitutional provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests. As the Eighth Circuit observed, mandatory retirement “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged judges,” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), and reduces the risk that age-related decline will impair judicial performance. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627–29 (7th Cir. 1979) (“[I]t is rational to treat judges differently from other officials on the ground that the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.”). State courts also rely on an age line to ensure predictable succession and to avoid the burdens of after-the-fact removal of judges of failing competence. *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver General*, 401 Mass. 427, 436, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). Given these rationales, Missouri’s provision passes rational-basis review.

The judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
