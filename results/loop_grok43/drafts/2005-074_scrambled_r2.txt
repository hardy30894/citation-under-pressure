ARGUMENT

The reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1251(a)(5) (1994), does not reach an alien whose reentry occurred before its 1997 effective date. Statutes operate only prospectively unless Congress expresses a contrary intent with unmistakable clarity. Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 390 (1798). The text contains no such command. It therefore cannot disturb rights or liabilities fixed by conduct completed years earlier. Owings v. Speed, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 420, 423 (1820).

Even if the statute were read to apply, that construction would render it impermissibly retroactive. A law that “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws” is void. Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). Petitioner’s long residence and marriage to a citizen created settled expectations protected by the Due Process Clause. Murray's Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276 (1856). The government may not extinguish those expectations by later legislation. Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 724 (1893).

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion rests on the premise that no protectable interest arises until an application for adjustment is filed. That premise contradicts established doctrine. Once an alien has entered and remained, “he is entitled to the protection of the law.” Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276, 282 (1922). Depriving him of all forms of relief without a hearing violates the guarantee of an opportunity to be heard. Mahler v. Eby, 264 U.S. 32, 41 (1924).

The same result follows from the canon that ambiguous immigration statutes are construed to avoid harsh consequences. Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135, 147 (1945). Because the statute is at best silent on pre-enactment reentries, it must be read not to reach them. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 216 (1953).

The judgment should be reversed.
