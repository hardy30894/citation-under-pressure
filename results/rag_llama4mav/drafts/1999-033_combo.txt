The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted expansion of the Clause's protections.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See Norwood v. Baker, 172 U.S. 269 (1898); City of Joplin v. Southwest Missouri Light Co., 191 U.S. 150 (1903). In these cases, the Court has emphasized the importance of equal treatment under the law for individuals within a particular class. The Clause is not intended to provide a cause of action for individuals who claim to have been treated unfairly, but not as part of a larger class. See Skaneateles Water Works Co. v. Skaneateles, 184 U.S. 354 (1902); Superior Water, Light & Power Co. v. City of Superior, 263 U.S. 125 (1923).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause is not a license for courts to review the rationality of every state action. See Hetrick v. Village of Lindsey, 265 U.S. 384 (1924); Ohio Public Service Co. v. Ohio Ex Rel. Fritz, 274 U.S. 12 (1927). Rather, it is a guarantee that individuals within a particular class will be treated equally. See Rogers Park Water Co. v. Fergus, 180 U.S. 624 (1901); Consumers' Co. v. Hatch, 224 U.S. 148 (1912).

In United States v. Grizzard, 219 U.S. 180 (1911), the Court held that the government's actions must be evaluated in light of their impact on the individual, but this does not imply that an individual can bring an equal protection claim without alleging membership in a class. The Court's decision in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), is also instructive, as it emphasizes the importance of equal treatment under the law, but does so in the context of a class-based claim.

The Seventh Circuit's holding would effectively transform the Equal Protection Clause into a federal "fairness" doctrine, allowing courts to review the rationality of every state action. See Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27 (1885). This is not a role that the Clause was intended to play. By holding that a "class of one" can bring an equal protection claim, the Seventh Circuit has overstepped its authority and ignored the long line of cases that have limited the Clause's protections to class-based claims.

In conclusion, the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a cause of action for a "class of one." The Clause is intended to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group, and the Seventh Circuit's holding is an unwarranted expansion of its protections. The Court should reverse the Seventh Circuit's decision and reaffirm the traditional understanding of the Equal Protection Clause.
