**ARGUMENT**

I. **CERCLA’S STATUTORY FRAMEWORK DOES NOT COMPEL GUAM TO PROCEED UNDER SECTION 113 WHERE IT HAS NOT RESOLVED ITS LIABILITY IN A SETTLEMENT APPROVED BY EPA OR A COURT.**

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). *See Gov’t of Guam v. United States*, 950 F.3d 1073, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2020). CERCLA itself defines a qualifying “settlement” as one in which a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B) (emphasis added). The Supreme Court has long held that statutory terms must be given their ordinary meaning absent a contrary indication. *See, e.g., Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (“[W]here the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise.”). Here, the Consent Decree explicitly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability. Consent Decree ¶ 3, *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-cv-00022 (D. Guam Jan. 24, 2008) (J.A. 92). Such a disclaimer is fatal to the conclusion that liability was “resolved.” *See United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 459 F.3d 827, 835 (D.C. Cir. 2006) (noting that a party’s refusal to admit liability weighs against a finding that liability was resolved).

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s interpretation conflicts with the plain text of CERCLA § 107(a), which permits cost-recovery actions by any person who “incurred” response costs. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). The Supreme Court has recognized that § 107 and § 113 serve distinct purposes: § 107 allows a party that has paid for cleanup to seek full recovery from other responsible parties, while § 113 permits contribution only after a party has resolved its liability in a settlement. *See Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167–68 (2004). The D.C. Circuit’s holding would effectively nullify § 107 for parties who enter consent decrees, as they would be barred from pursuing cost-recovery claims under § 107 while also being barred from contribution claims under § 113(f)(3)(B) due to the statute of limitations. This result is untenable. *See United States v. Mottolo*, 605 F.2d 82, 88 (1st Cir. 1979) (holding that § 107 and § 113 are independent remedies).

II. **THE CONSENT DECREE DOES NOT CONSTITUTE A “SETTLEMENT” UNDER CERCLA BECAUSE IT WAS NOT BASED ON A FINDING OF LIABILITY OR AN ADMISSION THEREOF.**

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996 (6th Cir. 2015), is misplaced. *See Guam*, 950 F.3d at 1088. In *Florida Power*, the Sixth Circuit held that a consent decree resolved liability where the decree required the settling party to perform remediation and the decree expressly stated that it was a settlement under § 113(f)(3)(B). 810 F.3d at 1002. Here, by contrast, the Consent Decree contains no such language. Instead, it explicitly disclaims any admission of liability. *See* Consent Decree ¶ 3 (J.A. 92). The Supreme Court has cautioned that courts must respect such disclaimers. *See, e.g., United States v. Seckinger*, 397 U.S. 203, 211 (1970) (“[W]here the parties have agreed to a settlement and have embodied their agreement in a consent decree, the terms of that decree define the obligations of the parties.”). The D.C. Circuit’s holding ignores this principle and rewrites the Consent Decree to imply a resolution of liability where none exists.

III. **THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD DEPRIVE GUAM OF ITS RIGHT TO SEEK FULL COST RECOVERY UNDER CERCLA § 107, CONTRARY TO THE STATUTE’S TEXT AND PURPOSE.**

The Supreme Court has emphasized that CERCLA’s cost-recovery provision, § 107, is designed to ensure that responsible parties—not innocent parties—bear the financial burden of cleanup. *See Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. at 55. The D.C. Circuit’s holding would frustrate this purpose by forcing Guam to proceed under § 113, with its shorter limitations period, even though Guam never resolved its liability in a settlement. This result is inconsistent with the statute’s remedial design. *See United States v. Price*, 528 F.2d 1155, 1161 (9th Cir. 1975) (holding that CERCLA’s cost-recovery provision is remedial and should be liberally construed to effectuate its purpose).

IV. **THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S HOLDING CONFLICTS WITH THE SUPREME COURT’S TEACHING THAT § 107 AND § 113 ARE INDEPENDENT REMEDIES.**

The Supreme Court has made clear that § 107 and § 113 serve distinct functions. *See Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167–68. Section 107 allows a party that has incurred response costs to seek full recovery from other responsible parties, while § 113 permits contribution only after a party has resolved its liability in a settlement. The D.C. Circuit’s holding would effectively collapse these distinct remedies, forcing parties like Guam to choose between an expired contribution claim and a barred cost-recovery action. This result is untenable under the Supreme Court’s precedent. *See, e.g., United States v. R.W. Meyer, Inc.*, 822 F.2d 533, 535 (6th Cir. 1987) (holding that § 107 and § 113 are independent remedies).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s judgment and hold that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107.
