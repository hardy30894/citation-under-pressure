ARGUMENT

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage only when disability or death results from injury “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” That language imposes a geographic limitation on the place of injury. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54 (1932) (federal compensation statutes are construed according to their “plain words”).

Maritime law has long required that the injurious event itself occur within the defined federal situs. The Plymouth, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 20, 36 (1866), held that admiralty jurisdiction “depends upon the locality of the act” and does not reach injuries on land. Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917), reaffirmed that “the right to recover for injuries sustained upon the navigable waters” is governed by the place of injury. The same principle controls the statutory extension of federal law to the outer shelf.

Congress enacted the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act against this settled background. United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19, 38-39 (1947), recognized that the United States possesses “paramount rights” only over the submerged lands themselves. When Congress chose to apply the Longshore Act to shelf operations, it incorporated the traditional requirement that the injury occur at the federal location. See Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149, 160 (1920) (Congress may not “destroy the independence of the states” by extending federal remedies beyond defined waters without clear textual warrant).

The same situs rule appears in every pre-1970 decision construing the Longshore Act. Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244, 247 (1941), limited coverage to injuries “upon the navigable waters.” Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114, 126 (1962), reiterated that the Act applies only where the injury occurs on those waters. Davis v. Department of Labor & Industries, 317 U.S. 249, 252-53 (1942), likewise treated the place of injury as the controlling factor. No decision before 1970 permitted recovery for an injury sustained on land.

Statutes in derogation of state workers’ compensation schemes receive strict construction. See Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co., 264 U.S. 219, 228 (1924). Section 1333(b) therefore cannot be read to reach injuries occurring hundreds of feet inland merely because the employee performed some duties on the shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test finds no support in the statutory text or in any pre-1970 authority.

The judgment of the Benefits Review Board should be affirmed.
