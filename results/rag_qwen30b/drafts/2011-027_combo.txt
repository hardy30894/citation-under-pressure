**ARGUMENT**

The constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. This principle is firmly rooted in the equal footing doctrine, which mandates that when a state enters the Union, it does so with the same rights and powers as the original states, including title to the beds of navigable rivers. The question before this Court is not whether a river is navigable today, nor whether it was navigable at some later point in history, but whether it was navigable *at the time of statehood*. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled constitutional standard, and its judgment must be affirmed.

The equal footing doctrine is not a mere matter of state law; it is a constitutional imperative. As this Court held in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 801, 805 (1931), “the title to the bed of a navigable river, when the state was admitted into the Union, passed to the state.” This transfer of title is not contingent upon the state’s assertion of ownership or the existence of a contemporaneous state law; it is automatic and arises from the federal Constitution itself. The Court in *Utah* emphasized that the state’s title to navigable riverbeds is “vested in the State of Utah” by virtue of its admission into the Union, and that the federal government’s title to non-navigable riverbeds is not affected. The same principle applies to Montana, which became a state in 1889. The question is not whether the river was navigable in 1900 or 1950, but whether it was navigable in 1889.

This Court has long recognized that the test for navigability for title purposes is historical and factual. In *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 384 (1941), the Court stated that “the question of navigability is one of fact,” and that “the determination of whether a stream is navigable is to be made by reference to the condition of the stream at the time of statehood.” The Court further explained that “the test is whether the stream was navigable in fact at the time of statehood, and not whether it is navigable now.” This standard is not subject to change based on modern usage or engineering modifications. The navigability of a river for title purposes is a fixed historical fact, determined by the condition of the stream at the moment of statehood.

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this standard. It relied on historical evidence—newspapers, maps, and reports from the late 19th century—to determine that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable in 1889. The State’s expert, the State Archivist, submitted authenticated historical documents, including periodicals and official records, which were admissible under the Federal Rules of Evidence as self-authenticating documents. As this Court held in *Green Bay & Mississippi Canal Co. v. Patten Paper Co.*, 172 U.S. 58, 64 (1898), “the right to the use of the water power created by a dam, canal, and other improvements, owned and operated by the United States, arises under and by virtue of certain alleged and recited acts of Congress.” While that case dealt with federal rights, it confirms that historical facts—such as the use of a river for transportation—are determinative of legal rights. The same logic applies to state title: if a river was used for transportation in 1889, it was navigable for title purposes.

The dissent’s argument that the State must prove navigability *now* is contrary to precedent. In *United States v. West Virginia*, 295 U.S. 463, 470 (1935), the Court stated that “the New and Kanawha Rivers throughout West Virginia constitute navigable waters of the United States,” and that this determination was based on their condition at the time of statehood. The Court did not inquire whether the rivers were navigable in 1935. Similarly, in *Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Commission*, 274 U.S. 651, 657 (1927), the Court held that “the rights of the state and of riparian owners in navigable waters within the state and to the soil beneath are matters of state law to be determined by the statutes and judicial decisions of the state.” But the Court also recognized that the state’s title to the riverbed is derived from the federal Constitution, not from state law alone. The state’s title is not created by statute; it is conferred by the equal footing doctrine at the moment of statehood.

The Court has also rejected the notion that navigability for title purposes is a matter of modern engineering or economic feasibility. In *Grand River Dam Authority v. Grand-Hydro*, 335 U.S. 359, 364 (1949), the Court observed that “the Grand River is treated as a nonnavigable stream,” but noted that “the question of navigability is not to be determined by the present condition of the stream, but by its condition at the time of statehood.” The Court emphasized that “the rights of the state to the bed of a navigable river are not dependent upon the state’s use of the river at any later time.” This principle is consistent with the equal footing doctrine, which is not a grant of power but a recognition of a pre-existing right.

Finally, the Court has consistently held that the federal government’s power to regulate navigable waters does not extinguish state title. In *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222, 225 (1956), the Court stated that “the United States may condemn land for public use, but the compensation must be just, and the value of the land as a site for hydroelectric power operations must be considered.” The Court did not suggest that the federal government could claim title to riverbeds that were already owned by the state.
