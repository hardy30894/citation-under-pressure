# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 355(f) Confines Judicial Review to Final Decisions on the Merits, Not Discretionary Refusals to Reopen.

Section 355(f) of the Railroad Retirement Act authorizes circuit court review only of a "final decision of the Board." 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). That language does not sweep in every order the Board issues; it reaches only the decision that finally disposes of a claim on its merits after the claimant has exhausted the administrative appeals the statute affords him. Congress could have written a broader provision reaching "any decision" or "any order," but it did not. Where an agency's organic statute limits review to "final" action, courts must respect that limitation rather than expand it to reach subsequent, discretionary agency responses to a claimant's request that the agency reconsider a matter it has already conclusively decided. See Federal Communications Comm'n v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co., 309 U.S. 134, 143 (1940) (explaining that "the very purpose" of a statutory finality requirement is to channel judicial review to the point at which the agency has completed its statutory task, and that courts may not "displac[e] the primary judgment of the [agency]" by expanding review beyond that point).

Salinas's 2006 application became final on February 9, 2007, when he failed to pursue further administrative appeal. That was the final decision under § 355(f), and Salinas could have sought review of it at that time. He did not. What he obtained in 2015 was not a redetermination of his 2006 claim on the merits; it was the Board's discretionary refusal, under its own regulations, to disturb a determination that had already become final years earlier. See 20 C.F.R. § 261.2 (limiting reopening to a four-year window absent new and material evidence or administrative error). A decision declining to exercise that discretionary grace is analytically distinct from — and does not revive — the original final decision.

## II. This Court's Construction of the Analogous Social Security Act Provision Confirms That a Refusal to Reopen Is Not a Reviewable Final Decision.

The Railroad Retirement Act's judicial-review provision was drawn from the same mold as the review provision of the Social Security Act, and the courts that first construed the Social Security Act's parallel language correctly held that an agency's refusal to reopen a final benefits determination is not itself a reviewable "final decision." In Cappadora v. Celebrezze, 356 F.2d 1 (2d Cir. 1966), Judge Friendly held that the Secretary's denial of a petition to reopen a final determination is not a "final decision . . . made after a hearing" within the meaning of the statute, because reopening exists as a matter of administrative grace rather than statutory entitlement. As the court explained, to treat every refusal to reopen as reviewable would effectively "nullify" the statute's time limits on seeking review of the original determination, since a claimant could indefinitely resurrect a stale claim simply by asking the agency to reconsider it and then appealing the refusal. Id. at 5–6. The same reasoning obtains here: Salinas's 2015 reopening request was, in substance, an attempt to resurrect a 2006 claim that had already become unreviewable years before.

The Ninth Circuit reached the identical conclusion under the same Social Security Act provision in Filice v. Celebrezze, 319 F.2d 443 (9th Cir. 1963), holding that the Secretary's discretionary refusal to reopen a prior, final determination did not constitute a decision "made after a hearing" subject to judicial review. The court recognized that Congress's finality requirement was designed to protect the integrity of the administrative process by ensuring that determinations, once final, remain final absent the narrow, discretionary reopening the agency itself chooses to allow. Id. at 445.

Because the Railroad Retirement Act's review provision was patterned on this same statutory model, and because the Board's own regulations governing reopening mirror the discretionary, grace-based reopening procedure at issue in Cappadora and Filice, this Court should apply the same construction here. A discretionary refusal to reopen a claim that became final in 2007 is not a "final decision" within the meaning of § 355(f).

## III. Szostak Does Not Support a Contrary Result.

Petitioner will likely invoke Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), which held that a Board refusal to reopen was reviewable. But Szostak stands as an outlier even within its own circuit: the same year it was decided, the Second Circuit in Cappadora construed the materially identical language of the Social Security Act to preclude review of an analogous refusal to reopen. The two decisions cannot be reconciled on the theory that Congress intended sister statutes, modeled on one another and serving the same administrative purpose, to carry opposite finality rules depending only on which agency happens to administer them. Cappadora's reasoning — that treating reopening refusals as reviewable would undermine the statutory scheme's finality and time limits — is the sounder construction, and it should guide this Court's reading of § 355(f).

## CONCLUSION

The Board's discretionary refusal to reopen Salinas's long-final 2006 claim is not a "final decision" reviewable under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The judgment below should be affirmed.
