## ARGUMENT

### I. The district court erred by holding DISH liable on calls that were not made “using” an artificial or prerecorded voice within the meaning of the TCPA.

The TCPA prohibits making a call to a cellular telephone “using any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice” absent prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). Statutory text matters. “Using” means employing the prohibited technology as part of the call; it does not mean that a caller is liable whenever a prerecorded sound is merely present somewhere in the dialing process. See, e.g., Mims v. Arrow Financial Services, LLC, 565 U.S. 368, 372-73 (2012) (describing the TCPA’s structure and private right of action); Facebook, Inc. v. Duguid, 592 U.S. 395, 401-02 (2021) (reaffirming that the TCPA’s text must be read according to its ordinary meaning).

Here, the district court held DISH liable on the theory that its Cisco Dialer “admitted” to using a prerecorded voice because the system could play a prerecorded message when it detected a “positive voice.” But that overreads the record and the statute. The relevant question is whether DISH actually made the seven calls “using” a prerecorded voice in violation of § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). A device that merely detects a human voice or voicemail greeting and then permits a message to be played does not itself establish that every such call was made using an unlawful prerecorded voice. See Saunders v. Dyck O’Neal, Inc., 319 F. Supp. 3d 907, 911-12 (E.D. Va. 2018) (holding that TCPA liability requires proof that the call employed the prohibited technology); Gragg v. Gamma Telcom Services, Inc., 995 F.3d 237, 241-42 (5th Cir. 2021) (TCPA claims turn on the statutory elements actually proved).

The district court effectively imposed strict liability from the mere existence of prerecorded content in a call platform. That is inconsistent with the statute’s command and with the Supreme Court’s caution that courts should not expand the TCPA beyond its text. See Facebook, Inc. v. Duguid, 592 U.S. at 402-03. At minimum, summary judgment was improper as to the four disputed calls because the record required a factfinder to determine whether a prerecorded voice was in fact used on those calls, rather than inferred from generalized device capability. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248-52 (1986); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986).

### II. Ybarra lacked prior express consent because the consent he sought to enforce was given by a different person for a different subscriber.

Even if the Cisco Dialer could implicate the TCPA, the district court still erred because the undisputed evidence shows DISH had prior express consent to call the number from the original customer. Under the FCC’s longstanding interpretation, a caller may rely on prior express consent from the subscriber who provided the number, and consent is tied to that number unless and until it is revoked or superseded. See In re Rules & Regulations Implementing the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991, 23 F.C.C. Rcd. 559, 564-65 ¶¶ 10-11 (2008); Meyer v. Portfolio Recovery Associates, LLC, 707 F.3d 1036, 1043-44 (9th Cir. 2012).

DISH obtained consent from the then-current customer in June 2012 to contact the 3475 number and to use that number to collect unpaid amounts. That consent was valid when given. The district court’s ruling effectively transformed a caller’s lawful reliance on consent into liability merely because the number was later reassigned. But the TCPA does not impose strict liability for calls made to a reassigned number when the caller previously obtained consent from the subscriber. See Osorio v. State Farm Bank, F.S.B., 746 F.3d 1242, 1254-55 (11th Cir. 2014) (consent analysis turns on the status of the called party and the consent given); N.L. by Lemos v. Credit One Bank, N.A., 960 F.3d 1164, 1169-70 (9th Cir. 2020) (recognizing the importance of reassigned-number issues under the TCPA).

At the very least, whether DISH had a reasonable basis to believe the number remained associated with the consenting customer was a factual issue that precluded summary judgment on the challenged calls. See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 248-52.

### III. The judgment should be reversed as to the four disputed calls.

DISH concedes liability on three calls. But the remaining four calls were decided on an erroneous legal premise: that the Cisco Dialer’s prerecorded-message capability, standing alone, established a TCPA violation, regardless of whether DISH actually used a prerecorded voice on those calls or had consent to contact the number. Because summary judgment may be entered only when “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact” and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a); Anderson, 477 U.S. at 247-48, the judgment on those four calls should be reversed and rendered in DISH’s favor. If the Court concludes any factual issue remains, the proper disposition is remand for trial on the disputed calls. See Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322-23.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment insofar as it imposes liability on the four disputed calls and render judgment for DISH on those calls, or at minimum remand for further proceedings.
