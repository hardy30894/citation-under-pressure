**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Homero Meruelo failed to establish a “bona fide indebtedness” running directly to him from Merco of the Palm Beaches, Inc., under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1) and Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The record demonstrates that Meruelo made actual economic outlays to Merco through a series of structured, contemporaneous, and documented intercompany transfers, all of which were consistently treated as loans by the parties and their tax preparer. The Tax Court’s rigid application of a “directness” test that ignores the substance of the transactions and the parties’ intent contravenes settled principles of tax law and misapplies binding precedent.

**I. The Tax Court Misapplied the “Bona Fide Indebtedness” Standard by Failing to Recognize the Substance of the Transactions**

To qualify for basis under § 1366(d)(1), a shareholder must establish a “bona fide indebtedness” that “runs directly” to him. *See* Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). However, the “directness” requirement does not demand that the debt be evidenced by a single promissory note from Merco to Meruelo. Rather, the Supreme Court has long held that “the substance of the transaction, not its form, controls for tax purposes.” *See* *Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co.*, 348 U.S. 426, 431 (1955). The IRS may not disregard a valid economic relationship simply because the debt was channeled through intermediate entities.

Here, Meruelo’s $9 million in claimed debt basis arose from a series of intercompany transfers between Merco and affiliated entities in which Meruelo held an ownership interest. These transfers were not mere “gifts” or “capital contributions,” but were structured as repayable advances. The record shows that Merco’s affiliates paid over $15 million in expenses on Merco’s behalf from 2004 to 2008, while Merco repaid less than $6 million. The net result—over $9 million in unpaid obligations—was consistently reflected in Merco’s books as accounts payable to affiliates. *See* *Henderson v. Commissioner*, 137 T.C. 145, 155 (2011) (holding that “the existence of a debt is determined by the substance of the transaction, including the intent of the parties and the economic reality”).

Moreover, the Tax Court’s conclusion that Meruelo failed to make an “actual economic outlay” is legally untenable. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a taxpayer must personally disburse funds to establish basis. *See* *Barnes v. Commissioner*, 303 U.S. 197, 202 (1938) (a taxpayer may establish basis through “a contribution of money or property to the corporation, or through a loan to it, whether made directly or indirectly”). Meruelo’s $4.985 million capital contribution was made directly to Merco via Akoya, and the remainder of the $9 million was funded through a network of affiliated entities that acted as conduits for Meruelo’s economic support. The fact that the funds passed through third parties does not negate the economic reality that Meruelo bore the risk of loss and provided financial support to Merco.

**II. The Tax Court Erred in Disregarding the Consistent Treatment of the Debt as a Loan**

The Tax Court failed to give proper weight to the consistent treatment of the intercompany obligations as loans. The record shows that Luis Carreras, a certified public accountant with decades of experience, prepared the tax returns for Merco and its affiliates. Carreras consistently reported Merco’s net accounts payable to affiliates as “shareholder loans” on Merco’s tax returns and allocated those amounts to Meruelo based on his ownership interests. *See* *Henderson*, 137 T.C. at 156 (a taxpayer’s consistent treatment of a transaction as a loan is strong evidence of its nature). This treatment was not a one-time accounting choice but a recurring practice over five years, reflecting a clear intent to treat the obligations as debt.

Furthermore, in March 2004, Carreras drafted a promissory note purporting to establish a $10 million unsecured line of credit from Meruelo to Merco at 6% interest. While the note may not have been executed, its existence and use in annual tax filings demonstrate the parties’ intent to treat the relationship as a loan. *See* *Barnes*, 303 U.S. at 202 (intent to create a debt is a key factor in determining its existence). The Tax Court’s refusal to consider this documentation as evidence of a bona fide debt ignores the principle that “a taxpayer’s intent and the manner in which he treats a transaction for tax purposes are relevant to determining its character.” *See* *Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co.*, 348 U.S. at 431.

**III. The Tax Court’s “Directness” Test Is Misapplied and Contrary to Precedent**

The Tax Court’s insistence on “directness” as a strict legal requirement—requiring a single, unbroken chain from Meruelo to Merco—misreads the regulatory framework. *See* Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i) (a debt “runs directly” to the shareholder if it is “a debt owed by the corporation to the shareholder”).
