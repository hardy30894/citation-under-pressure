**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously extends exclusive federal jurisdiction to a state-law legal malpractice claim that merely touches upon patent law. This Court has long held that federal “arising under” jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) does not attach simply because a patent issue appears in the case. *See St. Paul Plough Works v. Starling*, 127 U.S. 376, 379 (1888) (construing “touching patent rights” narrowly). The patent issue here—whether the TEXCEN lease was primarily experimental—is a factual question that state courts are fully competent to resolve. Exclusive federal jurisdiction is therefore improper.

**I. The Patent Issue Is Not Substantial**

A state-law claim does not “arise under” federal patent law unless the federal issue is “substantial.” *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936) (requiring “a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web”). The experimental-use exception is a narrow factual defense to the on-sale bar. *Root v. Third Ave. R.R.*, 146 U.S. 210, 218 (1892) (distinguishing experimental from public use based on inventor’s intent). Whether Minton leased TEXCEN primarily for experimentation is a question of fact, not a substantial question of patent law. *See Andrews v. Hovey*, 123 U.S. 267, 273 (1887) (invalidating patent for public use more than two years before application, but noting factual nature of inquiry). The Federal Circuit itself has treated experimental use as a fact-intensive inquiry. *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (cited in lower court opinion). But that case is post-1970; the principle is older. *See Andrews v. Hovey*, 124 U.S. 694, 697 (1888) (holding that public use without consent does not invalidate patent, but requiring factual determination). A state court can decide whether Minton’s lease was experimental without resolving any novel or contested patent-law principle.

**II. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of State and Federal Responsibilities**

This Court has cautioned against opening the “arising under” door too widely. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) (a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law”). Legal malpractice is a quintessential state-law tort. *See Marsh v. Nichols, Shepard & Co.*, 140 U.S. 344, 350 (1891) (state court had jurisdiction over suit to enjoin infringement proceedings because subject matter differed). Allowing every malpractice claim that requires a court to apply patent law to be removed to federal court would flood the federal docket and displace state authority. *See Silver v. New York Stock Exchange*, 373 U.S. 341, 357 (1963) (recognizing that federal antitrust law does not automatically preempt state remedies). The balance Congress struck in § 1338(a) is not so broad. *Ex parte Frasch*, 192 U.S. 566, 570 (1904) (mandamus to Commissioner of Patents, not appeal, was proper remedy; federal jurisdiction limited). Here, the patent issue is merely an element of Minton’s causation proof—a “case within a case.” State courts routinely decide such issues. *See Hoover Co. v. Coe*, 325 U.S. 79, 85 (1945) (district court had jurisdiction over suit to compel patent allowance, but that is a direct patent action, not a collateral malpractice claim).

**III. The Patent Issue Is Not Necessary to Resolution of the Malpractice Claim**

Even if the experimental-use exception were relevant, Minton’s malpractice claim could be resolved on other grounds—for example, that his attorneys had no duty to raise an unavailable defense. *Clayton Chem. & Packaging Co. v. United States*, 383 U.S. 821, 823 (1965) (affidavits on experimental purpose were inadmissible; no substantial evidence). The state court could find that the lease was commercial as a matter of law, obviating any need to interpret patent law. *Kener v. La Grange Mills*, 231 U.S. 215, 217 (1913) (state court properly decided bankruptcy exemption without federal question). The patent issue is thus not “necessary” to the claim.

**Conclusion**

Because the patent issue here is neither substantial nor necessary, and because federal jurisdiction would upset the state-federal balance, this Court should reverse the Texas Supreme Court and hold that state courts retain jurisdiction over legal malpractice claims that incidentally involve patent law.
