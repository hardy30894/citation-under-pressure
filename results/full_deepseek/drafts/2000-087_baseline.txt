**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Language and Structure of § 1231(a)(6) Do Not Authorize Indefinite Detention.**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), provides that certain removable aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” It does not specify a maximum duration. Read in isolation, the phrase “may be detained” could be seen as conferring discretion without temporal limit. But this Court has long held that statutes should not be interpreted to produce “absurd” or “constitutionally problematic” results absent a clear statement from Congress. *See United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 200 (1957) (construing a detainer statute to avoid indefinite detention).

The structure of § 1231(a) confirms that Congress intended detention to serve a narrow purpose: ensuring the alien’s presence during the removal period and, if removal is not immediately accomplished, during a reasonable period thereafter. The 90-day removal period is the primary mechanism for removal. Section 1231(a)(6) is a supplementary provision, not a grant of authority for permanent incarceration. As this Court recognized in *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 689 (2001), the statute’s “basic purpose” is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, that purpose evaporates, and continued detention serves no statutory end.

**II. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires a Limiting Construction.**

Even if the statute were ambiguous, this Court must construe it to avoid serious constitutional questions. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Indefinite detention of a resident alien who cannot be removed raises grave concerns under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. This Court has repeatedly held that “[f]reedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that Clause protects.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690.

The government’s interpretation—that the statute permits indefinite detention so long as “good faith efforts” to remove continue—would authorize potentially permanent confinement. That result would be constitutionally suspect. As *Zadvydas* held, “the statute, read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal from the United States.” *Id.* at 699. The Court further stated that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” *Id.* at 703.

This approach follows the established rule that “every reasonable construction must be resorted to, in order to save a statute from unconstitutionality.” *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988). The government’s reading would require the Court to confront a serious constitutional question—whether the Fifth Amendment permits the indefinite civil detention of a resident alien who poses no threat to national security and has committed no new crime. The canon of avoidance compels the Court to adopt the limiting construction instead.

**III. Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Violates Substantive Due Process.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall be “deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” This guarantee applies to all persons within the United States, including aliens lawfully admitted. *See Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67, 77 (1976). The liberty interest at stake here is fundamental. *See Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (indefinite detention of a person not dangerous violates due process).

The government’s asserted interest in detaining Zadvydas is to prevent flight and ensure removal. But when removal is not reasonably foreseeable, that interest becomes speculative. As *Zadvydas* explained, “the choice is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large.’ It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” 533 U.S. at 701. Supervised release adequately protects the government’s interests while respecting the alien’s liberty.

Moreover, the statute’s application is not limited to a narrow class of dangerous individuals. It applies to a broad range of removable aliens, including those who overstayed a tourist visa. *Id.* at 699. Indefinite detention of such persons without individualized findings of dangerousness or flight risk would transform the immigration detention system into a tool of preventive punishment, in violation of the Due Process Clause. *See Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997) (civil commitment requires a showing of dangerousness and mental illness).

**IV. The Six-Month Presumption Provides a Workable Standard.**

This Court in *Zadvydas* adopted a “presumptively reasonable” six-month period of post-removal detention. 533 U.S. at 701. After that period, the alien bears the burden of providing “good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future.” *Id.* at 701. The government then must rebut that showing. This standard protects both the government’s interest in effectuating removal and the alien’s right to be free from arbitrary detention.

Applying that standard here, Zadvydas had been detained for over three years beyond the removal period—far longer than the six-month presumption. He provided evidence that Germany, Lithuania, and other countries had refused to accept him. The government offered no evidence that removal was likely in the foreseeable future. As the Fifth Circuit recognized on remand, Zadvydas “has provided good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 285 F.3d 398, 400 (5th Cir. 2002). His continued detention is therefore unauthorized.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed, and the district court’s order releasing Zadvydas on supervised conditions should stand.
