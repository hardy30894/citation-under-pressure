**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment, as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest in ensuring safe access to reproductive health care facilities, and leaves ample alternative channels for communication. This Court’s precedent in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 120 S.Ct. 2480, 147 L.Ed.2d 597 (2000), squarely governs this case and forecloses the petitioners’ challenge. The petitioners’ attempt to re-litigate the facial constitutionality of the statute—now barred by the law of the case doctrine—is meritless. Moreover, their as-applied claims fail because they have not demonstrated that the buffer zone prevents them from engaging in effective speech. The judgment below must be affirmed.

### I. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY APPLIED HILL V. COLORADO AND THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE

This Court has long recognized that “the right to speak does not include the right to speak at all times and places.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. In *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado law prohibiting any person from approaching within 100 feet of a healthcare facility entrance to engage in “persuasive” speech directed at patients or staff. Id. at 716, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Court emphasized that such areas are “quintessential public forums,” yet still found the restriction constitutional because it was content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and narrowly tailored to protect patient safety and free access to medical services. Id. at 715–16, 120 S.Ct. 2480.

Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law is indistinguishable in substance and purpose. Like the Colorado statute, it is content-neutral: it applies equally to all speech regardless of message. It is viewpoint-neutral: no distinction is made between pro-life and pro-choice speakers. And it is narrowly tailored to serve the compelling state interest in protecting the physical safety and privacy of individuals seeking reproductive health care. As the First Circuit observed, the law “protects the rights of prospective patients and clinic employees without offending the First Amendment rights of others.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 181 (1st Cir.2009). The Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that such regulations are permissible when they do not burden more speech than necessary. *See, e.g., Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 798–99, 109 S.Ct. 2746, 105 L.Ed.2d 661 (1989) (upholding time, place, and manner restrictions that leave open ample alternative channels).

The petitioners argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled. But this Court has never retreated from *Hill*, and there is no basis for doing so now. The decision rests on sound principles of First Amendment jurisprudence: the government may regulate the *manner* of speech in public spaces where competing interests—such as personal safety and access to medical care—must be balanced. To overturn *Hill* would undermine decades of settled law and invite chaos at clinics nationwide. The Court has consistently declined to revisit *Hill* when presented with similar challenges. *See, e.g., Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155, 165, 135 S.Ct. 2218, 192 L.Ed.2d 236 (2015) (reaffirming *Hill*’s framework); *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 454, 131 S.Ct. 1207, 179 L.Ed.2d 172 (2011) (noting *Hill*’s enduring validity). The petitioners offer no compelling reason to depart from this precedent.

### II. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RELITIGATION OF FACIAL CHALLENGES

The petitioners’ claim that the First Circuit erred in refusing to reconsider the facial constitutionality of the Act is foreclosed by the law of the case doctrine. Under this well-established principle, “a court is bound by its prior decisions in the same case unless there is a compelling reason to depart.” *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir.2011). The First Circuit previously upheld the Act’s facial constitutionality in *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176–81, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari, thereby confirming the correctness of that ruling. *McCullen v. Coakley*, — U.S. —, 130 S.Ct. 1881, 176 L.Ed.2d 362 (2010).

The petitioners seek to circumvent this doctrine by invoking recent cases like *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 130 S.Ct. 876, 175 L.Ed.2d 753 (2010), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, — U.S. —, 131 S.Ct. 2653, 180 L.Ed.2d 544 (2011). But these cases involve entirely different contexts—campaign finance and data use—not the regulation of protest near medical facilities. The petitioners’ argument amounts to a mere assertion of “new” First Amendment principles, which is insufficient to justify overturning a prior appellate decision. *Ahmed v. Holder*, 611 F.3d 90, 98 (1st Cir.2010) (“Appellate arguments advanced in a perfunctory manner, unaccompanied by citations to relevant authority, are deemed waived.”). No controlling legal authority has changed in a way that warrants revisiting *McCullen I*.
