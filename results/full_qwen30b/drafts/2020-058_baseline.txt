**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is one of statutory interpretation, and the answer must be grounded in the text, structure, context, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That interpretation distorts the meaning of “admission,” conflates status with entry, and undermines the carefully calibrated framework Congress established for adjusting immigration status. We respectfully submit that a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission, and that the District Court’s contrary conclusion must be reversed.

**I. THE TEXT OF THE INA CLEARLY DISTINGUISHES “ADMISSION” FROM “LAWFUL STATUS”**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive and exclusive. It requires both physical entry and formal inspection and authorization. As this Court has held, “admission” is a “physical event” involving entry into the country, not a mere change in legal status. *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). The grant of TPS, by contrast, does not involve any such entry or inspection. Indeed, § 1254a explicitly requires that an alien be “present in the United States” to qualify for TPS. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Thus, TPS is not a mechanism of entry but a form of relief for those already present.

Appellees’ argument—that being “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” under § 1254a(f)(4) equates to admission—is legally untenable. The statute does not say that TPS recipients are “admitted”; it says they are “considered” in lawful status. The distinction is not semantic—it is constitutional in significance. *See* *Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”). Congress used the term “admission” in § 1255(a) and “lawful status” in § 1254a(f)(4), and it did so deliberately. To equate the two is to ignore the plain meaning of the text.

**II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE INA REINFORCES THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN ADMISSION AND STATUS**

The structure of the INA confirms that “admission” and “status” are separate and independent concepts. Section 1255(a) sets a threshold requirement: only those who were “inspected and admitted or paroled” may adjust status. Section 1255(c)(2) bars adjustment for those who “failed to maintain continuously a lawful status since entry,” and § 1255(k)(1) explicitly requires that the alien be “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission” to qualify for adjustment. These provisions treat “admission” and “lawful status” as distinct prerequisites.

If a grant of TPS were deemed an admission, then § 1255(k)(1) and § 1255(c)(2) would be rendered superfluous. Under Appellees’ theory, any TPS recipient—regardless of how they entered—would satisfy the “lawful admission” requirement, thereby eliminating the need for the statutory exceptions and bars. That would violate the cardinal principle that “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). Congress did not intend for TPS to bypass the admission requirement; it created a separate, more limited path for adjustment through § 1254a(h), which requires a supermajority of the Senate to pass legislation adjusting TPS recipients’ status. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h)(2). To read § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255(a)’s admission requirement would effectively circumvent that deliberate, high bar.

**III. THE PURPOSE OF TPS REQUIRES A LIMITED, TEMPORARY RELIEF, NOT A PATH TO PERMANENT RESIDENCE**

The purpose of TPS is not to provide a route to permanent residence but to shield individuals already in the United States from removal during extraordinary conditions in their home countries. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The program is “limited, temporary” relief. *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 963 (9th Cir. 2017). To interpret § 1254a(f)(4) as conferring admission would transform TPS from a temporary stay into a de facto permanent status, contrary to Congress’s intent.

Appellees argue that requiring TPS recipients who entered illegally to reenter lawfully would undermine TPS’s purpose, especially if their home countries remain unsafe. But that concern is misplaced. TPS recipients may remain in the United States indefinitely so long as the Secretary extends TPS for their country. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(c). They are not required to leave. The fact that they cannot adjust status under § 1255 does not force them to depart; it merely means they cannot become lawful permanent residents unless they meet the statutory prerequisites, including admission. Congress has not provided a shortcut for those who entered without inspection.
