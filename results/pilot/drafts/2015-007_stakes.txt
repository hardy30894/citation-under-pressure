**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a jury need not unanimously find aggravating circumstances or determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 122 S.Ct. 2428, 153 L.Ed.2d 556 (2002), held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the existence of aggravating factors that increase the penalty for a crime beyond the statutory maximum. However, *Ring* did not require that such findings be unanimous, nor did it mandate that a jury determine a defendant’s mental retardation as a threshold bar to execution. The Florida Supreme Court correctly interpreted *Ring* and its progeny to permit a non-unanimous jury recommendation and a judicial determination of mental retardation, consistent with long-standing precedent and the constitutional framework of capital sentencing.

**I. The Florida Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Sixth Amendment as Clarified by *Ring***

*Ring* did not transform every fact that increases a defendant’s sentence into a “element” requiring jury determination. Rather, the Court held that “the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find any fact on which the maximum penalty depends.” *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 589, 122 S.Ct. 2428. The Court emphasized that the Arizona statute allowed the judge to impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances that were not required to be submitted to the jury. Id. at 592, 122 S.Ct. 2428. The Court concluded that because those aggravating factors were “essential to the imposition of the death penalty,” they were “elements” of the offense and thus subject to jury determination. Id. at 602, 122 S.Ct. 2428.

This holding was limited to the specific procedural structure of Arizona’s capital sentencing scheme. The Court did not hold that every capital sentencing scheme must require jury unanimity or that a jury must determine every factual predicate for the death penalty. Indeed, in *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 355, 136 S.Ct. 616, 193 L.Ed.2d 504 (2016), the Supreme Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme—under which the judge, not the jury, determines aggravating circumstances—violated the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s advisory role was not sufficient to satisfy the constitutional requirement that a jury find the facts necessary for the death penalty. *Hurst*, 577 U.S. at 364, 136 S.Ct. 616. The Court did not, however, address the issue of jury unanimity or the determination of mental retardation. The Court’s opinion in *Hurst* was limited to the fact that the judge, not the jury, made the critical factual findings necessary to impose the death penalty. Id. at 364, 136 S.Ct. 616.

The Florida Supreme Court correctly distinguished *Hurst* from the present case. In *Hurst*, the jury had no role in determining the aggravating circumstances. Here, the jury was tasked with making a recommendation of death, and it did so by a seven-to-five vote. The jury was instructed on the aggravating circumstances and was permitted to consider them in reaching its recommendation. The trial court, not the jury, made the final determination of whether the aggravating circumstances applied. But the jury’s recommendation was not advisory in the sense that it was ignored; rather, it was a critical component of the sentencing process. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a jury’s recommendation in capital cases is constitutionally significant, even when the final decision rests with the judge. See *Spencer v. State*, 476 So.2d 409, 414 (Fla.1985) (holding that a jury’s recommendation is “an essential part of the sentencing process”).

The Sixth Amendment does not require that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances. The Court has never held that unanimity is constitutionally required. In *Hurst*, the Court did not require unanimity, nor did it suggest that a non-unanimous jury recommendation violates the Sixth Amendment. The Court’s focus was on the role of the jury in making factual findings, not on the degree of agreement among jurors. The Florida Supreme Court’s interpretation of *Ring* and *Hurst* is consistent with the Court’s precedent and does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

**II. The Florida Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, but it does not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 122 S.Ct. 2242, 153 L.Ed.2d 335 (2002), the Court held that executing mentally retarded individuals violates the Eighth Amendment. The Court emphasized that the determination of mental retardation is a legal question, not a factual one, and that states are free to define the criteria for mental retardation. Id. at 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242. The Court did not require that a jury make this determination. Rather, it left the method of enforcement to the states.

Florida’s three-pronged test for mental retardation—subaverage intellectual functioning, adaptive deficits, and onset before age eighteen—is consistent with *Atkins*. The burden of proof is on the defendant to establish mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. § 921.137(4), Fla. Stat. (2012). This standard is not constitutionally infirm. The Supreme Court has upheld the use of clear and convincing evidence in capital cases. See *Tennessee v. Lane*, 541 U.S. 509, 534, 124 S.Ct. 1956, 158 L.Ed.2d 820 (2004) (upholding clear and convincing standard in civil rights cases).
