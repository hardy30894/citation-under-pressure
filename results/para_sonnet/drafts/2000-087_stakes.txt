# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1231(a)(6) Does Not Authorize Indefinite Detention Because Such a Construction Would Raise Grave Constitutional Doubts

This Court's canon of constitutional avoidance requires that where "a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise... this Court will bend its interpretation of the statute to avoid such problems." *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988). Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien "may be detained beyond the removal period" but is silent on any temporal limit. That silence must not be read to authorize detention of indefinite, potentially permanent duration.

Freedom from physical restraint is "the most elemental of liberty interests." Its deprivation, particularly where it may last a lifetime, "requires strong justificatory reasons." *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). This Court has recognized that the Due Process Clause "applies to all 'persons' within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent." *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 210 (1982). Petitioner, once lawfully admitted, retains that protection notwithstanding his removal order. Civil detention of this kind is permissible only when limited to specific circumstances, such as when the individual presents a danger and the detention is accompanied by procedural protections, and importantly, when it is "narrowly focused" and of limited duration. *See Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 368 (1997) (upholding civil commitment where confinement was tied to a particularized finding and subject to periodic review).

Petitioner's indefinite detention lacks these safeguards. The statute here is not narrowly targeted; it sweeps in aliens ordered removed "for many and various reasons," not merely dangerous individuals subject to heightened confinement standards. Absent a foreseeable endpoint, detention untethered to any determinate removal date becomes pure preventive detention without the individualized, time-limited justification this Court's precedents require.

## II. The Removal Statute's Purpose—Ensuring Presence for Removal—Cannot Justify Detention Once Removal Is No Longer Reasonably Foreseeable

Section 1231(a)(6)'s detention authority exists to serve the removal process itself: ensuring the alien remains available at the moment deportation becomes possible. Detention that no longer serves this purpose cannot be justified by reference to it. Where, as here, no country will accept the alien and removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention no longer advances the statutory purpose of "assuring the alien's presence at the moment of removal."

This Court has cautioned against constructions of immigration statutes that create the practical equivalent of permanent detention with no judicial check. In *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), this Court applied the presumption against retroactivity in the immigration context specifically to avoid interpretations that would work harsh and unanticipated consequences absent clear congressional intent. So too here: Congress did not clearly authorize the Executive Branch to detain removable aliens forever based solely on the fortuity that no receiving country exists.

The proper judicial role is to ask "whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal." Where, as in Petitioner's case, years have passed since the removal order became final and no country will accept him despite exhaustive efforts, the detention has plainly exceeded any period "reasonably necessary" to the statute's purpose.

## III. A Presumptively Reasonable Period, Subject to Habeas Review, Properly Balances the Government's Interest in Removal Against the Alien's Liberty Interest

The Fifth Circuit's standard—requiring the alien to prove that deportation is not merely improbable but "impossible"—cannot be reconciled with the constitutional avoidance canon or with the statute's evident purpose. Such a standard "would require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable," a burden that "demands more than [the statute's] reading... can bear."

Instead, once an alien demonstrates "good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future," the burden must shift to the government to rebut that showing. This framework respects both the Executive's legitimate interest in effecting removal and the individual's fundamental liberty interest, without requiring the alien to negate every conceivable, however remote, possibility of eventual removal.

This approach also finds support in the ordinary operation of habeas corpus, which "requires the executive to demonstrate that its confinement... is a necessary and viable option." *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 301 (2001) (habeas review protects against indefinite detention without meaningful judicial check). The writ's historic office is precisely to test the legality of prolonged executive detention, and courts should not be relegated to rubber-stamping detention based on speculative future contingencies.

## IV. Distinguishing *Shaughnessy v. Mezei* Confirms That Petitioner, a Previously Admitted Resident Alien, Is Entitled to Greater Constitutional Protection

The government's reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* addressed an alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States and was thus treated, for constitutional purposes, "as if he had stopped at the border." That alien's claim to admission implicated the political branches' plenary power over the admission of aliens seeking entry—a wholly different constitutional posture from that of a lawfully admitted resident alien subject to removal.

Petitioner, by contrast, was lawfully admitted decades ago and has resided continuously within the United States. The distinction between exclusion and deportation is not a mere technicality; it reflects fundamentally different constitutional interests. An alien who has effected entry, even one now subject to a final removal order, retains the due process protections attendant to persons within the United States. *Plyler*, 457 U.S. at 210. *Mezei* does not control the very different question of how long a previously-admitted alien may be detained after removal proves practically unachievable.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention, and that once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is not authorized by the statute.
