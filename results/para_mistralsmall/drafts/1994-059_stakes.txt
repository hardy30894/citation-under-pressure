**ARGUMENT**

**I. Amendment 73 Violates the Exclusive Qualifications Clause of the U.S. Constitution**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly alters the qualifications for federal office set forth in the Constitution. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that no person shall be a Representative who “shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. Similarly, Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 states that no person shall be a Senator who “shall not have attained to the Age of thirty Years, and been nine Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State for which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that these clauses establish the *exclusive* qualifications for congressional office. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person duly elected by the people of a State who meets the constitutional requirements for membership.”); *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 804 (1995) (“[T]he qualifications for service in Congress established in the text of the Constitution are fixed and may not be altered by the States.”).

Arkansas’s attempt to impose additional ballot-access restrictions on candidates for Congress conflicts with this constitutional framework. The Court in *Thornton* expressly rejected the argument that states may circumvent the Qualifications Clauses by regulating ballot access rather than directly altering qualifications. *Id.* at 805 (“The Framers decided that the qualifications of members of Congress were to be fixed in the Constitution and that the States were not endowed with the authority to add to or otherwise vary those qualifications.”). Amendment 73’s ballot-access restriction—denying ballot placement to candidates who have served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate—is, in substance, an additional qualification and thus unconstitutional. *Id.* at 806 (“The fact that the Arkansas provision is framed in terms of a ballot access restriction does not take it out of the category of a qualification.”).

**II. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Declared Amendment 73 Invalid**

The Arkansas Supreme Court properly invalidated Amendment 73 on two independent grounds: (1) it violated the U.S. Constitution’s exclusive qualifications scheme, and (2) it lacked an Enacting Clause as required by the Arkansas Constitution. *See* Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 12-13, 18-19. This Court should affirm on either ground.

First, the Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution prohibit states from imposing additional restrictions on congressional candidates. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 804-05. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does precisely that by barring candidates from appearing on the ballot based on prior service in Congress. This is not a permissible exercise of the state’s reserved powers but an unconstitutional attempt to alter the qualifications for federal office. *Id.* at 806.

Second, the Arkansas Constitution requires that initiated constitutional amendments include an Enacting Clause in the style of “Be It Enacted by the People of the State of Arkansas.” Ark. Const. amend. 7. The absence of this clause in Amendment 73 rendered it procedurally invalid under state law. Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 12-13. This Court should defer to the Arkansas Supreme Court’s interpretation of its own constitution. *See Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1040-41 (1983) (federal courts should not overturn state court interpretations of state law unless they are “plainly erroneous”).

**III. Severability Does Not Save Amendment 73**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly declined to sever the unconstitutional federal provisions from the rest of Amendment 73. Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 20-21. The Court reasoned that the state and federal term-limit provisions were presented to voters as a single, inseparable package. *Id.* This conclusion is consistent with this Court’s precedent on severability. *See, e.g., Ayotte v. Planned Parenthood of N. New Eng.*, 546 U.S. 320, 331 (2006) (severability is inappropriate where the unconstitutional provision is “inescapably linked” to the rest of the statute).

Amendment 73’s ballot title and preamble explicitly lumped state and federal officials together under the rubric of “elected officials,” and the severability clause itself provided no meaningful guidance to voters. Ark. Sup. Ct. Op. at 20. Under these circumstances, the Arkansas Supreme Court did not err in invalidating the entire amendment. *See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750 (1964) (severability depends on whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Arkansas Supreme Court’s judgment invalidating Amendment 73. The ballot-access restrictions on congressional candidates violate the U.S. Constitution’s exclusive qualifications scheme, and the amendment’s procedural defects under Arkansas law further justify its invalidation.
