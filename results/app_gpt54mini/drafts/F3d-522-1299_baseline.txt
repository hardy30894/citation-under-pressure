**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in holding claims 15, 18, 31, 35, 38, 40, and 49 invalid as anticipated. A patentee may obtain the benefit of an earlier filing date under 35 U.S.C. § 120 only if the earlier application satisfies the written-description requirement of 35 U.S.C. § 112. *Ariad Pharms., Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co.*, 598 F.3d 1336, 1351–52 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (en banc); *In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d 527, 537 (C.C.P.A. 1981). But written description does not demand verbatim recitation of the later claim language; it asks whether the original disclosure reasonably conveyed to persons of ordinary skill that the inventor had possession of the later-claimed subject matter as of the filing date. *Ariad*, 598 F.3d at 1351; *Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar*, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563–64 (Fed. Cir. 1991).

Here, the asserted claims are entitled to the February 6, 1997 filing date of the original application because the original disclosure described the very invention later claimed: a vending machine that provides telecommunications access to a customer, including the customer’s interface with the system, payment control, and access circuitry. The district court instead relied on “substantial new matter” added in the 2000 continuation-in-part and treated that later language as the sole basis for construing “customer interface” to include an interface on a customer’s laptop. That was error. When priority is in dispute, claim scope must be determined from the properly supported disclosure, not from unsupported later-added text. *Lockwood v. Am. Airlines, Inc.*, 107 F.3d 1565, 1571–72 (Fed. Cir. 1997); *Cooper Cameron Corp. v. Kvaerner Oilfield Prods., Inc.*, 291 F.3d 1317, 1322 (Fed. Cir. 2002).

The written-description inquiry is claim-by-claim and limitation-by-limitation. *In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d at 537; *Ariad*, 598 F.3d at 1351. A patentee may rely on the original application if it discloses the later-claimed invention “in sufficient detail that one skilled in the art can clearly conclude that the inventor invented the claimed invention as of the filing date.” *Lockwood*, 107 F.3d at 1572; *Regents of Univ. of Cal. v. Eli Lilly & Co.*, 119 F.3d 1559, 1566–67 (Fed. Cir. 1997). The question is not whether the original application used the exact later phrasing “customer interface” or expressly stated that a laptop could serve as the interface. Rather, the question is whether the disclosure as a whole conveyed possession of a vending machine that enabled a customer to connect a laptop to a telecommunications channel through the machine’s control and access architecture. *Vas-Cath*, 935 F.2d at 1563–64.

That standard is satisfied. The claims trace back through a continuous chain to the original application filed in 1997. The original specification already taught the core architecture recited in the asserted claims: a vending machine for vending telecommunications channel access, a payment mechanism, a status indicator, circuitry that determines when the vending transaction is completed, access circuitry connected to an external telecommunications channel, a connector for customer equipment, and a control unit that receives payment information and controls the access circuitry. Those disclosures are not isolated fragments; together they describe the same invention later claimed. See *In re Rasmussen*, 650 F.2d 1212, 1214–15 (C.C.P.A. 1981) (earlier application adequate where disclosure reasonably conveys possession of claimed subject matter); *In re Wright*, 866 F.2d 422, 424–26 (Fed. Cir. 1989).

The district court’s contrary conclusion rested on a legally flawed premise: that the later-added 2000 CIP language was necessary to support the term “customer interface” and therefore the asserted claims could not reach back to 1997. But later-added descriptive material cannot retroactively enlarge the scope of earlier disclosure or create entitlement to an earlier filing date. *Lockwood*, 107 F.3d at 1572; *TurboCare Div. of Demag Delaval Turbomachinery Corp. v. Gen. Elec. Co.*, 264 F.3d 1111, 1118–19 (Fed. Cir. 2001). A continuation-in-part receives the benefit of an earlier filing date only for subject matter actually disclosed in the earlier application. *In re Chu*, 66 F.3d 292, 297 (Fed. Cir. 1995); *Gentry Gallery, Inc. v. Berkline Corp.*, 134 F.3d 1473, 1479–80 (Fed. Cir. 1998). Because the original application supported the asserted claims, the district court should have given those claims the 1997 date.

Once the 1997 priority date is restored, T-Mobile’s anticipation theory fails. Anticipation requires that a single prior art reference disclose each and every claim limitation, expressly or inherently. *Finisar Corp. v. DirecTV Grp., Inc.*, 523 F.3d 1323, 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2008); *Lewmar Marine, Inc. v. Barient, Inc.*, 827 F.2d 744, 747 (Fed. Cir. 1987). T-Mobile relied on the MobileStar Network’s pre-June 15, 1999 public use and sale, but that activity post-dates the 1997 filing date to which the asserted claims are entitled. Prior art under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) cannot anticipate claims that are earlier in time. *In re Katz*, 687 F.2d 450, 455 (C.C.P.A. 1982); *In re Epstein*, 32 F.3d 1559, 1568 (Fed. Cir. 1994).

Because the asserted claims were supported by the original application, they are not invalid under § 102(b) based on the MobileStar Network. The summary judgment should be reversed.
