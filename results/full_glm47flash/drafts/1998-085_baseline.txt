**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CIVIL RIGHTS ACT OF 1991 IMPOSES A HEIGHTENED STANDARD FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES THAT REQUIRES MORE THAN SIMPLY PROVING INTENTIONAL DISCRIMINATION.**

The ADA argues that the district court erred in remanding this case for a trial on punitive damages. While the Civil Rights Act of 1991 authorizes punitive damages when a defendant acts "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual," 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), the accompanying legislative history indicates that the conduct itself must be "egregious" to justify such an award. The House Report on the 1991 Act explicitly states that the statute "demands of plaintiff 'an even higher standard' than simple proof of intentional discrimination." H.R. Rep. No. 101-108, at 12 (1990) (reprinted in 1991 U.S.C.C.A.N. at 70). This language suggests that mere intentional discrimination is insufficient for punitive damages; the conduct must be more blameworthy than the standard required for liability.

**II. SENATOR DOLE’S STATEMENT CONFIRMS THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES ARE RESERVED FOR "EXTRAORDINARILY EGREGIOUS CASES."**

Further, the legislative history clarifies that the availability of punitive damages is not coextensive with liability. Senator Dole stated that "plaintiffs can recover for punitive damages only in 'extraordinarily egregious cases.'" 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). This distinction is critical. The ADA’s conduct, while discriminatory, did not rise to the level of "egregious" or "outrageous" behavior warranting punishment. The district court’s finding of intentional discrimination does not automatically satisfy the statutory requirement for punitive damages. Therefore, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and uphold the district court’s denial of punitive damages.
