**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment and denying habeas relief. The prosecutor’s testimony followed by his continued participation, with the assistant prosecutor then urging the jury to disbelieve Riddle on the basis of that testimony, denied due process.

A prosecutor “is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty . . . whose interest . . . is that justice shall be done.” Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). When a prosecutor becomes a witness, that role collapses. The Fifth Circuit has long held that a prosecutor who testifies on a contested issue must withdraw from further participation. United States v. Treadway, 445 F.2d 141, 144 (5th Cir. 1971). Allowing the same office to continue and to exploit the testimony at summation creates an impermissible appearance that the government’s own credibility is being vouched for by its advocate. See United States v. Navarro, 169 F.3d 228, 236 (5th Cir. 1999).

The error is not harmless. The credibility contest was central: Riddle’s account of consent and self-defense was the only defense, and the State’s rebuttal rested on the prosecutor’s own testimony that he had never threatened Riddle. Once that testimony was introduced, the assistant prosecutor’s closing argument that Riddle “was a liar” because the district attorney had shown it placed the prestige of the prosecutor’s office behind the credibility determination. See United States v. Dorr, 636 F.2d 117, 120 (5th Cir. 1981).

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1), the state-court rejection of this claim was contrary to clearly established federal law as determined by the Supreme Court in Berger and by this Court’s consistent application of the advocate-witness prohibition. The district court therefore erred in denying the writ.
