The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. As the Supreme Court held in Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), interpreting the statute to permit indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns. To avoid these concerns, the Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, beyond which detention is no longer authorized.

The Court's holding in Zadvydas is grounded in the principle that indefinite detention of aliens who have been admitted to the United States but subsequently ordered removed is constitutionally suspect. The Court noted that the statute "does not apply narrowly to 'a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,' ... but broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations." Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 691. This broad application, combined with the lack of a clear time limit on detention, raised concerns about the statute's constitutionality.

To resolve these concerns, the Court established a presumptively reasonable period of detention of six months, after which the government must provide evidence sufficient to rebut an alien's showing that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Id. at 701. The Court emphasized that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by statute." Id. at 699.

In this case, the petitioner, Kestutis Zadvydas, has been detained for an extended period beyond the 90-day removal period, and efforts to deport him have been unsuccessful. Under the framework established by the Supreme Court in Zadvydas, the government must demonstrate that Zadvydas's removal is still reasonably foreseeable. If the government cannot make this showing, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.

The Fifth Circuit's prior decision in this case, which held that the government may detain Zadvydas as long as "good faith efforts to effectuate ... deportation continue" and Zadvydas fails to show that deportation will prove impossible, is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Zadvydas. The Supreme Court rejected this standard, noting that it "would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal-no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable-which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear." Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 702.

In light of the Supreme Court's decision in Zadvydas, the District Court's order granting habeas relief to Zadvydas should be affirmed, as modified to allow the INS to seek to return Zadvydas to custody or modify the conditions of his release if circumstances change. The government's continued detention of Zadvydas beyond a reasonable period is not authorized by the statute, and the Court should therefore affirm the District Court's judgment.
