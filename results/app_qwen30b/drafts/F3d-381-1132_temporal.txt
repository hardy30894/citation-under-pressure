**ARGUMENT**

The United States Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government and denying Tuthill Ranch, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment on the ground that the government’s installation and operation of fiber optic cables across Tuthill’s property—exceeding current operational needs and leased to third parties—did not constitute a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The undisputed facts establish that the government has permanently occupied Tuthill’s land with a physical structure that exceeds the scope of the easement and serves purposes unrelated to the original purpose of the easement. This constitutes a taking under settled precedent, which holds that a permanent physical occupation of land, even under a preexisting easement, is a per se taking requiring just compensation.

The Fifth Amendment provides: “No person shall be… deprived of property, without due process of law; nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation.” *Kohl v. United States*, 91 U.S. 367, 373 (1875). This provision has long been interpreted to prohibit the government from taking private property without compensation, whether through direct appropriation or through a permanent physical occupation. In *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316, 324 (1917), the Supreme Court held that “a permanent physical occupation of land by the government, even though it is done under a statutory authority, is a taking within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment.” The Court emphasized that “the right to use the land for a purpose not authorized by law, or for a purpose beyond the scope of the authority, is not a right which the government may exercise without compensation.” Id. at 325.

Here, the government installed fiber optic cables across Tuthill’s land under a perpetual easement for “signal lines necessary in connection therewith.” The easement was granted in 1953 for the purpose of facilitating the operation of electrical power transmission lines. The government’s current use of the fiber optic cables—exceeding current needs and leased to for-profit telecommunications companies—goes far beyond the original purpose of the easement. The cables are not used solely for the control or operation of the power lines, but instead serve a commercial telecommunications function. This use is not incidental to the operation of the power lines, nor is it “necessary in connection therewith” as required by the easement. The government’s own projections confirm that the excess capacity was installed to accommodate future growth, not current necessity. Yet, the mere anticipation of future need does not justify a permanent physical occupation that exceeds the scope of the original grant.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a taking occurs when the government’s use of private property exceeds the bounds of a lawful easement. In *United States v. Central Eureka Mining Co.*, 281 U.S. 678, 684 (1930), the Court stated: “The right to use the land for a purpose not authorized by law, or for a purpose beyond the scope of the authority, is not a right which the government may exercise without compensation.” The government’s leasing of unused fiber capacity to third parties constitutes a use entirely unrelated to the operation of the power grid. The fact that the government may have a legitimate interest in future expansion does not justify a permanent physical occupation of private land for commercial purposes not contemplated by the original easement.

Moreover, the Court of Federal Claims erred in concluding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings inquiry. The Court of Federal Claims stated that “the scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking.” Tuthill, slip op. at 6. This is incorrect. The scope of the easement is central to determining whether the government’s use constitutes a taking. As the Court held in *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. 373, 378 (1945), “the government may not use its power to take property under a general grant of authority to do so in a manner that exceeds the limits of the grant.” The government’s use of the fiber optic cables for commercial telecommunications is not within the scope of the original easement, and thus constitutes a taking.

The government’s argument that “no additional physical intrusion” has occurred is also flawed. The installation of the fiber optic cables was a physical intrusion that occurred in 1995. The mere fact that no new poles or wires were added does not negate the permanence of the occupation. The cables remain physically present on Tuthill’s land, and their use for purposes beyond the original easement constitutes a continuing occupation. In *United States v. Cress*, the Court held that a permanent physical occupation—even if not accompanied by new construction—constitutes a taking. 243 U.S. at 324. The government’s continued use of the cables for commercial purposes is a permanent occupation that exceeds the scope of the easement and thus triggers the Fifth Amendment’s compensation requirement.

Finally, the Court of Federal Claims’ suggestion that Tuthill may have a remedy in tort for “misuse of an easement” is beside the point. The Fifth Amendment does not permit the government to avoid compensation by asserting that a private remedy exists. As the Court held in *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 750 (1947), “the right to compensation is not dependent upon the existence of a private cause of action.” The government’s actions here constitute a per se physical taking, and Tuthill is entitled to just compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

For these reasons, the Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government. The government’s permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s land, exceeding the scope of the easement and serving commercial purposes unrelated to the original purpose, constitutes a per se taking under the Fifth Amendment. Tuthill is entitled to just compensation.

**WHEREFORE**, Tuthill Ranch, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims and remand for entry of summary judgment in favor of Tuthill.
