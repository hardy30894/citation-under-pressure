Fernandez-Vargas respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not apply to him because Congress did not clearly make the reinstatement statute reach aliens who had already reentered and established themselves in the United States before IIRIRA’s effective date. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that statutes are presumed to operate prospectively absent clear congressional direction. See Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964); United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). That presumption is especially strong where retroactive application would attach new legal consequences to past conduct. See Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 71 (1949).

Section 241(a)(5) did not merely alter procedure. It stripped previously available avenues of relief and authorized automatic reinstatement of a prior removal order. In Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534-35 (1922), the Court held that legislation should not be construed to disturb settled rights or impose new burdens based on past events without unmistakable language. Likewise, the Court has long declined to infer retroactive reach where Congress has not spoken clearly. See United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926); Greene, 376 U.S. at 160. Under those principles, § 241(a)(5) should not be read to reach an alien who completed the triggering act of illegal reentry years before the statute existed.

Even if the Court reaches retroactivity, applying § 241(a)(5) here is impermissible. A statute is retroactive when it changes the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date. See Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304, 314-16 (1945); Mathews v. Kidder, Peabody & Co., 161 U.S. 625, 629-30 (1896). Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982, long before IIRIRA. For nearly fifteen years he lived under a legal regime in which he could pursue ordinary immigration remedies, including adjustment through marriage to a United States citizen. The government’s new reading of § 241(a)(5) would extinguish that settled opportunity based solely on earlier conduct. In Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134, 147-48 (1938), the Court explained that retroactivity concerns are at their height when new legislation imposes additional burdens on past transactions. That is precisely what happened here.

The government will argue that the statute regulates the alien’s continuing unlawful presence, not merely his past entry. But the Court has rejected that kind of characterization when a statute’s operative effect depends on antecedent conduct. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994), the Court explained that a new rule is retroactive if it attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment. Earlier cases are the same. In White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545, 554 (1903), and Railroad Commission v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 216 U.S. 579, 584-85 (1910), the Court emphasized that later-enacted legal disabilities may not be imposed on completed acts absent clear statutory command. Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 reentry is the completed act that makes § 241(a)(5) operate against him.

Nor can the government avoid retroactivity by pointing to Fernandez-Vargas’s failure to leave after IIRIRA. That continuing presence was not the legal event Congress targeted; the statute’s force derives from the prior deportation and reentry. The Court has long distinguished between genuinely prospective regulation and legislation that revives old penalties or extinguishes vested claims. See Ogden v. Blackledge, 2 Cranch 272, 275 (1805); Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 2 Gall. 105, 139-40 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). Here, the old conduct is indispensable to the new disability. That is classic retroactive effect.

Finally, the statute should be construed to avoid the serious due process concerns that arise when Congress eliminates all meaningful relief without fair notice. See Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 391-92 (1798); Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 276-77 (1856). Fernandez-Vargas could not have structured his conduct in 1982 to avoid a 1997 statute that did not yet exist. The Court should therefore hold that § 241(a)(5) does not apply to pre-IIRIRA reentrants like Fernandez-Vargas, and, if it does, that such application is impermissibly retroactive.
