**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (“NPS”) properly exercised its regulatory authority over navigable waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”), 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c), does not strip NPS of the power to regulate non-federally owned lands and waters within conservation system units (“CSUs”) for purposes other than fish and wildlife management. The court of appeals correctly held that the hovercraft ban, a safety and resource-protection regulation, falls outside § 103(c)’s limited carve-out. *See Sturgeon v. Frost*, 872 F.3d 883, 894 (9th Cir. 2017) (en banc). That decision should be affirmed.

**I. Section 103(c) of ANILCA Does Not Eliminate NPS Authority Over Non-Federal Lands Within CSUs.**

ANILCA § 103(c) states that the Act’s designation of CSUs “shall not … be deemed to constitute an express or implied reservation of any Federal water rights” and shall not “enlarge or diminish the authority of the State … to manage (including the regulation of fish and wildlife) on non-federally owned lands and waters within the boundaries of any conservation system unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). By its plain text, this provision preserves state authority to manage *fish and wildlife* on non-federal lands; it does not curtail NPS’s independent regulatory authority over those same lands for purposes such as public safety, noise pollution, or habitat protection. “[T]he statute’s language is plain and unambiguous,” and the court “must give effect to that language.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that “§ 103(c) does not strip the NPS of its general regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within CSUs.” *Sturgeon*, 872 F.3d at 894.

The hovercraft ban is a classic safety regulation. The NPS prohibits hovercraft under 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e) because they “damage vegetation, create noise, disturb wildlife, and disrupt other visitors’ enjoyment of the parks.” *Id.* at 891. Nothing in § 103(c) suggests that Congress intended to allow states to operate hovercrafts—or any other harmful vehicles—on non-federal lands inside CSUs simply because the federal government could not regulate fish and wildlife management there. To the contrary, ANILCA § 1319 provides that “[n]othing in [the Act] shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing … existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The NPS Organic Act, 54 U.S.C. § 100101 *et seq.*, grants the Secretary broad authority to “promulgate and enforce regulations concerning boating and other activities on or relating to waters located within areas of the National Park System.” *Sturgeon*, 872 F.3d at 894. Section 103(c) does not silently override that longstanding authority.

**II. Sturgeon’s Hovercraft Use Is Not “Fish and Wildlife Management,” So § 103(c) Is Inapplicable.**

Even if § 103(c) could be read to limit NPS regulation of non-federal lands, the hovercraft ban does not implicate the only subject the statute protects: state authority over “fish and wildlife” management. Sturgeon used his hovercraft for moose hunting—a recreational activity, not a management activity. The Ninth Circuit properly found that “the hovercraft ban is not a regulation of ‘fish and wildlife’ management.” *Id.* at 895. The term “management” in § 103(c) refers to “the control, manipulation, or conservation of fish and wildlife populations,” not to every activity that incidentally affects fish or wildlife. *Id.* (citing dictionary definitions). Congress used the phrase “fish and wildlife” repeatedly in ANILCA to describe state conservation and allocation functions, not general recreational conduct. *See, e.g.*, 16 U.S.C. §§ 3102(4), 3111. Thus, the state’s authority to “manage” fish and wildlife under § 103(c) does not give Sturgeon a right to operate a hovercraft simply because he hunts moose.

**III. The NPS’s Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference.**

Even if the statute were ambiguous—which it is not—the NPS’s longstanding interpretation that it retains regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs is reasonable and entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). The NPS has consistently applied its regulations to non-federal lands in Alaska since at least 1996. *See Sturgeon*, 872 F.3d at 897-98 (summarizing agency position). That interpretation effectuates the central purpose of ANILCA: to ensure that CSUs “be managed to maintain the natural ecosystems and public enjoyment they provide.” *Id.* at 898. Permitting states to allow hovercrafts on non-federal inholdings would undermine that congressional objective. “Agency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency’s interpretation under the Chevron framework.” *Nat’l Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005).

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. Section 103(c) of ANILCA does not prohibit NPS from regulating non-federal lands within CSUs for purposes unrelated to fish and wildlife management, and the hovercraft ban is a lawful exercise of NPS’s general regulatory authority.
