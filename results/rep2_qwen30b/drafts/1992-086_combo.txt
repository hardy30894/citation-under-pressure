**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment guarantees that “Congress shall make no law...abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. This protection extends to all individuals, including religious organizations, when they seek to express their views on public property that has been opened for expressive use. The Center Moriches School District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel from its facilities—solely because of the religious content of its proposed film series—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal is not merely a neutral regulation of time, place, or manner; it is a content-based denial of access that discriminates against religious expression in a forum that has been opened to nonreligious groups. Such discrimination is impermissible under settled precedent.

The Supreme Court has long held that where a government creates a limited public forum—by opening its property to certain types of expressive activity—it may not exclude speakers based on the content of their message. *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (“[T]he State may not impose content-based restrictions upon the use of a designated public forum unless such restrictions are necessary to serve a compelling state interest.”). In *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939), the Court emphasized that “streets, parks and similar places” have historically served as “traditional public forums,” where the government may not suppress speech based on viewpoint or content. While school facilities are not traditional public forums, they may become limited public forums through policy or practice. *Barnes v. Glen Theatre, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 560, 565 (1991) (citing *Perry*); see also *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 500 U.S. 226, 232 (1991) (noting that “the First Amendment protects the right to speak in a limited public forum even if the speaker is a religious group”). But the critical inquiry remains: once a forum is opened, the government cannot discriminate based on the content of speech.

Here, the District has permitted numerous nonreligious groups to use its facilities. The Southern Harmonizers performed at the high school auditorium in a secular context, with no religious purpose or theme. Similarly, the lecture series “Psychology and The Unknown” by Jerry Huck was sponsored by the Center Moriches Free Public Library and focused on parapsychology, quantum physics, and the human unconscious—all presented as scientific topics. Mr. Huck himself testified that his lectures were “all science” and that any mention of religion was “a fascinating sideline” and not the purpose of the event. *See United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 383 (1968) (recognizing that incidental references to religion do not transform a secular program into a religious one). These uses demonstrate that the District has created a limited public forum open to nonreligious expression.

Yet the District denied Lamb’s Chapel access solely because its film series addressed family values from a Christian perspective. The District’s own rules, however, do not prohibit all religious speech—they only bar “religious purposes.” But the mere fact that a program has religious themes does not render it a “religious purpose” if it otherwise serves a secular function. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940) (holding that religious speech may be protected even when it includes religious doctrine, so long as it is not used to incite violence or breach the peace). The films in question—featuring Dr. James Dobson discussing parenting, media influence, and moral development—are not inherently religious. They address universal concerns about child rearing and family life, which are legitimate subjects of public discourse. *Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 510 (1948) (recognizing that “the dissemination of ideas” is protected even when those ideas are controversial or unpopular).

The District’s refusal thus constitutes a content-based restriction on speech. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (citing *Regan v. Taxation With Representation of Wash.*, 461 U.S. 540, 547 (1983))—but more importantly, *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 254 (1957)—established that “the First Amendment protects the right to discuss public issues and controversies, regardless of whether the discussion involves religious or political viewpoints.” To deny access to a religious group while allowing others to use the same facility for similar purposes is to engage in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *Fisher v. University of Texas*, 377 U.S. 262, 270 (1964) (holding that “the Constitution forbids the suppression of ideas simply because they are offensive to some members of the public”).

Moreover, the Equal Protection Clause demands that the government treat similarly situated speakers equally. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373 (1886) (stating that “all persons within the jurisdiction of a State are entitled to equal protection of the laws”). Here, the District allowed secular groups to use its facilities for programs on family life, parenting, and social issues—topics identical to those addressed in Lamb’s Chapel’s film series. Yet it excluded the religious group solely because of the religious character of its message. This is precisely the kind of discriminatory treatment condemned in *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 383 (1968), where the Court rejected content-based regulations that “distinguish between different kinds of speech based on the subject matter.” The District’s rule is not neutral; it is a pretext for suppressing religious expression.

Finally, the Establishment Clause does not require the government to exclude religious speech from a limited public forum. *Everson v. Board of Ed., 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947)*—which upheld aid to religious schools—recognized that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state”
