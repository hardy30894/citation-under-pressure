# ARGUMENT

## THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT RECOGNIZE A "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION WHERE THE PLAINTIFF ALLEGES NO MEMBERSHIP IN ANY CLASS OR GROUP

The Seventh Circuit's holding that a single property owner may state an equal protection claim by alleging only that she was treated differently from other similarly situated landowners, without any allegation of class-based discrimination, cannot be reconciled with this Court's equal protection jurisprudence. The Equal Protection Clause guarantees that "[n]o State shall . . . deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. From its inception, this guarantee has been understood as a protection against invidious classifications that burden identifiable groups, not as a general fairness-in-government-action requirement enforceable by any individual who believes she has been singled out.

### A. This Court's Equal Protection Cases Have Consistently Required Class-Based Treatment

This Court's foundational equal protection decisions all involved classifications drawn along lines of race, national origin, or other group characteristics. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), the Court held that racial segregation in public schools violated equal protection because it denied "to the children of the minority group equal educational opportunities." *Id.* at 493. The constitutional violation inhered in the *classification* of children by race, not in individualized treatment of a single plaintiff. Similarly, in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886)—the very case the Seventh Circuit invoked below—this Court's condemnation of the discriminatory administration of a laundry licensing ordinance rested on the fact that the ordinance was "applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand" against Chinese laundry operators as a class. *Id.* at 373-74. The plaintiffs in *Yick Wo* were not a single litigant claiming idiosyncratic mistreatment; they represented a class of over two hundred similarly situated Chinese laundrymen denied permits while non-Chinese applicants were uniformly granted them.

The doctrinal architecture erected atop these decisions—rational basis review, heightened scrutiny for suspect classifications, and strict scrutiny for classifications touching fundamental rights—presupposes the existence of a *class*. Tiers of scrutiny exist to test whether a *classification* is adequately justified; they do not exist to test whether a municipality's isolated interaction with a single citizen was well-reasoned. Respondent's theory would collapse this entire framework into a freestanding due process-style inquiry into the rationality of individual government decisions, untethered from the classification analysis that has always defined equal protection doctrine.

### B. The "Class of One" Theory Would Constitutionalize Ordinary Zoning and Permitting Disputes

The practical consequences of the Seventh Circuit's rule confirm that it cannot be correct. Municipalities across the country make thousands of individualized decisions daily regarding easements, permits, zoning variances, and municipal service conditions. Some degree of variation in treatment among similarly situated applicants is inevitable and often reflects legitimate site-specific considerations—soil conditions, engineering requirements, or negotiating history—that a complaint may not fully capture. If any citizen who received a less favorable outcome than a neighbor may transform that grievance into a federal constitutional tort merely by alleging that the differential treatment was "irrational" or "wholly arbitrary," federal courts will become the arbiters of local land-use and permitting decisions that this Court has long recognized are matters of local concern.

This Court's substantive due process cases counsel against precisely this kind of transformation. In *Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 405 U.S. 1 (1974), the Court emphasized the deference owed to local land-use judgments, and in *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926), the Court cautioned that judicial interference with municipal zoning determinations is warranted only where a regulation is "clearly arbitrary and unreasonable, having no substantial relation to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare." *Id.* at 395. The vehicle for such arbitrariness review has traditionally been substantive due process, not equal protection—and even under that more forgiving rubric, courts have shown considerable restraint before displacing local decisionmakers.

### C. Respondent's Reliance on Isolated Language From This Court's Precedent Is Misplaced

The Seventh Circuit derived its "spite" standard from scattered dicta describing equal protection violations in terms of governmental action "motivated solely by a 'spiteful effort to get him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.'" This formulation, even if descriptively accurate in isolated contexts involving retaliation against individuals for exercising constitutional rights—as in *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441 (1923), where the Court addressed unequal tax assessment—was never divorced from the requirement of comparative treatment tied to a broader pattern of discrimination against an ascertainable group of similarly situated taxpayers. *Sioux City Bridge* involved systemic under-assessment of an entire class of property, not a single arbitrary decision affecting one landowner in isolation.

Extending isolated language from cases involving tax assessments and other systemic administrative schemes to ad hoc single-plaintiff claims ignores the analytical predicate on which such statements rested: an underlying course of conduct establishing that the government was treating the plaintiff *as a member of an identifiable group* subjected to differential treatment, not merely making one discretionary decision that happened to differ from another.

### D. The Absence of a Class Allegation Is Fatal to Respondent's Claim

Respondent's complaint identifies no racial, national origin, or other suspect classification. It alleges no membership in any disfavored group. It asserts only that the Village required an 18-foot larger easement from her than it required from certain other applicants for water service. Standing alone, this is not an equal protection injury; it is, at most, a claim that a discretionary municipal decision was mistaken or unfair—a claim sounding in administrative or due process law, not in the equal protection guarantee that has always been about equality *among groups*, not equality in every individualized governmental transaction.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed, and the Equal Protection Clause should not be construed to permit "class of one" claims absent an allegation of class-based or group-based discrimination.
