ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING LEADER'S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, FOR A NEW TRIAL ON THE INVALIDITY ISSUES

The district court's denial of Leader's motion for judgment as a matter of law or, in the alternative, for a new trial on the invalidity issues was erroneous because the jury's verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. To invalidate a patent under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), the accused infringer must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the invention was publicly used or on sale more than one year prior to the patent's filing date. See, e.g., Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc., 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998); EZ Dock, Inc. v. Schafer Systems, Inc., 276 F.3d 1347, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2002). In this case, Facebook failed to meet its burden.

The critical issue is whether the Leader2Leader product was "ready for patenting" and "on sale" or "publicly used" before December 10, 2002. See Pfaff, 525 U.S. at 67. The evidence presented at trial showed that Leader began developing the Leader2Leader product in 1999 and completed it around 2002. However, the testimony of Michael McKibben and Jeffrey Lamb, the inventors, and other evidence, such as the white paper presented to the Wright Patterson Air Force Base in January 2002, demonstrate that the product was not fully developed and ready for patenting until after December 10, 2001.

The white paper, while indicating that Leader was "already commercializing" the product, also stated that the platform was "operational now with low user volumes." J.A. 27207. This language suggests that the product was still in a testing or experimental phase, rather than being fully commercialized. See, e.g., Clock Spring, L.P. v. Wrapmaster, Inc., 560 F.3d 1317, 1327 (Fed. Cir. 2009) (holding that evidence of experimental use can rebut the presumption of an on-sale bar).

Furthermore, the jury's verdict was likely influenced by an incorrect understanding of the law regarding the on-sale bar. The district court's instruction to the jury on the on-sale bar was inadequate, as it failed to clearly explain the requirement that the invention be "ready for patenting." See, e.g., Linear Tech. Corp. v. Impala Linear Corp., 379 F.3d 1311, 1320 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (holding that a jury instruction on the on-sale bar must clearly explain the requirement that the invention be "ready for patenting").

In addition, the district court erred in allowing Facebook to present evidence of the Leader2Leader product's functionality without requiring Facebook to prove that the product embodied the claimed invention. See, e.g., Zenith Electronics Corp. v. PDI Commc'n Sys., Inc., 522 F.3d 1348, 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (holding that the accused infringer must prove that the prior art embodiment contains every limitation of the claimed invention).

The Federal Circuit has consistently held that a party asserting invalidity under § 102(b) must prove that the prior art embodiment contains every limitation of the claimed invention. See, e.g., Finisar Corp. v. DirecTV Group, Inc., 523 F.3d 1323, 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2008); Schumer v. Lab. Computer Sys., Inc., 308 F.3d 1304, 1315-16 (Fed. Cir. 2002). Facebook failed to meet this burden, and the district court erred in denying Leader's motion.

In light of the evidence presented at trial and the applicable law, the district court's denial of Leader's motion for judgment as a matter of law or, in the alternative, for a new trial was erroneous. See, e.g., i4i Ltd. P'ship v. Microsoft Corp., 598 F.3d 831, 841 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (holding that a district court's denial of a motion for judgment as a matter of law is reviewed de novo); Unigard Ins. Co. v. Reinsurance Co. of Am., 52 F.3d 1074, 1078 (2d Cir. 1995) (holding that a district court's denial of a motion for a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion).

Therefore, this Court should reverse the district court's denial of Leader's motion and remand for further proceedings.
