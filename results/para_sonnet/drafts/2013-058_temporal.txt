# ARGUMENT

## I. The Bankruptcy Code's Exemption for "Retirement Funds" Must Be Construed According to Its Terms, Not Expanded to Reach Assets That No Longer Bear the Character the Statute Requires.

This Court has long held that when a statute's language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds"—not any asset that once bore that character in a decedent's hands, and not any account that retains favorable tax treatment. The statute's function is to shield savings held for a debtor's own retirement, and the exemption cannot be stretched beyond the actual attributes of the property at issue simply because doing so might further some generalized savings-and-bequest policy. Respondent does not dispute that Congress meant to protect retirement savings from creditors; the dispute concerns whether Heidi Heffron-Clark's inherited account, on the date the Clarks filed their bankruptcy petition, was in fact a fund of that kind.

This Court's exemption jurisprudence has always examined the property's actual character as of the date of filing. In *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1925), the Court held that "the bankrupt's right to a homestead exemption . . . became fixed as of the date of the filing of the petition," so that the nature and status of the claimed property must be assessed at that fixed point rather than by reference to its history. *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459, 471 (1913), likewise looked to the character of the property under the law governing it as of the petition date to determine exemptibility. And in *Myers v. Matley*, 318 U.S. 622, 626 (1943), this Court reiterated that exemption depends on the qualities the property possesses under the governing law at the relevant time, not on qualities it once possessed in someone else's hands.

Applying that settled snapshot approach here confirms that the inherited IRA was not, on the date of filing, a retirement fund of the Clarks or of anyone else. Ruth Heffron's account was unquestionably "retirement funds" while she held it: she alone bore the penalty for early withdrawal and the incentive to defer distribution until her own retirement years. Once she died, however, those retirement-specific incidents disappeared. Heidi could add nothing to the account, could not defer withdrawals until her own retirement, and was compelled by law to draw down the balance on a fixed schedule unrelated to any retirement date of her own. Under *White v. Stump* and *Burlingham v. Crouse*, the relevant inquiry is what the asset was on the date the petition was filed—and on that date it was a mandatory, time-limited payout vehicle, not a retirement reserve.

## II. Exemption Statutes Are Not Enlarged Beyond Their Terms Merely Because a Broader Reading Might Serve the Statute's Underlying Purpose.

Petitioners' argument—that treating inherited IRAs as available to creditors will discourage elderly savers from preserving funds for their heirs—asks this Court to extend the exemption beyond what its language permits in order to maximize a policy goal. This Court rejected an analogous invitation in *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905), where it construed the bankruptcy exemption for life-insurance proceeds according to the actual terms Congress had enacted, declining to read the provision more expansively than its text would bear even though a broader reading might have better served the general aim of protecting family security. The lesson of *Holden* is that Congress, not the courts, draws the boundaries of an exemption, and courts enforce those boundaries as written.

That principle finds further support in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), where this Court explained that the bankruptcy exemption scheme exists to give the debtor "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." The animating concern is the debtor's own future earning capacity and ability to provide for retirement from that capacity—not the preservation of a windfall inheritance for discretionary use. An inherited IRA, mandatorily distributed on a schedule untethered to the beneficiary's own working life or retirement, does not serve the "fresh start" function this Court identified in *Local Loan*. It is, instead, available for "current consumption," exactly the kind of freely accessible asset creditors are entitled to reach.

## III. The Functional, As-Held Character of an Asset—Not Its Provenance—Determines Its Treatment in Bankruptcy.

This Court's approach to characterizing property interests for bankruptcy purposes has consistently asked what the asset functionally is in the debtor's hands, not merely what it once was. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966), the Court determined the scope of "property" includible in the estate by examining the practical, present character of the interest at the time of bankruptcy, rather than resting on formal labels attached to its origin. The same functional inquiry compels the conclusion that an inherited IRA, whatever its label under the Internal Revenue Code, is not "retirement funds" within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Code once it has passed to a non-spouse beneficiary subject to mandatory, non-deferrable distribution.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and consistent with this Court's longstanding exemption jurisprudence under *Holden*, *White v. Stump*, *Burlingham*, *Myers*, *Local Loan*, and *Segal*, the judgment below should be affirmed.
