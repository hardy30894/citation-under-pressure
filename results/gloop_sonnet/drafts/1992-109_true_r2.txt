ARGUMENT

I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A SEPARATE, HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR A DEFENDANT WHO PLEADS GUILTY OR WAIVES COUNSEL.

A. This Court Has Never Recognized Two Tiers of Mental Competence.

The Ninth Circuit held that a defendant may be competent to stand trial yet incompetent to plead guilty or discharge counsel unless he also satisfies a "reasoned choice" standard. That bifurcated approach has no footing in this Court's due process jurisprudence. Where this Court has confronted the mental capacity of a defendant to make fundamental decisions about his own case, it has applied a single, unitary inquiry into competence, not a hierarchy of competency standards keyed to which right is at stake.

In United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the state court's competency finding—affirmed as consistent with due process—asked simply whether "there was nothing in this record which convinces us that this relator was insane when he committed the murder charged or when he pleaded guilty or at the time he was sentenced to death." Id. at 561. That formulation makes no distinction between the mental capacity required to stand trial and the mental capacity required to plead guilty; sanity, not some elevated "reasoned choice" faculty, was the touchstone.

Carter v. Illinois, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), likewise treated the waiver of counsel as governed by ordinary capacity to choose: "The right to be represented by counsel is one which the defendant may waive or claim, as he shall determine." Id. at 174. Nothing in Carter suggests that determination requires proof of a superior mental faculty beyond what is necessary to stand trial.

And in Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134 (1951), this Court held that "the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to afford defendants assistance of counsel in noncapital criminal cases when there are special circumstances showing that without a lawyer a defendant could not have an adequate and a fair defense." Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134, 134 (1951). That inquiry is contextual and fairness-based; it is not a freestanding heightened competency test distinct from the standard applicable to standing trial.

B. The Constitutional Benchmark Is a Knowing and Voluntary Waiver, Not "Reasoned Choice."

This Court's settled test for the validity of a waiver of trial rights is whether it reflects an "intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966) (quoting Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938)). Applying that test, the Court in Brookhart asked only whether the petitioner had "intelligently and knowingly waived his right to cross-examine the witnesses whose testimony was used to convict him." Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966). That standard already accounts for the seriousness of what is waived; it does not require importing an additional, undefined mental-capacity threshold beyond intelligent and knowing waiver.

McCarthy v. United States, 394 U.S. 459 (1969), confirms that the safeguards surrounding a guilty plea are satisfied when the trial court ascertains that the defendant understands the consequences of his plea and the rights he is relinquishing. There, the district judge "asked petitioner if he desired to plead guilty and if he understood that such a plea waived his right to a jury trial and subjected him to imprisonment for as long as five years and to a fine as high as $10,000." McCarthy v. United States, 394 U.S. 459, 460 (1969). That colloquy illustrates that the constitutional inquiry is properly focused on understanding and voluntariness, precisely the components already embedded in ordinary competency and waiver analysis.

Arsenault v. Massachusetts, 393 U.S. 5 (1968), likewise addressed the validity of an uncounseled guilty plea by reference to whether the proceeding at which it was entered was itself constitutionally infirm, not by asking whether the defendant possessed a distinct competency to make "reasoned" decisions. See id. at 5–6.

C. A Novel "Reasoned Choice" Standard Would Disserve the Values Underlying Habeas Review of State Convictions.

Federal courts sitting in habeas review of state criminal judgments have long been cautioned against layering new substantive constitutional standards onto proceedings that were otherwise regularly conducted. In Matter of Moran, 203 U.S. 96 (1906), this Court examined a habeas petition premised on claimed defects in the trial court's jurisdiction and organization, recognizing that the grounds asserted there "are alleged to go to the jurisdiction of the trial court." Matter of Moran, 203 U.S. 96, 97 (1906). That decision reflects the settled principle that habeas review properly reaches fundamental defects of the kind identified by the petitioner in that case, not freestanding revisions of the applicable substantive competency standard such as the one the Ninth Circuit invented here.

Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966), is instructive by contrast: when doubt arose concerning a habeas petitioner's capacity to withdraw his own petition, this Court did not invent a special "reasoned choice" standard. Rather, counsel there advised the Court that "he could not conscientiously accede to these instructions without a psychiatric evaluation of Rees because evidence cast doubt on Rees' mental competency." Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312, 313–14 (1966). This Court thus proceeded on the ordinary competency framework applicable to any determination of a defendant's capacity to make decisions affecting his own legal proceedings. That approach—one competency standard, faithfully applied to the facts—is the approach due process requires.

* * *

The competency standard this Court articulated for standing trial, see Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), together with the knowing-and-voluntary waiver requirement of Brookhart v. Janis and Johnson v. Zerbst, fully protects a defendant's rights when he chooses to plead guilty or proceed without counsel. Respondent Moran was examined by two psychiatrists and found to understand the charges against him and to be capable of assisting in his defense—precisely the inquiry due process demands. No additional "reasoned choice" standard is required by the Constitution, and the Ninth Circuit erred in imposing one.
