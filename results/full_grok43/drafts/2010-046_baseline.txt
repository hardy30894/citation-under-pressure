**ARGUMENT**

The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies every prerequisite of the Prison Litigation Reform Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3626. Section 3626(a)(3)(E) authorizes a prisoner-release order only when “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and “no other relief will remedy the violation.” The record establishes both findings.

First, the district court determined after exhaustive proceedings that “overcrowding is the primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations. That determination rests on the same standard applied in *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981), which requires proof that conditions “inflict unnecessary or wanton pain” or are “grossly disproportionate to the severity of the crime.” The evidence showed that severe overcrowding produced “systemwide” deficiencies in medical and mental-health care that no other remedy could cure. *See Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 834 (1994) (Eighth Amendment violated when officials knowingly expose inmates to “substantial risk of serious harm”).

Second, the order is the least intrusive means of correcting the violations. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) demands that injunctive relief be “narrowly drawn,” “extend[] no further than necessary,” and give “substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety.” The panel reduced the population only to 137.5 percent of design capacity—the level at which the State’s own experts testified that constitutional care could be delivered. It authorized the State to choose the means of reduction, expressly permitting “good-time credits, parole, and sentencing reform” rather than indiscriminate release. *See Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000) (PLRA “preserv[es] courts’ authority to issue the ‘least intrusive’ relief necessary”).

The State’s contrary arguments—that the order exceeds the PLRA because it affects sentencing or public safety—were rejected in *Gilmore v. California*, 220 F.3d 987, 1007–08 (9th Cir. 2000), which upheld population caps when crowding itself violates the Constitution. The panel’s findings, made after a full trial and supported by “substantial evidence,” are entitled to deference. *See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573–74 (1985).

Because the order meets every statutory command, it does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act.
