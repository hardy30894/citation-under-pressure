**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Was a Permissible Subject-Matter Restriction in a Limited Public Forum, Not Unconstitutional Viewpoint Discrimination.**

The Milford Central School District created a limited public forum by permitting community use of its facilities for social, civic, recreational meetings and entertainment events while expressly excluding religious purposes. Because the school is not a traditional public forum, the State may control the use of its property as long as restrictions are reasonable and not based on the speaker’s viewpoint. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). The school’s policy is a reasonable subject-matter limitation that reserves the forum for secular activities only.

The Good News Club’s proposed activities—prayer, Bible memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as personal Savior—are not a viewpoint on a secular subject but rather religious instruction and worship. The Club’s meetings are quintessentially religious, as the court below correctly held. The school may properly exclude such content because the forum has not been opened for religious speech. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 406 (1953) (upholding permit requirement for religious meeting in public park); *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575–76 (1941) (parade permit fee valid because government may regulate use of public property). A permit system that excludes religious worship is constitutional when, as here, the standards are clear and applied neutrally. *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 293–94 (1951) (invalidating ordinance lacking standards, but recognizing that a properly drawn law may restrict religious speech in a nonpublic forum).

The Club argues that its activities are merely a religious viewpoint on the subject of morals and character. But the Supreme Court has long distinguished between religious belief and religious conduct subject to regulation. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303–04 (1940). The Club’s prayer, scripture memorization, and proselytizing are conduct that goes beyond the expression of a viewpoint; they constitute the very subject matter of religion. The school’s policy does not discriminate against a viewpoint—it excludes an entire category of speech that is not within the forum’s purpose. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 162–63 (1939) (upholding anti-littering ordinance as reasonable regulation of conduct). The other organizations allowed to use the school—Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, 4-H—focus on secular character development; none engage in religious instruction or prayer. The school’s decision to exclude the Club is therefore a permissible subject-matter distinction, not viewpoint discrimination.

**II. Even If the Exclusion Were a Viewpoint Restriction, It Is Justified by the School’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.**

The school has a compelling interest in not appearing to endorse religion, particularly in a public elementary school setting. The First Amendment mandates that the government make no law respecting an establishment of religion. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1947). Permitting the Good News Club to meet on school premises immediately after school hours, with the school’s express permission, could lead young students to perceive that the school sponsors or endorses the Club’s religious message. *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948) (invalidating religious instruction in public schools). The school’s policy is a reasonable measure to avoid that perception. *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430 (1962) (school prayer invalid); *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 222 (1963) (Bible reading invalid). The risk of endorsement is especially acute because the Club targets children aged six to twelve, who are particularly susceptible to coercion. The school may act to protect its students from such pressures. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 315 (1952) (upholding off-premises released time, but emphasizing that schools must not inject religion into the classroom).

The school’s concern is not speculative; the Club’s own materials distinguish between saved and unsaved children and invite children to accept Jesus privately after meetings. Allowing such activities on school property would place the school’s imprimatur on religious proselytizing. The school’s policy is narrowly drawn to avoid this constitutional violation. Therefore, even if the exclusion were a viewpoint restriction, it is justified by the compelling interest in compliance with the Establishment Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
