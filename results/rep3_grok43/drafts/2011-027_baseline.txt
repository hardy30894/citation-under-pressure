The constitutional test for title navigability requires proof that the river was “used, or … susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce” at the time of statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870); see also United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) (“[T]he test … is whether the river … was navigable in fact at the time of the admission of the State.”). This inquiry is conducted on a segment-by-segment basis because title to submerged lands passes to a state only for those portions meeting the test. United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926); Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922).

The equal-footing doctrine confirms that the date of statehood fixes both the existence and the geographic extent of the title. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-28 (1894); Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845). Evidence of later or earlier use is relevant only insofar as it illuminates conditions at that historic moment. United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co., 174 U.S. 690, 698 (1899).

Montana’s evidence—contemporaneous accounts, government surveys, and commercial records from 1889—demonstrated that the relevant reaches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers satisfied the Daniel Ball standard at statehood. The trial court’s findings therefore comported with the constitutional requirement that navigability be determined for each discrete stretch as it existed when Montana entered the Union.
