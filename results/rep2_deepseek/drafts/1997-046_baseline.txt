**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Executive Clemency**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Woodard has no constitutionally protected interest in the clemency process itself. *See Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). Clemency is an act of grace, not a right. The Ohio Constitution vests the Governor with “almost unfettered discretion” to grant or deny clemency. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994). As this Court recognized in *Dumschat*, “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” 452 U.S. at 464 (citing *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976)).

The court of appeals nonetheless held that Woodard retains a residual life or liberty interest in clemency because Ohio has made the procedure “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating guilt or innocence.” That reasoning conflicts with *Dumschat* and with the historical understanding of clemency as a separate, executive function. The fact that clemency occurs after a final judgment does not transform it into an adjudicative stage. Unlike the first appeal of right in *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), clemency is not a continuation of the trial process. It is a discretionary, executive act that “may be granted for any reason, or for no reason.” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 466.

Even if some minimal due process were to attach, as the Sixth Circuit suggested, the content would be “barely perceptible.” But this Court has never required due process for clemency. To do so would create “disincentives for States to offer clemency petitioners an opportunity to have a personal interview.” *See Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 482 (1995) (warning against extending due process to discretionary prison procedures). The state’s voluntary interview process imposes no coercion and creates no liberty interest. An inmate’s hope for clemency is no more than “a unilateral hope,” not a protected entitlement. *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979).

**II. The Voluntary Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment**

Woodard’s Fifth Amendment claim is premature and meritless. The Ohio regulation provides that if a prisoner *requests* a personal interview, only the prisoner and assigned board members are present. The regulation does not require the prisoner to answer any question. He may “say as much or as little … as he wishes.” As in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976), where the Court found a similar claim premature because no adverse consequences had been threatened, Woodard faces no compulsion. The state has not conditioned clemency on a waiver of Fifth Amendment rights. The mere possibility that a voluntary statement might later be used against him in a hypothetical future proceeding does not create an actual case or controversy. *Id.* at 324.

The Sixth Circuit’s remand for the district court to “determine more fully the nature of the APA’s clemency procedures” and to assess whether the interview “might” impose an unconstitutional condition invites speculation. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against such “abstract” inquiries. *See, e.g., Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 429 (1984) (no Fifth Amendment violation where “the State did not impose any penalty” for remaining silent). The state has no interest in compelling incriminating testimony; it simply offers a voluntary opportunity for the inmate to present his case. If Woodard fears self-incrimination, he may decline the interview. That choice does not implicate the Fifth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Clemency is not subject to due process constraints, and the voluntary interview option does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
