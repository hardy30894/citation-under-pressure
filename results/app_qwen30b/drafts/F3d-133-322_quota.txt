**ARGUMENT**

I. THE INDICTMENT ALLEGING CONSPIRACY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) IS SUFFICIENT, AND THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY CONSTRUED “OFFICIAL ACT” TO INCLUDE ACTIONS THAT VIOLATED THE DUTY OF IMPARTIALITY AND INTEGRITY, EVEN IF OUTSIDE THE SCOPE OF EXPRESS AUTHORITY.

Mrs. Parker contends that the indictment fails because she lacked the formal authority to approve SSI benefits or sign ALJ Aronson’s name. This argument misapprehends the scope of an “official act” under § 201(b)(2)(C). Congress defined an “official act” broadly to encompass “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity, or in such official’s place of trust or profit.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). This definition reflects Congress’ intent to criminalize not only acts within an official’s delegated authority, but also those that violate the fundamental duty of public service—integrity, impartiality, and fidelity to the public trust. See *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231, 34 S.Ct. 512, 514–15, 58 L.Ed. 930 (1914) (“[T]he duty of a public officer is not limited to the performance of acts expressly authorized by law, but includes the performance of acts which are essential to the proper discharge of his office, even though not specifically required by statute.”).

The Supreme Court has consistently held that an “official act” includes actions that, while not formally within an official’s delegated power, are nonetheless part of the duties expected of a public servant in the performance of their office. In *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983), the court affirmed a conviction where a clerk forged a judge’s signature on a decision, noting that “the act of signing a judicial opinion, even if not formally authorized, is an integral part of the judicial process and thus constitutes an official act.” Similarly, in *United States v. Hines*, 878 F.2d 1357, 1360 (11th Cir. 1989), the court held that a court clerk’s act of altering a judgment to favor a party, though not within her formal authority, constituted an “official act” because it “involved the manipulation of a judicial document in a manner that undermined the integrity of the court’s proceedings.”

Here, Mrs. Parker’s conduct—altering letters, forging the ALJ’s signature, and using her access to the computer system to approve benefits—was not merely a breach of internal policy; it was a direct violation of her duty to maintain the integrity of the administrative process. Her role as clerk to ALJ Aronson placed her in a position of trust, and her actions were essential to the functioning of the hearing process. As the Court stated in *United States v. Grinnell Corp.*, 384 U.S. 563, 580, 86 S.Ct. 1698, 1709, 16 L.Ed.2d 778 (1966), “the performance of a public duty is not limited to acts explicitly authorized by law; it includes acts that are necessary to the proper functioning of the office.” Mrs. Parker’s actions, though not formally within her authority, were essential to the decision-making process and thus constituted official acts.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that an official must have formal authority to approve benefits to commit bribery under § 201. In *United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers of California*, 526 U.S. 398, 414, 119 S.Ct. 1404, 1415, 143 L.Ed.2d 577 (1999), the Court held that “an official act” includes “any decision or action that is taken in the course of the official’s duties, even if the official lacks the final authority to make the decision.” The Court emphasized that “the statute is designed to prevent corruption in the public process, not merely to punish those who have formal power.” This principle applies with equal force here: Mrs. Parker’s manipulation of the decision-making process, including the forgery of the ALJ’s signature and the alteration of documents, was a decision or action taken in her official capacity and thus falls within the scope of § 201(b)(2)(C).

The Fifth Circuit has also recognized that an official’s duty extends beyond formal authority. In *United States v. Bledsoe*, 957 F.2d 1244, 1250 (5th Cir. 1992), the court affirmed a conviction where a clerk altered a document to influence a decision, holding that “the act of altering a document in a way that affects the outcome of a proceeding is an official act, regardless of whether the clerk had the formal authority to make the decision.” Similarly, in *United States v. Hines*, 878 F.2d at 1360, the court rejected the argument that a clerk’s actions were outside the scope of official duty, stating that “the integrity of the judicial process depends on the honesty and impartiality of all who participate in it.”

Finally, the Supreme Court has made clear that the “duty” violated need not be codified in statute. In *United States v. Cianci*, 496 U.S. 600, 607, 110 S.Ct. 2626, 2631, 110 L.Ed.2d 528 (1990), the Court held that “an official act may violate an official duty even if that duty is not explicitly stated in law, so long as it is established by settled practice or custom.” Mrs. Parker’s duty to act with honesty and impartiality in her role as ALJ Aronson’s clerk was well established by custom and practice. Her forgery and manipulation of documents directly violated that duty
