**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court's Subject-Matter Jurisdiction Is Reviewed De Novo, and ECMC's Challenge Was Not Waived.**

Whether a bankruptcy court possesses subject-matter jurisdiction is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. See Grausz v. Englander, 321 F.3d 467, 471 (4th Cir. 2003). Because subject-matter jurisdiction "can never be forfeited or waived," Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998), ECMC's failure to request quantification of interest and collection costs below does not bar it from challenging the bankruptcy court's authority to have decided those questions at all. Indeed, a court is obligated to police the boundaries of its own jurisdiction even when the parties do not raise the issue. Id. at 95.

**II. The Bankruptcy Court's "Related To" Jurisdiction Did Not Extend to Quantifying Post-Discharge Interest and Collection Costs on an Excepted Debt in a Closed Case.**

Bankruptcy jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b) reaches civil proceedings "arising under," "arising in," or "related to" a case under Title 11. That grant is broad, reflecting Congress's intent "to grant comprehensive jurisdiction to the bankruptcy courts so that they might deal efficiently and expeditiously with all matters connected with the bankruptcy estate." Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 308 (1995). But breadth is not limitlessness. Once a plan is confirmed—and still more once the case is closed and discharge entered—the estate ceases to exist, and jurisdiction over post-confirmation, post-closure disputes survives only where there is a "close nexus to the bankruptcy plan or proceeding" sufficient to support the continued exercise of federal jurisdiction. Valley Historic Ltd. P'ship v. Bank of N.Y., 486 F.3d 831, 836 (4th Cir. 2007) (adopting the "close nexus" test). Under that test, "matters affecting the interpretation, implementation, consummation, execution, or administration of the confirmed plan will typically have the requisite close nexus." In re Craig's Stores of Tex., Inc., 266 F.3d 388, 391 (5th Cir. 2001); Valley Historic, 486 F.3d at 836 (relying on Craig's Stores).

No such nexus exists here. Kirkland's Complaint asked the bankruptcy court to determine one thing only: whether claim no. 9 had been "paid in full and discharged except to the extent of any interest that may have" accrued during the pendency of the case. J.A. 5. She expressly conceded that post-petition interest was nondischargeable and that she remained personally liable for it. The bankruptcy court correctly resolved that narrow question, finding that no payment had ever been applied to claim no. 9 and that the principal therefore remained due in full—$4,737.27. That ruling implemented the plan and disposed of the dischargeability dispute the parties actually placed before the court. It had the requisite nexus to the confirmed plan. See Valley Historic, 486 F.3d at 836.

But the bankruptcy court did not stop there. It went on to fix the precise dollar amount of post-petition interest—$184.40—and to adjudicate ECMC's entitlement to collection costs, ruling that ECMC had "not provided any statutory or factual basis for the accrual of such costs." J.A. 87. Neither party asked the court to perform that accounting. Kirkland sought only a declaration regarding dischargeability; ECMC opposed a dischargeability finding on undue-hardship grounds. The computation of a nondischargeable debt's ongoing interest and collection costs—an obligation that, by Kirkland's own admission, survived the bankruptcy and belonged to the post-discharge relationship between guarantor and borrower—bears no relationship to "the interpretation, implementation, consummation, execution, or administration" of the confirmed Chapter 13 plan. Craig's Stores, 266 F.3d at 391. The plan had already been fully administered; the Trustee had filed his final report, the discharge order had been entered, and the case had been closed under 11 U.S.C. § 350 years before Kirkland filed her adversary proceeding. Whatever interest or costs accrue on this nondischargeable debt going forward is a matter of state-law contract and guaranty obligations having nothing to do with plan administration. Cf. Travelers Indem. Co. v. Bailey, 557 U.S. 137, 151 (2009) (post-confirmation bankruptcy jurisdiction depends on the terms of the plan and confirmation order themselves, not on the mere fact that a claim once passed through the bankruptcy case).

Put simply, the accrual and quantification of interest and collection costs on a debt Kirkland herself agreed was nondischargeable is a freestanding creditor-debtor dispute, "related to" the bankruptcy only in the attenuated sense that the underlying loan happened to have been scheduled in a since-closed case. That is not enough. See Valley Historic, 486 F.3d at 836–37 (rejecting jurisdiction over post-confirmation disputes lacking the requisite nexus, notwithstanding a tangential connection to the bankruptcy proceeding).

**III. The Proper Remedy Is Vacatur of the Interest and Collection-Cost Rulings, Leaving ECMC Free to Pursue Those Amounts in a Forum of Competent Jurisdiction.**

Because the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to quantify interest and collection costs, its rulings on those points—capping interest at $184.40 and denying collection costs outright—are void and cannot bind ECMC. See Steel Co., 523 U.S. at 94 (judgments issued without jurisdiction are nullities). This Court should reverse the district court's affirmance of that portion of the bankruptcy court's judgment, vacate the interest and collection-cost determinations, and remand with instructions that ECMC's rights to post-petition interest and collection costs on the nondischargeable balance be adjudicated, if at all, in a court of competent, non-bankruptcy jurisdiction. The principal-amount discharge determination, which did bear the necessary nexus to the plan, should stand undisturbed.
