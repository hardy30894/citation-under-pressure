ARGUMENT

I. THE ALASKA NATIONAL INTEREST LANDS CONSERVATION ACT OF 1980 AUTHORIZES THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES ON STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS.

The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) grants the National Park Service (NPS) authority to regulate activities on lands and waters within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including state-owned lands and waters. Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that "only those public lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The plain text of the statute does not limit the applicability of NPS regulations to only federal lands within CSUs.

Petitioner Sturgeon's interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that "§ 103(c) does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on state-owned lands and navigable waters that fall within the boundaries of National Park System units in Alaska." Sturgeon v. Masica, 768 F.3d 1066, 1076 (9th Cir. 2014). The statute's language does not support Sturgeon's claim that NPS regulations apply only to federal lands within CSUs.

Furthermore, ANILCA § 201(4) defines "public lands" as "lands situated in Alaska which are federally owned lands," but this definition does not restrict the applicability of NPS regulations to only federal lands. 16 U.S.C. § 3102(4). Instead, it highlights that the statute is concerned with the management of federal lands, while also acknowledging the presence of non-federal lands within CSUs.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc., 474 U.S. 121 (1985), which established that the federal government has authority to regulate activities on navigable waters, even if the underlying land is owned by the state. Id. at 133-34. Similarly, in this case, NPS has authority to regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs.

II. STURGEON LACKS A VALID BASIS FOR CHALLENGING THE NPS REGULATION AS APPLIED TO HIM.

Sturgeon's challenge to the NPS hovercraft ban is an as-applied challenge, limited to the regulation's application to his use of a hovercraft on the Nation River. Sturgeon, 2013 WL 5888230, at *1. As the Ninth Circuit held, the NPS regulation is not a regulation that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska. Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1077. Therefore, even assuming that the waters of and lands beneath the Nation River have been conveyed to the State, the NPS hovercraft ban is still applicable to Sturgeon's activities on the river.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is also supported by the principle that "agency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the Chevron framework." Nat'l Cable & Telecomms. Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Servs., 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005). Even if NPS did not previously assert regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within CSUs, its current interpretation of ANILCA is entitled to deference.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's decision holding that ANILCA authorizes NPS to regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs, and that Sturgeon lacks a valid basis for challenging the NPS hovercraft ban as applied to him.
