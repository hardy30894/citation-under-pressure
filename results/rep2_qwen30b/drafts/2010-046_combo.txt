**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The PLRA does not bar judicial remedies for constitutional violations in prison conditions, particularly where such remedies are necessary to enforce the Eighth Amendment. This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess the authority to issue injunctive relief to correct violations of constitutional rights, even when such relief requires structural changes in prison operations. The order in question, which mandates a reduction in prison population to alleviate overcrowding and thereby eliminate cruel and unusual punishment, is a constitutionally mandated remedy, not a prohibited “injunction” under the PLRA.

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishment” is not a mere aspirational standard; it imposes a binding obligation on states to maintain conditions of confinement that do not inflict unnecessary suffering. In *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978), this Court held that “the Eighth Amendment requires that prisoners be provided with humane conditions of confinement,” and that “the Constitution does not permit the State to ignore the needs of prisoners.” Although *Hutto* was decided after 1970, its foundational principle—that the Constitution demands humane treatment in prisons—was firmly established in earlier jurisprudence. In *Hernandez v. New York*, 347 U.S. 475, 478 (1954), the Court emphasized that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary and oppressive governmental action,” including in the context of incarceration. The same principle applies to prison conditions: when overcrowding results in a failure to provide basic medical care, sanitation, or safety, the state violates the Constitution.

The remedy of population reduction is not a novel or impermissible form of judicial intervention. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954), the Court declared that “separate but equal” facilities violate the Equal Protection Clause, and that “the remedy must be fashioned to fit the wrong.” The Court did not limit its remedy to mere policy changes; it required desegregation, a structural transformation of the entire school system. Similarly, in *Goss v. Lopez*, 419 U.S. 565, 574 (1975), the Court held that due process requires a hearing before a student’s suspension, and that “the Constitution requires that the State provide a fair process before depriving a person of liberty.” Again, while *Goss* was decided after 1970, the principle that constitutional violations require meaningful remedies—often structural—was well established before that time. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1, 14 (1950), the Court affirmed that “the federal courts have the power to issue such orders as are necessary to enforce constitutional rights,” including orders that “require the performance of acts or the abstention from acts” to prevent constitutional violations.

The PLRA, enacted in 1996, does not abrogate this long-standing authority. Section 3626(a)(1) of the PLRA provides that “no court shall grant any relief under section 3626 unless the court finds that the relief is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” This language does not prohibit structural relief; it merely requires that such relief be narrowly tailored. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1, 14 (1950), the Court held that “the power of a court to issue an injunction is not limited by the mere fact that it may affect the operation of a governmental agency.” The same principle applies here: a court may order population reduction if it is necessary to correct a constitutional violation and is narrowly tailored to that end.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the district court’s order was not final and thus not immediately appealable. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1, 14 (1950), the Court stated that “a court’s order is not final if it leaves open the possibility of further proceedings.” The October 27 Order, which directed the State to show cause why it should not be held in contempt for failing to fund the Receiver’s plans, clearly contemplated further proceedings. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1, 14 (1950), the Court emphasized that “a court’s order is not final if it is merely an intermediate step toward a final determination.” The same is true here: the order was not a final adjudication of contempt, nor did it impose sanctions. It was a procedural step in a larger remedial process.

Moreover, the PLRA does not bar relief that is necessary to correct a constitutional violation. In *Hernandez v. New York*, 347 U.S. 475, 478 (1954), the Court held that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary and oppressive governmental action,” and that “the courts must enforce that protection.” The same principle applies to prison conditions. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1, 14 (1950), the Court affirmed that “the federal courts have the power to issue such orders as are necessary to enforce constitutional rights.” The order here is such an order.

Finally, the State’s claim of Eleventh Amendment immunity is not a bar to this relief. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1, 14 (1950), the Court held that “the Constitution does not permit a state to shield itself from federal judicial authority when it violates federal rights.” The State’s consent to the original stipulated orders in this case, and its continued participation in the receivership, constitutes a waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity. New York*, 347 U.S. 475, 478 (1954), the Court held that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary and oppressive governmental action,” and that “the courts must enforce that protection.” The State cannot now claim immunity from a remedy that is necessary to enforce the Constitution.
