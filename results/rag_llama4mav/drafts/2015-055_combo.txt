The issue presented is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) requires a minimum sentence of 15 years for individuals with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. Welch argues that his 1996 Florida conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA.

The Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments require that a criminal statute provide fair warning of the conduct it prohibits (Bouie v. City of Columbia, 378 U.S. 347, 351 (1964)). In Giaccio v. Pennsylvania, 382 U.S. 399 (1966), the Court held that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide definite standards to govern its application. The residual clause of the ACCA, which includes action that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another" in the definition of a violent felony, is unconstitutionally vague (Johnson v. United States). This clause violates the Due Process Clauses because it is so vague that it fails to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punishes.

The Court has long recognized that a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime (United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954)). In Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196 (1948), the Court held that a conviction under a statute that is substantially different from the one under which the defendant was tried and convicted is a denial of due process. Similarly, in Williams v. Kaiser, 323 U.S. 471 (1945), the Court held that a defendant's right to counsel is a fundamental right that is essential to a fair trial.

The application of the Johnson rule to Welch's case is necessary to ensure that his conviction and sentence are consistent with the Due Process Clauses. The Florida robbery statute under which Welch was convicted in 1996 was vague and did not provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibited. At the time of his conviction, Florida law was divided on whether a snatching, as of a purse or jewelry, amounted to robbery (McCloud v. State, 335 So.2d 257, 258-59 (Fla.1976); Goldsmith v. State, 573 So.2d 445, 445 (Fla.2d Dist.Ct.App.1991); A.J. v. State, 561 So.2d 1198, 1198 (Fla.3d Dist.Ct.App.1990)). 

The rule announced in Johnson v. United States should be applied retroactively to Welch's case. The vagueness of the residual clause is a substantive issue that affects the validity of Welch's sentence. As the Court held in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), a defendant's fundamental rights, such as the right to be sentenced under a valid statute, must be protected. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing in accordance with the rule announced in Johnson.

In Lynumn v. Illinois, 372 U.S. 528 (1963), the Court emphasized the importance of ensuring that a defendant's conviction and sentence are consistent with the requirements of due process. Similarly, in Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court held that a guilty plea must be voluntary and intelligent to be valid. Welch's conviction and sentence should be reevaluated in light of the Johnson rule to ensure that they comply with the requirements of due process.

Furthermore, the Court has consistently held that a statute that is unconstitutionally vague cannot be used to convict or sentence a defendant (Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912); Reynolds v. Cochran, 365 U.S. 525 (1961)). In Gilbert v. California, 384 U.S. 985 (1966), the Court considered the constitutionality of a conviction based on evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search and seizure. Similarly, Welch's sentence was based on an unconstitutionally vague statute, and therefore, it should be vacated.

In conclusion, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States should be applied retroactively to Welch's case. The vagueness of the residual clause is a substantive issue that affects the validity of Welch's sentence. The Due Process Clauses require that a criminal statute provide fair warning of the conduct it prohibits, and the ACCA's residual clause fails to meet this requirement. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing in accordance with the rule announced in Johnson.
