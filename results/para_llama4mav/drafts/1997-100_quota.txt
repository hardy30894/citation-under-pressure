The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969)), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its decisions in Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), provide guidance.

In Bullington, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they share "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" (Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439). The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding involved a trial-like process, with the prosecution presenting evidence and the jury making a determination based on that evidence. Similarly, in Rumsey, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a capital sentencing proceeding in Arizona, noting that the sentencing hearing "resembled and, indeed, in all relevant respects was like the trial on the question of guilt or innocence" (Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209-210).

The same logic applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, such as the one in this case. Under California law, a defendant has a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations (Cal. Penal Code § 1025). The prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt (People v. Tenner, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993)), and the defendant has the right to confront witnesses and present evidence (People v. Reed, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224, 228, fn. 6 (1996)). These procedural protections are similar to those in a trial on guilt or innocence, and thus, the Double Jeopardy Clause should apply.

The California Supreme Court's attempt to distinguish Bullington is unpersuasive. The Court's reliance on Caspari v. Boles, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), is misplaced, as Caspari did not hold that Bullington is limited to capital cases. Instead, the Court in Caspari merely noted that Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases and that their reasoning was based on the unique circumstances of capital sentencing proceedings (Caspari, 510 U.S. at 392). This observation does not preclude the application of Bullington to noncapital sentencing proceedings.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. 117 (1980), is distinguishable. In DiFrancesco, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar an appeal by the government of a sentence imposed under the federal dangerous special offender statute. However, the Court noted that the statute provided for a trial-like process, but the defendant was not acquitted or convicted of a different offense (DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. at 136-137). In contrast, the prior conviction allegation in this case was tried to a judge, and the Court of Appeal's reversal was based on insufficient evidence.

The application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to noncapital sentencing proceedings is consistent with the principles established in Bullington and Rumsey. The Clause protects a defendant's interest in avoiding multiple prosecutions for the same offense (Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-188 (1957)). By applying the Double Jeopardy Clause to noncapital sentencing proceedings, we ensure that defendants are not subjected to multiple trials for the same prior conviction allegation.

In conclusion, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The trial-like process and procedural protections afforded to defendants in these proceedings, as well as the Supreme Court's decisions in Bullington and Rumsey, support this conclusion. Therefore, the judgment of the Court of Appeal should be affirmed, and the prior conviction allegation should not be retried.
