**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s state judges are excluded from the ADEA’s coverage under 29 U.S.C. § 630(f), which defines “employee” to exclude “any appointee on the policymaking level.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603 (8th Cir. 1990), *aff’d*, 501 U.S. 452 (1991). This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the ADEA, as well as the Supreme Court’s instruction that exemptions under the statute must be construed narrowly. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), *aff’d sub nom. Gregory*, 501 U.S. 452.

The ADEA’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended the policymaking exception to apply to high-level executive and judicial officials. The Conference Report explains that the exception covers “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments, such as cabinet officers, and persons with comparable responsibilities at the local level.” 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. As the Eighth Circuit recognized, judges exercise significant policymaking authority, including the power to interpret statutes, develop common law, and supervise court operations. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 605-06. This is not mere mechanical application of settled law; judges resolve ambiguities, fill gaps, and shape legal standards that guide future conduct. *Id.* at 605 (quoting *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55). Thus, state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” and fall outside the ADEA’s reach.

Petitioners’ argument that the exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees is foreclosed by the statute’s text and purpose. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606. The Eighth Circuit correctly rejected the contention that the exception’s placement in a series of exemptions “primarily” applicable to the executive and legislative branches limits its scope. *Id.* The plain language of § 630(f) contains no such limitation, and construing it narrowly—as petitioners urge—would render the judicial branch entirely unprotected by the ADEA’s exemptions, a result Congress could not have intended. *Id.* at 606-07.

**II. THE ADEA DOES NOT EXPLICITLY AND UNEQUIVOCALLY EXTEND TO STATE JUDGES, AND THEREFORE DOES NOT PREEMPT MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION.**

Even if judges were not excluded by the policymaking exception, the ADEA does not preempt Missouri’s constitutional mandatory retirement provision because Congress did not clearly and manifestly intend to abrogate state judicial retirement laws. *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 460 (1991) (citing *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971)). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that, in areas implicating federalism concerns—such as the structure of state government—courts must not infer preemption absent “clear evidence” of congressional intent. *Id.* at 460 (quoting *Bass*, 404 U.S. at 349).

The ADEA contains no express reference to state judges or judicial retirement provisions. *Id.* at 461. The statute’s silence is particularly telling given that Congress amended the ADEA in 1978 and 1986 to expand its coverage, yet did not address state judges. *Id.* at 462. This omission is dispositive. As the Court explained in *Gregory*, “[i]f Congress had wanted to subject state judges to the ADEA’s comprehensive framework, it could have said so expressly.” *Id.* at 463. The absence of such language compels the conclusion that Congress did not intend to preempt state judicial retirement laws.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Orzel v. City of Wauwatosa Fire Dep’t*, 697 F.2d 743 (7th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. *Orzel* involved a state statute, not a constitutional provision, and the Seventh Circuit’s preemption analysis did not account for the heightened clear-statement rule applicable to state governmental structures. *Gregory*, 501 U.S. at 460. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Gregory* makes clear that *Orzel* is not controlling here. *Id.* at 463.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT’S EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Eighth Circuit correctly upheld Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision against petitioners’ equal protection challenge. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 608-09. Under the rational basis test—which applies to age-based classifications unless they burden a fundamental right or target a suspect class—Missouri’s law need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 312-13 (1976) (per curiam).

The Eighth Circuit identified several legitimate state interests served by the mandatory retirement provision: ensuring judicial competence, reducing delays caused by the death or disability of older judges, and maintaining public confidence in the judiciary. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 608-09. These interests are sufficient to survive rational basis review. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314 (upholding a similar retirement age for state troopers); *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (upholding mandatory retirement for Illinois judges), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979).

Petitioners’ arguments to the contrary are unavailing. The fact that judges may retain their mental faculties beyond age 70 does not render the classification irrational, as age is not a precise proxy for competence. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314. Nor does the provision violate equal protection because it applies only to judges; the state may treat different classes of officials differently based on their unique roles. *Id.* at 313. The Eighth Circuit’s conclusion that Missouri’s law survives rational basis review is consistent with Supreme Court precedent and must be affirmed.
