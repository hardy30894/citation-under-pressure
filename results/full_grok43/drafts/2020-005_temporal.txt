The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident corporate defendant whose in-state activities create a substantial connection to the litigation, even absent a strict causal link between those contacts and the plaintiff’s particular claim. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945) (jurisdiction proper where defendant’s “minimum contacts” with the forum make it reasonable to require defense of the suit there); Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940) (due process satisfied when defendant’s relationship to the state is “such as to make it reasonable” to subject it to suit).

Ford Motor Company has long maintained systematic and continuous contacts with Montana by advertising its vehicles, selling replacement parts, and servicing its products through authorized dealers in the State. These activities constitute purposeful availment of the privilege of conducting business in Montana. Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia, 339 U.S. 643, 647 (1950) (sustained solicitation and sales within a state subject the corporation to the “burdens as well as the benefits” of local regulation and adjudication). The claims here—alleging design defect and failure to warn in a Ford Explorer—directly implicate the safety and quality of the very products Ford markets and supports within Montana. Such a connection renders the exercise of jurisdiction consistent with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe, 326 U.S. at 316.

Requiring Ford to answer in Montana does not offend due process merely because the particular vehicle reached the State through a later resale rather than a direct sale by Ford. The defendant’s extensive in-state presence supplies the requisite affiliation between the forum, the defendant, and the litigation. Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958) (jurisdiction turns on whether the defendant’s own conduct creates a “substantial connection” with the forum). Montana’s interest in providing a convenient forum for its residents injured by allegedly defective products sold and serviced there further supports jurisdiction. McGee v. International Life Ins. Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957).

Accordingly, the Montana courts properly exercised specific personal jurisdiction.
