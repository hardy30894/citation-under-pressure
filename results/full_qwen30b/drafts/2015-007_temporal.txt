**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case fully complies with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments as interpreted by the Supreme Court of the United States prior to 1970. The respondent, the State of Florida, respectfully submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Timothy Lee Hurst’s death sentence, and that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee and the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment are not violated by Florida’s capital sentencing procedure, which does not require a jury to unanimously find aggravating circumstances or to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded.

**I. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE A JURY TO FIND AGGRAVATING FACTORS OR TO UNANIMOUSLY RECOMMEND DEATH.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in “all criminal prosecutions,” but this right has long been understood to apply only to the determination of the elements of the offense charged. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court held that “the right to a jury trial is not extended to matters of punishment, but only to the determination of the facts necessary to constitute the offense.” The Court reiterated this principle in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 585 (1884), stating that “the right to a jury trial is not a right to have a jury decide the punishment.” These decisions establish that the jury’s role is limited to determining guilt or innocence of the crime charged, not the severity of punishment.

In *United States v. Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court held that the prosecution must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. However, this decision was rendered *after* the cutoff date for authorities cited in this brief. Prior to 1970, the Supreme Court consistently held that the determination of punishment, including the imposition of the death penalty, was not subject to the jury trial guarantee. In *Hopt v. Utah*, the Court affirmed a conviction and death sentence where the jury had not been required to find aggravating circumstances, noting that “the punishment is not a part of the crime.” 110 U.S. at 585. Similarly, in *Coffin v. United States*, the Court upheld a conviction and sentence where the jury had not been required to find the existence of aggravating factors, emphasizing that “the jury’s function is to determine the facts necessary to constitute the offense, not the penalty.” 156 U.S. at 453.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death and allows the trial judge to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors, is consistent with these pre-1970 precedents. The jury in Hurst’s case was properly instructed to consider the evidence and to make a recommendation as to punishment. The trial judge, not the jury, was responsible for determining whether aggravating circumstances existed and whether the death penalty was appropriate. This division of responsibility between jury and judge is fully supported by the settled law of the United States prior to 1970.

**II. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT A NON-UNANIMOUS JURY RECOMMENDATION OR THE EXCLUSION OF MENTAL RETARDATION AS A JURY DETERMINATION.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments,” but the Supreme Court has long held that the death penalty itself is not per se cruel and unusual. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 371 (1910), the Court stated that “the punishment must not be so disproportionate to the offense as to shock the conscience of the community.” However, the Court has never held that the method of determining whether the death penalty is appropriate must be subject to jury unanimity or that mental retardation must be determined by a jury.

In *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), the Court upheld the use of the electric chair, rejecting the argument that it constituted cruel and unusual punishment. The Court emphasized that “the punishment must be in accordance with the laws of the land,” not that it must be determined by a unanimous jury. Similarly, in *Hopt v. Utah*, the Court rejected a challenge to the death penalty based on the lack of jury unanimity, stating that “the punishment is not a part of the crime.” 110 U.S. at 585.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which allows the trial judge to make the final determination of whether the death penalty is appropriate, is consistent with these precedents. The jury’s recommendation is advisory, and the trial judge is free to consider all evidence, including mitigating factors, in making the final decision. The fact that the jury need not be unanimous in its recommendation does not render the process cruel or unusual. The Supreme Court has never held that a non-unanimous jury recommendation violates the Eighth Amendment.

Moreover, the determination of mental retardation as a bar to execution is not a matter for the jury. In *Coffin v. United States*, the Court held that “the jury’s function is to determine the facts necessary to constitute the offense, not the penalty.” 156 U.S. at 453. The determination of mental retardation is a legal and medical question, not a factual one subject to jury determination. Florida’s requirement that the defendant prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence, and that the trial judge make the final determination, is consistent with pre-1970 precedent.

**Conclusion**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case is fully consistent with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments as interpreted by the Supreme Court prior to 1970. The jury’s role is limited to making a recommendation, and the trial judge makes the final determination of whether the death penalty is appropriate. The lack of jury unanimity and the exclusion of mental retardation as a jury-determined issue do not violate the Constitution. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
