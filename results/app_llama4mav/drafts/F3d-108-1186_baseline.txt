## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Clarify the Jury's Verdict

The district court's refusal to clarify the jury's ambiguous verdict was reversible error. When a jury returns an inconsistent or ambiguous verdict, the trial court has a duty to clarify the verdict to ensure that it accurately reflects the jury's intentions. See, e.g., University Computing Co. v. Lykes-Youngstown Corp., 504 F.2d 518, 548 n. 43 (5th Cir. 1974) ("When a verdict is ambiguous or inconsistent, the trial court has a duty to require the jury to clarify the verdict."). The court's failure to do so here resulted in a misinterpretation of the verdict that prejudiced Unit.

Unit requested that the trial judge question the jury to clarify the damage awards before the jury was discharged, but the court refused. This refusal was error, as it denied Unit the opportunity to have the jury clarify its intentions. See, e.g., Dickerson v. Pritchard, 706 F.2d 256, 258-59 (8th Cir. 1983) (holding that the trial court's refusal to clarify an ambiguous verdict was reversible error).

### II. The District Court Erred in Interpreting the Jury's Verdict

The district court's interpretation of the jury's verdict was incorrect. The court subtracted the amount of Enron's set-off ($86,494.30) from the amount of damages awarded to Unit on Form 1 ($70,768.02), resulting in a net award of zero damages to Unit. However, this interpretation ignores the possibility that the jury had already taken into account Enron's set-off when awarding damages to Unit on Form 1.

A more plausible interpretation of the verdict is that the jury intended to award Unit $70,768.02 after taking into account Enron's set-off. This is supported by the fact that the difference between the amount of the unpaid invoices ($157,262.36) and the amount of Enron's set-off ($86,494.30) is $70,768.06, which is very close to the amount awarded to Unit on Form 1 ($70,768.02). The jury's verdict should be interpreted in a way that gives effect to its apparent intention to award Unit damages. See, e.g., Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963) ("[A] verdict should be construed, if possible, in a manner that will uphold it.").

### III. The Jury Instructions Were Confusing and Prejudicial

Although Unit waived its right to claim error in the jury instructions by failing to object specifically at trial, the instructions were nonetheless confusing and prejudicial. The verdict forms were complex and open to multiple interpretations, and the trial court's failure to provide clear guidance to the jury resulted in an ambiguous verdict.

The confusion in the verdict forms could have been cured with a simple instruction telling the jury not to award Enron a set-off on Form 3 if they had already taken into account Enron's damages when awarding damages to Unit on Form 1. Unit's general objection to the complexity of the verdict forms was sufficient to put the trial court on notice of the potential for confusion. See, e.g., Aspen Highlands Skiing Corp. v. Aspen Skiing Co., 738 F.2d 1509, 1515-16 (10th Cir. 1984) (holding that a general objection to jury instructions can be sufficient to preserve error if it puts the trial court on notice of the issue).

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
