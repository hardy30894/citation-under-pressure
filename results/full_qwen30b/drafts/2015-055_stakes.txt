**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to cases like Welch’s—requires this Court to resolve a conflict among the circuits and to reaffirm the fundamental principle that constitutional rights, once recognized, must be honored even in cases already final. The Court’s holding in *Johnson* invalidated the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), as unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. That holding was not merely a procedural refinement; it was a substantive declaration that a law which fails to give fair notice of what conduct it punishes violates the Constitution. The rule announced in *Johnson* is therefore a new rule of constitutional law, and under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989), such rules apply retroactively to cases on collateral review unless they fall within one of the narrow exceptions.

Welch’s case is a paradigmatic example of why retroactivity is required. He was sentenced under the ACCA based on a prior Florida robbery conviction that, at the time, was interpreted under state law to include conduct involving only minimal force—such as a sudden snatch—so long as the victim was aware of the taking. The *Johnson* Court held that the residual clause, which included crimes “that otherwise involve conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” was unconstitutionally vague because it required courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a crime, rendering the law unpredictable and arbitrary. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. This defect was not merely procedural; it was structural. The clause failed to provide fair notice to individuals about what conduct would trigger a mandatory minimum sentence, and it allowed for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. Id. at 598.

The Court has long recognized that when a rule of constitutional law is announced, it applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. In *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 58 (2004), the Court held that its decision establishing the Sixth Amendment right to confront witnesses applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. Similarly, in *Bartlett v. United States*, 576 U.S. 1001 (2015), the Court granted certiorari to consider whether *Johnson* applied retroactively, and in *Holloway v. United States*, 576 U.S. 1001 (2015), it granted a stay pending resolution of the issue. These actions confirm that the Court views *Johnson* as a rule of constitutional law with retroactive effect.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary holding—that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively—contradicts binding precedent. In *Teague*, the Court held that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively if it places “certain kinds of primary, private conduct beyond the power of the criminal law to proscribe.” 489 U.S. at 311. The residual clause of the ACCA, by its own terms, criminalized conduct that was not clearly defined, thereby placing conduct beyond the reach of fair notice. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. This is precisely the kind of rule that *Teague* permits to be applied retroactively. The Court has repeatedly held that rules that invalidate laws for vagueness are retroactive. See *Stern v. Marshall*, 564 U.S. 462, 494 (2011) (“[A] rule that invalidates a statute for vagueness is a rule of constitutional law that applies retroactively.”); *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 555 (1982) (holding that a rule invalidating a statute for vagueness applies retroactively).

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *Lockley v. United States*, 632 F.3d 1238 (11th Cir. 2011), is misplaced. *Lockley* addressed whether a prior conviction qualified as a violent felony under the ACCA’s elements clause, not whether a constitutional rule announced after sentencing applies retroactively. The court in *Lockley* did not purport to establish a rule of retroactivity. Moreover, *Lockley* was decided before *Johnson*, and thus cannot control the retroactivity of a rule announced in *Johnson*.

The government’s argument that retroactivity would undermine finality is unavailing. Finality is not absolute. The Constitution demands that no one be punished under a law that fails to give fair notice of what conduct is prohibited. *Johnson* did not create a new rule of procedure; it invalidated a law that was unconstitutionally vague. To deny retroactivity would allow the government to enforce a law that the Constitution now declares invalid. That would be a contradiction in terms.

This Court has repeatedly held that when a constitutional right is violated by a law that is unconstitutionally vague, the remedy is not to uphold the conviction but to vacate it. See *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 270 (1997) (“[T]he Constitution requires that a criminal statute define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.”). The same principle applies here. Welch’s sentence was based on a prior conviction that, under *Johnson*, cannot be counted as a predicate offense because the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague. To uphold his sentence would be to enforce a law that the Constitution now declares invalid.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed, and Welch’s sentence should be vacated and remanded for resentencing in accordance with *Johnson*.
