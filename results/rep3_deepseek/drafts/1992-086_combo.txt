**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Denial of Access Constituted Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination in a Public Forum.**

The Court below erred in classifying the school facilities as a “limited public forum” and in holding that the exclusion of religious speech was viewpoint-neutral. Even assuming the forum was limited, the District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups—while permitting secular groups addressing the same subjects—is discrimination based on the viewpoint of the speaker, not the subject matter. This Court has long held that the First Amendment forbids the government from granting access to a forum for discussion of a topic while denying access to speakers whose views on that topic are religiously motivated.

In *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558 (1948), this Court struck down a licensing scheme that gave an official unbridled discretion to deny use of public address systems based on the content of the speech. More directly, in *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), this Court reversed a conviction for using a public park without a permit where the denial was based on the religious views of the speakers, holding that the “lack of standards in the ordinance” permitted “uncontrolled official suppression” of speech. *Id.* at 272. The principle is clear: once a forum is open to certain speakers or topics, the government may not exclude speakers because of the particular views they hold.

Here, the District permitted use of its facilities by the Boy Scouts, the Southern Harmonizers, and a lecture series on “Psychology and The Unknown.” These groups addressed family values, child rearing, and moral development. Lamb’s Chapel sought to address the identical subjects—family values and child rearing—from a Christian perspective. The District’s denial was not a neutral time, place, or manner restriction; it was a flat ban on religious speech. As this Court stated in *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the First Amendment embraces “the right to speak freely and to advocate ideas” and “the right to communicate” those ideas, *id.* at 310, and that right is not lost when the ideas are religious.

**II. The District’s Rule Is an Unconstitutional Prior Restraint on Religious Speech.**

The District’s Local Rule No. 7, which provides that “[t]he school premises shall not be used by any group for religious purposes,” operates as a prior restraint on speech. This Court has consistently held that prior restraints are presumptively invalid. *See Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697 (1931). In *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), this Court invalidated a city ordinance that gave the police commissioner discretion to permit or deny religious meetings on public streets, holding that the ordinance was an unconstitutional prior restraint because it gave “an administrative official discretionary power to control in advance the right of citizens to speak on religious matters.” *Id.* at 293–94.

The District’s rule is even more restrictive than the ordinance in *Kunz*: it is an absolute, categorical ban on all religious speech, with no discretion and no standards. Such a blanket prohibition cannot survive scrutiny. In *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), this Court reversed a conviction of a Jehovah’s Witness for holding a religious service in a public park, holding that the state could not “deny to one group the use of public property because of the content of their speech” while permitting other groups to use it. *Id.* at 69–70.

**III. The District’s Distinction Between Religious and Secular Speech Is Unconstitutional.**

The District argues that it has not opened its facilities to religious groups and therefore may exclude them. But this argument conflates subject matter with viewpoint. The District has opened its facilities to groups discussing family values, child rearing, and moral instruction. Lamb’s Chapel sought to discuss those same subjects. The only difference is the religious perspective from which the discussion would proceed. This Court has long held that the government may not discriminate against speech because of the speaker’s religious viewpoint. *See Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105 (1943) (invalidating a license tax on door-to-door sales of religious literature, holding that the First Amendment “can neither be narrowed nor be allowed to be narrowed” by a tax on religious speech). *See also Follett v. Town of McCormick*, 321 U.S. 573 (1944) (same).

The District’s rule is not a content-neutral regulation of the time, place, or manner of speech. It is a content-based exclusion of an entire category of speech—religious speech—from a forum otherwise open to the public. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), this Court upheld a parade permit requirement only because it was a neutral regulation of time, place, and manner. Here, the District’s rule is not neutral; it singles out religious speech for exclusion. As this Court held in *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946), the First Amendment’s protections apply with full force to speech on property that is open to the public, and the state may not “impose criminal punishment for the exercise of First Amendment rights” on such property. *Id.* at 509.

**IV. The District’s Justification—Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation—Is Meritless.**

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation does not justify the exclusion. This Court has repeatedly held that the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech from public forums. In *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), this Court held that the First Amendment does not require “a hostility to religion” and that government may accommodate religious speech. *Id.* at 314. The District’s fear of an Establishment Clause violation is unfounded, as the mere access of a religious group to a public forum on equal terms with secular groups does not constitute an establishment of religion. *See Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947) (holding that the Establishment Clause requires neutrality, not hostility, toward religion).

**V. The District’s Denial Violates the Equal Protection Clause.**

Finally, the District’s denial of access to Lamb’s Chapel, while granting access to secular groups addressing the same topics, violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. This Court has held that the government may not discriminate against religious speakers in the distribution of public benefits. *See Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 268 U.S. 510 (1925) (recognizing the right of religious groups to equal treatment under the law). The District’s rule creates an impermissible classification based on the religious content of speech, which cannot survive even rational-basis review, let alone the strict scrutiny applicable to content-based restrictions on speech.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
