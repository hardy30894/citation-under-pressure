**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE RELEASE DOES NOT BAR BANCORP’S CLAIMS BECAUSE ITS LANGUAGE IS LIMITED TO CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE DECISION TO CLOSE THE BANK, NOT TO THE FDIC’S SUBSEQUENT CONDUCT AS RECEIVER**

A release, like any other contract, is governed by ordinary principles of contract construction, and its scope is limited to what the parties actually intended to relinquish. *Mangini v. McClurg*, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969) (a release is to be interpreted in light of “the controversy being settled” and the intention of the parties as manifested by the language used). This Court has likewise recognized that releases executed under federal receivership settlements are construed under state contract law principles, giving effect to the plain terms the parties chose. *Bank of Am. Nat’l Trust & Sav. Ass’n v. Gillaizeau*, 766 F.2d 709, 715 (2d Cir. 1985) (releases are “governed by principles of contract law” and construed according to the parties’ intent).

The release Bancorp signed on June 6, 1988, discharged the FDIC only “from any and all claims which Bancorp … has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language is textually confined to the *closure decision* itself—a discrete administrative act by the OCC, not the FDIC. It says nothing about the FDIC’s subsequent administration of the receivership: the manner in which it liquidated assets, allocated expenses, or paid itself post-insolvency interest years later. Those acts are temporally and substantively distinct from the “decision … to close the Bank.” As the New York Court of Appeals has cautioned, a release “may not be read to cover matters which the parties did not intend to cover,” however broad its literal wording. *Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292, 299 (1959). Because the exchange of releases in 1988 arose out of settlement of the *Chuang* and *May 1986* actions—disputes concerning the closure and its immediate aftermath—there is no basis to read the release as anticipating or covering the FDIC’s later self-dealing in administering the receivership estate. The district court therefore erred in extending the release beyond its stated subject matter.

**II. BANCORP’S CLAIMS ARE NOT TIME-BARRED BECAUSE, AS TO A RECEIVER’S BREACH OF FIDUCIARY DUTIES IN ADMINISTERING AN ESTATE, THE LIMITATIONS PERIOD DOES NOT BEGIN TO RUN UNTIL THE RECEIVERSHIP TERMINATES**

New York applies a six-year limitations period to claims for breach of fiduciary duty, unjust enrichment, and corporate waste. N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). But the running of that period depends on when the cause of action *accrues*, and New York law has long recognized that where a fiduciary relationship—such as a trust or receivership—continues, the statute does not begin to run until that relationship ends or is openly repudiated. *Spallholz v. Sheldon*, 216 N.Y. 205, 209 (1915) (holding that the statute of limitations does not run against a beneficiary “until there has been an open repudiation” of the fiduciary’s obligations or the relationship is otherwise terminated). The same principle applies to a trustee’s duty to account, which does not accrue until the trust is closed. *In re Barabash*, 31 N.Y.2d 76, 80 (1972).

The FDIC, acting as receiver of the Bank, occupied a fiduciary role functionally equivalent to that of a trustee administering an estate for the benefit of creditors and, ultimately, the shareholder—Bancorp. See *Chuang*, 690 F. Supp. at 193 (describing FDIC’s receivership role in marshaling and distributing the Bank’s assets). Bancorp’s claims—that the FDIC unjustly enriched itself through post-insolvency interest payments, breached its fiduciary duty in liquidating assets, wasted corporate assets by charging the estate for its own expenses, and failed to render an adequate accounting—are all claims that concern the FDIC’s conduct *as fiduciary* during the pendency of that receivership. Under the *Spallholz* and *Barabash* line of authority, such claims do not accrue, and the limitations clock does not begin, while the fiduciary relationship persists.

Here, the receivership did not terminate until November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed this action on October 31, 1995—one day *before* termination, while the FDIC’s fiduciary obligations to account for and properly administer the estate were still ongoing. Because the limitations period for claims arising from that fiduciary administration could not have begun to run before the receivership’s termination, Bancorp’s complaint was necessarily timely: it was filed before the six-year period had even started to run, let alone expired. The district court’s contrary conclusion, measuring the limitations period from the dates of the individual challenged transactions (e.g., the FDIC’s first post-insolvency interest payment in 1991), improperly treated each administrative act as an isolated, self-contained wrong rather than as part of the FDIC’s continuous fiduciary administration of the receivership estate—an approach inconsistent with the settled New York rule that a continuing fiduciary relationship tolls accrual until its termination.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings on the merits of Bancorp’s claims.
