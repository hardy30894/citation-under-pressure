**ARGUMENT**

**I. Bond Lacks Standing to Assert a Tenth Amendment Challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229**

The Government respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Supreme Court has long held that private parties lack standing to assert Tenth Amendment challenges to federal legislation. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, the Court rejected a challenge to the TVA, stating that "the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment." 306 U.S. at 144. This principle was reaffirmed by the First Circuit, which noted that "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes." *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d at 35.

This standing issue is jurisdictional and must be resolved before the merits. In *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, the Supreme Court held that "courts must decide jurisdictional issues before considering the merits of a claim." 523 U.S. at 101. Because Bond's Tenth Amendment challenge implicates the scope of federal power, the Court must dismiss it for lack of standing.

Bond’s claim is an undifferentiated grievance against the federal government. The Eighth Circuit has held that "private parties lack standing to assert a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal statute." *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d at 525. Similarly, the Tenth Circuit has rejected such challenges, finding that "the Tenth Amendment is a limitation on the federal government, not a source of rights enforceable by private litigants." *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284. Bond’s claim is not a concrete injury but a generalized grievance about the scope of federal power.

While the Government concedes that § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power under *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432, the validity of the statute does not confer standing on Bond. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Lara* explained that "the Treaty Power permits the President, 'by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties.'" 541 U.S. at 201. Even if the statute were valid, Bond cannot use it to challenge the federal government’s authority. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary holding in *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis* is an outlier and has been criticized for ignoring the clear precedent of *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* and the structural limitations of the Tenth Amendment. 185 F.3d at 703. Therefore, the Court lacks jurisdiction to consider Bond’s Tenth Amendment claim.
