## Argument

The decision below erroneously holds that the Merit Systems Protection Board’s dismissal of a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds must be reviewed in the Federal Circuit rather than a district court. That holding conflicts with the plain language of the governing statutes and with fundamental principles of federal jurisdiction. Because Mr. Perry alleged both an adverse action appealable to the Board and discrimination prohibited by federal law, his case is a “mixed case” under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), and judicial review lies exclusively in district court.

### I. The Statutory Text Unambiguously Requires District Court Review of All Mixed Cases, Regardless of the Board’s Basis for Dismissal

The Civil Service Reform Act provides that when an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination,” the case is a mixed case. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). For such cases, “the employee may file a civil action” in district court. *Id.* § 7703(b)(2). The statute does not carve out an exception for cases in which the Board later dismisses the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. When the language of a statute is plain, “the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Courts may not add limitations that Congress did not place in the text. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) (“[P]enal laws are to be construed strictly.”).

The D.C. Circuit’s distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals reads an extra-textual condition into the statute. The statute defines a mixed case by reference to the *action* affecting the employee and the *allegation* of discrimination—not by the Board’s ultimate decision on its own jurisdiction. Mr. Perry’s complaint alleged that he was suspended and constructively retired—actions that are appealable to the Board under 5 U.S.C. § 7512—and that those actions were motivated by discrimination. That is sufficient to make his case a mixed case at the time of filing. The Board’s later determination that the settlement was voluntary and thus outside its jurisdiction does not retroactively alter the character of the case as pleaded.

### II. The Character of a Case Is Determined by the Pleadings, Not by the Agency’s Subsequent Jurisdictional Ruling

It is a settled principle of federal jurisdiction that “the question whether a case is one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States is to be determined by the allegations of the complaint.” *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913). This well-pleaded complaint rule applies with equal force to administrative appeals. *See Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908) (jurisdiction depends on plaintiff’s statement of his own claim). Here, Mr. Perry’s appeal to the Board alleged that he was affected by a suspension and retirement—actions that are appealable under the statute—and that those actions resulted from discrimination. The Board’s subsequent jurisdictional ruling cannot change the fact that, at the time of filing, Mr. Perry presented a mixed case. The proper forum for review is therefore district court.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary approach would require employees to predict whether the Board will later find jurisdiction before choosing a reviewing court. That creates precisely the kind of uncertainty that the well-pleaded complaint rule is designed to avoid. *See Mollan v. Torrance*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 537, 539 (1824) (jurisdiction is determined at the commencement of the suit). An employee who in good faith alleges discrimination and an appealable action should not be forced to litigate the threshold jurisdictional question in the Federal Circuit—a court that lacks expertise in discrimination law—simply because the Board later disagrees with the employee’s characterization of the action as involuntary.

### III. The Board’s Jurisdictional Determination Is Intertwined with the Merits of the Discrimination Claim, Reinforcing the Need for District Court Review

In this case, the Board’s jurisdictional ruling turned on whether Mr. Perry’s retirement and suspension were voluntary. That question is inextricably linked to his discrimination claim: if the agency coerced his settlement through discriminatory animus, the settlement was involuntary and the Board had jurisdiction. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 54–55 (1932) (recognizing that jurisdictional facts may be reviewed de novo when they are “intertwined with the merits”). The D.C. Circuit acknowledged this overlap but nevertheless held that the Federal Circuit should decide the jurisdictional issue in isolation. That approach fragments the case and forces the discrimination claim to be litigated in a forum that does not regularly handle such claims. District courts, by contrast, have “original jurisdiction” over discrimination actions under Title VII and related statutes. *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) (per curiam). Congress intended discrimination claims to be heard in district court, where they can receive de novo review and where the court’s equitable powers are fully available.

### IV. The Federal Circuit’s Exclusive Jurisdiction Over Non-Mixed Cases Does Not Extend to Mixed Cases Dismissed on Jurisdictional Grounds

The Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from the Board in cases that do not involve discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). But that default rule yields when the employee brings a mixed case. *Id.* § 7703(b)(2). The D.C. Circuit’s decision effectively resurrects the default rule for a subset of mixed cases, contrary to the statutory scheme. An agency cannot expand its own jurisdiction by characterizing a dismissal as jurisdictional. *Interstate Commerce Commission v. Cincinnati, New Orleans & Texas Pacific Ry. Co.*, 167 U.S. 479, 502 (1897) (agency cannot “enlarge its jurisdiction by its own order”). Here, the Board’s characterization of its dismissal as jurisdictional does not transform Mr. Perry’s mixed case into a non-mixed case. The statute defines the forum for review by reference to the employee’s allegations, not the Board’s reasoning.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case should be transferred to the appropriate district court for review of the Board’s decision.
