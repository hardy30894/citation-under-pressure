## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Concluding That Shapiro Demonstrated a Likelihood of Success on the Merits

A preliminary injunction is an extraordinary remedy warranted only when the movant establishes “(a) irreparable harm and (b) either (1) likelihood of success on the merits or (2) sufficiently serious questions going to the merits to make them a fair ground for litigation and a balance of hardships tipping decidedly in the movant’s favor.” *Jackson Dairy, Inc. v. H.P. Hood & Sons, Inc.*, 596 F.2d 70, 72 (2d Cir. 1979). The district court erred in finding that Shapiro satisfied this standard, because the requested accommodation—immediate allocation of a parking space outside the established waiting list—is not “reasonable” within the meaning of the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f).

**A. The Accommodation Is Not Reasonable Under the FHAA**

The FHAA makes it unlawful to “discriminate against any person in the terms, conditions, or privileges of sale or rental of a dwelling, or in the provision of services or facilities in connection with such dwelling, because of a handicap.” 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(2). Discrimination includes “a refusal to make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford such person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling.” 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). An accommodation is “reasonable” only if it “does not impose an undue hardship or a fundamental alteration in the nature of the program.” *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Management Co.*, 29 F.3d 1413, 1416 (9th Cir. 1994); *see also Roe v. Housing Authority*, 909 F.2d 594, 598 (1st Cir. 1990) (same).

Here, the accommodation Shapiro demands—immediate assignment of a parking space without regard to the building’s first-come/first-served waiting list—would fundamentally alter Cadman Towers’ parking allocation system. That system is neutral, applies equally to all residents, and is designed to fairly allocate a scarce resource: only 66 parking spaces exist for 302 apartments at 101 Clark Street. See Record at ___. Bypassing the waiting list for Shapiro would necessarily displace another resident who has waited years for a space, or would require the cooperative to create a new space where none exists, which is physically impossible. Such an accommodation “goes beyond what is required” because it would “impose an undue burden” on the housing provider. *Smith & Lee Associates v. City of Taylor*, 13 F.3d 920, 929 (6th Cir. 1993) (affirming that a requested accommodation is unreasonable if it “would result in a fundamental alteration of the nature of the program”).

Moreover, the FHAA does not require a housing provider to grant a person with a disability “priority over other residents,” especially where the provider maintains a “neutral waiting list” that is applied without regard to disability. *Hunt v. Aimco Properties*, 814 F. Supp. 1055, 1060 (M.D. Fla. 1993). The district court’s ruling effectively forces Cadman Towers to adopt a disability-based preference in the allocation of parking spaces, which would undermine the cooperative’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory system and could disadvantage others who also have disabilities or health-related needs. *Cf. City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432, 448 (1985) (holding that even legitimate accommodations must be balanced against the rights of others).

**B. The First-Come/First-Served Policy Is Not Discriminatory**

Cadman Towers’ waiting list is a facially neutral rule that does not discriminate against persons with disabilities. Shapiro had the same opportunity as every other resident to request a parking space and place her name on the list. The fact that her disability makes street parking more difficult does not render the policy discriminatory. The FHAA does not require housing providers to change their rules “merely because a person with a disability would derive greater benefit from a different arrangement.” *Bangerter v. Orem City Corp.*, 46 F.3d 1491, 1503 (10th Cir. 1995) (explaining that reasonable accommodation must be “necessary” to afford equal opportunity, not merely convenient). The district court failed to apply this demanding standard.

### II. Shapiro Failed to Demonstrate Irreparable Harm

Irreparable harm must be “imminent and not remote or speculative.” *Plaza Health Laboratories v. Perales*, 878 F.2d 577, 580 (2d Cir. 1989). Shapiro had managed for two years without a parking space, using public transportation and car services. Her urinary difficulties, while serious, were not shown to be irreparable in the sense that no remedy at law exists. She had alternative means of transportation, and her delay in obtaining a space did not mean she was being denied housing altogether. The district court’s finding of irreparable harm improperly rested on the assumption that any inconvenience constitutes irreparable injury.

### III. The Balance of Equities and Public Interest Favor Denying the Injunction

Granting Shapiro a parking space outside the waiting list harms other residents—including at least one elderly resident who was permitted to have her son use her space—by skewing a fair, established process. The public interest lies in preserving the ability of housing providers to maintain neutral allocation systems that treat all residents equally. *See Bangerter*, 46 F.3d at 1504. The district court’s injunction, if upheld, would create a precedent requiring cooperatives to subvert their rules whenever a resident with a disability requests priority, thereby inviting disputes among tenants and undermining the cooperative’s governance structure.

For these reasons, the preliminary injunction should be vacated.
