The United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim against the United States is barred by the statute of limitations under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA). The plain language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party that has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from any person who is not a party to the settlement. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA constitutes such a settlement, as it required Guam to undertake specific remedial actions, including closing the Ordot Dump and designing a cover system.

The Consent Decree's substantive provisions demonstrate that it "resolved" Guam's liability within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B). See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) (explaining that § 113(f)(3)(B) "provide[s] a cause of action for contribution in the absence of a suit"). By agreeing to the Consent Decree, Guam assumed obligations consistent with a finding of liability, thereby triggering its right to seek contribution under § 113(f). See Asarco LLC v. Atl. Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (noting that parties often refuse to admit liability under a settlement agreement while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability).

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree's disclaimer of liability precludes a finding that it "resolved" Guam's liability is unpersuasive. As the Ninth Circuit observed in Asarco, the mere fact that a party refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt a consent decree from the reach of § 113(f)(3)(B). Id. The substantive terms of the Consent Decree, which required Guam to undertake significant remedial actions, demonstrate that it "resolved" Guam's liability for purposes of § 113(f)(3)(B).

Furthermore, Guam's contention that the Consent Decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it addressed Clean Water Act violations is without merit. The Complaint demanded that Guam complete construction of a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate, which is a "remedial action" under CERCLA § 101(24). 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). Therefore, the Consent Decree is a settlement that triggers Guam's right to seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B).

As a result, Guam's claim against the United States is time-barred under CERCLA § 113(g)(3), which imposes a three-year statute of limitations on contribution claims. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). The Court of Appeals correctly held that Guam's claim is barred by the statute of limitations, and this Court should affirm that decision.

The decision below is consistent with the principles of statutory interpretation and the plain language of CERCLA. See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (explaining that CERCLA's provisions should be interpreted according to their plain meaning). Guam's arguments to the contrary are unpersuasive, and the Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
