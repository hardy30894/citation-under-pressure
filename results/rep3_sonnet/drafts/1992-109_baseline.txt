# ARGUMENT

## THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR COMPETENCY TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL

### A. The Competency Standard for Pleading Guilty or Waiving Counsel Is the Same as the Standard for Competency to Stand Trial

This Court has long held that the standard for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and has "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960). This Court reaffirmed that standard in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), holding that due process requires that a defendant possess "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 171 (quoting *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402).

The Ninth Circuit erred by grafting onto this settled standard a new and higher requirement—that a defendant possess the capacity for "reasoned choice" among available alternatives—whenever a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel. Nothing in this Court's precedents supports bifurcating competency into different standards depending on what right is being waived. The *Dusky* standard focuses on whether a defendant can rationally understand the proceedings and communicate with counsel; it does not vary based on the particular decision the defendant faces. A defendant who can rationally understand the charges against him and consult with an attorney is, by definition, capable of understanding the choice between pleading guilty and standing trial, or between representing himself and accepting counsel.

The Ninth Circuit's approach conflates two distinct due process inquiries: (1) whether the defendant is *competent*, and (2) whether the defendant's waiver of trial rights is *knowing and voluntary*. This Court has clearly distinguished those inquiries. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that a valid waiver of the right to counsel requires an "intelligent and competent waiver," *id.* at 465, and that courts must determine whether, "under the particular facts and circumstances surrounding [the] case," the accused knowingly and intelligently waived his rights. *Id.* at 464. Similarly, in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), this Court required that a guilty plea be entered "voluntarily and understandingly" as a matter of due process, *id.* at 244, but did not suggest that the competency threshold itself changes when a defendant chooses to plead guilty rather than proceed to trial.

The voluntariness inquiry protects against pleas induced by coercion, misinformation, or lack of understanding of the specific rights being waived. That inquiry is separate from, and additional to, the basic competency determination under *Dusky*. A trial court that finds a defendant competent under *Dusky* and separately determines that his plea or waiver was knowing and voluntary has fully satisfied due process. No third, heightened competency standard is constitutionally required.

### B. A Single, Unitary Competency Standard Is Consistent with This Court's Functional Approach to Due Process

This Court's competency jurisprudence has consistently focused on functional capacities relevant to a fair trial process, not on the gravity of the particular decision being made. In *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that a defendant may not be tried unless he "has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer" and possesses "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him," reiterating the *Dusky* formulation. *Id.* at 387 (citing *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402). This capacity-based standard applies regardless of whether the defendant faces a full trial or seeks to forgo one through a guilty plea.

The reasoning underlying the *Dusky* standard applies with equal force to decisions to waive counsel or plead guilty. A defendant who has the rational and factual understanding necessary to stand trial necessarily has the capacity to understand the alternative of pleading guilty—which is, after all, a less complex proceeding than a full trial. It is illogical to require a *higher* threshold of competency for a *simpler* legal proceeding. The Ninth Circuit's rule inverts the relationship between the complexity of a proceeding and the competency required to navigate it.

### C. The Ninth Circuit's "Reasoned Choice" Standard Lacks Grounding in This Court's Precedent and Improperly Expands Due Process Requirements

The Ninth Circuit relied on its own prior decisions—*Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny—to fashion the "reasoned choice" standard. But those decisions are not binding on this Court, and they misread the constitutional requirements this Court has articulated. This Court has never held that competency to waive constitutional rights during a criminal proceeding demands "a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial," as the Ninth Circuit asserted below. 972 F.2d at 267. That proposition finds no support in *Dusky*, *Drope*, *Pate*, or *Johnson v. Zerbst*.

Due process is satisfied when a state trial court applies the *Dusky* standard to determine competency and separately ensures that any waiver of rights is knowing and voluntary under *Johnson v. Zerbst* and *Boykin*. The Nevada trial court did precisely that here: it ordered a psychiatric evaluation, found Moran competent under the constitutionally required standard, and then conducted a colloquy to ensure his waiver of counsel and guilty plea were knowing and voluntary. No more was constitutionally required.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that due process requires only the *Dusky* competency standard—not a separate, heightened "reasoned choice" standard—for a defendant seeking to plead guilty or waive counsel.
