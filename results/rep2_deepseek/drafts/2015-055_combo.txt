**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Rule Announced in *Johnson v. United States* Is a Substantive Rule of Constitutional Law That Must Be Applied Retroactively on Collateral Review.**

The question presented is whether the constitutional rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (“ACCA”), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is void for vagueness—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. It does. The rule is substantive, not procedural, and therefore falls within the narrow class of new constitutional rules that this Court has long held must be given retroactive effect.

**A. The *Johnson* Rule Is Substantive Because It Decriminalizes a Class of Conduct and Alters the Range of Conduct That the Law Punishes.**

This Court has consistently held that a new rule is substantive—and thus retroactive—when it “places ‘certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.’” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989) (quoting *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring)). The *Johnson* rule does exactly that. By invalidating the residual clause, the Court held that a felony cannot qualify as a “violent felony” under ACCA merely because it “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). That clause is now a nullity. A defendant cannot be sentenced under ACCA based on a prior conviction that qualifies only under the residual clause. The rule thus narrows the class of persons eligible for the 15-year mandatory minimum and forbids the punishment of conduct that Congress—through the now-void clause—had purported to reach.

This Court’s retroactivity jurisprudence has never limited substantive rules to those that decriminalize conduct outright. Rather, a rule is substantive if it “narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute” or “alter[s] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). *Johnson* does precisely that. It does not merely regulate the manner of determining guilt or sentencing; it removes an entire category of offenses from the reach of ACCA’s enhanced penalties. A defendant whose sentence was enhanced under the residual clause has been punished for conduct that the Constitution no longer permits Congress to punish in that manner.

**B. The *Johnson* Rule Is Not a “Watershed” Procedural Rule; It Is a Rule of Substantive Due Process.**

The government may argue that *Johnson* announced a procedural rule because it concerns the process by which courts evaluate predicate offenses. That argument mischaracterizes the holding. The Court in *Johnson* did not announce a new evidentiary standard or a new method of judicial review. It held that the residual clause “denies fair notice to defendants” and “invites arbitrary enforcement by judges,” in violation of the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. Vagueness is a substantive constitutional defect. A statute that is void for vagueness is void *ab initio*; it cannot be applied to anyone, at any time, in any proceeding.

This Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause prohibits vague criminal statutes. *See Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926) (“[A] statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law.”). A conviction or sentence imposed under a vague statute is not merely procedurally flawed; it is substantively invalid. The rule in *Johnson* is therefore indistinguishable from other substantive rules this Court has applied retroactively. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Coin & Currency*, 401 U.S. 715, 724 (1971) (applying retroactively a rule that a statute was void for vagueness).

**C. This Court’s Pre-*Teague* Precedents Confirm That Constitutional Rules Invalidating Punishment Must Be Applied Retroactively.**

Long before *Teague*, this Court held that a new constitutional rule that affects the substantive validity of a conviction or sentence must be applied to cases on collateral review. In *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965), the Court explained that retroactivity is required when a new rule “places certain kinds of primary conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *See also* *Mackey*, 401 U.S. at 692 (Harlan, J., concurring) (retroactivity is required for rules that “place certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe”). The *Johnson* rule does exactly that: it places the residual clause’s category of offenses beyond the power of Congress to punish under ACCA.

Moreover, this Court has applied new rules retroactively when they invalidate a statute on vagueness grounds. *See, e.g., Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 362 (1964) (applying retroactively a rule that a state statute, as construed, was void for vagueness). The principle is simple: a defendant cannot be punished under a law that the Constitution does not permit to exist. *Johnson* is such a law.

**D. The Eleventh Circuit’s Decision Below Conflicts with the Retroactive Application of Substantive Rules.**

The Eleventh Circuit denied Welch a certificate of appealability before *Johnson* was decided, and it did not revisit that decision after the Court’s ruling. But the court’s underlying determination—that Welch’s Florida strong-arm robbery conviction qualified as a violent felony under the residual clause—cannot survive *Johnson*. The court below relied on the residual clause to affirm Welch’s ACCA enhancement. *See Welch v. United States*, 604 F. App’x 838, 841 (11th Cir. 2015). That reliance is now unconstitutional. Because the residual clause is void, Welch’s sentence cannot stand.

**II. The Rule in *Johnson* Must Be Applied Retroactively Because It Is a “Substantive” Rule Under This Court’s Post-*Teague* Framework.**

Even under the framework this Court adopted in *Teague*, the *Johnson* rule is substantive. *Teague* held that new rules are generally not retroactive on collateral review, but it recognized an exception for “substantive” rules—those that “prohibit[] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense.” *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989). The *Johnson* rule prohibits a category of punishment—the 15-year ACCA mandatory minimum—for a class of defendants whose prior convictions qualify only under the residual clause. That is a substantive rule.

The Court’s decision in *Johnson* itself recognized the substantive nature of the rule. The Court noted that the residual clause’s vagueness “raises the risk of arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement” and “denies fair notice.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. These are substantive due process concerns, not procedural ones. A rule that invalidates a statute on vagueness grounds is necessarily substantive because it renders the statute void for all purposes.

**III. Conclusion**

The rule announced in *Johnson* is substantive and must be applied retroactively. Welch’s sentence was enhanced under a statute that the Constitution forbids. This Court should reverse the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment and remand for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.
