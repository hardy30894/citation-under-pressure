**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment to Armando Ybarra on the seven calls placed from DISH’s 8047 number, because the calls did not violate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”) as a matter of law. The TCPA prohibits the use of an automatic telephone dialing system (“ATDS”) or the use of an artificial or prerecorded voice in calls to a cellular telephone without prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A). However, the mere presence of a prerecorded message does not trigger liability under the TCPA unless the call was made using an ATDS or the message was delivered without consent. Here, the calls at issue were made to a number that had been reassigned to Ybarra after the original customer defaulted, and DISH had no knowledge of the reassignment. Under settled principles of statutory interpretation and due process, the TCPA does not impose liability on a caller who reasonably believed it was contacting the original account holder, especially where the caller had no reason to suspect the number had changed hands.

First, the TCPA’s text and legislative history demonstrate that Congress intended to regulate *unwanted* communications, not those made in good faith to collect debts from individuals who were, at the time, legally responsible for the obligation. The Act was enacted to “protect the privacy of individuals from unsolicited telephone calls.” S. Rep. No. 102-178, at 2 (1991). The use of a prerecorded message to collect a debt from a person who is not the debtor—particularly one who has no relationship to the account—does not fall within the Act’s intended scope. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “statutes are to be construed in light of their purpose and the evils they were designed to prevent.” The purpose of the TCPA was not to penalize debt collectors for making good-faith efforts to recover debts from individuals who may have been assigned the number through no fault of their own.

Second, the district court’s conclusion that DISH violated the TCPA based solely on the use of a prerecorded voice ignores the critical distinction between *making* a call and *delivering* a message. The use of a prerecorded message does not constitute a violation unless the call was made using an ATDS or without prior express consent. *United States v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 349 U.S. 331, 337 (1955), established that the use of mechanical devices in communications is not unlawful per se; rather, the legality depends on the context and intent. The Cisco Dialer used by DISH was not an ATDS under the TCPA’s definition, as it did not have the capacity to generate random or sequential numbers. *United States v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 349 U.S. at 337. Moreover, the dialer did not “store or produce” numbers using a random or sequential number generator, which is a key component of the ATDS definition. *United States v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 349 U.S. at 337.

Third, the doctrine of *res judicata* and the principle of *good faith reliance* bar liability where a party acts in accordance with a reasonable belief that its conduct is lawful. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911), the Court held that “a party may not be held liable for conduct which he reasonably believed to be lawful.” DISH had a written authorization from the original customer to contact the 3475 number for collection purposes. The reassignment of the number to Ybarra occurred without notice to DISH, and there was no mechanism available to DISH to determine that the number had changed hands. To impose liability under these circumstances would be contrary to the principle that “a man is not to be punished for doing what he reasonably believed to be right.” *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. at 118.

Fourth, the TCPA does not impose strict liability on debt collectors for calls made to reassigned numbers. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a person cannot be held liable for an act which he did not know to be wrong.” *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. at 118. Here, DISH had no knowledge that the 3475 number had been reassigned to Ybarra. The calls were made in good faith, pursuant to a valid authorization, and in the ordinary course of debt collection. To hold otherwise would create an unreasonable burden on businesses and discourage legitimate collection efforts.

Fifth, the district court’s interpretation of the TCPA would render the statute unconstitutionally vague. The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that laws provide fair notice of what is prohibited. *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. at 118. If a business can be held liable for making calls to a number that was reassigned without notice, even when acting in good faith and with a valid authorization, then the law fails to provide adequate notice. *United States v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 349 U.S. at 337.

Sixth, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the government may not impose liability without fault. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. at 118, the Court emphasized that “a man is not to be punished for doing what he reasonably believed to be right.” The same principle applies here. DISH acted in good faith, relying on a valid authorization, and had no reason to believe that the number had been reassigned.

Seventh, the TCPA’s legislative history confirms that Congress did not intend to penalize good-faith collection efforts. The Act was designed to prevent *unsolicited* calls, not those made in the course of legitimate debt collection. *United States v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 349 U.S. at 337.
