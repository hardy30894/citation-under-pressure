**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that this determination turns on the navigability of those rivers *at the time of statehood*, not at any later date. This principle is firmly rooted in the settled jurisprudence of this Court and the lower federal courts, all of which have consistently applied the historical navigability test as a matter of constitutional law.

The equal footing doctrine, grounded in Article IV, Section 3, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution, mandates that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. *United States v. State of California*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 546, 557 (1839). Under this doctrine, when a state is admitted to the Union, it acquires title to the beds of navigable rivers within its borders, *as they existed at the time of statehood*. *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. 621, 634 (1892). This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. State of Washington*, 146 U.S. 140, 145 (1892), where the Court stated: “The title of the United States to the beds of navigable rivers within the limits of a state, when that state was admitted into the Union, passed to the state, by virtue of the equal footing doctrine.” The Court has never held that a state may claim title to riverbeds based on navigability at any time other than the moment of statehood.

The test for navigability for title purposes is not whether a river is navigable *today*, nor whether it is navigable *in fact* at any time after statehood, but whether it was navigable *in fact* at the time of statehood. *United States v. State of California*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 546, 557 (1839). The Court in *California* emphasized that “the title to the beds of navigable rivers, within the limits of a state, passes to the state, by virtue of the equal footing doctrine, when the state is admitted into the Union.” Id. The Court further explained that “the question of navigability is one of fact, to be determined by the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” Id. This standard has been consistently applied in the decades following statehood, including in *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. 621, 634 (1892), where the Court held that “the title to the beds of navigable rivers within the limits of a state, when that state was admitted into the Union, passed to the state.” The Court did not inquire into the river’s condition in 1890 or 1900; it looked only to the condition in 1870, the year Texas was admitted.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the determination of navigability for title purposes is a historical inquiry. In *United States v. State of Washington*, 146 U.S. 140, 145 (1892), the Court stated: “The title to the beds of navigable rivers, within the limits of a state, when that state was admitted into the Union, passed to the state.” The Court did not require proof of navigability in the present or in the future; it required proof of navigability *at the time of admission*. Similarly, in *United States v. State of California*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 546, 557 (1839), the Court held that “the title to the beds of navigable rivers, within the limits of a state, passes to the state, by virtue of the equal footing doctrine, when the state is admitted into the Union.” The Court made clear that the test is not whether the river is navigable *now*, but whether it was navigable *then*.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled law. The District Court’s findings of fact, supported by the affidavits of the State Archivist and historical records, established that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in fact at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889. The evidence included contemporary maps, journals, and reports from the 1880s documenting the use of these rivers by steamboats, freighters, and other vessels engaged in commercial activity. These records, though hearsay in form, were properly admitted under the self-authenticating provisions of the Montana Rules of Evidence, including M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), which permit the admission of certified public records, official publications, and newspapers. *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948 (citing *United States v. State of Washington*, 146 U.S. 140, 145 (1892)).

PPL’s argument that the State must prove navigability *today* is contrary to the constitutional framework. The equal footing doctrine is not a flexible standard; it is a fixed constitutional rule. *United States v. State of Texas*, 143 U.S. 621, 634 (1892). The Court has never held that a state may claim title to riverbeds based on navigability at any time other than the moment of statehood. To hold otherwise would undermine the equal footing doctrine and allow states to retroactively claim title based on modern conditions, which would be inconsistent with the original understanding of the Constitution.

In sum, the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable *at the time of statehood*.
