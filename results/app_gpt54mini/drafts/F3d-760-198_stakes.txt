**ARGUMENT**

### I. The district court erred by truncating Moll’s hostile-work-environment claim and refusing to consider the totality of the alleged conduct.

A hostile work environment claim under Title VII is evaluated by looking to “all the circumstances,” not by isolating only conduct that is facially sexual. Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 23 (1993). The Supreme Court has made clear that actionable harassment exists where the workplace is “permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult” that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment. Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986). This Court likewise has held that the inquiry must consider the “totality of the circumstances.” Cruz v. Coach Stores, Inc., 202 F.3d 560, 570 (2d Cir. 2000).

The district court departed from those principles when it treated Moll’s hostile-work-environment theory as limited to the most overtly sexual incidents and then dismissed the claim because no such incident fell within the limitations period. That approach conflicts with this Court’s decision in Alfano v. Costello, which holds that a plaintiff may rely on facially sex-neutral acts so long as the evidence permits a reasonable inference that they were motivated by gender. 294 F.3d 365, 375-76 (2d Cir. 2002). As Alfano explains, “facially neutral incidents may be included” among the relevant acts, because harassment is often “subtle and indirect.” Id. at 375. Sexually hostile conduct may also take non-explicit forms. Id.

Here, Moll alleged a course of conduct spanning years: inappropriate notes from Irving, repeated calls to her hotel room on a business trip, the note stating he thought of her when showering, limits on email and telephone access that required in-person meetings, exclusion of women from work events, denial of telework and vacation benefits afforded to male colleagues, and disparate treatment in promotion and pay. Taken together, these allegations were sufficient to permit a jury to find a hostile work environment. See Harris, 510 U.S. at 23; Cruz, 202 F.3d at 570. The court’s segmented analysis improperly stripped the claim of context.

Nor could the court dismiss the claim merely because it believed no timely “sexually offensive” act occurred within the limitations period. A hostile work environment claim is by nature composed of a series of acts that “collectively constitute one ‘unlawful employment practice.’” National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan, 536 U.S. 101, 117 (2002). Under Morgan, so long as one contributing act falls within the filing period, earlier related acts may be considered as part of the same hostile environment. Id. at 117-18. The district court’s ruling ignored that rule and required the exact opposite.

### II. The district court also erred in giving dispositive effect to the “sham issue of fact” doctrine.

A court may disregard a party’s later affidavit that contradicts prior sworn testimony only where the later statement is a transparent attempt to create a fact issue by contradiction. See Perma Research & Dev. Co. v. Singer Co., 410 F.2d 572, 578 (2d Cir. 1969); Hayes v. N.Y.C. Dep’t of Corr., 84 F.3d 614, 619-20 (2d Cir. 1996). But the doctrine is applied with caution, and it does not authorize a court to disregard every subsequent inconsistent statement in the record. See Jeffreys v. City of New York, 426 F.3d 549, 554-55 (2d Cir. 2005).

Here, Verizon relied on the doctrine to minimize the testimony of a non-party witness whose later account differed from an earlier statement. That was error. The doctrine addresses attempts by a party to manufacture an issue of fact through self-serving contradiction. See Perma Research, 410 F.2d at 578. It does not compel a court to discard a non-party witness’s later testimony simply because it conflicts with the witness’s prior statement. Summary judgment requires the court to resolve ambiguities and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmovant, not to make credibility determinations. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). By rejecting the later testimony categorically, the district court invaded the province of the jury.

### III. The court should reverse the judgment and permit Moll’s remaining claims to proceed.

Because the district court applied an unduly narrow view of hostile work environment and misused summary judgment principles, the judgment should be vacated insofar as it granted Verizon’s motion for summary judgment on that claim. See Morgan, 536 U.S. at 117-18; Alfano, 294 F.3d at 375-76. Moll was entitled to have the full record considered as a whole, and a jury—not the court—must decide whether the conduct she alleged was severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of her employment. See Harris, 510 U.S. at 23; Meritor, 477 U.S. at 67.

Accordingly, the Court should reverse the dismissal of the hostile-work-environment claim and remand for further proceedings.
