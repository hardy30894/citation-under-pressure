The district court correctly declined to instruct the jury on punitive damages. Title VII authorizes such relief only upon a showing that the employer engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This standard incorporates the common-law requirement that the defendant’s conduct be egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind. See Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 46-48 (1983) (punitive damages available only where conduct is “of the sort that calls for deterrence and punishment over and above that provided by compensatory awards”); Pacific Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1991) (same).

The 1991 amendments did not displace this settled rule. Congress modeled § 1981a on the standards governing punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. See Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994). Those standards, in turn, demand conduct that is “egregious” or “outrageous.” See BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 574-75 (1996); TXO Prod. Corp. v. Alliance Res. Corp., 509 U.S. 443, 453-55 (1993). The same requirement applies to analogous “willful” violations under the ADEA. Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins, 507 U.S. 604, 615-17 (1993); Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston, 469 U.S. 111, 125-26 (1985).

Kolstad’s evidence falls short. Although the jury could find that ADA’s stated reasons for promoting Spangler were pretextual, McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 804-05 (1973), and St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993), establish that rejection of an employer’s nondiscriminatory explanation permits an inference of intentional discrimination but does not itself demonstrate the additional culpability needed for punitive damages. The record contains no evidence that ADA officials knew their conduct violated Title VII or acted with the conscious disregard of federal rights that would render the discrimination “egregious.” See Smith, 461 U.S. at 48.

Because the statutory text and governing precedents require an independent showing of outrageous conduct, the district court properly withheld the punitive-damages instruction.
