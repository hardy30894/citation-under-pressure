# ARGUMENT

## THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR COMPETENCY TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that due process requires a competency standard higher than the standard this Court established in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), for competency to stand trial. Nothing in this Court's precedents supports bifurcating competency into different standards depending on which right a defendant chooses to exercise or forgo. The *Dusky* standard—"whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him," *id.* at 402—is the constitutional floor for all decisions a defendant makes during a criminal proceeding, including the decision to plead guilty or waive counsel.

### A. This Court's Competency Jurisprudence Establishes a Single, Unitary Standard

This Court has long held that the *Dusky* standard governs competency determinations. *See Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975) (defining competency as "the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense"); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966). Nothing in *Dusky*, *Drope*, or *Pate* suggests that a defendant's competency fluctuates depending on the particular decision at issue. The standard asks whether the defendant possesses the requisite *mental capacity*—not whether he exercises that capacity wisely.

The Ninth Circuit's holding that "[c]ompetency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial," 972 F.2d at 267, finds no support in this Court's cases. The court below relied exclusively on its own prior decisions—*Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981); and *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884 (9th Cir. 1986)—none of which derive the "reasoned choice" standard from any decision of this Court. A standard of constitutional dimension cannot be manufactured from circuit precedent alone.

### B. Competency and Voluntariness Are Analytically Distinct Inquiries

The Ninth Circuit's error stems from conflating two separate constitutional inquiries: (1) whether a defendant is *competent*, and (2) whether his waiver is *knowing and voluntary*. This Court addressed precisely this distinction in *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), which held that a defendant has a right to self-representation once he "knowingly and intelligently" waives counsel, *id.* at 835, but nowhere suggested that the *capacity* to waive counsel differs from the capacity to stand trial. The "knowing and voluntary" inquiry asks whether the defendant *understands* the consequences of his choice and *chooses* it deliberately—a separate question from whether he has the requisite mental competency in the first instance. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right").

The *Dusky* competency standard and the *Johnson v. Zerbst* voluntariness standard operate independently, and both must be satisfied. But conflating them, as the Ninth Circuit did, produces the erroneous conclusion that competency itself must be measured on a sliding scale correlated to the significance of the right waived. The Constitution's competency requirement does not vary in this manner. See *Godinez v. Moran*, 972 F.2d at 267 (imposing a "higher level of mental functioning" requirement not found in *Dusky*, *Drope*, or any decision of this Court).

### C. A Bifurcated Standard Is Unworkable and Inconsistent with the Rationale Underlying Competency Requirements

The rationale for requiring competency to stand trial—ensuring the defendant can understand the proceedings and participate meaningfully, *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 171—applies with equal force regardless of whether the defendant is contesting guilt, pleading guilty, or waiving counsel. A defendant who meets the *Dusky* standard is, by definition, capable of understanding the nature of the proceedings and communicating rationally with counsel (or, if he waives counsel, with the court). There is no principled reason why that same defendant becomes incapable of understanding the consequences of a guilty plea or the waiver of counsel.

Moreover, the "reasoned choice" standard offers no discernible content beyond *Dusky*'s requirements. The Ninth Circuit did not explain how a defendant could satisfy *Dusky*'s rational-understanding requirement yet fail some higher threshold of "reasoned choice." The standard is untethered to any concrete psychiatric or legal metric, inviting exactly the kind of ad hoc, after-the-fact relitigation that occurred in this case, where the court of appeals second-guessed the state court's competency finding based on the defendant's use of medication and monosyllabic answers—factors equally relevant, if at all, to the separate voluntariness inquiry, not to competency itself.

### D. The State Court's Competency Finding Was Entitled to a Presumption of Correctness

Because the correct standard is the unitary *Dusky* standard, the state court's finding—based on the reports of Dr. O'Gorman and Dr. Jurasky that Moran was "knowledgeable of the charges being made against him" and could "assist his attorney"—satisfied the constitutional requirement. That finding was entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *See Marshall v. Lonberger*, 459 U.S. 422, 431–32 (1983) (state-court factual findings, including competency determinations, are entitled to deference on federal habeas review). The Ninth Circuit's rejection of that finding rested solely on its erroneous legal premise that a higher standard applied—not on any finding that Moran failed to satisfy *Dusky*.

### CONCLUSION

The Due Process Clause requires no more than the *Dusky* competency standard for a defendant's decision to plead guilty or waive counsel. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
