The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court must determine whether the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood to resolve the question of title to the riverbeds underlying PPL's hydroelectric dams. The equal footing doctrine, which governs the determination of navigability for title purposes, requires that a court assess the navigability of a waterway at the time of statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75-76 (1931). This doctrine is rooted in the principle that when a new state enters the Union, it acquires title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders, just as the original thirteen colonies did. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 228-229 (1845).

The test for navigability for title purposes is well-established: a waterway is considered navigable if it was used or susceptible of being used as a highway for commerce in its natural and ordinary condition at the time of statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test, relying on evidence presented by the State, including historical documents and expert testimony, to determine that the relevant stretches of the rivers were navigable at the time of statehood in 1889.

PPL's argument that the trial court erred in considering hearsay evidence on summary judgment is unpersuasive. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the historical documents submitted by the State were admissible under various exceptions to the hearsay rule, including the public records and reports exception, M. R. Evid. 803(8), and the ancient documents exception, M. R. Evid. 901(8). See Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc., 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79-81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948. The court's decision to consider this evidence on summary judgment was proper, as it was authenticated by the State Archivist and met the requirements of M. R. Civ. P. 56(e).

The Montana Supreme Court's decision is consistent with the principles established by this Court in Utah, 283 U.S. at 75-76, and The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563. The trial court's determination of navigability at the time of statehood is a necessary step in resolving the question of title to the riverbeds. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
