# ARGUMENT

## I. The Common Law Has Always Required Proof of Aggravated, Outrageous Misconduct Before Exemplary Damages May Be Assessed

Title VII's punitive-damages provision did not spring from nothing. Congress legislated against a deep common-law tradition holding that punitive damages are reserved for conduct that is itself extraordinary, not merely for conduct accompanied by a culpable state of mind. This Court recognized that tradition more than a century ago in Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852), explaining that a jury awarding exemplary damages must have "in view the enormity of his offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." Id. at 371. The touchstone was never simply whether the defendant knew its conduct was wrongful; it was whether the conduct itself was so extreme that society's condemnation, not the plaintiff's compensation, justified the award.

This Court reaffirmed that limitation in Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), where it explained that a jury may depart from simple compensation and award exemplary damages only where the injury has been inflicted "maliciously or wantonly, and with circumstances of contumely or indignity." Id. at 562. The word "circumstances" is doing real work in that formulation—it directs the fact-finder's attention to the aggravated nature of the act, not solely to the actor's subjective awareness of its unlawfulness. A garden-variety employment decision, even one a jury ultimately concludes was motivated by sex, does not carry the "circumstances of contumely or indignity" that the common law has always demanded.

Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), confirms that exemplary remedies historically attached to conduct of a qualitatively different character than ordinary civil wrongs—conduct that was "willful" and "wanton" in a sense far exceeding the intent necessary to establish liability itself. The distinction between liability-level intent and punishment-level culpability is the entire point of these decisions, and it is the distinction Kolstad asks this Court to erase.

## II. Where Punitive Liability Rests on the Acts of Employees Rather Than the Principal Itself, Egregiousness of the Underlying Conduct Is Especially Necessary

Title VII liability, like the liability at issue in this case, is typically established through the acts of individual supervisors or managers, not through any personal wrongdoing by the corporate employer. The common law has long treated that circumstance as an independent reason to demand more than ordinary culpability before punishing the principal for the acts of its agent. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818), this Court refused to impose exemplary damages on ship owners for the wanton acts of the vessel's master where the owners themselves had not participated in or ratified the misconduct, recognizing that vicarious exemplary liability threatens to punish parties for wrongs they did not themselves commit. That concern applies with full force here: ADA's Executive Director and Washington office director, not the organization's governing body, made the challenged decision, and nothing in the record suggests the organization itself embraced or ratified any invidious motive.

This Court's later corporate-liability decisions built directly on that foundation. In Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), the Court held that punitive damages against a corporation require proof that the corporation's own governing agents, not merely a subordinate employee, acted with the requisite culpability, because "damages, of a punitive character," must reflect "the actual guilt of the defendant." Id. at 107. Similarly, in New York, Lake Erie & Western Railroad Co. v. Estill, 147 U.S. 591 (1893), the Court reiterated that a corporate defendant's liability for exemplary damages depends on proof that the corporation, acting through those who direct its policy, engaged in conduct of an aggravated character—not that a single employee harbored an improper intent. And in Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), the Court cautioned that punitive damages should not be imposed on a principal for an agent's act absent the principal's own participation or ratification, observing that "gross negligence" of an agent, standing alone, does not translate into the kind of corporate culpability that justifies punishment. Id. at 493–94.

Lower federal courts applying these principles have likewise insisted on aggravated conduct. In Southern Railway Co. v. Madden, 235 F.2d 198 (4th Cir. 1956), the Fourth Circuit held that punitive damages require conduct that is "wanton" and "reckless" in a manner exceeding ordinary fault, reflecting the same demand for egregiousness that runs through this Court's own decisions.

## III. Section 1981a Should Be Read Against This Backdrop

Congress used the terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" in § 1981a without abandoning the substantial body of common-law authority defining when exemplary damages are appropriate. Given Day, Barry, Humes, Arms, The Amiable Nancy, Prentice, Estill, and Madden, a showing of the employer's state of mind alone—divorced from any independent showing that the underlying conduct was itself egregious or outrageous—cannot suffice. The court of appeals correctly required more than intent to discriminate; it required conduct commensurate with the punitive remedy Congress authorized.
