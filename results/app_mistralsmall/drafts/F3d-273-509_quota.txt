**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Release Does Not Bar Plaintiff’s Claims Because It Does Not Expressly Cover Them.**

Plaintiff’s claims are not barred by the 1988 release because the release’s language is limited to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” The release does not encompass claims arising from the FDIC’s post-closure administration of the receivership, including the payment of post-insolvency interest, the charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate, or the alleged breach of fiduciary duty in asset sales. Under New York law, a release is strictly construed against the party seeking its protection and will not be extended beyond its clear terms. *See Matter of State of New York v. Avco Fin. Serv. of N.Y., Inc.*, 50 N.Y.2d 383, 389 (1980) (“A release is strictly construed against the party claiming its protection and will not be extended by implication.”); *accord In re Coudert Bros. LLP*, 11 N.Y.3d 25, 31 (2008) (same).

The FDIC’s argument that the release covers all claims arising from the OCC’s closure decision is belied by the release’s plain language. The release does not mention the FDIC’s post-closure actions, such as the payment of post-insolvency interest or the charging of receivership expenses. Where a release is unambiguous, its scope is determined by its four corners. *See Avco Fin. Serv.*, 50 N.Y.2d at 389. The FDIC cannot bootstrap claims into the release that are not expressly covered. *See id.* (“[A] release will not be read to extinguish claims not clearly within its terms.”).

**II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run Until the Termination of the Receivership on November 1, 1995.**

Plaintiff’s claims are not barred by New York’s six-year statute of limitations, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), because the limitations period did not begin to run until the FDIC terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. Under New York law, a cause of action accrues when the plaintiff “discovers the injury or when, with reasonable diligence, it should have discovered the injury.” *O’Brien v. Cunard S.S. Co.*, 280 N.Y. 112, 119 (1939); *accord Ackerman v. Price Waterhouse*, 84 N.Y.2d 535, 541 (1994). Where the defendant is in control of the relevant information or the plaintiff is unaware of the injury, the limitations period is tolled until the plaintiff discovers or should have discovered the injury. *See O’Brien*, 280 N.Y. at 119.

Here, the FDIC controlled the receivership estate and the relevant financial records. Plaintiff could not have discovered the alleged misconduct—such as the payment of post-insolvency interest or the charging of legal and office expenses—until the FDIC provided an accounting or terminated the receivership. The FDIC’s argument that the limitations period began to run at the time of the OCC’s closure decision in 1985 is untenable. *See In re WorldCom, Inc. Sec. Litig.*, 346 F.Supp.2d 627, 640 (S.D.N.Y. 2004) (“The limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff discovers or should have discovered the injury.”).

Moreover, the FDIC’s post-closure actions—such as the payment of post-insolvency interest and the charging of receivership expenses—were ongoing and continuous. Under New York law, where a defendant’s conduct is continuous, the limitations period does not begin to run until the conduct ceases. *See Guterman v. Schneider*, 72 N.Y.2d 279, 282 (1988) (“Where the defendant’s conduct is continuous, the limitations period does not begin to run until the conduct ceases.”). The FDIC’s actions did not cease until the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. Thus, the limitations period did not begin to run until that date.

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC.**

The District Court erred in granting summary judgment to the FDIC because there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the scope of the release and the accrual of the limitations period. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986) (“Summary judgment is appropriate only if there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.”). The Court’s conclusion that the release barred plaintiff’s claims and that the limitations period had expired is unsupported by the record and the law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed.
