## Argument

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). That holding conflicts with this Court’s precedent, creates an unjustified circuit split, and undermines Congress’s carefully calibrated response to the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country.

### I. Nichols v. United States Forecloses Respondent’s Sixth Amendment Claim

This Court squarely held in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction is valid for purposes of enhancing a subsequent sentence so long as no term of imprisonment was imposed for the prior offense. *Id.* at 746-47. The reasoning of *Nichols* applies with equal force when a prior conviction is used as an element of a recidivist offense rather than as a sentencing enhancement.

Section 117(a) punishes the *current* act of domestic assault, not the prior convictions. As this Court has consistently recognized, recidivist statutes “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747; *see also United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (“None [of the punishment] is for the prior convictions.”). The prior convictions merely establish the defendant’s status as a repeat offender, a factual determination that does not implicate the Sixth Amendment right to counsel at the trial for the current offense.

The Ninth Circuit sought to distinguish *Nichols* on the ground that it involved a sentencing enhancement rather than an element of the offense. But that distinction is irrelevant to the constitutional question. Whether the prior conviction is used to increase punishment at sentencing or to establish an element of a recidivism offense, the defendant is not entitled to relitigate the validity of the prior conviction under the Sixth Amendment. *See Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748 n.10 (rejecting distinction between guilt and sentencing phases for Sixth Amendment purposes).

### II. Tribal Court Convictions Do Not Violate the Sixth Amendment Because the Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Courts

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to tribal court proceedings. *See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes). Tribal courts are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, which provides a right to counsel but not one coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. *See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 61-62 (1978). Respondent’s tribal court convictions were obtained in full compliance with ICRA and are therefore constitutionally valid.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that tribal court convictions must satisfy Sixth Amendment standards to be used as predicates—effectively imposes federal constitutional requirements on tribal courts where Congress has chosen not to do so. This contravenes fundamental principles of tribal sovereignty and comity. *See Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997) (federal courts must “respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems”).

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Reliance on United States v. Ant Is Misplaced

The panel below relied on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), which held that uncounseled tribal court convictions cannot be used to prove guilt in a subsequent federal prosecution. *Ant* was incorrectly decided and is inconsistent with *Nichols* and subsequent authority.

Every other circuit to address this issue has rejected *Ant*’s reasoning. The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that valid tribal court convictions may serve as predicates under § 117(a) even if they would not satisfy the Sixth Amendment. *See United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary position creates an untenable geographic disparity: a defendant in Utah or New Mexico can be prosecuted under § 117(a), while a defendant in Arizona faces no federal consequences for identical conduct.

### IV. The Decision Below Frustrates Congress’s Intent

Congress enacted § 117(a) as part of the Violence Against Women and Department of Justice Reauthorization Act of 2005 to address the devastating rates of domestic violence in Indian country. Congress expressly included tribal court convictions as predicates, recognizing that tribal courts are the primary forum for prosecuting misdemeanor domestic violence offenses in Indian country. The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively nullifies this statutory scheme, allowing serial domestic abusers to escape federal prosecution merely because their prior convictions were obtained in tribal court.

This Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that valid tribal court convictions—even those obtained without counsel—may serve as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).
