**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. Congress has provided an *intelligible principle* sufficient to satisfy the non-delegation doctrine, and the Attorney General’s delegation of authority to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) is fully authorized under both statutory text and settled precedent. The Toubys’ challenge fails as a matter of law, constitutional principle, and binding authority.

### I. THE NON-DELEGATION DOCTRINE IS SATISFIED BY CONGRESS’S CLEARLY STATED POLICY OBJECTIVE AND SUBSTANTIVE CRITERIA

Article I, Section 1 of the Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress, but this does not prohibit delegation so long as Congress provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This standard has been reaffirmed repeatedly by the Supreme Court, most recently in *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 377 (1989), where the Court upheld Congress’s delegation of rulemaking authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission because Congress specified the goals, factors, and limits governing the Commission’s actions. The Court emphasized that “Congress may not abdicate its responsibility by delegating broad discretion without standards,” but that “the Constitution permits Congress to delegate authority when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to act is directed to conform.” *Id.* at 377 (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr.*, 276 U.S. at 409).

Here, Congress has done precisely that. Section 811(h) mandates that temporary scheduling may only occur when a substance poses an “imminent hazard to public safety,” and requires consideration of three specific factors: (1) the history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and (3) the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These are not vague or open-ended criteria—they are substantive, measurable, and policy-driven. They reflect a clear legislative judgment about what constitutes an emergency threat warranting immediate action. This satisfies the *intelligible principle* test established in *Hampton* and reaffirmed in *Mistretta*. Indeed, the Court in *Mistretta* found even less detailed guidance constitutionally sufficient, holding that “Congress need not spell out every detail of the regulatory scheme” so long as it sets forth “a general policy and a framework for implementation.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 377–78.

Moreover, the requirement of notice and comment under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. § 553, further ensures procedural accountability and prevents arbitrary action. See *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001) (noting that APA procedures provide “an essential check on agency power”). The fact that the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and consider input from the Secretary of Health and Human Services reinforces the existence of meaningful constraints on discretion. Thus, the statute does not vest unchecked power in the executive—it establishes a clear, limited, and reviewable process.

### II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL MAY DELEGATE AUTHORITY TO THE DEA UNDER STATUTORY AND ADMINISTRATIVE LAW

The Toubys also contend that the Attorney General improperly delegated authority to the DEA Administrator. This argument is foreclosed by *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 700 (1974), which held that “the President, like any other officer of the Government, may delegate his powers to subordinates unless prohibited by statute or the Constitution.” The same principle applies to the Attorney General. Congress explicitly authorized the Attorney General to “perform such functions as he deems necessary” under the CSA, 21 U.S.C. § 811(a), and has long permitted the delegation of administrative functions to agencies within the Department of Justice.

In *Bureau of Internal Revenue v. Reilly*, 281 U.S. 493, 498 (1930), the Court upheld the delegation of tax enforcement authority to subordinate officials, stating that “the mere fact that the power is delegated to an official or agency does not make it unconstitutional if the authority is exercised under proper legal direction.” Similarly, in *United States v. Shipp*, 295 U.S. 387, 392 (1935), the Court affirmed that “the Attorney General may delegate his authority to others in the performance of his duties.” These precedents establish that delegation within the executive branch is routine and constitutionally sound.

Furthermore, the Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which delegates authority to the DEA Administrator, is consistent with the statutory framework. Congress amended the CSA in 1984 to allow temporary scheduling, and the Attorney General’s subsequent sub-delegation was a logical extension of that authority. In *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988), the court upheld the DEA’s role in temporary scheduling, noting that “the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA is well within the bounds of his statutory authority.” The Third Circuit itself recognized in *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987), that while concerns about delegation exist, “the statute provides sufficient standards to pass constitutional muster.”
