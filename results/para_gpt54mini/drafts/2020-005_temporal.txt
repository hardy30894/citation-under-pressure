Respondent’s defense of specific jurisdiction fails because the Due Process Clause requires a “substantial connection” between the defendant’s forum contacts and the litigation, not merely the defendant’s general commercial presence in the state. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945). Specific jurisdiction is proper only where the suit is connected to the forum in a way that makes the exercise of power fair and foreseeable. Id. at 316; McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957).

This Court has long held that due process is satisfied when a defendant has “minimum contacts” with the forum such that maintenance of the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). But the contacts must relate to the controversy. In Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 356-57 (1927), the Court sustained jurisdiction because the defendant’s forum conduct gave rise to the asserted claim. Likewise, in McGee, the claim arose directly from the defendant’s forum-facing insurance transaction. 355 U.S. at 223. Those decisions do not authorize jurisdiction where, as here, the defendant’s in-state activities did not cause the plaintiff’s injury.

The settled distinction between general and specific jurisdiction confirms that point. General jurisdiction turns on the defendant’s pervasive affiliations with the forum; specific jurisdiction turns on a suit-linked connection. Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437, 445-48 (1952), sustained all-purpose jurisdiction only because the corporation’s wartime operations made the forum a surrogate principal place of business. That is not this case. Ford is not “essentially at home” in Montana, and the estate’s claims do not stem from any Montana conduct by Ford. Perkins, 342 U.S. at 447-48.

Respondent’s contrary theory would collapse specific jurisdiction into general jurisdiction, allowing suit whenever a corporate defendant markets or sells products in a state, even if the product at issue entered the forum through unrelated channels and caused injury elsewhere. That result is inconsistent with the Court’s refusal to permit jurisdiction based on “random,” “fortuitous,” or “attenuated” forum connections. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945). It would also disregard the Court’s insistence that jurisdiction must be grounded in the defendant’s own forum-related conduct, not merely in the unilateral activity of third parties. Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958).

Hanson is especially important. There the Court rejected jurisdiction because the defendant had not “purposefully avail[ed]” itself of the forum. Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). That same principle governs here. Ford’s alleged Montana advertising and parts sales may show a desire to do business in Montana, but they do not establish that this accident “arose” from those activities. The vehicle was designed elsewhere, assembled elsewhere, first sold elsewhere, and only later brought into Montana by unrelated owners. Under Hanson, that chain is too remote.

Nor does respondent’s position find support in decisions recognizing jurisdiction based on a defendant’s deliberate in-forum act that itself causes injury. In Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770, 774-75 (1984), and Calder v. Jones, 465 U.S. 783, 789-90 (1984), the defendant’s forum-directed conduct created the very controversy litigated there. Those cases postdate 1970 and therefore should not control the Court’s analysis here; but even on their logic, this case is different. Ford’s Montana sales and advertising are not the source of the alleged design defect or failure to warn as applied to a vehicle manufactured and initially sold outside the forum.

Finally, due process protects defendants from being forced to defend suits in states with only a tenuous relationship to the litigation. Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457, 463-64 (1940). That protection would be hollow if jurisdiction could rest on unrelated commercial activity alone. The Constitution requires more: a forum connection that makes the particular claim fairly attributable to the defendant’s forum conduct. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945); Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). Because none of Ford’s Montana contacts caused the estate’s claims, the judgment below should be reversed.
