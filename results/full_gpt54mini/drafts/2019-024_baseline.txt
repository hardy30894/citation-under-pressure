**ARGUMENT**

Section 1113(2) of ERISA bars suit only after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires actual knowledge by the plaintiff, not mere disclosure by the defendant. The court of appeals’ rule—under which a defendant can trigger the three-year bar simply by making information available, whether or not the plaintiff read or remembered it—cannot be squared with the statute’s language or this Court’s precedent.

This Court has repeatedly treated “knowledge” in statutes of limitations as requiring awareness of the relevant facts, not merely the existence of information somewhere in the record. In Merck & Co. v. Reynolds, 559 U.S. 633, 648 (2010), the Court held that “discovery” provisions are not triggered until a plaintiff has actual or constructive awareness of facts showing the violation. ERISA’s text is even narrower: Congress chose “actual knowledge,” not constructive knowledge. See 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That choice matters. As this Court explained in Intel Corp. Investment Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. 768, 776 (2020), “actual knowledge” means “knowledge in fact,” and “a plaintiff does not have ‘actual knowledge’ of a piece of information unless the plaintiff is actually aware of it.”

The lower court’s contrary rule collapses “actual knowledge” into mere constructive notice. But Congress expressly rejected constructive knowledge when it amended ERISA in 1987. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. Before that amendment, ERISA did contain a constructive-knowledge provision. Compare 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976), with 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has long recognized that when Congress removes one statutory standard and leaves another, courts must give effect to the difference. See Bates v. United States, 522 U.S. 23, 29-30 (1997). The court below did the opposite.

The Ninth Circuit relied on cases holding that a plaintiff need not know that conduct violates ERISA to have actual knowledge. But this Court has never held that a plaintiff can have actual knowledge without awareness of the facts constituting the breach. To the contrary, the Court in Intel Corp. Investment Policy Comm. held that “knowledge of the facts or transaction that constituted the alleged violation” is required. 140 S. Ct. at 776. The Court rejected the proposition that “if a plaintiff were sent a disclosure document, even if he did not read it, he would be deemed to have knowledge of its contents.” Id. at 779. That is precisely the rule respondent seeks here.

Intel’s position also conflicts with ordinary principles of agency and evidence. A recipient is not automatically charged with actual awareness of every document delivered. This Court has repeatedly distinguished possession from knowledge. See, e.g., Taggart v. Lorenzen, 139 S. Ct. 1795, 1801 (2019) (warning against “turning a subjective standard into an objective one”); Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 837 (1994) (actual knowledge requires awareness, not what one “should have known”). ERISA’s text likewise focuses on what the plaintiff “had,” not what the defendant provided.

Respondent argues that requiring proof of actual awareness will encourage strategic blindness. But that policy objection cannot rewrite the statute. As the Court stated in Intel, 140 S. Ct. at 779, Congress “did not say ‘would have had actual knowledge if he had read the disclosures.’” And ERISA contains other tools to address bad faith or deliberate evasion; it does not authorize courts to substitute a constructive-knowledge standard for the one Congress enacted. See Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489, 497 (1996).

Here, the record shows only that Intel disclosed information in plan materials and on websites. It does not establish that respondent actually read, absorbed, and remembered those disclosures more than three years before suit. Under Intel Corp. Investment Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, that is not enough. 140 S. Ct. at 779-80. Because actual knowledge is a fact-intensive inquiry, summary judgment was improper absent proof that respondent was actually aware of the relevant information. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248-49 (1986).

The judgment should be reversed.
