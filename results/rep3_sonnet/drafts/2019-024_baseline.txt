# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 1113(2) REQUIRES ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE, NOT CONSTRUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE OR PROOF OF READING

Section 1113(2) bars suit three years "after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory analysis "begins with the language of the statute itself." *United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The word "actual" modifies "knowledge," and Congress's choice of that term is not accidental. When Congress enacted ERISA in 1974, § 1113 contained *both* an actual-knowledge trigger and a constructive-knowledge trigger keyed to when a plaintiff "could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge" from public filings. Pub. L. No. 93-406, § 413(a)(2)(B), 88 Stat. 829, 892 (1974). In 1987, Congress repealed the constructive-knowledge provision entirely. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330, 1330-89. That deliberate excision confirms that Congress intended to eliminate any inquiry into what a plaintiff *should* have known and to retain only what he *did* know.

The Ninth Circuit's rule—that a defendant's undisputed disclosure of information, without more, cannot establish actual knowledge if the plaintiff simply says he does not recall reading it—effectively resurrects the repealed constructive-knowledge standard under a different label, or worse, makes the limitations period turn entirely on a plaintiff's self-serving and unverifiable assertions of memory loss. This Court has cautioned against interpretations that render statutory text "insignificant, if not wholly superfluous." *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) (quoting *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001)). A standard permitting any plaintiff to defeat summary judgment merely by declaring he does not "recall" reading disclosures he was given, had access to, and had reason to consult, effectively restores the constructive-knowledge regime Congress deliberately discarded.

## II. THIS COURT'S PRECEDENTS CONFIRM THAT "ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE" MAY BE ESTABLISHED THROUGH CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE OF DISCLOSURE AND RECEIPT

This Court has recognized that "actual knowledge" in analogous federal statutes does not require direct testimonial admission; it may be proven through objective, circumstantial evidence establishing that a plaintiff was exposed to and possessed the relevant information. In *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633 (2010), this Court construed a similarly worded limitations trigger under the securities laws—there, "actual knowledge" (via inquiry notice) of scienter facts—and made clear that the standard is a factual one requiring examination of what information a plaintiff possessed, not simply what a defendant provided. *Id.* at 648. Nothing in *Merck* suggests that a plaintiff can indefinitely toll a limitations period through unsubstantiated denial of recollection where the objective record—receipt, access, and repeated presentation of disclosures over years—demonstrates the contrary.

Moreover, ERISA fiduciary jurisprudence has long held that formal receipt and availability of plan disclosures matters to a participant's legal position. In *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen*, 514 U.S. 73 (1995), this Court emphasized the centrality of written plan documents to the ERISA disclosure scheme, and in *Conkright v. Frommert*, 559 U.S. 506 (2010), the Court underscored that ERISA's disclosure regime is designed to give participants "a comprehensible summary" of the information governing their benefits, *id.* at 517 (internal quotation marks omitted), precisely so that fiduciaries and courts may rely on the fact of disclosure. Permitting a plaintiff's bare denial of memory to override years of Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and annual disclosures—each provided pursuant to ERISA's mandatory disclosure requirements, see 29 U.S.C. § 1024(b)—undermines the statutory disclosure scheme Congress carefully constructed.

## III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S RULE CREATES AN UNWORKABLE AND ASYMMETRIC EVIDENTIARY STANDARD THAT CONTRAVENES CONGRESSIONAL INTENT TO PROMOTE REPOSE

Section 1113 exists to provide finality. As this Court has observed, statutes of limitations "protect defendants and courts alike" from stale claims where evidence has been lost, memories faded, or witnesses unavailable. *Chevron USA, Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), while addressing different substantive law, exemplifies the broader administrative principle that Congress's chosen mechanisms for triggering time limits are entitled to respect rather than judicial expansion. The Ninth Circuit's approach—crediting a plaintiff's inability to recall disclosed information as sufficient to create a triable issue of fact—effectively means no ERISA fiduciary can ever obtain summary judgment on limitations grounds so long as the plaintiff testifies to forgetfulness, regardless of how extensive, repeated, or accessible the disclosures were.

The undisputed record below shows that Intel provided Fund Fact Sheets in 2010, 2011, and 2012; a Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice in 2011; a Summary Plan Description in 2012; and Annual Disclosures in 2012—each explaining the very asset-allocation strategy Sulyma now challenges. J.A. __–__. That evidentiary record satisfies any reasonable construction of "actual knowledge." The Ninth Circuit's holding that these documents are insufficient as a matter of law whenever a plaintiff denies recollection converts every ERISA limitations dispute into a jury question, defeating the very purpose of a statute designed, per its text and history, to provide a definite, administrable cutoff.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
