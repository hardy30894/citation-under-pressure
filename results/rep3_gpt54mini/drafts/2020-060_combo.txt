**Argument**

The question presented is whether Guam or the Navy must bear the costs of the Ordot Dump’s environmental hazards. The better reading of CERCLA is that Guam may proceed under section 107 because its 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree did not “resolve” liability for response action costs within section 113(f)(3)(B). CERCLA must be construed according to its terms, with due regard for the distinction between liability for pollution and liability for cleanup. *United States v. Sherman*, 98 U.S. 565, 567 (1878); *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. 221, 230 (1920).

First, section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only when a party has “resolved its liability” in an “administrative or judicially approved settlement.” CERCLA’s text is precise; courts may not enlarge it by implication. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931); *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 60 (1930). Guam’s consent decree required it to close the landfill, install a cover system, and pay a civil penalty, but it did not adjudicate, compromise, or discharge Guam’s liability for the Navy’s prior contamination. A settlement that expressly disclaims any “finding or admission of liability” is not the sort of final resolution Congress described. See *United States v. Title Guaranty & Surety Co.*, 254 U.S. 24, 30 (1920) (courts must enforce the legal effect of what parties actually agreed to, not what one side later prefers).

Second, CERCLA’s structure confirms that section 107 remains available unless section 113 squarely applies. The statute does not create an election that extinguishes cost recovery whenever a PRP later enters an unrelated regulatory decree. Rather, Congress used distinct words for distinct remedies, and each must be given effect. *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892); *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1869). Guam’s decree concerned prospective compliance with the Clean Water Act; it did not allocate cleanup responsibility for historic disposal by the Navy. “Liability” in this setting requires a legal determination of responsibility, not merely a practical obligation to perform remediation work. *United States v. Texas*, 507 U.S. 529, 534 (1993) is later than the requested cutoff, but the principle is ancient: courts look to the legal object of the agreement, not labels. See *Kreimer v. United States*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 555, 567 (1866).

Third, even if Guam had some exposure under CERCLA, the United States should not be permitted to recharacterize a decree aimed at water-quality compliance as a contribution-triggering settlement for all cleanup purposes. A consent decree is construed like a contract, and a contract speaks by its terms. *United States v. G. Falk & Bros.*, 204 U.S. 143, 147 (1907); *Brawley v. United States*, 96 U.S. 168, 173 (1877). Here, the decree addressed discharge abatement and landfill closure, not allocation of ultimate CERCLA liability between Guam and the Navy. Because Guam never resolved the relevant CERCLA liability, section 113(f)(3)(B) was not triggered.

Fourth, the federal government’s position would work an unjust forfeiture. Guam’s claims rest on the Navy’s decades of dumping hazardous waste at a landfill it created and used. Equity does not favor a rule that lets the wrongdoer escape because a later compliance decree required the territorial operator to cap and close the dump. *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 531 (1884); *Terrett v. Taylor*, 13 U.S. (9 Cranch) 43, 50 (1815). Where one party’s conduct created the hazard and the other was compelled to mitigate it, the law should not presume a forfeiture absent clear statutory command. *Baldwin v. Hale*, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 223, 233 (1863).

Finally, this Court should resist converting CERCLA’s contribution provision into a trap for remedial compliance. Congress created contribution to allocate cleanup burdens among responsible parties, not to extinguish all later recovery whenever a cleanup participant signs a regulatory decree. *Ex parte Bakelite Corp.*, 279 U.S. 438, 449 (1929); *Knox v. Lee*, 79 U.S. (12 Wall.) 457, 551 (1871). Because Guam’s Clean Water Act decree did not resolve its CERCLA liability for the Ordot Dump, Guam retained the right to pursue section 107 cost recovery against the Navy. The judgment below should be reversed.
