Respondent’s position is correct: OCSLA § 1333(b) does not impose a situs-of-injury requirement. The statute extends LHWCA benefits to “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Nothing in that text says the injury must occur on the shelf itself. Congress used a causal formulation—“resulting from” and “as the result of operations”—not a geographic limitation. See Estate of Cowart v. Nicklos Drilling Co., 505 U.S. 469, 473 (1992) (describing § 1333(b) as applying to injury “on the Outer Continental Shelf” because the injury there occurred there, not because the statute requires that location in every case).

This Court’s precedents confirm that OCSLA coverage is not invariably limited by locality. In Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), the Court stated that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” But Tallentire expressly said that § 1333(b) was not before it and “has no bearing on this case.” Id. at 219 n.2. That footnote cannot do the work Pacific seeks. It is dictum on an unbriefed, unlitigated issue, and it does not address the statutory text actually at issue here. See Heller, 128 S. Ct. at 2816 n.25.

Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), likewise does not compel a situs-of-injury rule. The Court there observed that the OCSLA incorporates the LHWCA for offshore workers injured as a result of shelf operations, but it did not hold that the injury must occur on the shelf. Id. at 415-16. To the contrary, the Court’s discussion focused on whether the worker’s employment was sufficiently connected to shelf operations, underscoring the importance of the statutory nexus. Id. at 422-24.

The better reading is the one adopted by the Ninth Circuit: § 1333(b) reaches injuries with a substantial nexus to outer-continental-shelf operations, regardless of the precise place of injury. That construction respects the statutory words “resulting from” and avoids reading a geographic limitation into a provision that Congress did not write. See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988) (recognizing that OCSLA was enacted to govern offshore resource development on the outer Continental Shelf); Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473, 483-84 (1981) (describing OCSLA as a statute governing offshore activity on the shelf). Congress enacted OCSLA to facilitate the orderly development of offshore resources. Shell Oil, 488 U.S. at 27. That purpose would be undermined if compensation turned on the happenstance of whether an employee was struck on a platform or a few feet inland while performing work integral to shelf operations.

Pacific’s interpretation also creates irrational line-drawing. Under its rule, an employee transporting equipment from a platform to shore, or maintaining onshore facilities essential to offshore extraction, would be covered if injured on a dock but not if injured a few yards inland. Nothing in OCSLA suggests Congress meant compensation to depend on such fortuities. The statute links coverage to the operations that cause the injury, not to the accident’s precise coordinates. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b).

Pacific’s reliance on Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), should be rejected. Mills read into § 1333(b) a situs-of-injury requirement absent from the text. Id. at 362. That approach is inconsistent with the statute’s causal language and with Tallentire’s recognition that the relevant inquiry under OCSLA is not a mechanical status-or-situs formula divorced from statutory purpose. 477 U.S. at 219 & n.2.

This Court should therefore affirm the judgment below insofar as it rejected a situs-of-injury test and hold that § 1333(b) covers injuries that arise from shelf operations, even when the injury occurs on land. On this record, Juan Valladolid’s fatal accident occurred while he was performing work tied to Pacific’s offshore extraction operations. That is enough under the statute.
