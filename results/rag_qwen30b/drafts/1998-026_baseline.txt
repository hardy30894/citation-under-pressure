**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) entitles him to habeas relief when he was, in fact, aware of that right—must be answered in the negative. The Court has long recognized that not every procedural violation, no matter how technical, warrants the extraordinary remedy of habeas corpus. Where the defendant was aware of his right to appeal and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to pursue it, the violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is harmless and does not deprive the defendant of due process.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires the district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal following a guilty plea. *See Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2)*. This requirement serves a legitimate purpose: to ensure that defendants are fully informed of their appellate rights and can make a deliberate choice about whether to appeal. *See United States v. Berrigan, 484 F.2d 724, 730 (3d Cir. 1973)*. However, the rule is not a jurisdictional mandate whose violation automatically invalidates a conviction or sentence. The Supreme Court has consistently held that procedural errors in criminal proceedings are subject to harmless-error review when the defendant was not prejudiced. *See Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967)*.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Manuel D. Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. This finding is not clearly erroneous and is supported by the record. The Court of Appeals affirmed, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) had been satisfied because the defendant knew his right to appeal. *See United States v. Peguero, 105 F.3d 827, 830 (3d Cir. 1997)*. That conclusion is sound and consistent with precedent.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that a defendant’s actual knowledge of his rights can cure a procedural defect. In *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52 (1985), the Court held that a defendant’s claim of ineffective assistance of counsel based on failure to advise him of parole eligibility did not entitle him to a hearing because he failed to demonstrate prejudice. The Court stated: “The defendant must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, he would have rejected the plea bargain.” *Id. at 59*. Similarly, in *Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82 (1946), the Court rejected a claim that a guilty plea was involuntary due to the absence of counsel, noting that the petitioner had been “informed of his right to counsel” and had “waived” it. *Id. at 85*. The Court concluded that the absence of counsel did not automatically invalidate the plea where the defendant understood his rights and chose to proceed without one.

The same logic applies here. Peguero was not misled or deceived. He was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it. The failure of the district court to recite the rule’s language does not alter the reality that the defendant was informed and made a deliberate decision. As the Court stated in *Blackledge v. Allison*, 431 U.S. 63 (1977), “a defendant’s claim of error must be evaluated in light of the totality of the circumstances.” *Id. at 73*. Where the defendant’s actual knowledge and voluntary choice are established, the procedural violation is not prejudicial.

Moreover, the rule’s purpose—ensuring informed consent—is fulfilled when the defendant knows his rights. *See United States v. Berrigan, 484 F.2d at 730*. The rule does not require a mechanical recitation; it requires substance. The Court has long recognized that “the Constitution does not require a perfect trial, but a fair one.” *See United States v. Agurs, 427 U.S. 97, 103 (1976)*. The same principle applies to sentencing procedures. A technical failure to advise does not render a fair process unfair when the defendant was, in fact, informed.

Finally, to hold otherwise would invite abuse. A defendant could delay appeal indefinitely, then claim that the court failed to advise him of his right, even if he knew it all along. Such a rule would undermine finality and encourage forum shopping. The Court has consistently rejected such outcomes. *See Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 300 (1989)* (holding that new rules of constitutional law do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review).

For these reasons, the failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless error. The defendant was aware of his right and chose not to appeal. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
