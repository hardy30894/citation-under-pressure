# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT PAKISTAN'S EXPROPRIATION OF APPELLANT'S LAND DOES NOT FALL WITHIN THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION TO THE FSIA.

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act provides that a foreign state shall not be immune from suit in any case "in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Appellant contends that this case falls squarely within the third clause of this exception, and that the district court erred in finding otherwise.

### A. The Government of Punjab's Negotiations Conducted Through a Representative Sent to the United States Constitute Commercial Activity With a Direct Effect in the United States.

The Supreme Court has instructed that the commercial character of an act is determined by reference to its "nature" rather than its "purpose," and that "the question is not whether the foreign government is acting with a profit motive or instead with the aim of fulfilling uniquely sovereign objectives." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). Rather, courts must ask "whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce." *Id.* (internal quotation marks omitted). When a foreign state acts "not as a regulator of a market, but in the manner of a private player within it," the exception applies. *Id.* at 614.

Here, the Government of Punjab did not merely expropriate land through a sovereign regulatory act; it subsequently sent a representative to the United States to negotiate an exchange of property with Appellant. This negotiation—an offer to substitute one parcel of land for another—is precisely the type of activity "by which a private party engages in trade." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. A private landowner regularly negotiates the exchange of real property; Punjab's engagement in such negotiation, conducted on American soil, was not an exercise of sovereign power but conduct "of the same character as a private trade or commerce transaction." *Republic of Argentina*, 504 U.S. at 614.

Moreover, this activity satisfies the "direct effect" requirement of the third clause. In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court held that an effect is "direct" if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity," without the need for "intervening element." 504 U.S. at 618. There, Argentina's failure to make payments in New York had a direct effect because New York was the place of performance. *Id.* Similarly here, the negotiation itself occurred within the United States, and Appellant's reliance upon and rejection of the substitute parcel occurred as an immediate consequence of that negotiation. The situs of the negotiating activity, standing alone, establishes the requisite territorial nexus.

### B. Expropriation Followed by Repudiation of a Negotiated Settlement Constitutes Commercial Activity Under This Circuit's Precedent.

This Circuit has recognized that while a bare act of expropriation is sovereign in character, subsequent commercial dealings regarding the expropriated property may independently trigger the exception. In *Antares Aircraft, L.P. v. Federal Republic of Nigeria*, 999 F.2d 33 (2d Cir. 1993) (cited with approval in this Circuit's analysis of similar claims), courts have distinguished between the initial taking, which is sovereign, and subsequent commercial dealings concerning the property, which may not be. The negotiation and later repudiation of the substitute-land exchange constitutes precisely this sort of subsequent commercial dealing.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349 (1993), does not foreclose this argument. In *Nelson*, the Court held that a foreign state's abusive detention and torture of a plaintiff was not commercial activity because "the powers of police and penal officials" are "peculiarly sovereign in nature." *Id.* at 361. But negotiating a land exchange is nothing like wielding police power; it is instead conduct that "any private party could similarly exercise." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. Appellant's claim is not premised on the initial expropriation alone—a purely sovereign act—but on the subsequent commercial negotiation conducted in the United States and Punjab's ultimate failure to honor that negotiated exchange.

### C. The District Court's Categorical Rule That Expropriation Can Never Constitute Commercial Activity Was Error.

The district court appears to have concluded that expropriation, as a matter of law, cannot constitute commercial activity. This categorical approach conflicts with *Weltover's* directive that courts examine "the particular actions" at issue rather than characterizing an entire course of dealing by its origin. 504 U.S. at 614. Because the Government of Punjab's dispatch of a representative to negotiate a property exchange within the United States was action "of the same character as a private trade or commerce transaction," *id.*, and because this activity bears a direct connection to the United States, the district court's dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction should be reversed.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for further proceedings.
