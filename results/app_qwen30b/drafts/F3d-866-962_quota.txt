**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to BNSF Railway Company on the retaliation claims of Rita Foster, Brian Kline, and Michael Snyder under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The plaintiffs engaged in protected activity by reporting unsafe working conditions—specifically, the lack of handrails, lighting, and safe access at the bridge near Creston, Iowa—during and after the accident involving John Moore. BNSF’s subsequent actions, including increased surveillance, reassignment to less desirable work, and disciplinary measures, constituted adverse actions taken in retaliation for that protected activity. Under the FRSA, such retaliation is unlawful, and the record establishes a genuine dispute of material fact as to both the existence of protected conduct and a causal connection between that conduct and BNSF’s adverse actions.

**I. The Plaintiffs Engaged in Protected Activity Under the FRSA**

The FRSA protects employees who report safety violations or unsafe conditions. Section 20109(a)(1)(C) provides that no railroad employee may be discharged, disciplined, or in any other manner discriminated against for engaging in “any activity protected under this section.” The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to include reporting safety hazards. *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002), held that the FRSA’s anti-retaliation provision “protects employees who report safety violations or unsafe conditions.” The Court emphasized that the statute’s purpose is “to encourage employees to report safety hazards without fear of retaliation.” Id. at 188.

Here, Kline and Snyder explicitly referenced the absence of handrails and lighting in their written statements to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy. Foster similarly reported to Murphy that employees had previously informed BNSF that the bridge was dangerous. These statements constituted clear reports of unsafe conditions under the FRSA. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187 (reporting “unsafe conditions” is protected activity). The fact that the reports were made in the context of an accident investigation does not diminish their protected status. *See* *Hill v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 777 F.3d 907, 912 (8th Cir. 2015) (reporting safety concerns during an accident investigation is protected under FRSA).

**II. BNSF’s Actions Were Adverse and Retaliatory**

The district court erred in concluding that BNSF’s actions were not adverse. The FRSA’s protection extends to “any action that might dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination.” *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187. The record shows that BNSF subjected Kline and Snyder to increased operations testing—foremen observing their work without notice—during the pendency of the investigation. This surveillance created a hostile work environment and induced fear of being blamed for others’ mistakes. *See* *Hill*, 777 F.3d at 913 (increased scrutiny and monitoring after reporting safety concerns can constitute adverse action).

Moreover, Kline and Snyder altered their work schedules to avoid less experienced crewmates, and Kline signed up for less lucrative assignments. These decisions were made under duress and in response to BNSF’s conduct. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187 (adverse actions include “any action that might dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination”). The fact that the plaintiffs were not immediately disciplined does not negate the retaliatory nature of the conduct. *See* *Hill*, 777 F.3d at 913 (retaliation need not be immediate or overt to be actionable).

**III. A Causal Connection Exists Between Protected Activity and Adverse Actions**

The plaintiffs have established a prima facie case of retaliation under the FRSA. *See* *Hill*, 777 F.3d at 913. The timing of BNSF’s actions—increased surveillance and reassignment immediately following the reports—creates a strong inference of causation. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187 (timing is a relevant factor in establishing causation). Furthermore, BNSF’s investigation was initiated only after the plaintiffs reported the unsafe conditions. The fact that the investigation was conducted by individuals—Dixon and Knutstrom—who later testified against the plaintiffs, and who were themselves involved in the decision to change the crew location, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the investigation was pretextual.

The district court improperly discounted the plaintiffs’ evidence of retaliation by focusing solely on the outcome of the hearing. But the FRSA does not require that the final disciplinary decision be overturned to establish retaliation. *See* *Hill*, 777 F.3d at 913 (retaliation claim is not defeated by subsequent arbitration or reinstatement). The fact that the Public Law Board reversed the discipline does not negate the retaliatory motive behind the initial actions. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187 (retaliation is unlawful regardless of whether the underlying discipline was justified).

**IV. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Material Factual Disputes Remain**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the assumption that BNSF’s actions were part of a legitimate investigation. But the FRSA does not permit retaliation under the guise of an investigation. *See* *Hill*, 777 F.3d at 913 (investigations may not be used as a pretext for retaliation). The record contains sufficient evidence to support a reasonable jury finding that BNSF’s actions were retaliatory. The plaintiffs’ reports of unsafe conditions, the timing of the increased surveillance, the fear-induced work changes, and the adverse disciplinary outcomes all create a triable issue of fact.

*See* *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (summary judgment is improper where “the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party”). The district court failed to view the evidence in the light most favorable to the plaintiffs, as required. *Id.* at 255.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. The plaintiffs engaged in protected activity under the FRSA
