**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce expert testimony about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the use of Clark’s expert evidence to the issue of insanity, a determination governed by state law and consistent with long-standing constitutional principles. The Due Process Clause does not compel a state to allow a defendant to use psychiatric testimony to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such evidence is relevant to a recognized defense. Here, Clark sought to use expert testimony not to establish a defense, but to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof—a function that lies within the province of the state to define.

The Due Process Clause protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial, but it does not guarantee a right to introduce any and all evidence, even if that evidence might support a defense. As this Court held in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941), “the Due Process Clause does not require the State to permit the introduction of evidence which is irrelevant or immaterial.” Expert testimony regarding mental state may be relevant to the issue of insanity, but it is not automatically relevant to the question of criminal intent. The distinction between the two is well established in the law. In *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), the court observed that “the insanity defense is not a denial of guilt but a claim that the defendant lacked the capacity to understand the nature of his act or to conform his conduct to the law.” That distinction is rooted in precedent long before the modern era of mental health jurisprudence.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that states retain broad discretion in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto. In *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937), the Court stated that “the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a State to conform its criminal law to the standards of another State.” This principle applies with equal force to the rules governing the admissibility of evidence. A state may, as Arizona did, limit the use of expert testimony to the specific defense of insanity, even if such testimony might indirectly bear on the issue of intent. The Court has recognized that “the rules of evidence are largely within the control of the states,” as noted in *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74, 78 (1958), and that “the Constitution does not require a state to admit evidence that would be excluded under its own rules of evidence.” *Hawkins*, 358 U.S. at 78.

Moreover, the burden of proving criminal intent rests with the prosecution, and the defendant is not entitled to a jury instruction or evidentiary rule that would require the state to disprove a mental condition that negates intent unless such a condition is part of a recognized defense. In *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), the court emphasized that “the burden of proving insanity is on the defendant,” and that “the defendant may not use psychiatric testimony to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof unless such testimony is offered in support of a defense.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 975, and is consistent with the rule that “a defendant cannot compel the state to accept a defense not recognized under state law.” *Hawkins*, 358 U.S. at 78.

Clark’s argument that he was denied due process because he could not use expert testimony to rebut the prosecution’s intent claim ignores the fundamental principle that the state may define the scope of defenses. In *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. at 236, the Court held that “the Due Process Clause does not require the State to permit the introduction of evidence which is irrelevant or immaterial.” Expert testimony about mental state is not automatically relevant to criminal intent unless it is tied to a recognized defense. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly held that the state’s rules of evidence, including the limitation on the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense, were not violative of due process.

The Court has also long recognized that the state may impose reasonable limits on the admissibility of evidence to prevent jury confusion or to maintain order in the trial process. In *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 975, the court noted that “the trial judge has broad discretion in determining the admissibility of expert testimony.” This discretion includes the authority to limit the purpose for which such testimony may be used. In *Hawkins*, 358 U.S. at 78, the Court upheld the exclusion of evidence where the state’s rules of evidence were not violated. Similarly, in *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325, the Court affirmed that states may define their own criminal procedures, including the rules of evidence.

Finally, the Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that would effectively shift the burden of proof. In *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. at 236, the Court held that “the Due Process Clause does not require the State to permit the introduction of evidence which is irrelevant or immaterial.” Clark’s expert testimony, while potentially relevant to his mental state, was not admissible to negate criminal intent unless such a defense was recognized under Arizona law. Arizona law did not recognize such a defense, and the state was not constitutionally required to do so.

For these reasons, the Arizona trial court did not violate Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals is affirmed.
