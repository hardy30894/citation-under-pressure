**ARGUMENT**

**THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT GIVE RISE TO A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF ALLEGES ONLY A SPITEFUL, ARBITRARY DEMAND UNRELATED TO ANY LEGITIMATE STATE OBJECTIVE.**

The Equal Protection Clause directs that “no State shall . . . deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has long held that the Clause protects groups of persons from discriminatory treatment based on membership in a class, such as race, alienage, or national origin. *See, e.g., Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886). The Court has also recognized equal protection claims brought by individuals who allege intentional discrimination based on their membership in a “class of one,” but only where the plaintiff demonstrates that she has been “intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that there is no rational basis for the difference in treatment.” *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam). Critically, however, the “class of one” theory does not extend to every arbitrary or spiteful government act. Rather, the plaintiff must show that the government’s differential treatment lacked any “rational basis” and was motivated solely by “illegitimate animus” or “spite.” *Id.* at 565.

The Court of Appeals erred by holding that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim merely by alleging that state action was “motivated solely by a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386, 388 (7th Cir. 1998). That standard conflates a bare allegation of subjective ill will with a constitutional violation. This Court has never recognized a freestanding equal protection claim based solely on a government official’s personal animus, divorced from any showing that the plaintiff was treated differently from others similarly situated. *See Olech*, 528 U.S. at 565 (requiring “intentional treatment” different from “others similarly situated”).

The Court of Appeals’ approach would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general tort remedy for any citizen who feels singled out by a government decision. That result is inconsistent with the limited role of the federal courts in reviewing state and local government actions. *See, e.g., Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (the Equal Protection Clause does not “assure uniformity of judicial decisions” or “immunize” citizens from “every possible abuse of power”). Indeed, this Court has cautioned that the “class of one” theory must be applied with “care” to avoid turning “every run-of-the-mill dispute between a citizen and the government into a federal constitutional case.” *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 565 (Breyer, J., concurring).

Here, respondent Grace Olech alleged that the Village demanded a 33-foot easement as a condition of connecting her property to the municipal water supply, while requiring only a 15-foot easement from other property owners. She did not allege that she was a member of any identifiable class or group, nor did she provide evidence that the Village’s demand lacked any rational basis. Instead, she relied solely on the conclusory assertion that the Village acted out of “spite.” That is insufficient to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. *See, e.g., Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 601-02 (2008) (the “class of one” theory does not apply in the context of discretionary government decisions, where “the Equal Protection Clause is not a guarantee against incorrect or ill-advised decisions”).

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
