**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges touches the core of state sovereignty. The structure of state government—including the tenure of its judges—is a matter traditionally reserved to the States. *Parker v. Brown*, 317 U.S. 341, 362–63 (1943). This Court has long recognized that Congress must speak with unmistakable clarity before it may displace state authority in such “traditionally sensitive areas.” *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). The ADEA does not meet that standard.

The Act defines “employee” to exclude any “appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall squarely within this exclusion. In our common-law system, judges necessarily make policy when they decide cases of first impression, fill statutory gaps, and develop the common law. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (“It is emphatically the province of the judicial department to say what the law is.”). That duty inevitably involves the exercise of discretion and judgment that is indistinguishable from policymaking in other branches. *Swift v. Tyson*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1, 18–19 (1842) (recognizing that judges in the common-law tradition develop general principles of law). The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, establishes rules of professional conduct for the bar and resolves disputes within the judicial hierarchy—plainly policymaking functions.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would render the “policymaking level” exception a nullity for the entire judicial branch. Congress cannot have intended to exclude only elected judges (who are already exempted elsewhere) while including appointed judges who exercise identical powers. The structure of the exception confirms that Congress meant to exclude all high-level state officials who wield significant discretion, including judges.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Rational Basis Review**

Even if the ADEA applied, the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. Under rational basis review, a classification “must be sustained if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78–79 (1911). Missouri’s mandatory retirement age of 70 is rationally related to several legitimate interests: ensuring that judges possess the mental and physical vigor required for the demanding work of judging; promoting the orderly administration of justice by avoiding case-by-case removal proceedings; and maintaining public confidence in an independent judiciary.

The State need not produce evidence to support its reasoning; it is enough that “any state of facts reasonably can be conceived” that sustains the classification. *Id.* at 79. This Court has repeatedly upheld age-based classifications under rational basis review. *See Williamson v. Lee Optical, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) (rational basis does not require perfection); *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (state may address one aspect of a problem at a time). The fact that some judges remain competent past 70 does not render the classification irrational. *See Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920) (classification need not be mathematically precise). Nor does the availability of less restrictive alternatives undermine rationality. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961) (state may choose a bright-line rule over a more individualized approach).

Petitioners’ claim that judges do not make policy, and thus are not “appointees on the policymaking level,” is belied by the very nature of judging. A judge who resolves unsettled questions of law does what policymakers do: he creates law. *Marbury*, 5 U.S. at 177. The Equal Protection Clause does not require Missouri to treat judges like other employees whose work is purely ministerial. *Goesaert v. Cleary*, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948) (states may draw lines that reflect “the particular context of the activity”). 

**Conclusion**

Because Congress has not clearly expressed an intent to displace Missouri’s long-standing constitutional provision, and because that provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
