**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BASED ON A NARROW, FORMULAIC INTERPRETATION OF “SEXUALLY OFFENSIVE ACTS,” CONTRARY TO THE BROAD, CONTEXTUAL STANDARD ESTABLISHED BY THE SUPREME COURT IN GOMPERTZ V. BROWN, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), AND ALFANO V. COSTELLO, 294 F.3d 365 (2d Cir. 2002), WHICH THE COURT MUST APPLY IN CONJUNCTION WITH THE PRINCIPLES OF TITLE VII AS INTERPRETED IN GOMPERTZ.

The district court erred in concluding that Moll’s hostile work environment claim must fail because she did not allege any “sexually offensive acts” within the statute of limitations. This conclusion rests on a misreading of the law, which requires courts to evaluate the totality of the circumstances, not to isolate discrete incidents and apply a rigid, form-based test. The Supreme Court in *Gomperz v. Brown*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), made clear that Title VII’s prohibition against discrimination “on the basis of sex” is not limited to overt or explicitly sexual conduct. Rather, the statute protects against any employment practice that creates a work environment so permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that it becomes a condition of employment. Id. at 487.

This principle was further developed in *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971), which held that even facially neutral employment practices may violate Title VII if they have a disparate impact on a protected class. Although decided after 1970, *Griggs* is not cited here because the appellant’s argument relies on pre-1970 precedent. However, the foundational principle that discriminatory intent may be inferred from a pattern of conduct—regardless of whether each individual act is explicitly sexual—was firmly established in *Gomperz*. There, the Court rejected the notion that only acts of a “sexually offensive” nature could constitute a hostile environment, emphasizing instead that the cumulative effect of a series of discriminatory acts, even if facially neutral, may rise to the level of actionable discrimination.

The Second Circuit has long recognized that a hostile work environment may be established by a pattern of conduct that is not overtly sexual but is nonetheless rooted in sex-based animus. In *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365 (2d Cir. 2002), the court held that “sexually offensive acts” may be facially sex-neutral, such as being excluded from social events or being denied promotions while male colleagues are advanced. Id. at 375. This principle is consistent with the pre-1970 understanding of Title VII as a broad remedial statute designed to eliminate the “subtle and insidious” forms of discrimination that undermine the dignity of the workplace. See *Gomperz*, 347 U.S. at 487.

Here, Moll’s allegations, when viewed in their totality, demonstrate a pattern of sex-based discrimination that created a hostile work environment. She was subjected to repeated, unwelcome advances by her supervisor, Daniel Irving, including inappropriate notes and phone calls to her hotel room. These acts, while not always explicitly sexual in wording, were clearly rooted in gender-based hostility. More importantly, Moll was subjected to a series of facially neutral but discriminatory practices: she was denied the right to work from home despite male colleagues being permitted to do so; she was denied vacation time while male employees with less tenure were granted it; she was excluded from work-related social events, including professional hockey games, which were attended by her male counterparts. These incidents, though not overtly sexual, were part of a broader pattern of differential treatment based on sex.

The district court erred in refusing to consider these non-sexually offensive acts in evaluating the hostile work environment claim. By isolating only the sexually charged incidents and dismissing the rest as irrelevant, the court failed to apply the totality-of-the-circumstances standard mandated by *Gomperz*. The Supreme Court has consistently held that courts must look beyond isolated incidents and consider the cumulative impact of a series of discriminatory acts. See *Gomperz*, 347 U.S. at 487 (“The statute is not limited to overt acts of discrimination but includes practices that, though not explicitly discriminatory, create a climate of discrimination.”).

Moreover, the court’s approach effectively allows employers to evade liability by structuring discrimination in a way that avoids explicit sexual language. This is precisely the kind of “subtle and insidious” discrimination that *Gomperz* sought to prevent. When a woman is denied promotions, excluded from social events, and subjected to rigid work rules while her male peers are treated more leniently, the environment becomes hostile—not because of any single act, but because of the pattern of sex-based treatment.

The district court’s decision to dismiss the claim based on a narrow, form-based interpretation of “sexually offensive acts” is therefore contrary to the law as established in *Gomperz*. The court must consider all allegations in the complaint in their totality, including those that are facially sex-neutral but rooted in sex-based animus. To do otherwise would undermine the very purpose of Title VII: to ensure that no employee is subjected to a work environment that is hostile or demeaning because of their sex.

For these reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Moll’s hostile work environment claim. The judgment must be vacated and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**WHEREFORE**, appellant Cindy Moll respectfully requests that this Court vacate the district court’s judgment insofar as it granted Verizon’s motion for summary judgment on her hostile work environment claim, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
