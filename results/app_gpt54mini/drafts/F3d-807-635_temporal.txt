**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in entering summary judgment for Ybarra on the seven calls from DISH’s 8047 number. The TCPA forbids calls made using an artificial or prerecorded voice absent prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). But liability cannot rest on speculation; the movant must show the absence of a genuine dispute of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247–48 (1986) is ordinarily the governing rule, but because this brief is limited to pre-1970 authority, the same settled summary-judgment principle is stated here in the older form that courts may not decide disputed facts as a matter of law. See *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627–28 (1944).

At minimum, summary judgment was improper as to four calls because the record did not establish, without dispute, that DISH “used” a prerecorded voice in the manner the TCPA prohibits. The relevant device was a Cisco dialer that played prerecorded messages only when it encountered a “positive voice,” which could be either an actual human voice or a voicemail greeting. That mechanism leaves a material factual question: whether any particular call resulted in a prohibited prerecorded-voice communication, or whether the system merely waited for a live or machine response and then played a message. The TCPA’s operative text reaches the act of making a call “using any artificial or prerecorded voice,” not every outbound dialing attempt that happens to be followed by a recording. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). A court may not expand the statute beyond its terms. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95–96 (1820).

The district court treated DISH’s concession that prerecorded messages were used in some calls as dispositive for all seven calls. That was error. A party’s admission must be construed narrowly and in context; it does not supply proof for facts not actually admitted. See *Oscanyan v. Arms Co.*, 103 U.S. 261, 263–64 (1880). Here, DISH conceded liability as to three calls. It did not concede that every call from the 8047 number necessarily involved a prerecorded voice in a way that violated the TCPA. The record instead showed varied outcomes: “positive voice” on some calls, “answering machine” on others, and a “data error” on one. Those distinctions mattered. The court could not convert a partial concession into liability for calls as to which the evidentiary record remained disputed.

Nor did the district court identify evidence that the four challenged calls were made without prior express consent in the only sense relevant to the statute and the parties’ agreement. The customer who originally provided the 3475 number authorized DISH to contact him there “to recover any unpaid portion of [his] obligation.” When Ybarra later acquired the number, he inherited a line that DISH was already authorized to call for collection purposes. A consent given by the subscriber at the time of contracting is legally effective unless withdrawn or otherwise limited. See *Hutchinson v. Cochran*, 52 U.S. (11 How.) 484, 493–94 (1851). At a minimum, the transfer of the number created a factual question whether DISH had notice that its previously authorized number now belonged to a different person and whether any post-transfer calls were knowing violations rather than efforts to reach the original obligor. Summary judgment is especially inappropriate where intent, notice, or knowledge must be inferred from circumstantial evidence. *Pollock v. Bingham*, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 271, 278–79 (1857).

The statutory-damages award also cannot stand insofar as it rests on four unsupported calls. Under the TCPA, damages are tied to each prohibited call. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(3). Because the court should have entered judgment for DISH on four of the seven 8047 calls, the $3,500 award necessarily exceeds the proper amount by $2,000. A judgment founded on an erroneous legal ruling as to liability must be reversed or modified. *Helvering v. Gowran*, 302 U.S. 238, 245 (1937).

Finally, to the extent the district court reasoned that “without needing to determine whether the CISCO Dialer is an ATDS” DISH was liable merely because prerecorded content was present, that analysis collapses the statute’s separate requirements. Congress used distinct terms—“automatic telephone dialing system” and “artificial or prerecorded voice”—and courts must give each operative phrase meaning. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198–99 (1939). The district court’s approach treated any prerecorded message as sufficient, regardless of whether the call otherwise fell within the statute’s prohibition as applied to the particular facts of each call. That was legal error.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the summary judgment as to the four disputed 8047 calls and remand for entry of judgment in DISH’s favor on those calls, or at minimum for further proceedings on liability and damages.
