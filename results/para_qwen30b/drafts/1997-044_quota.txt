**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud, including statutory treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, may be discharged under Chapter 7 of the Bankruptcy Code—is governed by the clear language and settled precedent of 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The Bankruptcy Court’s determination that Edward S. Cohen’s debt to the tenants is nondischargeable, and that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are likewise nondischargeable, is fully supported by law and fact. This Court should affirm.

### I. The Debt Was Obtained by Actual Fraud Under § 523(a)(2)(A)

Section 523(a)(2)(A) renders nondischargeable any debt “to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 284, 111 S.Ct. 654, 658, 112 L.Ed.2d 755 (1991). The Supreme Court has made clear that “actual fraud” under this provision includes not only affirmative misrepresentations but also “misrepresentations by conduct, including silence or inaction where there is a duty to speak.” *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992); see also *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118 (3d Cir.1995) (intent to deceive may be inferred from reckless disregard of truth). Here, Cohen knowingly charged rents in excess of the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance, despite having no legal basis for doing so and having failed to consult the Rent Leveling and Stabilization Board or any attorney. His silence in the face of a statutory duty to comply with rent control laws constituted an implicit representation that the rents he charged were lawful. *See In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr.E.D.Va.1991) (failure to disclose material facts in context of financial transaction may constitute actual fraud).

Cohen’s conduct was not mere negligence. He had no inquiry into the law, failed to read the ordinance, and ignored a formal notice from the Board that he had overcharged tenants. This pattern of conduct—deliberate ignorance, failure to seek legal advice, and continued charging of unlawful rents—constitutes reckless indifference to the truth. *See In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. at 834 (reckless disregard is “unreasonable conduct in disregard of a known or obvious risk from which it is highly probable that harm would follow”). The Third Circuit has held that such conduct satisfies both the knowledge and intent prongs of § 523(a)(2)(A). *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d at 1118–19. The Bankruptcy Court’s finding that Cohen acted with reckless indifference is not clearly erroneous and must be upheld.

### II. Statutory Treble Damages Are Not Punitive and Are Nondischargeable

The central issue is whether the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are dischargeable. The Court of Appeals correctly held that they are not. *See In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir.1993) (punitive damages are not dischargeable where they are “inextricably linked” to the underlying fraud). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195, 198 (9th Cir.1991), which held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not “obtained by fraud,” is inconsistent with the broader policy of the Bankruptcy Code and has been rejected by multiple circuits.

The key distinction lies in the nature of the damages. In *In re St. Laurent*, the Eleventh Circuit emphasized that “the debt” includes not only compensatory damages but also statutory penalties that are “foreseeable and directly tied to the fraudulent conduct.” 991 F.2d at 679. The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages for violations of its provisions. N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq. This is not a discretionary award of punitive damages; it is a codified consequence of fraud. *See In re Bowers*, 132 F.3d 105, 108 (3d Cir.1997) (statutory penalties imposed for fraud are not dischargeable because they are “part of the debt incurred through the fraudulent act”).

This Court has recognized that “the term ‘debt’ in the Bankruptcy Code is broadly defined to include any liability on a claim.” *Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 305, 112 S.Ct. 711, 714, 116 L.Ed.2d 731 (1992). The treble damages here were not imposed as punishment in the abstract but as a direct consequence of Cohen’s fraudulent conduct. They were “obtained by” fraud in the statutory sense. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198 (acknowledging that “the debt” may include statutory penalties). The Third Circuit has held that “a debt resulting from a fraudulent act is not dischargeable, even if the damages are enhanced by statute.” *In re Bowers*, 132 F.3d at 108.

Moreover, the “fresh start” policy of the Bankruptcy Code does not extend to those who commit fraud. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. at 286–87, 111 S.Ct. at 659. The Code is designed to protect the “honest but unfortunate debtor,” not those who exploit others through deceit. *Id.* Cohen’s conduct—charging unlawful rents to vulnerable, non-English-speaking tenants, ignoring formal notice, and refusing to repay—demonstrates a pattern of intentional misconduct.
