The district court erred in sustaining the Bureau of Prisons’ denial of eligibility under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). That statute authorizes a reduction of up to one year for an inmate “convicted of a nonviolent offense” who completes the residential drug-treatment program. The governing regulation implements the statute by reference to the definition of “crime of violence” in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which requires that the offense either “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force” or “by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force … may be used in the course of committing the offense.” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995).

A conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 for conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance contains no such element and, by its nature, does not involve the requisite risk. See Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990) (“the language of § 924(e) … requires the sentencing court to look only to the fact of conviction and the statutory definition of the prior offense”). The two-level enhancement imposed under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 for possession of a firearm is a sentencing factor, not an element of the offense of conviction. The Bureau therefore lacked authority to reclassify the offense as violent on that basis.

Program Statement 5162.02, which directs the Bureau to treat any § 846 conviction enhanced for firearm possession as a crime of violence, is inconsistent with both the statute and the regulation. An agency interpretation that contradicts the plain language of the governing regulation is not entitled to deference. See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (“If the intent of Congress is clear, that is the end of the matter; for the court, as well as the agency, must give effect to the unambiguously expressed intent of Congress.”). Because Roussos’s offense of conviction is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3), he is eligible for the reduction authorized by § 3621(e)(2)(B).
