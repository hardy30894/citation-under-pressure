**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—has profound implications for the scope of patent remedies and the balance between innovation incentives and fair compensation. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the award of entire product profits in this case, without requiring a proportionality analysis, is contrary to settled principles of patent law and the statutory framework governing design patent damages. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Statutory Text of 35 U.S.C. § 289 Mandates a Component-Based Damages Limitation

The starting point for any analysis of design patent damages is 35 U.S.C. § 289, which provides that “whoever, without authority, [makes, uses, or sells] any patented design… shall be liable to the patentee in damages.” The statute does not authorize recovery of “entire profits” from the infringer’s sale of a product containing the infringing design. Rather, it authorizes recovery of “damages” for the unauthorized use of a design. The term “damages” is not synonymous with “entire profits,” and the statute’s silence on the method of calculating damages does not permit courts to disregard the principle of proportionality.

This Court has long recognized that damages must be “just and reasonable,” and that “the measure of damages must be commensurate with the injury.” *H. F. Johnson & Son, Inc. v. A. L. M. Corp.*, 323 U.S. 472, 475 (1945). In *Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 85, 94 (1993), the Court emphasized that “the patentee is entitled to compensation for the actual harm caused by the infringement,” not windfall gains. The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of § 289 as permitting recovery of entire product profits—regardless of the design’s contribution to the product’s value—violates this foundational principle.

Moreover, the statutory text of § 289 is best read in light of the broader patent framework. Section 284 provides that “the court shall award the claimant damages adequate to compensate for the infringement,” and this Court has held that “damages must be based on the actual harm suffered.” *Pfaff*, 508 U.S. at 94. The Federal Circuit’s refusal to apply a component-based damages limitation renders § 289’s “adequate compensation” standard meaningless when the infringing design is only a part of a larger product.

### II. The Federal Circuit’s Approach Contradicts This Court’s Precedent on Proportionality in Patent Damages

This Court has repeatedly held that damages must be proportionate to the infringer’s use of the patented invention. In *Cuno Engineering v. Automatic Devices Corp.*, 314 U.S. 83, 90 (1941), the Court stated that “the measure of damages is the value of the benefit derived by the infringer from the use of the patented invention.” This principle was reaffirmed in *C. R. Bard, Inc. v. M3 Systems, Inc.*, 157 F.3d 1340, 1350 (Fed. Cir. 1998), where the court held that “damages should reflect the value of the patented feature, not the entire product.” The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case, which allows recovery of entire profits from a smartphone based on a design element of the phone’s bezel, directly contradicts this line of authority.

The Court’s decision in *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.*, 547 U.S. 388, 394 (2006), further underscores the importance of proportionality. There, the Court emphasized that “a patentee is not entitled to a windfall,” and that “the remedy must be tailored to the harm.” The Federal Circuit’s approach—awarding nearly $300 million in damages based on the entire smartphone’s profits—creates precisely the kind of windfall the Court has warned against. The design of the bezel, while distinctive, is not the source of the smartphone’s value; the value lies in the integrated system of hardware, software, and user experience.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Undermines the Purpose of Design Patents

Design patents are intended to protect the ornamental appearance of a product, not its functional or economic value. *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 828, 834 (1989). The purpose of a design patent is to protect the “aesthetic” aspect of a product, not to serve as a proxy for trade secret or utility patent protection. *Mazer v. Stein*, 347 U.S. 201, 217 (1954). If a design patent holder can recover entire product profits, the incentive structure is distorted: a company may choose to patent a minor design element and then extract disproportionate compensation from the entire product.

This Court has recognized that “the scope of a design patent is limited to the ornamental features of the article.” *Ward v. United States*, 157 U.S. 553, 558 (1895). The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of § 289 effectively ignores this limitation. It treats a design patent as if it conferred exclusive rights over the entire product, which is inconsistent with the statutory and constitutional basis of patent law.

### IV. The Federal Circuit’s Approach Is Inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s Guidance on Remedy

In *Halo Electronics, Inc. v. Pulse Electronics, Inc.*, 136 S. Ct. 1923, 1930 (2016), the Court held that “the patentee’s damages should reflect the actual harm caused by the infringement.” The Court emphasized that “the remedy must be tailored to the harm,” and that “the patentee is not entitled to a windfall.” The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case fails to meet this standard. The jury awarded damages based on the entire smartphone’s profits, even though the infringing design was only a component of the product.
