STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews de novo a district court's determination that it lacks subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA"). Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 461 U.S. 480, 493-94 (1983) (holding that federal jurisdiction under the FSIA presents a question of law for the court).

ARGUMENT

I. The Commercial Activity Exception Applies Because Punjab's Agent Negotiated a Substitute-Property Agreement Within the United States.

The FSIA strips immunity where an action "is based . . . upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Whether conduct is "commercial" turns on its nature, not its purpose. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992) (holding that a state engages in commercial activity where it acts "in the manner of a private player within" the market, and that "the question is not whether the foreign government is acting with a profit motive," but "whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs . . . are the type of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce"). The district court examined only the initial expropriation, ignoring the second, separately actionable transaction: the Government of Punjab dispatched a representative to the United States who negotiated and offered Beg a substitute parcel in exchange for the taken land. That negotiation—an offer to convey title in settlement of a property dispute—is precisely the sort of bargaining a private landowner or developer routinely undertakes. It bears none of the hallmarks of a sovereign act such as regulation, taxation, or military requisition.

Under Saudi Arabia v. Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 356-57 (1993), courts must identify "the particular conduct on which the [plaintiff's] claim is based" and ask whether that conduct is commercial. Beg's claim is not merely that Pakistan took his land—it is that Punjab's agent induced him to accept substitute property through representations made on American soil, representations later repudiated when the Supreme Court of Pakistan found Punjab lacked good title to the substitute parcel. The "gravamen" of the complaint, Nelson, 507 U.S. at 358, thus includes an act performed in the United States in connection with commercial activity, satisfying the second clause of § 1605(a)(2) independent of any direct-effects analysis.

II. Even If the Original Taking Was Sovereign, Pakistan's Subsequent Commercial Dealings Are Severable From That Act and Independently Confer Jurisdiction.

Courts have long recognized that a sovereign's initial expropriation and its later commercial conduct respecting the same property are analytically distinct for FSIA purposes. In Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba, 425 U.S. 682, 695 (1976), the Supreme Court distinguished the "classic" sovereign act of expropriation from subsequent commercial dealings, holding that "the mere assertion of sovereignty as a defense to a claim arising out of purely commercial acts" is unavailing, because commercial activity does not become sovereign "simply because it is related to an expropriation." Following that logic, the Fifth Circuit in Callejo v. Bancomer, S.A., 764 F.2d 1101, 1109-10 (5th Cir. 1985), held that although a bank's expropriation was itself immune, the bank's later commercial handling of certificates of deposit tied to the expropriated assets remained subject to suit under the commercial activity exception. The Ninth Circuit reached the identical result in West v. Multibanco Comermex, S.A., 807 F.2d 820, 826-27 (9th Cir. 1987), severing sovereign expropriation from the bank's subsequent commercial conduct regarding the same funds.

The same severability applies here. Even accepting that the original taking of Beg's eleven and one-half acres was a sovereign act, Punjab's later offer, negotiation, and attempted conveyance of substitute property—including the representations made through its agent inside the United States—are commercial acts wholly distinct from the taking itself and independently satisfy § 1605(a)(2).

III. Alternatively, the Direct-Effects Clause Is Satisfied.

Should this Court find no act performed in the United States, jurisdiction still lies under the third clause of § 1605(a)(2), which reaches acts "outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." A direct effect exists where it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's . . . activity," without any intervening element. Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618 (rejecting any requirement that the effect be "substantial" or "foreseeable"). Beg, a resident who was pursued and negotiated with in the United States, suffered the financial consequence of Pakistan's repudiation directly in the United States, where he holds his assets and pursues his livelihood. See Zappia Middle East Constr. Co. v. Emirate of Abu Dhabi, 215 F.3d 247, 251-53 (2d Cir. 2000) (analyzing whether nonpayment causes a direct effect where the plaintiff's financial loss is felt). The nature-versus-purpose distinction, applied in de Sanchez v. Banco Central de Nicaragua, 770 F.2d 1385, 1393 (5th Cir. 1985), confirms that Punjab's conduct—offering and later disclaiming title to a specific parcel—was commercial in nature regardless of its governmental motivation.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the district court's dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction should be reversed and this case remanded for further proceedings.
