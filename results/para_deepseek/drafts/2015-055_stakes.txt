**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague—does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules of criminal procedure do not apply retroactively to cases that have become final unless the rule is a “watershed rule[] of criminal procedure” implicating the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the proceeding. *Id.* at 311. *Johnson* announced a new procedural rule, not a substantive one, and it is not a watershed rule.

**I. *Johnson* Announced a New Procedural Rule**

A rule is substantive if it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). Conversely, a rule is procedural if it “regulate[s] only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability.” *Id.* at 353. *Johnson* struck down the residual clause as unconstitutionally vague, but it did not decriminalize any conduct. The residual clause itself did not define a crime; it merely identified certain prior convictions that trigger a sentencing enhancement. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 596. Sentencing enhancements are “traditionally decided by judges” and are “not part of the criminal offense itself.” *Alleyne v. United States*, 570 U.S. 99, 111 (2013). Accordingly, *Johnson* altered the *method* by which courts determine whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony, not the *substance* of what conduct is punishable.

This Court has consistently held that rules governing sentencing enhancements are procedural. *See, e.g., Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 200 (2016) (distinguishing substantive rules that “prohibit[] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants” from procedural rules that “regulate only the manner of determining the appropriate punishment”). *Johnson* does not prohibit any punishment; it merely requires courts to apply a more precise definition of “violent felony.” That is a procedural change. *See also Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353 (holding that a rule requiring juries to find aggravating factors in capital cases is procedural because it “regulates the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability”).

**II. *Johnson* Is Not a Watershed Rule of Criminal Procedure**

Even if *Johnson* were a procedural rule, it would not apply retroactively unless it is a “watershed rule[] of criminal procedure” that is “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311 (internal quotation marks omitted). A rule qualifies as a watershed only if it “alter[s] our understanding of the bedrock procedural elements essential to the fairness of a proceeding.” *Sawyer v. Smith*, 497 U.S. 227, 242 (1990). This category is “extremely narrow.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 352.

*Johnson* does not meet that high bar. The rule does not prevent the conviction of an innocent person, nor does it ensure the accuracy of the underlying determination of guilt. *See *Whorton v. Bockting*, 549 U.S. 406, 418 (2007) (holding that a rule is watershed only if it “improves the accuracy of criminal trials”). *Johnson* addresses the *definition* of a predicate offense, not the reliability of the fact-finding process. Moreover, the rule does not affect the “fundamental fairness” of the proceeding because courts can still reliably determine whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony under the elements clause or the enumerated-offenses clause of § 924(e)(2)(B). *See Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 606 (noting that those clauses remain valid).

**III. The Residual Clause’s Vagueness Does Not Warrant Retroactive Application**

Welch’s collateral challenge cannot succeed because *Johnson* did not create a “substantive” rule that decriminalizes his prior conviction. The residual clause was not a criminal statute; it was a sentencing provision. *See Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 596. This Court has long held that “vagueness challenges to statutes that do not involve First Amendment freedoms must be examined in the light of the facts of the case at hand.” *United States v. Mazurie*, 419 U.S. 544, 550 (1975). The residual clause’s vagueness does not render Welch’s prior conviction invalid or his conduct lawful. *See Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 600 (explaining that the residual clause’s “serious potential risk” standard is “impossible to apply consistently”). That does not retroactively undo the fact that Florida robbery, as Welch committed it, involved force capable of causing physical pain or injury. *See United States v. Castleman*, 572 U.S. 157, 170 (2014) (defining “physical force” as “force capable of causing physical pain or injury”). Welch’s conviction under the residual clause was not “arbitrary” in his case; rather, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the categorical approach to find that Florida robbery categorically presents a serious potential risk of physical injury. *See James v. United States*, 550 U.S. 192, 207 (2007).

**CONCLUSION**

Because *Johnson* announced a new procedural rule that is not a watershed rule of criminal procedure, it does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
