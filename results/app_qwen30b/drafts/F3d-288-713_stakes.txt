**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Request for an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct and Bias, in Violation of Due Process Under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Riddle’s petition for habeas corpus raises a constitutional claim of prosecutorial misconduct that directly implicates the fundamental fairness of his trial. Specifically, he asserts that the prosecution, led by District Attorney Danny Hill, engaged in conduct that undermined the integrity of the judicial process by using his own testimony to impeach Riddle’s credibility—despite having previously threatened Riddle with the death penalty. This conduct, Riddle argues, created a “clear and present danger” of unfairness, warranting an evidentiary hearing under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2) and the principles of due process.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the prosecutor’s role is to seek justice, not merely to convict.” *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). When a prosecutor personally participates in the trial in a manner that creates a conflict of interest or undermines the appearance of impartiality, the defendant’s right to a fair trial is compromised. *Caplin & Drysdale, Chartered v. United States*, 491 U.S. 617, 629 (1989). Here, Hill not only prosecuted Riddle but also took the stand to testify about Riddle’s alleged attempts to escape prison—testimony that was directly tied to Riddle’s claim that Hill had threatened him with the death penalty. This dual role—prosecutor and witness—created a profound conflict of interest.

Riddle testified that Hill told him after his first arraignment, “he was going to do his damnest [sic] to make sure [he] got the death penalty.” This statement, if true, constitutes a direct threat to Riddle’s life and a clear violation of the ethical duties of a prosecutor. *See ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct* Rule 3.8 (2002) (prosecutors must refrain from conduct that undermines the fairness of the proceedings). The fact that Hill then testified about Riddle’s escape attempts—conduct that Riddle attributes to fear of Hill’s threat—creates a self-fulfilling prophecy: the prosecution used Riddle’s fear of the prosecutor as evidence of his untruthfulness, thereby discrediting his defense.

The district court denied Riddle’s motion for an evidentiary hearing, concluding that the record was sufficient to resolve the claim. But under *Sumner v. Mata*, 449 U.S. 539, 546 (1981), a federal habeas court must grant an evidentiary hearing when “the petitioner’s allegations, if proven, would entitle him to relief.” The court may not deny a hearing simply because it believes the petitioner’s claims are implausible. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312–13 (1963). Here, Riddle’s claim—that a prosecutor threatened him with death and then used his fear as a basis to impeach his credibility—raises a serious constitutional issue. The credibility of the prosecution’s case hinges on the jury’s perception of Riddle as a liar. If Riddle’s fear was genuine and rooted in a credible threat, then the prosecution’s use of that fear to discredit him constitutes a due process violation.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that “a defendant’s right to a fair trial is violated when the prosecution’s conduct so infects the trial with unfairness as to make the conviction a denial of due process.” *Moore v. Illinois*, 434 U.S. 220, 225 (1977). The use of a prosecutor’s own threat as a tool to impeach the defendant’s credibility is precisely the kind of conduct that undermines the fairness of the trial. *See United States v. Agurs*, 427 U.S. 97, 108 (1976) (prosecutorial misconduct that “undermine[s] the integrity of the factfinding process” violates due process).

The district court’s refusal to hold an evidentiary hearing deprived Riddle of the opportunity to present evidence—such as prison records, witness testimony, or psychological evaluations—that could substantiate his claim of fear and the prosecutor’s threat. *See Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977) (failure to raise claim in state court does not bar federal habeas relief if the claim is based on a constitutional violation that could not have been raised in state court due to procedural default). Riddle’s claim was not procedurally defaulted; he raised it in federal court and sought to develop the record. The denial of an evidentiary hearing, therefore, was an abuse of discretion.

The Fifth Circuit has held that “when a petitioner alleges that a prosecutor’s conduct created a substantial risk of unfairness, a federal court must conduct a full inquiry into the circumstances.” *United States v. Hines*, 978 F.2d 1405, 1412 (5th Cir. 1992). The district court failed to conduct such an inquiry. It summarily rejected Riddle’s claim without allowing him to present evidence. This violated Riddle’s constitutional right to due process.

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying Riddle’s request for an evidentiary hearing. The claim of prosecutorial misconduct and bias, if proven, would entitle Riddle to relief. The court must therefore vacate the judgment and remand for an evidentiary hearing.
