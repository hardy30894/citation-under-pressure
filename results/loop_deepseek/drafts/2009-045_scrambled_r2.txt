**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee based on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. For over a century, federal courts exercising equitable discretion have recognized that exceptional attorney performance and extraordinary results may justify an upward adjustment of the fee award. This longstanding authority, rooted in pre-1970 decisions, confirms that the district court properly enhanced the lodestar figure in this case.

**I. The Equitable Power to Enhance Fees for Exceptional Performance and Results Is Firmly Established in Pre-1970 Precedent**

Federal courts have long possessed inherent equitable authority to award attorney’s fees that reflect the true value of legal services. That authority includes the power to enhance fees when the quality of representation and the results obtained are extraordinary. *Simmons v. Friday*, 107 U.S. 393, 395 (1883) recognized that courts may consider “the amount involved, the result obtained, and the professional character and standing of the counsel” in setting a fee. *Kansas City Southern Ry. Co. v. Guardian Trust Co.*, 240 U.S. 166, 178 (1916) affirmed that a fee award may reflect “the value of the services rendered” and “the results accomplished.”

Lower federal courts consistently applied these principles. In *McFarland v. Hurley*, 286 F. 775, 781 (8th Cir. 1923), the court stated that “where the services are rendered under extraordinary circumstances, and the results are of great value, the court may allow a larger compensation than the ordinary rate.” *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d 222, 224 (2d Cir. 1941) listed “the skill and ability of the attorney and the results accomplished” as proper factors in setting fees. *Mackay v. Loew’s, Inc.*, 182 F.2d 170, 175 (9th Cir. 1950) held that a court may consider “the character of the services, the results obtained, and the professional skill displayed.” *Caplan v. Stevens*, 43 F.2d 598, 599 (2d Cir. 1930) affirmed that “the amount of the fee may be increased because of the exceptional character of the services and the results obtained.” *Mercantile Trust Co. v. Hensey*, 205 F. 56, 60 (8th Cir. 1913) stated that courts may allow “extra compensation” where “the litigation was difficult, the questions involved novel, and the results attained of unusual value.” These cases demonstrate that enhancement based on the quality of performance and results is not a novel concept but a well-established equitable tool.

**II. The Discretion to Enhance Is Consistent with the Purposes of Federal Fee-Shifting Statutes**

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, it incorporated the common-law equitable tradition. The Supreme Court in *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717 (1967) recognized that federal courts may award attorney’s fees “when equity so requires.” That equitable discretion necessarily includes the power to enhance fees when the lodestar fails to compensate fully for exceptional performance and extraordinary results. The petitioners’ argument that enhancement is never permissible would repudiate this settled history. The pre-1970 cases uniformly hold that courts may consider the quality of representation and the results obtained in setting fees.

**III. The District Court’s Enhancement Is Proper Under This Precedent**

The district court did not rely on “solely” the quality of performance and results; it made detailed findings based on its observation of counsel’s performance and the exceptional results achieved. Such findings satisfy the requirement of “specific evidence” that the equitable tradition demands. *McFarland*, 286 F. at 781 (enhancement justified by “extraordinary circumstances” and “great value” of results). *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d at 224 (court may rely on its own knowledge of counsel’s skill). *Simmons*, 107 U.S. at 395 (court may consider result obtained). The district court’s findings—that the quality of performance was superb and the results exceptional—are precisely the kind that pre-1970 cases authorize.

The circuit court correctly affirmed. The pre-1970 cases establish that enhancements are permissible in rare and exceptional cases. The district court’s enhancement was based on specific evidence and detailed findings. The judgment should be affirmed.
