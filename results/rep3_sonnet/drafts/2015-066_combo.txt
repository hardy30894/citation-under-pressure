**ARGUMENT**

**I. Because the Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, Bryant’s Tribal Convictions Were Not Constitutionally Infirm When Rendered, and Their Later Use as Section 117(a) Predicates Violates No Constitutional Right.**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel constrains the federal and state governments; it does not constrain the tribal governments that predate the Constitution and whose adjudicative authority flows from a separate source of sovereignty. As this Court held over a century ago, tribal courts exercise “not by virtue of the Constitution” but as an incident of tribes’ retained “powers of local government” that existed before the Constitution and were never surrendered. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896). Because that authority is “not held by the tribe as a grant from the government of the United States, but rather one of its original attributes of sovereignty,” *id.* at 384, the Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, simply does not attach to tribal proceedings. Congress’s plenary power over Indian affairs, recognized in *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 384–85 (1886), and the tribes’ own residual sovereignty, recognized as early as *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559–61 (1832), together establish that tribal courts operate under a distinct constitutional framework. A proceeding that never triggered the Sixth Amendment cannot retroactively become a “Sixth Amendment violation” merely because the conviction it produced is later used for another purpose in another sovereign’s court.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding therefore rests on a category error: it treats convictions that were valid when entered—because no federal constitutional counsel guarantee applied—as if they were void ab initio, akin to convictions obtained by trial courts that flouted an applicable Sixth Amendment right. That is incorrect. Compare *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 462–63 (1938) (holding that the Sixth Amendment secures counsel “in criminal prosecutions” conducted under the authority of the United States), with the tribal proceedings here, which were conducted under a wholly independent sovereign authority and governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act rather than the Sixth Amendment. There is no “violation” to launder into the federal prosecution because there was no constitutional guarantee to violate in the first instance.

**II. Section 117(a) Punishes Only the Instant Offense, for Which Bryant Received the Full Panoply of Sixth Amendment Protections, Including Appointed Counsel.**

Even setting aside tribal sovereignty, the use of a prior valid conviction as a status element of a recidivism statute does not offend the Sixth Amendment because such statutes do not reopen or repunish the prior offense. As this Court explained in rejecting a similar challenge to a habitual-offender enhancement, a recidivist sentence “is not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes,” but rather “a stiffened penalty for the latest crime, which is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one.” *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948). Section 117(a) operates the same way: it defines a new, distinct federal offense—domestic assault by a person with a defined recidivist history—and the prior convictions serve only as elements describing the defendant’s status, not as re-litigated adjudications of guilt for the earlier acts. Bryant was fully afforded appointed counsel, a jury trial, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt in the federal prosecution that actually punished him. That is all the Sixth Amendment requires.

This Court’s recidivism jurisprudence confirms that due process, not the Sixth Amendment’s counsel guarantee, governs the use of prior convictions in such statutes, and that guarantee is satisfied so long as the defendant may contest the fact and validity of the prior convictions in the current proceeding. *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 452 (1962) (recidivist statutes require only that “notice and an opportunity to be heard” attend the determination of prior-conviction status). Bryant received exactly that opportunity when he moved to dismiss the indictment below.

**III. Principles of Comity Require Federal Courts to Respect Tribal Judgments That Comply With Applicable Tribal Law, Just as They Respect Foreign Judgments That Comply With Foreign Law.**

This Court has long recognized that judgments of other sovereign systems are entitled to recognition when rendered under “a full and fair trial” consistent with that system’s own procedures, even where those procedures differ from federal court practice. *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 202–03, 205 (1895). Tribal courts, applying the procedural protections mandated by the Indian Civil Rights Act rather than the Sixth Amendment, are no less entitled to such respect. Nothing in *Chandler v. Fretag*, 348 U.S. 3, 9–10 (1954), which addressed the right to retained counsel in a state proceeding, suggests that a separate sovereign’s valid judgment becomes constitutionally suspect merely because that sovereign is not bound by the Sixth Amendment.

Nor does the use here resemble the use condemned in cases involving convictions rendered void by an actual violation of an applicable right to counsel. Unlike a state conviction obtained without counsel where counsel was constitutionally required and never waived, Bryant’s tribal convictions were lawful in the forum that rendered them. The tribal court did not disregard a right the Constitution guaranteed; it operated within the only legal framework the Constitution permits it—ICRA, not the Sixth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
