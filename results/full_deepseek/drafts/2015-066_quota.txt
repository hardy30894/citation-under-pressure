**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not constrain tribal courts, which are “unique aggregations” possessing “inherent sovereign authority” over their members. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978). The Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Only the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, governs tribal criminal procedure. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 62–63 (1978). ICRA does not require appointed counsel for indigent defendants in misdemeanor cases. *Id.* at 62 n.11. Thus, Bryant’s tribal-court convictions were valid under the only law that applies to them. The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a Sixth Amendment obligation where none exists, thereby “intrud[ing] needlessly on tribal self-government.” *Id.* at 71.

**II. Nichols v. United States Permits Use of Un counseled Misdemeanor Convictions as Predicate Offenses**

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction—valid because no term of imprisonment was imposed under *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)—may be used to enhance a later sentence. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746–47. The Court emphasized that recidivist statutes “penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Id.* at 747 (internal quotation marks omitted). The same logic applies here. Section 117(a) punishes the *current* domestic assault, using prior convictions solely to establish an element of the offense. Because the tribal-court convictions were valid when entered, they are “reliable enough” to serve as predicate offenses. *See Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748.

The Ninth Circuit mistakenly distinguished *Nichols* because it was a sentencing case, but that distinction is irrelevant. The Court in *Nichols* did not condition its holding on the guilt-punishment distinction; it relied on the reliability of the prior conviction and the absence of any Sixth Amendment violation. *Id.* at 746–48. Indeed, *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 661–62 (2002), confirms that a misdemeanor conviction itself remains valid even if the concomitant sentence of imprisonment is invalid. Thus, Bryant’s tribal-court convictions—which were never challenged on Sixth Amendment grounds because the Amendment does not apply—are unquestionably valid and may be used as predicate offenses.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent and Other Circuits**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a patent conflict with the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have upheld the use of uncounseled tribal-court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997–98 (10th Cir. 2011). Those circuits correctly recognized that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts and that *Nichols* controls. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding undermines Congress’s clear intent in enacting § 117(a) to “remove[] these recidivists from the communities that they repeatedly terrorize.” *See* dissent from denial of rehearing en banc, *United States v. Bryant*, No. 12-30168 (9th Cir. 2016) (Owens, J., dissenting). The result is a geographic anomaly: a defendant whose tribal-court convictions occurred in the Ninth Circuit is immune from federal prosecution, while an identical defendant in the Tenth Circuit faces felony charges. This Court should resolve the conflict.

**IV. Requiring Counsel in Tribal Courts Would Undermine Tribal Sovereignty and Congressional Policy**

Congress has carefully calibrated the scope of tribal-court jurisdiction and procedural rights. When it expanded tribal-court authority in the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010, it simultaneously required tribes to provide counsel for defendants facing *felony* prosecutions. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c). Congress did not impose the same requirement for misdemeanor cases. The Ninth Circuit’s rule effectively amends ICRA by judicial fiat, forcing tribes to provide counsel for all misdemeanor defendants who may later face federal charges. This “exercise[s] unnecessary judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance,” *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir. 1997), and violates the principle that “Congress, not the courts, should determine the appropriate level of federal oversight for tribal courts.” *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
