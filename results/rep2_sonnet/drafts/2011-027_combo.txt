# ARGUMENT

## THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR TITLE NAVIGABILITY REQUIRES A SEGMENT-BY-SEGMENT DETERMINATION OF NAVIGABILITY AT THE TIME OF STATEHOOD

Under the equal footing doctrine, title to the beds of navigable waters passes to a State upon its admission to the Union. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845). This Court has long held that the touchstone for determining whether a State acquired title to a riverbed is navigability **at the time the State was admitted**—not navigability at some later date, and not a generalized assessment of the river as a whole. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), articulated the controlling test: a river is navigable for title purposes if, "in their ordinary condition," the waters were "used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). Critically, *Utah* makes clear that this inquiry must be conducted with respect to the condition of the waterway "at the time of the admission of the State." *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75.

This temporal focus is not incidental to the test—it is the test. The equal footing doctrine transfers title at the moment of statehood by operation of constitutional law; title cannot be divested retroactively by post-statehood changes in a river's physical character, nor can it be created where none existed at the crucial moment. *Scott v. Lattig*, 227 U.S. 229 (1913), confirms that the sovereign's title vests "as of the time" of statehood and is unaffected by subsequent accretion, avulsion, or other changes to the watercourse. If subsequent events cannot divest title once vested, it follows a fortiori that a court cannot determine whether title vested in the first instance by reference to any time other than the moment of statehood itself.

*Utah* further demonstrates that this temporal inquiry must be conducted on a **segment-by-segment basis**. The Utah litigation concerned the navigability of the Colorado River, and this Court did not treat the river as a monolithic entity to be characterized as "navigable" or "non-navigable" in gross. Rather, the Court examined discrete stretches, recognizing that a river may be navigable in one reach and not in another, depending on the physical characteristics—rapids, gradient, depth—present in that particular segment at the relevant time. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87-92 (analyzing navigability of specific stretches including areas obstructed by rapids). This segment-specific approach reflects the practical reality that rivers are not uniform; a canyon reach choked with impassable rapids bears no resemblance, for title purposes, to a placid stretch capable of sustaining commercial traffic.

*United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), reinforces this segmented approach. There, the Court examined the navigability of Malheur Lake and its associated waterways not as an undifferentiated whole, but by assessing the specific character of each portion of the water body in question at the time of Oregon's admission. The Court's method necessarily required findings as to what conditions existed, segment by segment, "at the time" statehood was achieved. *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14.

The rationale for the statehood-specific, segment-specific test is rooted in the equal footing doctrine's core purpose: ensuring equality among the States and vindicating the sovereign authority each State possesses over its internal waters upon admission. *Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 230, teaches that the "shores of navigable waters, and the soils under them" belong to the States "in virtue of their sovereignty" as an incident of statehood itself. Because sovereignty attaches instantaneously at admission, the factual predicate for title—navigability—must likewise be assessed as of that instant. *Martin v. Waddell's Lessee*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 367 (1842), similarly grounds title in the sovereign character of the new State "when the Revolution took place," by analogy confirming that the relevant temporal reference point for title navigability is fixed at the moment sovereignty vests.

This Court's decision in *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77 (1922), further illustrates that navigability for title purposes is a historical, fact-bound inquiry tied to a specific point in time, not a modern assessment of a river's current condition or potential. The Court there examined the Arkansas River's navigability by reference to its condition when title questions arose under the equal footing doctrine, underscoring that courts must look backward to the relevant historical moment rather than to present-day characteristics of the waterway.

Montana's converse position—that navigability may be assessed by reference to a river's general characteristics without regard to the specific segment at issue or the specific moment of statehood—finds no support in this Court's precedent and cannot be reconciled with *Utah*'s explicit segment-by-segment methodology. The definition articulated in *The Daniel Ball* requires that the water be susceptible of use "in their ordinary condition"—language that necessarily contemplates the condition of a particular stretch of river at a particular time, not an abstracted, river-wide generalization untethered to either geography or chronology.

Accordingly, the constitutional test mandated by *Utah*, *Pollard*, *Scott*, and *Oregon* requires trial courts to determine, segment by segment, whether each stretch of a river was navigable **at the time of statehood**. Any other approach would permit courts to import irrelevant evidence of post-statehood commercial development or, conversely, to deny States their rightful title based on later-imposed obstructions—results flatly inconsistent with the equal footing doctrine's guarantee that title vests, once and finally, at the moment each State joins the Union.
