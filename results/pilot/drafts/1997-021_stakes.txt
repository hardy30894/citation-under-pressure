**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll of employees is not only inconsistent with the Act’s purpose but also fundamentally irrational. The Board’s standard renders polling—its own preferred method for testing union support—functionally useless in most circumstances, thereby undermining the very goal of stable collective bargaining relationships it claims to serve.

The Board’s position rests on a false dichotomy: it asserts that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), and thus must be strictly limited. Yet the Board simultaneously permits employers to withdraw recognition or seek a Board-supervised election—procedures that are equally disruptive, if not more so—when the same evidentiary threshold is met. See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). If polling is inherently problematic, then so too are elections and unilateral withdrawal. The Board’s refusal to apply a consistent standard across these three options reveals a policy-driven preference, not a rational interpretation of the Act.

Moreover, the Board’s own rationale for allowing polling—“to obtain more certain, precise information” than a mere “reasonable doubt” provides—Texas Petrochemicals, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063—undermines its own threshold. If polling is meant to resolve uncertainty, then it should be available when the employer has *some* basis for doubt, not only when that doubt is already “reasonable” under the withdrawal standard. The Board’s insistence on a higher bar for polling than for elections or withdrawal creates an absurd result: an employer may lawfully conduct a Board-supervised election after meeting the same evidentiary standard, but may not conduct a poll—even one with the same procedural safeguards—unless it already meets that same standard. This is not regulation; it is obstruction.

The courts of appeals have correctly recognized this flaw. In *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), the court held that an employer may poll employees “if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal. The same principle was affirmed in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), and *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). These decisions reflect a sound understanding of the Act: polling is a legitimate tool for employers to test union support when they have *some* objective basis for doubt, not only when they are ready to abandon the union entirely.

The Board’s contrary rule is not merely irrational—it is arbitrary and capricious. The Administrative Procedure Act requires that agency action be “not arbitrary or capricious.” 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The Board’s decision to deny polling unless the employer already has “reasonable doubt” fails this test. It creates a self-defeating cycle: no employer will poll unless it already doubts the union’s majority, but the only way to know whether the union still has majority support is to poll. The Board’s standard thus prevents employers from ever obtaining the very information it claims to allow them to seek.

II. THE BOARD’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective indications” of a loss of union support is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Administrative Law Judge found that seven employees had expressed opposition to the union, and the Board affirmed that finding. But the Board’s refusal to count additional statements—made by employees who were either not in the unit on January 25, 1991, or whose statements were deemed equivocal—was arbitrary and contrary to precedent.

The Board correctly applied the date of January 25, 1991—the date of Allentown Mack’s letter refusing recognition—as the relevant benchmark. See *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4 (5th Cir. 1978). Employees hired after that date could not have informed the company’s decision to poll. Similarly, employees no longer employed by January 25 were not part of the bargaining unit and thus their views were irrelevant. The Board’s exclusion of such individuals was proper.

However, the Board erred in discounting the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick, during a job interview, stated that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” The ALJ found this “at best equivocal,” but the Board failed to explain why. Ridgick had previously inquired about decertification in 1986, and his statement—made in the context of a new employer’s non-union stance—was not inherently insincere. The Board’s reliance on *Middleboro* is misplaced: that case held that statements made during interviews in a non-union context are not reliable *if* the employer has already signaled opposition. Here, the employer had not yet taken a position; Ridgick’s statement was made in response to a question about union representation, not as a pre-emptive declaration.
