The Third Circuit erred in affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) because the trial court's jury instruction on "true threats" did not require the government to prove Elonis' subjective intent to threaten. The First Amendment requires that a speaker have the subjective intent to threaten in order for their speech to be considered a "true threat" and thus outside the protection of the First Amendment. Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). In Black, the Supreme Court held that a "true threat" is a statement where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals. Id. The Court emphasized that the speaker's intent is crucial in determining whether a statement is a "true threat." Id. at 360.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat, is misplaced. The Kosma standard is an objective one that fails to account for the speaker's subjective intent. The Supreme Court's decision in Black superseded Kosma, requiring a subjective intent to threaten.

Other circuits have also recognized the importance of subjective intent in determining "true threats." For example, the Ninth Circuit has held that a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be a threat. United States v. Cassel, 408 F.3d 622, 633 (9th Cir. 2005). The First Circuit has also suggested that a subjective intent standard is appropriate for "true threats." United States v. Whiffen, 121 F.3d 18, 21 (1st Cir. 1997).

Requiring a subjective intent to threaten is consistent with the First Amendment's protection of speech. A subjective intent standard ensures that individuals are not prosecuted for speech that they did not intend to be threatening. Elonis' Facebook posts, while disturbing and violent, were made in the context of a rap lyrics and therapeutic self-expression. Without evidence of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten, his conviction under § 875(c) violates the First Amendment.

The government may argue that an objective standard is necessary to protect individuals from the fear of violence caused by "true threats." However, this concern can be addressed through other means, such as laws prohibiting harassment or stalking. The First Amendment requires that speech be protected unless it is intended to be threatening.

The Third Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Elonis' conviction under § 875(c) should be vacated due to the trial court's erroneous jury instruction on "true threats."
