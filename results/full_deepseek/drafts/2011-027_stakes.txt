## ARGUMENT

### I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Requires a Segment-Specific Navigability Inquiry as of the Date of Statehood

The "equal footing doctrine" provides that a State admitted to the Union acquires title to the beds of only those waters that were "navigable in fact" at the moment of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). This title passes to the State, not by grant, but by the constitutional principle that new States enter on an equal footing with the original Thirteen Colonies. *Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 228–29 (1845). Because the inquiry is constitutional, it is governed by federal law, not state law or state court determinations. *Id.* at 230; *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75.

The constitutional test for navigability is "navigable in fact" — that is, whether the watercourse "is used, or is susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). Critically, this inquiry is not a general historical assessment of a river's entire length. Rather, the United States has "consistently recognized" that navigability must be assessed "segment by segment." *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 552 (1981) (citing *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76–77). A river may be navigable in one reach and nonnavigable in another, and title to riverbeds follows the particular stretch at issue. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586–87 (1922) (holding that title to a riverbed "depends upon the navigability of the particular portion of the stream" claimed).

The time-of-statehood requirement is equally well settled. The "navigability of a waterway is to be determined by its natural and ordinary condition at the time of the admission of the State into the Union." *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75. Evidence of subsequent improvements or later changes in the river's character cannot retroactively confer or defeat title. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935); *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55–56 (1926) (emphasizing that the inquiry focuses on the river's condition at statehood). Consequently, a court must make a discrete finding of fact: was the *specific reach* of the river at issue actually navigable, or susceptible of being made so, in its ordinary condition, in 1889?

### II. The Montana Supreme Court Applied an Erroneous Whole-River and Ahistorical Test

The decision below departed from this constitutional framework in two fundamental respects. First, the Montana Supreme Court treated the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as unitary watercourses, permitting the State to establish title to any riverbed by showing that the river was navigable somewhere. That is contrary to the governing federal rule. As this Court explained in *Utah*, the "navigability of a river" for title purposes "may vary in its different parts," and the state's title extends "only to the river bed within the limits of the particular navigable reach." 283 U.S. at 76–77. The court below never identified the specific reaches on which PPL's dams are located, let alone made findings about the navigability of those reaches at statehood. Instead, it relied on generalized evidence of historical use — much of it concerning entirely different stretches of the rivers, or concerning the rivers as a whole. That approach cannot satisfy the constitutional test. *See Oklahoma*, 258 U.S. at 586–87.

Second, the lower court improperly used post-statehood evidence of commercial use and modern recreational boating to support its navigability findings. Its opinion references twentieth-century river traffic and other changed conditions to infer that the rivers must have been navigable in 1889. This is legally irrelevant. The Constitution's equal footing principle fixes the inquiry at the date of statehood; "the subsequent use of the river" cannot "have any retroactive effect" on the title question. *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 56. The court's reliance on later conditions conflates the "navigable-for-title" test with the broader "navigable-for-commerce" test under the Commerce Clause, which may be satisfied by modern uses. That conflation is reversible error. *Montana*, 450 U.S. at 556–57.

### III. The Appropriate Remedy Is Remand for Segment-Specific Findings at the Statehood Date

Because the Montana Supreme Court's navigability determination was made under the wrong legal standard, the damages award cannot stand. The "constitutional test" is one of "public law," and the State bears the burden of proving title to each riverbed it claims. *United States v. River Rouge Improvement Co.*, 269 U.S. 411, 419 (1926). The State has not met that burden, as the trial court made no findings that the precise segments at issue — the specific locations of PPL's dams on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers — were navigable in fact at the time of Montana's admission to the Union in 1889. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87–88 (remanding because the trial court failed to make "proper segregation" of navigable and nonnavigable stretches).

This Court's precedents require vacatur of the judgment and remand for the state courts to apply the correct segment-by-segment, time-of-statehood test. On remand, the trial court should be directed to make specific findings on each discrete reach of river underlying each PPL dam, considering only the natural and ordinary condition of those reaches in 1889. If the State cannot establish navigability on that basis, its claims for compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act necessarily fail.

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be reversed.
