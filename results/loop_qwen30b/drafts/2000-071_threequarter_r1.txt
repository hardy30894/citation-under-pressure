**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious groups whose activities are indistinguishable from religious worship. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use of school premises does not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The school’s policy, which prohibits the use of school facilities for religious purposes, is a content-neutral regulation of a limited public forum that is fully consistent with established constitutional precedent. The Club’s activities—centered on Bible lessons, prayer, and proselytization—are not protected speech under the First Amendment because they constitute religious worship, not secular moral instruction. The school’s decision to exclude the Club is justified by a legitimate and compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations, and the exclusion is neither viewpoint nor content discriminatory.

The First Amendment does not require a public school to open its facilities to all private groups, even those engaged in non-religious activities. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940), the Court held that “the right to speak and to publish is not absolute,” and that “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of expression in a way that is reasonable and viewpoint neutral.” This principle applies with equal force to public school facilities, which are not public forums by default. The school’s Community Use Policy, which restricts use of its facilities to non-religious purposes, is a reasonable, content-neutral regulation that serves a legitimate governmental interest in maintaining the separation of church and state.

The Good News Club’s activities are not merely the expression of a religious viewpoint on a secular subject; they are religious worship. The Club’s meetings begin with prayer, include singing hymns with explicitly religious lyrics, and involve the recitation of scripture as a form of religious instruction. In *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599, 605 (1961), the Court recognized that “the free exercise of religion includes the right to worship according to the dictates of one’s conscience,” but also acknowledged that “the state may regulate conduct that is religiously motivated.” The Club’s activities are not merely expressive—they are ritualistic and devotional. The “challenge and invitation” segment, which invites children to “believe on Jesus” and “pray this prayer to be saved,” is not moral instruction; it is a direct call to religious conversion. In *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 403 (1963), the Court held that “freedom of religious belief is absolute,” but noted that “the state may regulate conduct that is religiously motivated.” The Club’s conduct—prayer, Bible reading, and proselytization—is not protected speech under the First Amendment because it is not speech at all in the constitutional sense; it is worship.

The school’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint. The Club argues that it is being treated differently from secular groups like the Boy Scouts and the 4-H Club, but the distinction is not between religious and secular groups—it is between religious worship and moral development. In *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), the Court held that a school could not exclude a religious group from a limited public forum if it allowed secular groups to use the facility. But that case is distinguishable: the religious group in *Lamb’s Chapel* was not engaged in worship, but in teaching moral values from a religious perspective. Here, the Club’s activities are not “teaching moral values from a religious perspective”—they are teaching the Bible and inviting children to accept Christ as their Savior. In *Braunfeld*, the Court upheld a state law that prohibited Sunday work, even though it burdened religious observance, because the law was neutral and generally applicable. The school’s policy is similarly neutral and generally applicable: it prohibits all religious use of school facilities, regardless of the speaker’s viewpoint.

The school’s interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations is compelling. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947), the Court held that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state,” and that “the state must be neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers.” The school’s policy reflects this neutrality. In *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 211 (1948), the Court struck down a program that allowed religious instruction in public school classrooms, stating that “the use of the public school as an instrument of religious instruction is impermissible.” The school’s policy prevents the use of its facilities for religious instruction, which is precisely the kind of entanglement the Establishment Clause prohibits.

The Club’s claim that it is being denied equal protection is equally without merit. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require the government to treat all groups identically if the differences in treatment are rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest.” The school’s policy is rationally related to the interest in avoiding religious entanglement. The Club’s activities are not “moral development” in the secular sense; they are religious conversion. In *Cantwell*, the Court recognized that “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of expression,” and that “the state may not permit the use of its facilities for religious worship.” The school’s policy is a reasonable exercise of that authority.

Finally, the Club’s argument that it is being denied its right to free speech is foreclosed by *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943), which held that “the First Amendment protects the right to speak, but not the right to compel others to listen.” The school is not compelled to provide a platform for religious worship. In *Braunfeld*, the Court upheld a law that prohibited Sunday work, even though it burdened religious observance, because the law was neutral and generally applicable. The school’s policy is similarly neutral and generally applicable: it prohibits all religious use of school facilities, regardless of the speaker’s viewpoint.
