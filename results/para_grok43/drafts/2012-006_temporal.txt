**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner submits that proof of withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period commences places upon the government the burden of persuasion to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member during the period for which prosecution is sought.

In *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), this Court explained that a conspirator who has once joined “must withdraw” by some affirmative act if he is to escape liability for subsequent acts of his former associates. *Id.* at 369. The opinion makes clear that the act of withdrawal, once shown, severs the continuing agreement that otherwise keeps the statute of limitations from running. *See also Brown v. Elliott*, 225 U.S. 392 (1912) (same principle applied to multiple conspiracies).

The government’s burden to prove every element of the offense, including that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, follows directly. In *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), the Court described conspiracy as “a partnership in criminal purposes” that “may be renewed as to all whenever any of them recommences active execution of the original design.” *Id.* at 608. Because the continuing nature of the offense is an element the prosecution must establish, evidence that a defendant has withdrawn shifts to the government the task of proving, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant nevertheless participated during the actionable period.

This allocation of the burden is consistent with the fundamental rule that the prosecution must prove the existence of the crime within the time allowed by law. *See, e.g., United States v. Irvine*, 98 U.S. 687 (1878) (government must show offense committed within limitations period). Once withdrawal is in the record, the government cannot rest on the original agreement; it must affirmatively demonstrate renewed or continuing membership.

The court of appeals therefore erred in relieving the government of that burden. The judgment should be reversed.
