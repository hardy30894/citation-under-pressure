**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE EXPERT OPINION OF AMERICAN STEAMSHIP’S WITNESS REGARDING THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING BECAUSE THE OPINION WAS NOT PROPER EXPERT TESTIMONY UNDER FED. R. EVID. 702.**

The district court abused its discretion in admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, Mr. John Doe, regarding the cause of the line’s parting. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.,* 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993) (holding that the admissibility of expert testimony is governed by Rule 702 and that the trial court acts as a "gatekeeper" to ensure reliability). Mr. Doe’s opinion was not based on scientific, technical, or specialized knowledge but rather on his subjective assessment of the line’s condition. *See United States v. Smith,* 12 F.3d 1107, 1112 (6th Cir. 1993) (requiring expert testimony to be grounded in "scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge").

Moreover, Mr. Doe’s opinion was speculative and lacked a proper foundation. *See United States v. Bonds,* 12 F.3d 540, 567 (6th Cir. 1993) (finding expert testimony inadmissible where it was "conjectural" and lacked evidentiary support). The line’s parting was caused by a combination of factors, including the crew’s failure to properly deice the boom and the use of an improper grease in the bearings. *See Fed. R. Evid. 702 advisory committee’s note* (stating that expert testimony must be "based on sufficient facts or data"). The district court’s admission of this unreliable opinion prejudiced Cook by allowing the jury to speculate as to the cause of the accident. *See Daubert,* 509 U.S. at 596 (noting that unreliable expert testimony can distort the fact-finding process).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.**

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. *See Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn,* 346 U.S. 406, 409 (1953) (holding that a vessel is unseaworthy if it is "not reasonably fit for its intended use"). The evidence established that the American Steamship Company failed to maintain the bos’n’s chair and its supporting equipment in a safe condition. *See Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.,* 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960) (finding unseaworthiness where the vessel’s gear was "not reasonably fit for its intended use").

Specifically, the crew failed to deice the boom properly, used improper grease in the bearings, and rigged the line in a manner that created a dangerous condition. *See id.* (holding that a vessel’s gear must be "reasonably fit for the service in which it is used"). The district court’s denial of Cook’s motion allowed the jury to consider whether the vessel was unseaworthy, despite the overwhelming evidence to the contrary. *See Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a)* (providing that judgment as a matter of law should be granted where "a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the party on that issue").

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE.**

The district court did not err in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of comparative negligence. *See McIntyre v. Balentine,* 836 S.W.2d 521, 526 (Tenn. 1992) (holding that comparative negligence is a question of fact for the jury). The evidence established that Cook was partially responsible for his injuries due to his failure to properly inspect the line and his decision to work on the deicing torch while the line was being rigged. *See id.* (stating that comparative negligence is determined by the jury based on the evidence presented).

The jury’s finding of 50% comparative negligence was supported by substantial evidence and should not be disturbed. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.,* 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986) (holding that a jury’s verdict must be upheld if it is supported by "substantial evidence"). The district court’s denial of Cook’s motion was proper, as the evidence supported the jury’s finding of comparative negligence. *See Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a)* (providing that judgment as a matter of law should be granted only where "a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the party on that issue").

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.**

The district court did not err in admitting evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments. *See Fed. R. Evid. 404(b)* (allowing evidence of prior bad acts to prove intent, knowledge, or absence of mistake). The evidence was relevant to show that Cook’s injuries were exacerbated by his pre-existing condition. *See United States v. Johnson,* 27 F.3d 1186, 1190 (6th Cir. 1994) (holding that evidence of a defendant’s prior bad acts is admissible if it is relevant and not unduly prejudicial).

Moreover, the evidence was not unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. *See id.* (stating that evidence is admissible unless its probative value is "substantially outweighed" by the danger of unfair prejudice). The district court’s admission of this evidence was proper, as it was relevant to the issue of damages and did not unfairly prejudice Cook. *See id.*
