ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Does Not Violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population. The PLRA limits the authority of federal courts to grant prospective relief in prison conditions cases, but it does not preclude relief that is narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means necessary to correct a constitutional violation. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936). The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn, extend[s] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The district court's order meets this standard, as it is designed to alleviate unconstitutional overcrowding in California's prisons. See Talley v. Stephens, 247 F. Supp. 683 (E.D. Ark. 1965).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have the authority to order remedial relief, including population reduction, to address unconstitutional prison conditions. See, e.g., Buck v. Bell, 274 U.S. 200 (1927); Coffin v. Reichard, 143 F.2d 443 (6th Cir. 1944). The district court's order is consistent with this authority and is a necessary measure to correct the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

II. The District Court's Order Does Not Violate California's Eleventh Amendment Immunity

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar the district court's order, as it is a prospective injunction aimed at remedying ongoing constitutional violations. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts may enjoin state officials from violating federal law, even if the relief has a significant impact on the state's treasury. See, e.g., Smyth v. Ames, 169 U.S. 466 (1898); Missouri v. Fiske, 290 U.S. 18 (1933); Reagan v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co., 154 U.S. 362 (1894).

The district court's order is not a retroactive award of damages, but rather a prospective injunction designed to alleviate unconstitutional conditions. As such, it is not barred by the Eleventh Amendment. The State's reliance on Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), is misplaced, as that case involved a retroactive award of damages, not a prospective injunction. The district court's order is more akin to the relief approved in Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), and is therefore permissible under the Eleventh Amendment.

III. The District Court's Order Is a Permissible Exercise of Its Authority

The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The court has broad discretion to fashion relief that is necessary to correct constitutional violations. See, e.g., United States v. Montgomery County Bd. of Educ., 395 U.S. 225 (1969).

The district court's order is designed to alleviate unconstitutional overcrowding and is a necessary measure to correct the violations. The State has had ample opportunity to address the constitutional violations, but has failed to do so. The district court's order is a permissible exercise of its authority to remedy these violations.

In conclusion, the district court's order does not violate the PLRA or California's Eleventh Amendment immunity. It is a permissible exercise of the court's authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The order should be affirmed.
