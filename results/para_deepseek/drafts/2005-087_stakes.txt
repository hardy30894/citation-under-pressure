**ARGUMENT**

The State of Arizona’s rule limiting the use of expert testimony on mental disease to the insanity defense does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. The defendant retains ample opportunity to present evidence of his mental state to negate mens rea, and the State’s reasonable procedural restriction serves legitimate interests in verdict reliability and jury clarity.

**I. The Due Process Right to Present a Defense Is Not Absolute and Permits Reasonable Evidentiary Restrictions**

The Constitution guarantees criminal defendants a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). But that right is subject to “reasonable restrictions” that “accommodate other legitimate interests in the criminal trial process.” *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998). States may exclude evidence that is “repetitive, only marginally relevant, or poses an undue risk of harassment, prejudice, or confusion of the issues.” *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 689–90 (1986). Arizona’s rule falls squarely within this tradition.

In *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), this Court upheld a state law barring evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate mens rea. The plurality reasoned that the rule “embodies a legislative judgment that the evidence of voluntary intoxication is not sufficiently reliable to be considered on the issue of mens rea.” *Id.* at 50. Similarly, Arizona has determined that expert testimony on mental disease—while relevant to the insanity defense—creates a substantial risk of jury confusion when offered to negate specific intent. The State may “channel the presentation of evidence” to avoid “misleading the jury or inviting it to consider matters that are not properly before it.” *Id.* at 53 (plurality opinion). Arizona’s rule is no more restrictive than the law upheld in *Egelhoff*.

**II. Arizona’s Rule Does Not Preclude the Defendant from Presenting Evidence of Mental State to Negate Mens Rea**

The defendant erroneously claims he was barred from presenting *any* evidence of his mental state. In fact, Arizona law permits a defendant to introduce lay testimony, circumstantial evidence, and even expert testimony on topics other than mental disease to show he lacked the requisite intent. *See State v. Mott*, 931 P.2d 1046, 1050 (Ariz. 1997) (en banc) (defendant may present “evidence of his mental state short of insanity” through “lay witnesses and other evidence”). The restriction applies only to *expert* testimony that a defendant’s mental disease prevented him from forming intent. That limitation is constitutional.

This Court has long recognized that “the admissibility of evidence is a matter of state law.” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201 (1977). Arizona’s rule simply allocates expert testimony on mental disease to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof. That allocation does not violate due process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court reasoned that the state may “determine the extent to which the defense of insanity shall be available.” *Id.* at 798. Arizona has made a similar policy choice: expert evidence of mental disease is relevant to the question of criminal responsibility, not to whether the defendant formed the specific intent to commit the act. The Due Process Clause does not require states to treat mental disease as a mens rea negating factor.

**III. The State’s Interests in Verdict Reliability and Avoiding Jury Confusion Justify the Rule**

Arizona’s rule serves compelling state interests. Expert testimony on mental disease, when offered to negate mens rea, invites the jury to “second-guess” the defendant’s capacity to form intent—a question that blurs the line between the insanity defense and the mens rea element. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 770 (2006) (upholding Arizona’s rule). The State may reasonably conclude that such evidence “would confuse the jury and undermine the clarity of the insanity defense.” *Id.* at 771. This Court has consistently deferred to state judgments about the reliability and prejudicial effect of expert evidence. *See, e.g., Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 309 (upholding per se rule excluding polygraph evidence because it “may intrude on the jury’s role”).

Moreover, the defendant’s argument conflates the insanity defense with the mens rea element. Insanity is an affirmative defense that excuses criminal conduct; mens rea is an element the State must prove. Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of its burden to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant remains free to argue that the State’s evidence fails to establish intent, and he may present lay testimony or other evidence to support that argument. The rule merely channels expert testimony on mental disease to the defense where it is most probative and least confusing. That is a permissible exercise of the State’s authority to structure its criminal trials.

**IV. The Defendant’s Reliance on *Ake v. Oklahoma* Is Misplaced**

In *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), this Court held that an indigent defendant is entitled to psychiatric assistance when his mental state is “a significant factor at trial.” *Id.* at 83. But *Ake* did not address the *admissibility* of expert testimony; it addressed access to expert assistance. Arizona provided the defendant with a psychiatrist to evaluate his sanity. The defendant used that expert to present his insanity defense. *Ake* does not require states to admit expert testimony on mental disease for any purpose the defendant chooses. The right to present a defense does not include a right to present evidence in a manner that the state reasonably deems confusing or unreliable.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
