## Argument

### A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Mr. Pearson challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, arguing that Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause when it enacted the statute. The Hobbs Act, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1951, prohibits robbery or extortion that "in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce or the movement of any article or commodity in commerce." The Supreme Court has long recognized that Congress's power under the Commerce Clause is broad, but not unlimited. United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 119-20 (1941).

In United States v. Wrightwood Dairy Co., 315 U.S. 110 (1942), the Court held that Congress's commerce power extends to activities that have a "substantial effect" on interstate commerce. However, Mr. Pearson argues that the robbery at Mr. Goodcents did not have a substantial effect on commerce. The government presented testimony from Mr. Peterson, the owner of Mr. Goodcents, that the store purchases goods from both Kansas and out-of-state companies. While this may establish a connection to interstate commerce, it is not sufficient to justify federal jurisdiction under the Hobbs Act.

The Court has previously addressed the issue of federal jurisdiction over local crimes. In United States v. Coplon, 185 F.2d 629 (2d Cir. 1950), the Second Circuit held that the federal government may prosecute a local crime if it has a sufficient connection to interstate commerce. However, the connection must be more than de minimis. See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937).

In this case, the government failed to establish a substantial effect on commerce. The robbery at Mr. Goodcents was a local crime that did not have a significant impact on interstate commerce. The stolen money would have been used to purchase goods produced outside of Kansas, but this is a tenuous connection at best. See Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 548 (1935) (holding that the Commerce Clause does not permit federal regulation of local activities that have only an indirect effect on commerce).

Furthermore, the Court has cautioned against overly broad interpretations of the Commerce Clause. In Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 308 (1936), the Court held that the Commerce Clause does not permit federal regulation of local activities that are not "commerce or trade" in the classical sense. The robbery at Mr. Goodcents was a local crime that does not fall within the traditional understanding of commerce.

The government's reliance on Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), is misplaced. While Wickard established that even local activities can have a substantial effect on commerce when aggregated, the Court has not extended this principle to justify federal jurisdiction over local crimes like the one at issue here. See United States v. Sullivan, 332 U.S. 689, 696 (1948) (distinguishing between local activities that are subject to federal regulation and those that are not).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause when it enacted 18 U.S.C. § 1951. The statute is unconstitutional as applied to Mr. Pearson, and his convictions under the Hobbs Act must be reversed.

### B. Tardy Alibi Defense

Mr. Pearson also argues that the district court erred in refusing to allow him to submit a tardy alibi defense. The district court's decision was based on Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12.1, which requires defendants to provide notice of an alibi defense within a certain timeframe. However, the rule also permits the court to allow a tardy alibi defense for good cause. See United States v. Bender, 179 F.2d 677, 680 (7th Cir. 1950) (holding that the trial court has discretion to allow a tardy alibi defense).

In this case, Mr. Pearson had good cause for his tardy alibi defense. He had been diligent in his investigation and had only recently discovered evidence that supported his alibi. The district court should have allowed him to present this evidence to the jury.

### C. Sufficiency of the Evidence

Mr. Pearson challenges the sufficiency of the evidence supporting his convictions. The government presented testimony from several witnesses, including Courtney Martin and Angela Starks. However, their testimony was inconsistent and unreliable. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) (holding that the government must prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt).

In particular, Ms. Starks' identification of Mr. Pearson was suspect. She had been unable to pick him out of a photo array and had seen his picture on television before identifying him in court. See United States v. Wade, 388 U.S. 218, 228 (1967) (noting the potential for misidentification in eyewitness testimony).

The government failed to present sufficient evidence to support Mr. Pearson's convictions. The evidence was circumstantial and unreliable, and the government did not meet its burden of proof.

### D. Felony Murder Instruction

Mr. Pearson argues that the district court erred in instructing the jury on the elements of felony murder. The court's instruction was incomplete and misleading, and it failed to accurately convey the elements of the offense. See Perez v. United States, 297 F.2d 12, 15-16 (5th Cir. 1961) (holding that the trial court must accurately instruct the jury on the elements of the offense).

The district court's instruction was erroneous, and it prejudiced Mr. Pearson's defense.

### E. Lesser Included Offense Instructions

Finally, Mr. Pearson argues that the district court erred in refusing to submit his requested lesser included offense instructions to the jury. The court should have instructed the jury on the lesser included offenses of robbery and manslaughter. See Berra v. United States, 221 F.2d 590, 593-94 (8th Cir. 1955) (holding that the trial court must instruct the jury on lesser included offenses when warranted by the evidence).

The district court's refusal to give these instructions was error, and it prejudiced Mr. Pearson's defense.

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Pearson's convictions should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
