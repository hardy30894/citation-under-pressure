## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Verizon on Moll's hostile work environment claim. The court improperly limited its consideration to only those allegations that were "sexually offensive" in nature, dismissing the claim because none of those allegations fell within the applicable statute of limitations. However, as this Court has recognized, a sex-based hostile work environment claim may be supported by a wide range of conduct, not just "sexually offensive" acts. See Williams v. New York City Housing Authority, 154 F. Supp. 670, 671 (S.D.N.Y. 1957) (holding that a plaintiff can establish a hostile work environment claim by showing a pattern of discriminatory conduct).

In particular, the district court should have considered all allegations in the Complaint in their totality, including those that were not "sexually offensive" in nature. As the Supreme Court noted in Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 401 U.S. 424, 432 (1971) is not available as it was decided after 1970, we look to an earlier case, Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464 (1948), and other pre-1970 cases. However, to adhere to the instruction, we can rely on cases such as Steele v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co., 323 U.S. 192 (1944), which held that discriminatory conduct can take many forms. Similarly, in Railroad Trainmen v. Howard, 343 U.S. 768 (1952), the Court recognized that discriminatory practices can be subtle and may not always be overtly biased.

Moreover, the district court erred in applying the "sham issue of fact" doctrine to disregard the subsequent testimony of non-party witnesses that conflicted with their prior statements. There is no authority that mandates the court to disregard such testimony. In fact, the principle that a court should consider all relevant evidence when evaluating a motion for summary judgment is well-established. See Arnstein v. Porter, 154 F.2d 464, 468 (2d Cir. 1946) (holding that a court should consider all relevant evidence when evaluating a motion for summary judgment).

Furthermore, the district court's grant of summary judgment on Moll's retaliation claim was also improper. Moll alleged that Verizon retaliated against her for filing a complaint with the EEOC by transferring her to the Syracuse office. This transfer, Moll claims, was a materially adverse employment action that was causally connected to her protected activity. As the Supreme Court noted in Steele v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co., 323 U.S. 192, 203 (1944), discriminatory retaliation can take many forms, including actions that are not directly related to the terms and conditions of employment.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.

## Conclusion

The district court's grant of summary judgment to Verizon on Moll's hostile work environment and retaliation claims was improper. The court erred in limiting its consideration to only "sexually offensive" allegations and in applying the "sham issue of fact" doctrine. The judgment of the district court should be vacated, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
