# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL'S FILM SERIES CONSTITUTED IMPERMISSIBLE VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION IN VIOLATION OF THE FREE SPEECH CLAUSE

### A. Regardless of Forum Classification, the District May Not Discriminate on the Basis of Viewpoint

This Court has consistently held that the government's power to restrict speech in a nonpublic or limited public forum, while broader than in a traditional public forum, is not unlimited. Even where the State creates a forum for only limited purposes, "the State may be justified in reserving [it] for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics." *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). But that power is bounded by a critical constraint: any restriction on access "must not discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint." *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

The Second Circuit correctly identified the Center Moriches School District's facilities as a "limited public forum," yet failed to apply the viewpoint-neutrality requirement that governs even such limited fora. The court below reasoned that because the District had never permitted "religious" uses of any kind, the exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series was merely a permissible subject-matter limitation rather than forbidden viewpoint discrimination. This reasoning cannot be reconciled with the District's own rule, which the Second Circuit quoted at the outset of its opinion: "The school premises shall not be used by any group for religious purposes." That rule does not exclude a subject—it excludes a viewpoint on subjects the District otherwise permits.

### B. The District Permitted Discussion of Family Values and Child-Rearing from Secular Perspectives While Excluding the Same Topics from a Religious Perspective

The Second Circuit's own factual findings demonstrate that the District opened its facilities to civic and social organizations to discuss the very subject matter—family life, childrearing, and social values—that Lamb's Chapel sought to address. The court's opinion catalogs a Library-sponsored lecture series on "Psychology and The Unknown," addressing parapsychology and human consciousness, and a musical performance by the Southern Harmonizers. These programs, like the Dobson film series, concerned matters of family, psychology, and values. The District permitted secular and even quasi-spiritual discussion of these topics but drew the line specifically at religious perspectives on them.

This selective exclusion is precisely what this Court condemned in *Cornelius*. There, the Court explained that a forum restriction crosses from permissible subject-matter limitation into forbidden viewpoint discrimination when the government excludes speech "because public officials oppose the speaker's view," rather than because the topic itself is outside the forum's purpose. 473 U.S. at 812. The District did not exclude discussion of family values; it excluded only the Christian viewpoint on family values, while permitting secular and alternative-spiritual viewpoints on essentially the same subject matter.

### C. The Court of Appeals' Reliance on *Widmar* and *Mergens* to Distinguish This Case Was Erroneous

The Second Circuit attempted to distinguish *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), and *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), on the ground that those cases involved students rather than outside community groups. But the viewpoint-neutrality principle underlying *Widmar* does not depend on the forum-user's status as a student. In *Widmar*, this Court held that once a university "created a forum generally open for use by student groups," it could not deny access to a religious group "solely because the group wishes to use the forum for religious worship and religious discussion." 454 U.S. at 267, 269. The Court specifically rejected the argument that religious speech could be singled out for exclusion merely because it was religious, holding that such exclusion is "categorical discrimination against religious speech." *Id.* at 277.

That reasoning transfers directly to this case. The identity of the forum's users—students versus community members—affects whether a forum has been created at all, not whether, once created, viewpoint discrimination is permitted within it. Having found that the District's facilities constituted a "limited public forum" open to groups such as the Girl Scouts, the American Red Cross, and various community lecture and performance groups, the Second Circuit could not then permit the District to exclude Lamb's Chapel's viewpoint on subjects otherwise open for discussion.

### D. The District's Justification Rests Solely on the Religious Character of the Speech, Not on Any Legitimate, Content-Neutral Concern

The correspondence from the District's Clerk, reproduced in the Second Circuit's opinion, confirms that the sole basis for denial was the religious nature of the film series: "This film does appear to be church related and therefore your request must be refused." No pedagogical, administrative, or scheduling concern was ever offered as a basis for exclusion once the religious content was identified. This is the paradigmatic case of viewpoint discrimination that this Court's forum precedents forbid. As the Court explained in *Perry*, even content-based distinctions in a limited forum must be "reasonable and not viewpoint discrimination." 460 U.S. at 46. The District's rule and its application here fail even that minimal safeguard, because the District did not merely limit the *subject matter* of the forum—it excluded a particular *viewpoint*, religion, on subjects otherwise open to secular discussion.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed. The District's blanket exclusion of religious viewpoints, while permitting secular discussion of the same general subject matter, constitutes viewpoint discrimination forbidden by the First Amendment under *Perry*, *Cornelius*, and *Widmar*.
