**ARGUMENT**

Section 875(c) does not impose strict liability for speech that happens to be threatening in hindsight. The statute punishes one who “transmits in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another,” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), and criminal liability ordinarily requires proof of a culpable mental state. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–63 (1952). This Court has repeatedly rejected readings of federal criminal statutes that dispense with mens rea absent a clear statement from Congress. *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419, 425–33 (1985); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605–19 (1994).

The question here is whether that basic presumption applies to § 875(c). It does. Nothing in the text of § 875(c) specifies an objective standard, and the word “threat” does not itself eliminate the usual mens rea requirement. When Congress is silent, courts presume the government must prove the defendant “knowingly” engaged in the conduct that the statute criminalizes. *Bryan v. United States*, 524 U.S. 184, 191–93 (1998); *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 605–06. Because § 875(c) is a criminal statute targeting speech, that default principle should apply with special force. See *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 69–78 (1994).

A subjective-intent requirement also follows from the First Amendment’s “true threats” doctrine. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam), the Court treated context as critical in distinguishing political hyperbole from punishable threats. And in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359–60 (2003), the Court explained that “true threats” are serious expressions of intent to commit unlawful violence, and held that the State may punish such threats because they protect individuals “from the fear of violence” and “from the disruption that fear engenders.” The Court further stated that a speaker who “directs a threat to a person or group of persons with the intent of placing the victim in fear of bodily harm or death” may be punished. *Id.* at 360. That language is incompatible with a purely objective standard that punishes speech without proof that the defendant meant his words as threats or knew they would be understood that way.

The government and the court below relied on the idea that an objective test best protects listeners from fear. But the First Amendment does not permit criminal punishment for ambiguous, artistic, political, or emotional expression simply because a reasonable listener might perceive it as threatening. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708; *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359–60. The line between protected speech and a true threat must be drawn with a culpable mental-state requirement, or else protected expression will be chilled. See *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 382–86 (1992); *Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 535 U.S. 234, 244–46 (2002).

Here, the district court told the jury it could convict if a reasonable person would view Elonis’s posts as threatening. That instruction allowed conviction without proof that Elonis intended his posts as threats, knew they would be taken as threats, or recklessly disregarded that risk. A conviction under § 875(c) cannot rest on so diluted a standard. The Constitution “demands more than a mere possibility that a communication will be understood as threatening.” *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359–60. It requires proof of the defendant’s subjective culpability.

The judgment below should be reversed.
