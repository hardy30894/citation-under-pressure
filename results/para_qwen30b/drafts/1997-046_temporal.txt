**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATE’S CLEMENCY PROCEDURE CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST UNDER THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from the arbitrary deprivation of life or liberty without due process of law. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). This protection extends not only to the substantive rights inherent in life and liberty but also to those interests created by state law when such interests are “so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 97 (1923). The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process, though not a judicial proceeding, constitutes a critical stage in the final adjudication of a death sentence and thus implicates a constitutionally protected liberty interest.

In *Palko*, the Court recognized that the Due Process Clause “is not a mere formality, but a substance which protects the individual against arbitrary action by the State.” 302 U.S. at 325. The Court emphasized that “the protection of the individual against arbitrary action by the State is the essence of due process.” Id. The Ohio clemency process, by its very nature, is not a mere administrative formality. It is the final opportunity for a death row inmate to seek mercy from the executive branch after the conclusion of all judicial proceedings. The process is not discretionary in form only—it is mandatory under Ohio law. *Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07* requires the Parole Authority to initiate a clemency review 45 days before a scheduled execution, unless stayed. This mandatory timing, coupled with the State’s own recognition that clemency is a “critical component” of the death penalty system, demonstrates that the State has created a procedure that is not merely permissive but functionally essential.

The Supreme Court in *Moore v. Dempsey* held that a state’s failure to provide a fair trial, even in the absence of a constitutional right to a particular procedural form, could violate due process when the process is so essential to justice that its absence renders the outcome fundamentally unfair. 261 U.S. at 97. The Court observed that “the right to a fair trial is not a mere technicality, but a fundamental right.” Id. Similarly, the clemency process in Ohio is not a mere formality; it is the last chance for a condemned man to present his case to a body with the power to alter the finality of his sentence. To deny that this process creates a protected liberty interest is to ignore the reality that the State has made clemency a necessary part of its capital punishment system.

The Court in *Palko* further held that “the Fourteenth Amendment protects the individual against the arbitrary action of the State.” 302 U.S. at 325. The Ohio clemency process, by its structure and timing, is not arbitrary—it is a fixed, mandatory procedure. But the very fact that it is mandatory and final in effect creates a liberty interest that cannot be extinguished without due process. The State cannot, through the mere assertion of unfettered discretion, strip an individual of a meaningful opportunity to seek clemency without affording him the protections of due process. To hold otherwise would be to allow the State to create a process that is essential to the finality of a death sentence, yet deny the individual any constitutional protection in that process.

The Court in *Palko* rejected the notion that a state could “make a man’s life or liberty depend upon the arbitrary will of the State.” 302 U.S. at 325. The clemency process in Ohio, by its mandatory nature and its finality, places the inmate’s life in the hands of a state body whose decisions are not subject to judicial review. This is precisely the kind of arbitrary power the Fourteenth Amendment was designed to prevent. The State cannot create a process that is essential to the finality of a death sentence and then deny the individual any constitutional protection in that process. The liberty interest at stake—the right to be heard before the State takes life—is too fundamental to be left to the whim of an unreviewable executive body.

II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS THE STATE FROM FORCING A DEFENDANT TO WAIVE HIS RIGHT TO REMAIN SILENT

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” This right is not limited to trial proceedings; it applies to any situation in which a person is compelled to provide testimony that may be used against him in a criminal case. *Cummings v. Missouri*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 277, 322 (1845). The right to remain silent is a fundamental protection against self-incrimination and is “so essential to the administration of justice that it cannot be surrendered without the most deliberate and informed consent.” *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 537 (1884).

The Ohio clemency process, by its design, creates a situation in which an inmate is effectively compelled to choose between silence and the loss of a meaningful opportunity to seek clemency. The State offers a personal interview as part of the clemency process, but it does so in a way that makes participation appear mandatory. The inmate is informed that the interview will be considered at the clemency hearing, and that any statement made may be used in the decision-making process. This creates a coercive environment in which the inmate is pressured to speak, even if he fears that his words may later be used against him.

The Supreme Court in *Hurtado* emphasized that “the right to be free from compulsory self-incrimination is one of the most sacred rights of the citizen.” 110 U.S. at 537. The Court in *Cummings* held that “the right to remain silent is a fundamental right, and cannot be taken away by the State.” 44 U.S. at 322. The Ohio clemency process, by making participation in the interview a de facto condition of receiving clemency, violates this principle. The State cannot condition the receipt of a benefit—clemency—on the waiver of a constitutional right.
