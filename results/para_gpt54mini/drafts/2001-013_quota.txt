**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act authorizes OSHA to issue citations here because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over the specific working conditions at issue aboard Mallard Bay’s uninspected drilling barge. Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA coverage only when another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” as to the relevant working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The statute is one of preemption by actual, exercised regulation; a bare grant of authority is not enough. See *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235 (2002); *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976); *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 523 F.2d 152 (5th Cir. 1975).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that OSHA’s exemption is limited and that preemption does not arise from general federal oversight. In *Chao*, the Court held that “actual exercise of regulatory authority” is required to displace OSHA under § 4(b)(1). 534 U.S. at 247. That reading accords with *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119 (2d Cir. 1978), which held that OSHA remained applicable where the FAA had not regulated the precise hazard, and with *Southern Pacific*, 539 F.2d 386, which likewise rejected broad industry-wide displacement absent specific standards. See also *American Petroleum Institute v. Occupational Safety and Health Administration*, 581 F.2d 493 (5th Cir. 1978) (section 4(b)(1) requires actual federal regulation of the conditions cited). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot survive *Chao*.

The Fifth Circuit relied on its prior maritime cases, but those cases do not control this record. In *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), the court described Coast Guard regulation of “seamen” aboard “vessels in navigation.” 609 F.2d at 1121–22; 720 F.2d at 826–29. But neither case held that every uninspected vessel is comprehensively regulated as to all occupational hazards. *Chao* rejected that inference, explaining that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited and that such limited rules do not create a complete preemption of OSHA. 534 U.S. at 247–48. As *Donovan* itself recognized, § 4(b)(1) turns on whether another agency has actually regulated the condition at issue, not on whether that agency generally has maritime authority. 720 F.2d at 827, 829.

The Coast Guard’s uninspected-vessel regulations confirm the absence of comprehensive coverage. Congress authorized only narrow rules for uninspected vessels, including fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. pts. 25, 26. That patchwork of standards is the opposite of the “comprehensive regulation” the Fifth Circuit invoked. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 248–49. Where the relevant hazard is not regulated, OSHA remains available. See *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119; *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386.

The lower court’s “seamen” rationale also cannot expand the statute. OSHA’s jurisdiction depends on workplace standards, not labels. *Brock v. Cathedral Bluffs Shale Oil Co.*, 796 F.2d 533 (D.C. Cir. 1986), and *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission*, 725 F.2d 1237 (9th Cir. 1984), teach that § 4(b)(1) is triggered only by specific, exercised authority over the cited conditions. Likewise, *Donovan v. Red Star Marine Services, Inc.*, 739 F.2d 774 (2d Cir. 1984), held that OSHA coverage is not defeated by maritime context alone; the dispositive question is whether another agency has in fact regulated the working conditions.

Nor does the Fifth Circuit’s concern about uniformity justify reading § 4(b)(1) beyond its text. This Court has repeatedly rejected policy-based preemption where Congress chose a narrower rule. See *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 248; *American Petroleum Institute*, 581 F.2d 493. Congress could have exempted all vessels or all seamen, but it did not. Instead, it used the condition-specific language of § 4(b)(1). *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 247–48. Because the Coast Guard had not prescribed or enforced standards covering the cited hazards aboard Rig 52, OSHA retained jurisdiction.

The Court should therefore reverse and uphold OSHA’s citations.
