**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Navigability-for-Title Test by Focusing on the Rivers’ Condition at Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine vests a State with title to the beds and banks of rivers that are navigable for title purposes at the time of its admission to the Union. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); *Bonneville v. Columbia River Basalt Group*, 290 P.3d 76, 83 (Mont. 2012). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the critical inquiry is whether the river was capable of supporting interstate or foreign commerce at statehood. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied this test by examining historical evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers’ navigability in 1889. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 239 P.3d 66, 80 (Mont. 2010).

Contrary to PPL’s contention, the trial court did not err by considering post-statehood evidence to illuminate the rivers’ condition at statehood. Courts routinely rely on historical records—including journals, surveys, and legislative reports—to reconstruct navigability at the relevant time. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 86–87; *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14–15. The Montana Supreme Court properly deferred to the trial court’s factual findings, which were supported by substantial evidence, including expert testimony and historical documents. *PPL Montana*, 239 P.3d at 80–81. This Court accords deference to state courts’ interpretations of state property law, including navigability determinations. *Bonelli Cattle Co. v. Arizona*, 414 U.S. 313, 329 (1973).

**II. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt Montana’s Claims for Compensation.**

PPL erroneously asserts that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s ability to seek compensation for use of state-owned riverbeds. The FPA expressly preserves state authority over “proprietary rights” in navigable waters. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946); *Public Utility Dist. No. 1 of Pend Oreille Cty. v. Seattle*, 382 F.2d 666, 671–72 (9th Cir. 1967). The statute requires licensees to comply with state law regarding “beds and banks” and water appropriation. 16 U.S.C. § 802. This Court has recognized that the FPA establishes a “dual system” of federal and state regulation, not a scheme of exclusive federal control. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 167–68.

Moreover, the federal navigational servitude does not bar state compensation claims. The servitude extinguishes state title only to the extent necessary to effectuate federal commerce power; it does not immunize private parties from liability for use of state-owned lands. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the servitude does not preempt state law claims for compensation. *PPL Montana*, 239 P.3d at 75–76.

**III. The Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA) Validly Authorizes Montana’s Claims.**

The HRA, enacted in 1931, prohibits the sale of state power sites but permits leasing with compensation. Mont. Code Ann. §§ 77-4-201, -202. PPL’s argument that the HRA is preempted by the FPA fails because the FPA explicitly contemplates state regulation of proprietary rights. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175–76. The HRA’s lease provisions are consistent with the FPA’s framework, which requires licensees to respect state property rights. *Id.* at 161 n.6; 16 U.S.C. § 802.

PPL further contends that the HRA impermissibly retroactively imposes liability. However, the HRA does not impose new obligations; it merely enforces Montana’s pre-existing title to riverbeds under the equal footing doctrine. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75. The State’s claims arise from PPL’s unauthorized use of state lands, not from any legislative act altering PPL’s obligations. *See generally PPL Montana*, 239 P.3d at 76–78.

**IV. PPL’s Affirmative Defenses Fail as a Matter of Law.**

PPL’s prescriptive easement claim is foreclosed by Montana law, which requires proof of continuous, uninterrupted, and adverse use for the statutory period. Mont. Code Ann. § 70-19-401. The State’s ownership of the riverbeds at issue defeats any claim of adverse use. *See PPL Montana*, 239 P.3d at 85–86.

Equitable estoppel likewise fails because the State did not mislead PPL to its detriment. *See generally In re Estate of Streufert*, 2006 MT 278, ¶ 25, 334 Mont. 225. PPL’s laches and statute of limitations defenses are unavailing because the State’s claims accrued only upon PPL’s unauthorized use of state lands, which continued through the period at issue. *See Mont. Code Ann. § 27-1-201*.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the navigability-for-title test, and its decision is consistent with this Court’s precedents on the equal footing doctrine, federal preemption, and state proprietary rights. The judgment below should be affirmed.
