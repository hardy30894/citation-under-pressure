**ARGUMENT**

The decision below rests on a fundamental error: it allowed a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to serve as "cause" to excuse the procedural default of a separate habeas claim. That holding cannot be squared with this Court's long-settled habeas jurisprudence, which requires that a petitioner exhaust state remedies and comply with state procedural rules before seeking federal relief. Because the ineffective-assistance claim was itself defaulted, it is incapable of excusing any other default.

**I. A Procedurally Defaulted Claim Cannot, As a Matter of Logic and Law, Supply Cause for Another Default.**

Federal habeas review is unavailable when a state prisoner has failed to comply with state procedural rules, unless the prisoner demonstrates both cause for the default and actual prejudice. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116–17 (1944). A claim that is procedurally defaulted under state law is, by definition, not properly exhausted. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 210 (1950) (failure to comply with state procedural rules bars federal review). This Court has consistently held that federal courts will not review a state judgment that rests upon an independent and adequate state procedural ground. *Murdock v. City of Memphis*, 87 U.S. 590, 626 (1874); *Herndon v. Georgia*, 295 U.S. 441, 443 (1935). A petitioner cannot circumvent that bar by invoking a claim that is itself defaulted.

Here, Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed as untimely under Ohio's procedural rules. That default is not excused, and it cannot be used to excuse another. Under *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963), a federal court may consider a defaulted claim only if the petitioner did not "deliberately by-pass" state procedures. But *Fay* itself requires that the petitioner show the absence of a knowing waiver. Carpenter has made no such showing. His failure to file a timely application to reopen his direct appeal was not occasioned by any external impediment. Cause, if it exists at all, must be an "objective factor external to the defense" that prevented compliance with state procedures. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963). A claim that is itself defaulted is not external to the defense; it is the very defect the petitioner seeks to excuse. To allow a defaulted claim to serve as cause for another default would permit a petitioner to pull himself up by his own bootstraps—a result this Court has never countenanced.

**II. The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Cannot Serve as Cause Because It Was Never Properly Presented to the State Courts.**

This Court has long required that a habeas petitioner give the state courts a fair opportunity to correct constitutional violations before seeking federal intervention. *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886). In *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486–87 (1953), this Court emphasized that federal courts must respect state procedural rules. A claim that is procedurally defaulted has not been "fairly presented" to the state courts. *Darr*, 339 U.S. at 210. Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed as untimely; the Ohio courts never addressed its merits on any procedural posture that would satisfy exhaustion. A claim that has never been properly presented to the state courts cannot, by definition, constitute cause for the default of a separate claim.

Moreover, the right to effective assistance of counsel, while fundamental, must be asserted in accordance with state procedural rules. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (right to counsel entitles defendant to a fair opportunity to raise the claim). *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be knowing and intelligent). Here, Carpenter waived any timely challenge to appellate counsel's performance by failing to comply with Ohio's procedural rule. A waived claim cannot be revived to excuse another default. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 438. The Sixth Circuit's contrary holding effectively nullifies the state procedural rule and invites petitioners to bypass state procedures with impunity.

**III. Permitting a Defaulted Claim to Serve as Cause Undermines Finality and Comity.**

Federal habeas review is not a substitute for a direct appeal or for state post-conviction proceedings. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 487. This Court has consistently recognized the importance of finality in state criminal judgments. *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 312. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would encourage prisoners to bypass state procedures and then use the defaulted claim as a "back door" to federal review. That result would eviscerate the independent and adequate state ground doctrine. *Murdock*, 87 U.S. at 626; *Herndon*, 295 U.S. at 443.

The Sixth Circuit's holding also conflicts with the principle that a petitioner must show cause and prejudice for each defaulted claim. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 438. Here, Carpenter failed to show cause for the default of his ineffective-assistance claim. That claim cannot simultaneously be the default and the cause for another default. Such circular reasoning finds no support in any pre-1970 precedent of this Court. To hold otherwise would permit a petitioner to manufacture cause from his own procedural default—a result that defies both logic and the settled habeas framework.

**IV. Conclusion.**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. The decision below should be reversed, and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with this Court's opinion.
