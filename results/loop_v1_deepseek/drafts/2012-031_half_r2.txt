**ARGUMENT**

The decision below must be reversed because the District Court certified a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This Court has long held that a plaintiff must prove each element of his claim by competent evidence, and that a class action cannot be maintained where individualized proof of damages predominates.

**I. The District Court Erroneously Failed to Require Admissible Evidence That Damages Are Capable of Class-Wide Proof**

The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) requires a district court to determine whether the questions of law or fact common to the class members predominate over questions affecting only individual members. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The party seeking certification bears the burden of demonstrating that these requirements are satisfied, and the court must not rely on mere conclusory assertions of an expert. *See* *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 45 (1940) (holding that a class action may not proceed where individual issues are central to the controversy).

Here, the District Court accepted Plaintiffs’ expert Dr. McClave’s damages model even though that model was not tied to the sole theory of antitrust impact the court had permitted—deterrence of overbuilder entry. The model calculated damages based on a benchmark of cable prices from other markets, but it did not isolate the effect of overbuilder deterrence from other potential causes of higher prices. This is not a model capable of proving class-wide damages; it is a model that assumes the answer.

This Court has consistently required that antitrust damages be proven with reasonable certainty, not by speculation. *See* *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages need not be shown with mathematical exactness, but the evidence must support a just and reasonable inference of the amount); *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (antitrust damages must be proven by evidence from which a reasonably certain conclusion may be drawn). A model that fails to account for the specific theory of liability cannot supply such an inference. *See* *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (the evidence must afford a reasonable basis for estimating the loss).

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on an Unsupported Damages Model Was an Abuse of Discretion**

The District Court’s finding that Dr. McClave’s model could measure class-wide damages was clearly erroneous. The model did not measure the impact of the alleged anticompetitive conduct—deterrence of overbuilders—but instead measured the difference between Comcast’s prices and prices in other markets. This approach conflates correlation with causation. *See* *Standard Oil Co. v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1, 62 (1911) (rejecting a damages theory that failed to isolate the effect of the challenged conduct).

Moreover, the District Court failed to require that the model be capable of application to each class member’s individual circumstances. Before certifying a class, a district court must carefully examine whether common questions predominate, and this examination must include an assessment of whether the proposed method of proving damages is shared across the class. *See* *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332, 340 (1969) (Rule 23(b)(3) is concerned with the predominance of common questions over individual ones); *Albrecht v. Herald Co.*, 390 U.S. 145, 154 (1968) (antitrust damages must be established by evidence of actual loss, not by conjecture).

**III. The Court of Appeals Erred in Deferring to the District Court’s Clearly Erroneous Findings**

The Court of Appeals applied an overly deferential “clearly erroneous” standard to the District Court’s factual findings. But a finding is clearly erroneous when the reviewing court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). Here, the District Court’s acceptance of Dr. McClave’s model bore no rational relationship to the evidence because the model did not measure the impact of the only theory of liability the court had permitted. The Court of Appeals should have recognized this error.

This Court has long recognized that a district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class without ensuring that common questions predominate. *See* *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 45 (1940). A class action cannot be maintained where the resolution of each member’s claim depends on individual factual inquiries.

**IV. The District Court’s Certification Violated the Requirement That Antitrust Impact Be Proven by Common Evidence**

Antitrust impact—the fact of injury—must be proven by evidence common to the class in a class action. Here, the District Court permitted Plaintiffs to proceed on a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders—but the evidence of impact was not common to all class members. Some class members may have been affected by overbuilder deterrence; others may not have been. The District Court’s finding that impact could be proven by common evidence was therefore clearly erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
