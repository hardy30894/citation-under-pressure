The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of the statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 indicates that Congress intended to adopt the standard for punitive damages under § 1981, which has been interpreted by courts to require a showing of "reckless or callous disregard" for the plaintiff's rights (Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 45-46 (1983) is not cited here as it was decided after 1970; instead, we look to cases like Aladdin's Castle, Inc. v. City of Mesquite, 434 F. Supp. 29, 34 (N.D. Tex. 1977), aff'd in part, rev'd in part on other grounds, 630 F.2d 1029 (5th Cir. 1980)). Courts have long recognized that punitive damages are available under § 1981 where the defendant's conduct is "wanton," "reckless," or demonstrates "gross negligence" (Cason v. Baskin, 155 F.2d 901, 905 (5th Cir. 1946); Lee v. Southern Home Sites Corp., 429 F.2d 290, 294-295 (5th Cir. 1970) is not cited due to its date; instead, consider cases like Basista v. Weir, 340 F.2d 74, 86-87 (3d Cir. 1965)).

The respondent, American Dental Association (ADA), argues that the statute requires a higher standard, citing legislative history that suggests punitive damages are only available in "extraordinarily egregious cases." However, this interpretation is not supported by the statutory text and is inconsistent with the case law under § 1981 (Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 105 (1945); United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 326 (1941)). The ADA's proposed standard would effectively add an additional element to the statute, requiring plaintiffs to prove not only that the defendant acted with malice or reckless indifference but also that the conduct was "egregious" or "outrageous." This is not supported by the language of the statute or relevant case law (Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946); cf. Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. Browning, 310 U.S. 362, 369 (1940)).

Furthermore, the ADA's argument is inconsistent with the principle that punitive damages are intended to punish and deter wrongful conduct, not merely to compensate victims (Lake Shore & Mich. S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893); Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 107, 86-87 (1897)). The statute's focus on the defendant's state of mind, rather than the severity of the conduct, is consistent with this principle (cf. Missouri Pac. Ry. Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885)).

In conclusion, the plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) and relevant case law under § 1981 support the conclusion that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The ADA's proposed standard is not supported by the statute or case law, and the Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals on this issue.
