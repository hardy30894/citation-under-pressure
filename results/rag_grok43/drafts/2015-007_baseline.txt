**ARGUMENT**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies the Sixth Amendment. In *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any fact “on which the legislature conditions an increase in [the] maximum punishment.” *Id.* at 589. Florida’s statute conditions a death sentence on the trial judge’s independent determination that sufficient aggravating circumstances exist and that they outweigh mitigating circumstances. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3). The jury’s advisory recommendation is not a factual finding that increases the maximum penalty; the judge alone makes the findings required for death. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 648 (1990) (distinguishing Florida’s scheme). *Ring* overruled *Walton* only “in relevant part,” 536 U.S. at 609, leaving intact the principle that a judge may make the ultimate sentencing determination once the jury has convicted the defendant of first-degree murder.

The absence of a unanimous jury recommendation does not violate the Sixth Amendment. *Ring* requires only that the jury find the existence of an aggravating circumstance; it does not mandate unanimity on that finding or on the ultimate sentence. *See id.* at 597 n.4. Florida law permits a non-unanimous advisory verdict, and this Court has never held that the Sixth Amendment imposes a unanimity requirement in capital sentencing.

The Eighth Amendment likewise permits Florida’s procedures for determining mental retardation. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left “to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction” on executing the mentally retarded. *Id.* at 317. Florida has done so by requiring the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence in a post-trial hearing before the judge. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4). *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6 (2005) (per curiam), confirmed that states may assign this determination to the court rather than the jury. Nothing in *Atkins* or the Eighth Amendment requires the jury to decide the issue as an absolute bar.

Florida’s outlier status among death-penalty jurisdictions does not render its scheme unconstitutional. The Eighth Amendment inquiry turns on whether a practice offends “evolving standards of decency,” not on head-counting alone. *See Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 331 (1989). Because Florida’s procedures provide for jury consideration of mitigation, including evidence of mental retardation, and for judicial findings supported by the record, they satisfy both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
