Respondent’s position is straightforward: a court conducting plain-error review may consider the entire appellate record, including materials not submitted to the jury, when deciding whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights and whether correction is necessary to protect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Rule 52(b) authorizes correction of a forfeited error only when the appellant shows that the error is “plain,” affected substantial rights, and seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732-36 (1993). The substantial-rights inquiry is a prejudice inquiry, and it asks whether the error affected the outcome of the proceeding. Id. at 734. The Court has repeatedly explained that plain-error review is a limited, discretionary power designed to correct only particularly serious errors. Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 466-67 (1997); Puckett v. United States, 556 U.S. 129, 135 (2009).

Nothing in Rule 52(b) or this Court’s cases confines that prejudice inquiry to the four corners of the trial evidence admitted on the particular point. To the contrary, the Court has treated plain-error review as a practical inquiry into the whole record. In Johnson, the Court found no basis to reverse where the evidence of the omitted element was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted.” 520 U.S. at 470. In United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 632-33 (2002), the Court held that an Apprendi error did not warrant relief because the evidence supporting the omitted fact was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted,” and the Court emphasized that the error did not “seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 632-34. Those decisions necessarily looked beyond the jury’s explicit findings to the record as a whole. Id.; Johnson, 520 U.S. at 469-70.

The same approach governs here. Greer’s Rehaif claim was unpreserved, so he bore the burden of establishing plain-error prejudice. Greer v. United States, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2096-97 (2021). This Court held that “a defendant faces an uphill climb” in showing a reasonable probability that, but for the Rehaif error, the outcome would have been different. Id. at 2097. That is because “in a felon-in-possession case where the defendant was in fact a felon, the defendant faces an uphill climb,” since “convicted felons typically know they’re convicted felons.” Id. at 2097-98. The Court made clear that appellate judges are not required to ignore the defendant’s prior convictions when determining prejudice. Id. at 2098-99.

Indeed, the Court expressly approved consideration of the entire record, including prior convictions and other evidence not presented to the jury on the omitted knowledge element. Id. at 2098-100. The Court explained that, on plain-error review, an appellate court may consult the record to determine whether the defendant can make the requisite showing of prejudice and that, on the facts before it, Greer had not done so because he never asserted below or on appeal that he would have presented evidence he did not know he was a felon. Id. at 2097-98, 2100. That is fatal to Greer’s position here. A court cannot evaluate whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights without considering evidence elsewhere in the record bearing on what the defendant knew and what he likely would have said if the error had not occurred. Olano, 507 U.S. at 734; Greer, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-100.

Nor is there any basis to limit the fairness/integrity inquiry to trial evidence alone. Rule 52(b) asks whether the error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736, and Cotton held that overwhelming record evidence can defeat that showing even where an error exists. 535 U.S. at 632-34. If an appellate court were barred from consulting the full record, it could not properly assess whether leaving the conviction in place would produce a manifest miscarriage of justice. See United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 81-82 (2004) (prejudice requires a reasonable probability of a different result); Henderson v. United States, 568 U.S. 266, 273 (2013) (plain-error review operates against the backdrop of the whole record).

Greer’s contrary rule would convert plain-error review into a mechanical sufficiency test divorced from reality. That is not the law. Johnson, Cotton, and Greer all confirm that when an appellant seeks relief for an unpreserved Rehaif error, the appellate court may look to the entire record, including prior convictions and other materials outside the evidence formally presented to the jury, to decide whether the appellant has carried his burden under Rule 52(b). Greer, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-100; Cotton, 535 U.S. at 632-34; Johnson, 520 U.S. at 469-70.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed.
