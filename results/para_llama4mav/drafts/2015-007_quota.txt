The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously sentence a defendant to death.

First, regarding the mental retardation issue, the Supreme Court in Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left it to the states to determine the manner in which the constitutional restriction on execution of mentally retarded defendants will be enforced. Florida law sets forth a three-pronged test to determine mental retardation as a bar to the death penalty, which is a valid exercise of state authority. See Nixon v. State, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla.2009); § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial court's determination that Hurst is not mentally retarded is a factual finding that is supported by the evidence presented at the evidentiary hearing.

The Sixth Amendment claim is also without merit. In Ring, the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires that the presence of aggravating factors be determined by the jury. However, Florida's capital sentencing scheme is distinct from Arizona's, and the Supreme Court has not held that Ring applies retroactively or that it requires a unanimous jury verdict. See Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348 (2004) (holding that Ring does not apply retroactively). Moreover, the Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that Ring does not require a unanimous jury recommendation or a jury determination of mental retardation. See, e.g., Coday v. State, 946 So.2d 988 (Fla.2006); Steele v. State, 921 So.2d 538 (Fla.2005).

Furthermore, the Eighth Amendment claim is also unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of guided procedures in capital sentencing, but it has not required jury unanimity. See, e.g., Harris v. Alabama, 513 U.S. 504 (1995) (upholding Alabama's capital sentencing scheme, which allowed a judge to override a jury's recommendation). The fact that Florida is an "outlier" among states that impose the death penalty does not render its capital sentencing scheme unconstitutional. See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976) (upholding Georgia's capital sentencing scheme, despite differences with other states).

In addition, the Supreme Court has held that the Eighth Amendment requires "heightened reliability" in capital sentencing, but it has not mandated specific procedures. See Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). Florida's capital sentencing scheme, which includes a jury recommendation and a trial court's independent weighing of aggravating and mitigating factors, satisfies this requirement. See, e.g., Proffitt v. Florida, 428 U.S. 242 (1976) (upholding Florida's capital sentencing scheme).

The respondent's sentence of death is also consistent with the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on proportionality. See, e.g., Pulley v. Harris, 465 U.S. 37 (1984) (holding that proportionality review is not a constitutional requirement). The Florida Supreme Court has conducted a proportionality review and determined that Hurst's sentence is proportionate to other death sentences in the state. See, e.g., Lawrence v. State, 969 So.2d 294 (Fla.2007).

In conclusion, the Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The respondent's sentence of death should be affirmed.
