The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. A thorough examination of the statute and relevant case law reveals that indefinite detention is not authorized.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government's power to detain aliens is not unlimited. In Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), the Court held that aliens could not be subjected to punishment without a judicial trial. Similarly, in United States Ex Rel. Mensevich v. Tod, 264 U.S. 134 (1924), the Court emphasized that the government's authority to deport aliens is subject to certain limitations.

The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), provides that certain aliens "may be detained beyond the removal period." However, this provision must be read in light of the Constitution's demands, as established in Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952). In Carlson, the Court held that the Attorney General's discretion to detain aliens without bail is not unlimited and must be exercised in a manner consistent with due process.

Furthermore, the Court has consistently held that statutes authorizing detention must be construed narrowly to avoid serious constitutional concerns. In United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), the Court interpreted a statute authorizing the Attorney General to require certain aliens to provide information, holding that the statute should be construed to avoid raising serious constitutional questions. Similarly, in Lehmann v. United States Ex Rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685 (1957), the Court emphasized the importance of construing deportation statutes narrowly to avoid constitutional issues.

The legislative history and language of § 1231(a)(6) suggest that it was not intended to authorize indefinite detention. The statute's purpose is to ensure the alien's presence at the moment of removal, as noted in Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6 (1948). Indefinite detention would be inconsistent with this purpose, as it would not be reasonably necessary to secure the alien's removal.

Other cases, such as Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), and Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193 (1958), have established that the government's authority to detain or deport aliens is subject to certain limitations and must be exercised in a manner consistent with due process. Phillippides v. Day, 283 U.S. 48 (1931), and United States Ex Rel. Stapf v. Corsi, 287 U.S. 129 (1932), have also emphasized the importance of construing immigration statutes narrowly and in a manner consistent with constitutional requirements.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute must be construed to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, beyond which detention is no longer authorized. This interpretation is consistent with the Constitution's demands and the relevant case law.
