## ARGUMENT

### I. The government’s installation and commercial use of fiber-optic capacity beyond its own operational needs exceeded the easement and effected a per se physical taking.

A physical taking occurs when the government authorizes a permanent physical occupation of private property. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426-35 (1982). The rule is categorical because “a permanent physical occupation authorized by government is a taking without regard to the public interests that it may serve.” *Id.* at 426. The Federal Circuit has likewise recognized that when the government’s conduct amounts to a permanent physical invasion, compensation is required without the balancing applicable to regulatory takings. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 261-67 (1946); *Arkansas Game & Fish Comm’n v. United States*, 568 U.S. 23, 31-38 (2012).

The critical question here is not whether the BPA once had a right to install signal lines, but whether its present conduct remains within the scope of the easement it purchased. Easements are property interests defined by their terms, and a holder cannot enlarge them by self-help. *Preseault v. United States*, 100 F.3d 1525, 1537-39 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc). When government action exceeds the granted easement and imposes a new burden on the servient estate, the excess burden is a taking. *Id.* at 1540-42; *Cienega Gardens v. United States*, 331 F.3d 1319, 1330-34 (Fed. Cir. 2003).

The 1953 easement authorized “signal lines” “necessary in connection” with power transmission. It did not authorize the government to construct a commercial telecommunications network far larger than its own needs and then lease excess capacity to third parties for profit. That use is not merely an internal technological upgrade; it is an additional, ongoing occupation for the benefit of outside users. A permanent occupation by the government and its invitees is a taking. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 433-35; *Nollan v. California Coastal Comm’n*, 483 U.S. 825, 831-32 (1987) (government may not exact a property burden without a sufficient nexus); *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid*, 594 U.S. 139, 147-52 (2021) (government-authorized access by third parties is a per se physical taking).

The Court of Federal Claims erred in treating the dispute as one about mere “misuse” of an easement sounding only in trespass. Misuse and taking are not mutually exclusive where the government has physically occupied private property beyond its lawful authority. In *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748-49 (1947), the Court held that recurring government intrusion can become a compensable taking when it permanently burdens property. Similarly, in *Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co.*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 166, 177-80 (1871), the Court recognized that substantial physical invasion is compensable even where title remains with the owner. Here, the government has not merely trespassed sporadically; it has installed a permanent fiber-optic system and reserved surplus strands for commercial leasing. That is a continuing physical occupation, not a one-off trespass.

### II. The government’s reliance on projected future need does not eliminate the taking, because the added commercial capacity is not “necessary” to the easement’s purpose.

The government argues that the cable size was justified by anticipated future demand. But takings law asks whether the government has imposed a present physical burden outside the property interest it acquired. *Preseault*, 100 F.3d at 1538-42. A claimed future need cannot transform present excess capacity into property already paid for. The government’s own admissions show that it currently uses only a fraction of the fibers across Tuthill’s land and leases the remainder to a telecommunications company. That arrangement is the hallmark of an additional servitude. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261-67; *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 434-35.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected formalism that permits the government to avoid compensation by characterizing a physical appropriation as incidental to another purpose. In *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 180-81 (1979), the Court held that requiring public access to a privately improved marina effected a taking because it imposed a right of physical invasion. So too here: leasing excess fiber capacity creates a right of third-party use over Tuthill’s land, beyond the rights purchased in 1953. *Cedar Point Nursery*, 594 U.S. at 147-52.

### III. Summary judgment for the government was improper because undisputed facts establish a compensable taking as a matter of law.

On cross-motions for summary judgment, the court was required to determine whether the undisputed facts showed a taking. *Andrus v. Allard*, 444 U.S. 51, 65-66 (1979). They do. The government physically occupies Tuthill’s land with infrastructure larger than necessary for its own transmission needs and uses the excess for revenue-generating third-party service. That added use is a new burden on the servient estate, distinct from the 1953 grant. *Preseault*, 100 F.3d at 1540-42; *Cedar Point Nursery*, 594 U.S. at 147-52.

Because the government’s conduct effects a permanent physical occupation beyond the easement’s scope, Tuthill is entitled to just compensation under the Fifth Amendment. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 426; *Pumpelly*, 80 U.S. at 177-80; *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261-67. The judgment should be reversed.
