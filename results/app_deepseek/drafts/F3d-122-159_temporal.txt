**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BOP’S CLASSIFICATION OF A DRUG CONSPIRACY AS A “CRIME OF VIOLENCE” MERELY BECAUSE OF A WEAPONS SENTENCE ENHANCEMENT CONTRADICTS THE PLAIN STATUTORY TERM “NONVIOLENT OFFENSE.”**

Section 3621(e)(2)(B) unambiguously grants a discretionary sentence reduction for a prisoner convicted of a “nonviolent offense.” The statute does not define that term, but the governing regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, ties eligibility to whether the current offense is a “crime of violence” as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). That definition requires that force be an element of the offense or that the offense “by its nature” involve a substantial risk of physical force. The offense of conviction here, conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846, has no element requiring any use, attempted use, or threatened use of force. Its nature is defined by its elements: an agreement to commit a drug offense and an overt act. *In re Snow*, 120 U.S. 274, 281 (1887); *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 550 (1875). The mere possession of a firearm stored at a vacation home, away from the drug transaction, cannot be said to create a substantial risk that force will be used “in the course of committing the offense.”

The Supreme Court has consistently held that penal statutes are to be construed strictly. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) (“The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself.”). This Court must not enlarge the term “nonviolent offense” by implication. *United States v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, 344 U.S. 218, 221-22 (1952). Nor may it stretch an ordinary word beyond its plain and accepted meaning. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25 (1931). A drug conspiracy is not a crime of violence, even where a fact-specific sentencing finding indicates the defendant possessed a weapon. The sentence enhancement imposed under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 is a discretionary aggravating factor; it does not become an element of the offense and cannot retroactively transform the character of the underlying agreement from nonviolent to violent.

**II. THE BOP’S PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 IS INVALID BECAUSE IT CONFLICTS WITH THE GOVERNING STATUTE AND THE AGENCY’S OWN REGULATIONS.**

The BOP’s Program Statement commands that a drug conviction be deemed a “crime of violence” whenever the sentencing court imposes a sentence enhancement for possession of a dangerous weapon. This internal directive is inconsistent on its face with fundamental principles of administrative law. An agency cannot expand its statutory jurisdiction through regulation or internal guidance. *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 129 (1958) (administrative agencies may not extend their authority by implication). The agency’s own regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, adopts the statutory definition of “crime of violence” in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which demands an inquiry into the offense’s nature. The Program Statement rewrites that regulation, substituting a bright-line rule based solely upon a sentence enhancement for the congressionally mandated inquiry into whether the offense itself “by its nature” poses a risk of force.

The BOP’s interpretation of its own regulation is controlling only if it is not “plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation.” *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410, 414 (1945). Here, the Program Statement is plainly inconsistent with the very regulation it purports to implement. Section 924(c)(3) requires an assessment of the offense’s inherent characteristics. The Program Statement abandons that analysis, instead deeming every drug offense with a weapon enhancement violent. This impermissibly enlarges the meaning of “crime of violence” beyond the statutory definition, contrary to the principle that legislative words must be construed in their ordinary, common sense meaning. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940). Where an agency regulation departs from the statutory framework enacted by Congress, or diminishes a statutory grant of benefits to prisoners, the agency action must be set aside. *Bridges v. Wixon*, 326 U.S. 135, 160 (1945); *United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 25 (1948).

Ultimately, the BOP has looked behind the offense of conviction and applied a discretionary sentencing factor retroactively to redefine the crime. Mr. Roussos pleaded guilty to a conspiracy. He completed a rigorous 500-hour drug treatment program. He is eligible for early release under the plain terms of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The district court erred in sustaining the BOP’s contrary conclusion. This Court must reverse.
