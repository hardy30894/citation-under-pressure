**ARGUMENT**

Federal appellate courts reviewing for plain error may consult the entire trial record, including undisputed facts not submitted to the jury, to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit correctly consulted Greer’s prior convictions to conclude that he suffered no prejudice from the omission of a *Rehaif* instruction.

**I. The plain-error standard permits review of the full record to assess prejudice.**

Under plain-error review, the defendant bears the burden of establishing that an error “affected his substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). This requires a “reasonable probability” of a different outcome. *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81-82 (2004). Courts routinely consult the entire record when making that determination. *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002) (holding that *Dominguez Benitez*’s “reasonable probability” standard “must be measured against the record as a whole”). Indeed, *Vonn* rejected the argument that a reviewing court “must confine itself to the portion of the record that includes the portion of the plea proceeding that gave rise to the claimed error.” *Id.* at 62.

This Court has repeatedly relied on record evidence beyond the trial transcript to assess prejudice under plain-error review. In *Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090 (2021), this Court held that a defendant challenging a *Rehaif* error must “make an affirmative showing that he did not know he was a felon.” *Id.* at 2095. The Court explained that “the record in many cases will include” evidence of the defendant’s knowledge, such as “stipulations, prior conviction records, or testimony.” *Id.* at 2097. The Court cited with approval Circuits that had “consulted the record” to determine that the defendant “could not show prejudice” because his prior convictions “would have been obvious to a jury.” *Id.* Nothing in *Greer* limited review to the evidence actually presented to the jury.

**II. The full record, including Greer’s prior convictions, demonstrates no prejudice.**

The Eleventh Circuit properly looked to Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions—which he stipulated to in his plea agreement—to conclude that he knew he was a felon. This Court has long held that evidence of prior convictions is admissible to prove knowledge of felon status. *See Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 190 (1997) (recognizing that prior convictions are probative of the defendant’s knowledge that he is a felon). And this Court has specifically held that “if a person is a felon, he ordinarily knows he is a felon.” *Greer*, 141 S. Ct. at 2097 (quoting *United States v. Rehaif*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2198 (2019)).

Greer’s extensive criminal history—five convictions, all for serious violent felonies—made his knowledge of felon status “all but certain.” *United States v. Burghardt*, 939 F.3d 773, 776 (7th Cir. 2019). Indeed, the Seventh Circuit, applying *Rehaif* on plain-error review, held that a defendant with “multiple prior felony convictions” could not show prejudice because “the jury would have inevitably found that he knew he was a felon.” *United States v. Williams*, 946 F.3d 968, 973 (7th Cir. 2020).

**III. Considering matters outside the trial record serves the interests of fairness and judicial economy.**

The plain-error standard’s fourth prong asks whether the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. This inquiry necessarily considers the full record, including undisputed facts, because fairness is not assessed in a vacuum. *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 633 (2002) (reversing the Fourth Circuit’s refusal to consider “overwhelming” evidence of drug quantity not submitted to the jury).

The Fourth Circuit, in *United States v. Lockhart*, 947 F.3d 187 (4th Cir. 2020) (en banc), held that reviewing courts “may consider all of the evidence in the record” when deciding whether to exercise discretion under plain-error review. *Id.* at 196. So too the D.C. Circuit: *United States v. Ruiz*, 983 F.3d 545, 552 (D.C. Cir. 2020) (“The entire record is available to us in assessing prejudice.”).

Limiting review to the trial evidence would produce absurd results. A defendant with twenty prior felony convictions who stipulated to his felon status could obtain reversal of an otherwise valid conviction based on a *Rehaif* error, even though he plainly knew he was a felon. No principle of fairness requires such an outcome.

**IV. Greer’s contrary position finds no support in this Court’s precedents.**

Greer contends that appellate courts must ignore evidence of his prior convictions because the jury did not hear it. But this Court has never held that reviewing courts must blind themselves to undisputed facts. *Cf. United States v. Nobles*, 422 U.S. 225, 238-39 (1975) (noting that “the trier of fact is entitled to evaluate” all relevant evidence). To the contrary, in *Cotton*, this Court considered evidence of drug quantities from the defendant’s own stipulation—evidence never presented to the jury—to conclude that any error did not seriously affect the fairness of the trial. 535 U.S. at 634.

The Eleventh Circuit’s approach is consistent with this Court’s instruction that plain-error review is not “an inexorable command that all plain errors should be noticed.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 735. Rather, it is a “limited” remedy reserved for “exceptional circumstances.” *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985). Greer’s case—involving a defendant with five prior felony convictions—hardly presents exceptional circumstances.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
