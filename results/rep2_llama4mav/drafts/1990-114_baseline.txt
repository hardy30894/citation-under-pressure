The Missouri constitutional provision mandating the retirement of state court judges at age seventy does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause.

I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Missouri's Appointed State Court Judges

The ADEA exempts from its coverage "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). As the Eighth Circuit correctly held, Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption. Judges, by the very nature of their office, make policy through their decisions, which establish precedent and guide future judicial decision-making. EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) ("'Policymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.").

Moreover, Missouri's judges exercise significant policymaking authority through their supervisory powers. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. This level of responsibility demonstrates that Missouri's appointed judges are indeed "on the policymaking level."

The legislative history of the ADEA's exemptions, while not dispositive, supports this interpretation. The conferees' statement that the exemption should be construed narrowly does not preclude the inclusion of judges within its scope. Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The term "agency" in the context of state government can reasonably be interpreted to include the judiciary, given its significant role in the administration of justice.

II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause

The Equal Protection Clause requires that a state's action be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's mandatory retirement provision for judges is rationally related to several legitimate government interests, including ensuring that judges remain capable of performing their duties, reducing the risk of death or disability on the bench, and promoting a judiciary that is representative of the population it serves. Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The state's interest in maintaining a capable and representative judiciary is a legitimate one, and the mandatory retirement provision is a rational means of achieving this end. While the petitioners may disagree with the state's policy choice, the rational basis test does not require that the state's action be the most effective or the most fair means of achieving its goals. It is sufficient that the provision is rationally related to a legitimate government interest.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
