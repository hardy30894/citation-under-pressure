Respondent should prevail because ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of its general authority to regulate activities within Alaska park units merely because some underlying lands or waters are state, Native, corporate, or private property. The statutory text, structure, and controlling precedent confirm that § 103(c) is a land-ownership provision, not a jurisdiction-stripping clause.

Section 103(c) provides that, within National Park System units in Alaska, the lands “are not to be deemed to be included” in any conservation system unit “except to the extent necessary for the purpose of managing the unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). That language limits how Alaska inholdings are treated for purposes of unit designation; it does not create a categorical immunity from otherwise valid federal regulation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that statutes must be read in context and in light of their structure. United States v. Cleveland Indians Baseball Co., 532 U.S. 200, 217 (2001); Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., 519 U.S. 337, 341 (1997). Here, ANILCA expressly preserves existing federal regulatory powers. Section 1319 states that “[n]othing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing … existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. That savings clause is irreconcilable with petitioner’s reading, which would erase preexisting federal park regulations across vast categories of Alaska land and water.

This Court’s decision below correctly held that the NPS hovercraft ban is a regulation that applies to a mode of conduct within park boundaries, not a regulation directed solely at public lands. Sturgeon v. Frost, 577 U.S. ___ (2016). That conclusion accords with the longstanding distinction between ownership of submerged lands and federal regulatory authority. The Constitution does not disable federal regulation simply because a State owns the bed or waterway. See United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 426-27 (1940); Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 540-41 (1976). Congress may regulate conduct affecting federal lands and waters through the Property Clause, which is “without limitations.” United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927). Indeed, federal power under the Property Clause extends beyond the land itself when necessary to protect federal interests. Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525-26 (1897); Hunt v. United States, 278 U.S. 96, 100 (1928).

Petitioner’s reading also conflicts with the canonical rule that repeals by implication are disfavored. See Posadas v. Nat’l City Bank, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936). If Congress had meant to divest the Park Service of its ordinary regulatory authority over inholdings and navigable waters merely because they lie within a park boundary, it would have spoken far more clearly. Instead, Congress preserved federal agency authority in § 1319 and repeatedly recognized federal regulation of Alaska conservation units. See Alaska v. Babbitt, 72 F.3d 698, 703-05 (9th Cir. 1995) (recognizing the federal government’s continuing authority over Alaska park lands and waters under ANILCA’s text and structure). The lower court’s interpretation is also consistent with John v. United States, 247 F.3d 1032, 1038-40 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc), and John v. United States, 720 F.3d 1214, 1228-30 (9th Cir. 2013), which construed ANILCA to preserve significant federal regulatory authority in Alaska’s national preserves.

Petitioner cannot overcome this framework by invoking Alaska’s title to certain riverbeds. Title and regulatory authority are distinct. The Supreme Court has recognized that federal power may coexist with state title, especially where navigation and federal land management are concerned. Illinois Cent. R.R. Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387, 435-36 (1892); United States v. Alaska, 521 U.S. 1, 34-35 (1997). The fact that Sturgeon was operating on a state-owned river therefore does not defeat NPS regulation enacted under federal authority applicable within a park unit.

Finally, petitioner’s broader theory would upset the settled understanding that Congress may protect federal lands by regulating conduct on adjacent or intermingled nonfederal lands when necessary to effectuate federal purposes. See United States v. Yellowstone Park Co., 278 U.S. 282, 289-90 (1929); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-17 (1911). ANILCA did not silently abandon that settled power. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
