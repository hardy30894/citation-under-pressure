**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage to workers injured on land. The text of OCSLA, its structure, and this Court's precedents compel the conclusion that the situs of the injury must be on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Plain Language of OCSLA Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

OCSLA's workers' compensation provision states that LHWCA benefits extend to the "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase "operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" defines not merely the source of the injury but the geographical locus of the covered event. This Court has long held that "the language of the statute is the starting point for its interpretation," and when the text is clear, judicial inquiry ends. *Boston Sand & Gravel Co. v. United States*, 278 U.S. 41, 48 (1928); *see also Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("where the language of an act is clear and unambiguous, it is the duty of the court to give it effect").

The statutory phrase "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" cannot bear the Ninth Circuit's construction. The word "occurring" modifies "injury," establishing a direct link between the physical location of the injury-producing event and the shelf. *See United States v. Moore*, 95 U.S. 760, 763 (1878). If Congress had intended to cover all injuries with any connection to shelf operations, it would have said so—as it did in other provisions of OCSLA. *Compare* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (extending state law to "artificial islands and fixed structures"), *with* § 1333(b) (limiting coverage to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on" the shelf). The deliberate distinction in phrasing demonstrates congressional intent to confine compensation coverage to the shelf itself. *Cf. FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941).

**II. This Court's Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Coverage Turns on Situs**

In *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947), this Court held that the federal government has paramount rights in the submerged lands of the continental shelf, establishing that OCSLA's reach is geographically limited to the shelf itself. The reasoning of *California* is dispositive: OCSLA's compensation provision is tied to the physical place of injury, not merely the nature of the employment.

Similarly, in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), this Court explained that the scope of OCSLA's coverage is determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed. The Court further noted that section 1333(b) adds a status requirement—covering only "employees"—on top of the otherwise determinative geographic situs requirement. *Id.* at 219 n.2. That statement reflects the consistent understanding of this Court. *See also United States v. Louisiana*, 339 U.S. 699, 703-04 (1950) (submerged lands are "subject to the exclusive jurisdiction and control of the United States" only within the defined shelf area).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. *Curtis* conflicts with this Court's instruction that OCSLA's "clear geographic limitation" governs. *See United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947). That geographic limitation bars compensation for injuries occurring onshore.

**III. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Is Consistent with the Statutory Scheme**

OCSLA was enacted to govern activities "on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act's purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf's natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers' compensation program for offshore workers. *See United States v. Ray*, 423 F.2d 16, 19 (5th Cir. 1970). Extending OCSLA to onshore injuries would disregard the Act's limited territorial reach. *Cf. United States v. Louisiana*, 339 U.S. 699, 703-04 (1950) (statutory construction must respect jurisdictional boundaries).

The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test—requiring only a connection between the injury and shelf operations—improperly expands OCSLA beyond its text. This Court has rejected such open-ended standards when Congress has spoken clearly. *See United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 485 (1917) ("It is a settled rule of construction that statutes enacted for the punishment of crimes and offenses must be strictly construed, and cannot be extended by implication."). Workers' compensation statutes, while remedial, are still subject to the plain meaning of their terms. *See Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 92-93 (1946) (recognizing that the LHWCA's coverage is defined by statutory text, not judicial expansion).

**IV. Applying the Correct Situs Standard, Valladolid's Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility located on the California coast. Because his injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit's remand for application of its "substantial nexus" test was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
