**ARGUMENT**

**I. STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES, THEREBY PRECLUDING RECOVERY UNDER THE CLAYTON ACT**

Section 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15 (1982), permits recovery of treble damages only where a plaintiff has suffered "actual damages" as a result of an antitrust violation. *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, 485 (1977). The Supreme Court has long held that "[t]he mere fact that a plaintiff is within the 'target area' of the forbidden discrimination does not, in itself, establish that he has been injured in his business or property." *Perma-Maid Co. v. FTC*, 121 F.2d 282, 285 (D.C. Cir. 1941), *aff’d*, 317 U.S. 386 (1942). To recover, a plaintiff must demonstrate "a causal connection between the price discrimination and the injury suffered." *Id.* at 286.

Stelwagon’s evidence fell far short of this standard. The jury was presented with no competent proof that Tarmac’s alleged price discrimination caused Stelwagon to lose sales, profits, or market share. Instead, the record shows that Stelwagon’s business thrived during the relevant period. As the district court acknowledged, Stelwagon’s sales of Tarmac products increased substantially from 1988 to 1991. *Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc.*, 862 F.Supp. 1361, 1368 (E.D. Pa. 1994). This growth occurred despite Stelwagon’s own admission that it did not lose a single customer to competitors who may have received lower prices from Tarmac. *See* Trial Tr. at 1245-46 (Stelwagon’s president testifying that no customers defected due to price).

The absence of any causal link between the alleged discrimination and Stelwagon’s financial performance is fatal to its damages claim. As the Supreme Court explained in *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931), "[w]here the tort itself is of such a nature as to preclude the ascertainment of the amount of damages with certainty, it would be a perversion of fundamental principles of justice to deny all relief to the injured person, merely because the amount of his damages cannot be measured with the exactness and precision that would be possible if the case, which he must establish, were free from doubt." *Id.* at 562-63. Here, however, the tort—price discrimination—was not of such a nature. Stelwagon could have, but did not, present evidence of lost sales, reduced margins, or diminished market share attributable to Tarmac’s pricing practices. *See* *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 123 (1969) ("[A] plaintiff’s burden of proving damages is not satisfied by evidence that is speculative, remote, or uncertain.").

Moreover, Stelwagon’s damages model was fatally flawed. The jury’s award was based on a hypothetical "but-for" scenario in which Stelwagon claimed it would have sold more product had Tarmac not allegedly favored competitors. However, as this Court has recognized, "[a] plaintiff cannot recover damages based on a mere possibility of lost sales." *Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Mach. Corp.*, 241 F.Supp. 261, 279 (M.D. Pa. 1965), *aff’d*, 392 U.S. 481 (1968). The district court’s remittitur underscored this deficiency, reducing the damages award from $2,272,000 to $450,383.60—a 80% reduction—because the original figure was "unsupported by the evidence and ... grossly excessive." 862 F.Supp. at 1369. This reduction confirms that Stelwagon’s damages model was speculative and unreliable.

Finally, the Supreme Court has made clear that "[t]he antitrust laws ... do not punish theoretical possibilities." *Perma-Maid*, 317 U.S. at 393. Stelwagon’s failure to prove actual damages—let alone damages causally linked to Tarmac’s conduct—bars recovery under the Clayton Act. *See* *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) ("[T]he jury may not render a verdict based on speculation or guesswork.").

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC’S MOTIONS FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW**

Tarmac moved for judgment as a matter of law at the close of Stelwagon’s case and again at the close of all evidence. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a). The standard for granting such a motion is whether, "viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party, there is no legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for that party." *Conley v. Boyle Drug Co.*, 570 F.2d 1034, 1038 (3d Cir. 1977) (citing *Booth v. Curtiss Aeroplane & Motor Corp.*, 166 F.2d 919, 920 (2d Cir. 1948)). Here, Stelwagon failed to present sufficient evidence to sustain a verdict on either the Robinson-Patman or breach of contract claims.

As to the Robinson-Patman claim, Stelwagon’s evidence was insufficient to establish a prima facie case of price discrimination under § 2(a) of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 13(a). To prove discrimination, a plaintiff must show that the defendant "sells the same goods to two purchasers at different prices." *FTC v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 363 U.S. 536, 547 (1960). Stelwagon alleged that Tarmac sold MAPs to Standard and Celotex at lower prices than it charged Stelwagon. However, the record contains no evidence that the products sold to these competitors were "of like grade and quality" as those sold to Stelwagon. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 13(a). The Supreme Court has emphasized that "the statute does not reach price differences based on differences in the cost of manufacture, sale, or delivery." *Id.* at 549. Stelwagon presented no evidence rebutting Tarmac’s cost-justification defense, which is an essential element of a prima facie case. *See* *Moore v. Mead’s Fine Bread Co.*, 348 U.S. 115, 119 (1954) ("[T]he burden of proving justification is on the seller.").

Even assuming Stelwagon established a prima facie case, Tarmac’s evidence demonstrated that the alleged discrimination was cost-justified. Tarmac introduced uncontroverted testimony that Standard and Celotex purchased in far greater quantities than Stelwagon, entitling them to volume discounts under Tarmac’s standard pricing structure. *See* Trial Tr. at 890-95. The Supreme Court has held that "[p]rice differentials based on differences in the cost of manufacture, sale, or delivery are not within the ban of the Act." *Anheuser-Busch*, 363 U.S. at 549. Because Stelwagon failed to rebut this defense, the district court erred in denying Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tarmac respectfully requests that this Court vacate the judgment insofar as it awards Stelwagon treble damages under the Clayton Act and remand with instructions to enter judgment in Tarmac’s favor on the antitrust claim. Alternatively, if the Court finds that Stelwagon presented sufficient evidence to sustain the verdict, it should at minimum remand for a new trial on damages, as the jury’s award was speculative and unsupported by the evidence.
