ARGUMENT

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Act is a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies intermediate scrutiny. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 725-26 (2000) (upholding analogous 100-foot buffer zones around health-care facilities because they “do not ban any form of communication” and “leave open ample alternative channels”).

The Act is content neutral on its face. It “applies equally to all demonstrators, regardless of viewpoint,” and “the First Amendment does not demand that the State allow” every form of expression in every location. Id. at 719, 726. Recent decisions addressing speaker-based or content-based restrictions in other contexts do not alter this analysis. See, e.g., Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 340 (2010) (distinguishing “political speech” from “the special concerns of public-sidewalk protests around abortion clinics”).

The Act is narrowly tailored. It “serves a significant governmental interest” in protecting patient access and clinic safety, and “the restrictions are ‘narrowly tailored to serve’” those interests. Hill, 530 U.S. at 725-26 (quoting Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989)). The 35-foot fixed zones are “less restrictive” than the floating zones upheld in Hill and leave open “a substantial portion of the sidewalk.” Id. at 726.

Ample alternative channels remain. Petitioners may “speak at a normal conversational distance,” display signs, distribute literature, and pray outside the zones. Id. at 729. The record confirms that these means have permitted continued communication with patients. See McGuire v. Reilly (McGuire II), 386 F.3d 45, 52 (1st Cir. 2004) (similar restrictions left “adequate, if not perfect, alternative means”).

Hill v. Colorado controls and should not be overruled. Stare decisis requires adherence to that precedent, which has “been repeatedly reaffirmed.” Hill, 530 U.S. at 735 (Kennedy, J., dissenting in part but acknowledging the holding). No subsequent decision has undermined its core reasoning.

The law of the case doctrine precludes relitigation of facial validity. See United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). Petitioners’ as-applied claims likewise fail because the Act imposes no viewpoint discrimination and leaves sufficient communicative opportunities at each clinic.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
