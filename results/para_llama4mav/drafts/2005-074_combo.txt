The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. Even if it did, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive.

First, the statute's language and legislative history do not clearly indicate that it applies retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T.R. Co., 270 U.S. 1 (1926), the Court held that a statute should not be given retroactive effect unless Congress clearly intended it. Here, the statute's text is ambiguous, and there is no clear indication of congressional intent to apply it retroactively. See also United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. 399 (1806) (holding that a statute should not be applied retroactively unless explicitly stated).

Second, even if the statute were intended to apply retroactively, its application to Fernandez-Vargas would still be impermissible. In Davis v. Mills, 194 U.S. 451 (1904), the Court held that a statute cannot be applied retroactively if it would impair vested rights or create new obligations. Fernandez-Vargas had been living in the United States for over 15 years before the statute was enacted and had taken steps to adjust his status. Applying the reinstatement statute to him would impair his vested right to seek relief under the INA. See also Funkhouser v. J.B. Preston Co., 290 U.S. 163 (1933) (holding that a statute cannot be applied retroactively if it would disturb vested rights).

The government's argument that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is unpersuasive. In Lynch v. United States, 292 U.S. 571 (1934), the Court held that a statutory right can be considered vested if it has been relied upon by the individual. Fernandez-Vargas had relied on the availability of relief under the INA when he married a U.S. citizen and applied to adjust his status. See also De La Rama S.S. Co. v. United States, 344 U.S. 386 (1953) (holding that a statutory right can be considered vested if it has been relied upon by the individual).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that retroactive application of a statute can be impermissible if it would be unfair or unjust. In Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. 386 (1798), the Court held that a statute cannot be applied retroactively if it would impair vested rights or create new obligations. Similarly, in Danforth v. Groton Water Co., 178 Mass. 472 (1901), Justice Holmes held that a statute should not be applied retroactively if it would be "contrary to the first principles of justice." Applying the reinstatement statute to Fernandez-Vargas would be unfair and unjust, as it would deprive him of his right to seek relief under the INA.

Additionally, the principle of lenity, as established in United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. 76 (1820), also supports the conclusion that the statute should not be applied retroactively. The Court in Wiltberger held that ambiguous statutes should be construed in favor of the individual. In this case, the ambiguity in the statute's language and the lack of clear congressional intent to apply it retroactively should be resolved in favor of Fernandez-Vargas.

The government's interpretation of the statute would also be inconsistent with the principle that statutes should be construed to avoid harsh or unjust results. In United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. 482 (1868), the Court held that a statute should be interpreted in a way that avoids absurd or unjust results. Applying the reinstatement statute to Fernandez-Vargas would be harsh and unjust, as it would result in his deportation without consideration of his eligibility for relief under the INA.

Finally, the Court's decision in Chew Heong v. United States, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), is also relevant. In Chew Heong, the Court held that a statute should be construed in light of its purpose and the context in which it was enacted. The purpose of the INA is to provide a fair and orderly process for immigrants to adjust their status. Applying the reinstatement statute to Fernandez-Vargas would be inconsistent with this purpose.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
