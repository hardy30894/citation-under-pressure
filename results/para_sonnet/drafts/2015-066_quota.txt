# ARGUMENT

## The Government's Use of Bryant's Uncounseled Tribal Convictions to Establish an Element of a Federal Felony Violates the Sixth Amendment

### A. Uncounseled convictions that would offend the Sixth Amendment in state or federal court cannot be used to prove an essential element of a subsequent federal offense.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees "the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel" in any criminal prosecution that results in a sentence of imprisonment. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342-43 (1963); *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979) (holding that "actual imprisonment" is the touchstone triggering the right to appointed counsel). Had Bryant's tribal-court prosecutions occurred in state or federal court, the resulting convictions—each of which carried a term of imprisonment without the benefit of appointed counsel—would have been constitutionally infirm under *Scott* and its progeny. *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 661-62 (2002) (reaffirming that "a suspended sentence that may 'end up in the actual deprivation of a person's liberty' may not be imposed unless the defendant was accorded 'the guiding hand of counsel'" (quoting *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 40 (1972))).

The Supreme Court has long held that such presumptively void convictions may not be used against a defendant in a later proceeding to establish guilt or to increase punishment. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), the Court held that "[t]o permit a conviction obtained in violation of Gideon v. Wainwright to be used against a person . . . is to erode the principle of that case." The Court explained that an uncounseled conviction is "inherently unreliable," *id.* at 114, and that its use to prove an element of a subsequent offense "renders the ["]new["] conviction just as invalid as [the] prior conviction," *id.* at 115. *United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 449 (1972), applied the same reasoning to sentencing, holding that a sentencing court could not rely on convictions "which were wholly unreliable" because they were obtained without counsel.

### B. Nichols v. United States does not authorize use of uncounseled tribal convictions to prove guilt.

The government's reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* held only that a valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction—one that did not violate the Sixth Amendment because no imprisonment was imposed, see *Scott*, 440 U.S. at 373-74—could be considered at sentencing to calculate a defendant's criminal-history score. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47. Critically, the Court's holding rested on procedural safeguards unique to the sentencing context: the government need only prove a sentencing factor by a preponderance of the evidence, and the defendant retains the opportunity to contest the "reliability" of the prior conviction before the sentencing court. *Id.* at 747-48; *id.* at 752 (Souter, J., concurring). Those accommodations are entirely absent when an uncounseled conviction is used, as here, to satisfy an element the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) ("[T]he Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime.").

*Nichols* never purported to bless the use of an uncounseled conviction to satisfy Winship's beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard at the guilt phase of a later prosecution. See *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1396 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that "a conviction which is invalid for purposes of imposing sentence due to a defendant's lack of counsel is also invalid for purposes of proving guilt in a subsequent criminal proceeding"). Nor does the recidivism principle recognized in *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008)—that a repeat-offender statute punishes only "the last offense committed by the defendant"—resolve the constitutional defect here. Whatever may be true of sentencing enhancements, § 117(a) does not merely enhance punishment for a subsequent offense; it uses the prior uncounseled convictions as substantive elements that the government must prove to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt to obtain a conviction in the first instance. That distinction is precisely what separates this case from *Nichols* and aligns it with *Burgett* and *Tucker*.

### C. That the predicate convictions arose in tribal court does not diminish the Sixth Amendment's reliability concerns.

The government's suggestion that tribal-court convictions stand on different footing because the Sixth Amendment does not of its own force apply to tribes, see *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) ("the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes"), does not answer the question presented. The relevant inquiry is not whether the tribal court violated the Sixth Amendment—by definition it could not, since the Amendment does not bind tribes—but whether the federal government, in seeking a federal felony conviction, may rely on convictions obtained without counsel to satisfy an element of guilt that the Constitution otherwise requires be proved reliably. As this Court recognized in *Lewis v. United States*, 445 U.S. 55, 60-62 (1980) (distinguishing *Burgett* and *Tucker* on the ground that the statute there required no showing of reliability), the constitutional infirmity of the underlying process—not the identity of the tribunal that produced it—drives the analysis. Because Bryant's tribal convictions resulted in imprisonment without appointed counsel, they carry the same reliability deficit that rendered the convictions in *Burgett* and *Tucker* unusable, regardless of the forum in which they arose.

For these reasons, and those set forth below, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
