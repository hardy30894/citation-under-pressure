**ARGUMENT**

**THE THREE-YEAR STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS IN 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) BARS A SUIT WHERE THE DEFENDANT DISCLOSED ALL RELEVANT INFORMATION BUT THE PLAINTIFF CHOSE NOT TO READ OR COULD NOT RECALL HAVING READ IT.**

**I. The plain text of § 1113(2) requires “actual knowledge” of the underlying conduct, not subjective awareness of its imprudence.**

Section 1113(2) provides that an ERISA action must be brought within three years of “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). Congress’s use of “actual knowledge” unambiguously demands that a plaintiff know *of the facts constituting the breach*—not that he appreciate the legal significance of those facts. *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985) (rejecting argument that “actual knowledge” required knowledge of illegality). As *Blanton* held, § 1113 “is triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.” *Id.* at 992. The Ninth Circuit has consistently applied this rule: in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, the court explained that where a plaintiff receives reduced benefit checks, “he had knowledge of the transaction, though he may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.” 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988). And in *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, the court rejected a “continuing breach” theory because actual knowledge “is measured from the ‘earliest date’ on which [the plaintiff] knew of the breach.” 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991).

Sulyma does not dispute that Intel disclosed all relevant information about the Funds’ alternative investments and their risks. Intel’s disclosures, including Fund Fact Sheets, Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notices, and Summary Plan Descriptions, explicitly informed participants that the Funds were invested in hedge funds and private equity. These documents revealed the very conduct Sulyma now claims was imprudent. Under *Blanton* and *Meagher*, Sulyma had “actual knowledge” of that conduct when Intel made the information available to him—regardless of whether he read or remembered it. *See Blanton*, 760 F.2d at 991-92.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement of subjective awareness conflicts with the statute’s text and purpose.**

The Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment because Sulyma declared he was “unaware” of the alternative investments and did “not recall seeing any documents” disclosing them. The court effectively held that “actual knowledge” requires subjective, conscious awareness of the breach—a standard that invites plaintiffs to evade the statute of limitations by simply claiming ignorance or lack of memory. This holding contravenes § 1113(2)’s plain meaning.

The term “actual knowledge” distinguishes between actual and constructive knowledge—it does not impose a heightened subjective-awareness requirement. When Congress enacted ERISA in 1974, § 1113 included both an “actual knowledge” provision and a “constructive knowledge” provision that was triggered when a report “could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge of such breach.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976). Congress repealed the constructive knowledge provision in 1987, leaving only the “actual knowledge” requirement. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b). This repeal eliminated liability based on what a plaintiff “could reasonably be expected” to know. But it did not eliminate liability based on what a plaintiff *actually did know* because information was provided to him. The distinction between actual and constructive knowledge concerns whether the plaintiff *received* the information; it does not turn on whether the plaintiff subjectively attended to that information. *See Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that actual knowledge existed when administrator informed plaintiff of the transaction).

Sulyma’s testimony that he was “unaware” of the investments and did not “recall” seeing disclosures does not create a genuine dispute of material fact. If the statute allowed a plaintiff to defeat summary judgment merely by claiming he did not read or remember information provided to him, the three-year limitations period would be rendered meaningless. Plan participants would have an incentive to ignore disclosures to preserve their claims indefinitely, undermining ERISA’s goal of finality. *See Phillips*, 944 F.2d at 520 (holding that a contrary rule “essentially reads the ‘actual knowledge’ standard out of the statute”).

**III. Sulyma had actual knowledge as a matter of law because Intel provided him with comprehensive disclosures regarding the challenged investments.**

Intel established, through undisputed evidence, that it provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, notices, and other documents that (1) disclosed that the Funds were invested in alternative assets like hedge funds and private equity; (2) explained the strategy behind those investments; and (3) warned of associated risks. Sulyma accessed some of this information on Intel’s websites, yet he now claims he did not read or remember it. But “actual knowledge” does not require that a plaintiff *consciously assimilate* information that has been communicated directly to him. *Blanton*, 760 F.2d at 991 (plaintiffs who “made the decision to undertake the transaction” had actual knowledge). When a plan participant receives clear disclosures of the facts underlying an alleged breach, the participant has “actual knowledge” of that breach for purposes of § 1113(2). *Ziegler*, 916 F.2d at 552.

The district court correctly found no genuine dispute of material fact. Sulyma’s self-serving declaration of unawareness does not overcome Intel’s evidence that he received and accessed the disclosures. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and hold that Sulyma’s claims are time-barred.
