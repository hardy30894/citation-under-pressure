## ARGUMENT

### I. The district court abused its discretion by striking four expert witnesses for discovery noncompliance.

A district court may impose sanctions for failure to obey discovery orders, but the sanction must be proportionate and supported by the record. See *Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée*, 456 U.S. 694 (1982); *National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc.*, 427 U.S. 639 (1976). In this Circuit, exclusion of evidence is a severe sanction and is appropriate only when the violation results from willful disobedience or bad faith and lesser sanctions would not suffice. *Mann v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 488 F.2d 75 (5th Cir. 1973); *Davidson v. Great Northern Railway Co.*, 446 F.2d 889 (5th Cir. 1971).

That standard was not met here. The record shows a discovery dispute over whether Plaintiffs’ experts had completed their opinions and whether depositions should proceed on the schedule set by Defendants’ notices. Plaintiffs sought a protective order, and the district court itself responded by resetting the deposition deadline to July 30, 1993. A sanction striking four experts entirely was disproportionate, especially where the court had already recognized that the expert testimony was still developing. This Court has long required district courts to consider the full context of the violation before imposing the harshest discovery sanctions. *Batson v. Neal Spelce Associates, Inc.*, 765 F.2d 511 (5th Cir. 1985); *Marshall v. Segona*, 621 F.2d 763 (5th Cir. 1980).

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the Federal Rules favor decisions on the merits rather than default by sanction. *Societe Internationale pour Participations Industrielles et Commerciales, S.A. v. Rogers*, 357 U.S. 197 (1958). Exclusion of four experts here effectively determined the case before trial. That result is especially troubling because the district court’s order did not identify specific prejudice caused by the missed July 30 deadline, nor did it explain why a continuance, renewed deposition order, monetary sanction, or limitation on testimony would not have sufficed. See *Flaska v. Little River Marine Construction Co.*, 389 F.2d 885 (5th Cir. 1968).

### II. The court erred in excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under Daubert.

Expert testimony is admissible if it is both relevant and reliable. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). But *Daubert* does not authorize exclusion simply because an expert’s opinions are contested, incomplete, or based on scientific judgment that the opponent disputes. The trial judge must act as gatekeeper, not as factfinder. *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136 (1997); *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999).

This Court has repeatedly held that doubts about an expert’s assumptions, factual basis, or methodology ordinarily go to weight, not admissibility. *Christophersen v. Allied-Signal Corp.*, 939 F.2d 1106 (5th Cir. 1991) (en banc), overruled on other grounds by *Daubert*, 509 U.S. 579; *Huss v. Gayden*, 571 F.3d 442 (5th Cir. 2009); *Moore v. Ashland Chemical Inc.*, 151 F.3d 269 (5th Cir. 1998) (en banc). The district court’s ruling appears to have treated the experts’ inability to complete all desired testing or review all materials by the court’s deadline as a reliability defect under *Daubert*. That was legal error. Reliability turns on whether the methodology is scientifically valid, not whether counsel or the court believes the record was complete enough by an imposed date. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. 579; *Moore*, 151 F.3d 269.

Moreover, exclusion under *Daubert* must be tied to the actual proffer. *Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136. The district court’s record here does not show a careful analysis of each challenged opinion or why no part of the testimony could reach the jury. Summary exclusion of key causation experts on a paper record was an abuse of discretion. *Viterbo v. Dow Chemical Co.*, 826 F.2d 420 (5th Cir. 1987).

### III. The summary judgment cannot stand because it rested on the erroneous exclusion of Plaintiffs’ experts.

Summary judgment is proper only when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317 (1986); *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986). Where the nonmovant’s proof is excluded through legal error, summary judgment must be vacated if the excluded evidence would create a triable issue. *Lavespere v. Niagara Machine & Tool Works, Inc.*, 910 F.2d 167 (5th Cir. 1990), abrogated on other grounds by *Little v. Liquid Air Corp.*, 37 F.3d 1069 (5th Cir. 1994) (en banc).

Here, Plaintiffs’ claims depended on expert proof of exposure, causation, and injury. By striking four experts and excluding two more, the court deprived Plaintiffs of the evidence necessary to oppose summary judgment. That cascade of rulings was reversible error because it converted contested scientific and factual issues into a judgment as a matter of law without a fair merits determination. *Celotex*, 477 U.S. 317; *Anderson*, 477 U.S. 242.

### IV. The Court has jurisdiction notwithstanding the timing of the notice of appeal.

A notice of appeal filed after a decision that leaves one claim unresolved may become effective when final judgment is later entered. *Bankers Trust Co. v. Mallis*, 435 U.S. 381 (1978); *Budinich v. Becton Dickinson & Co.*, 486 U.S. 196 (1988). This Court has likewise treated premature notices as effective where finality is later achieved and no party is prejudiced. *United States v. Cooper*, 135 F.3d 960 (5th Cir. 1998); *Martin v. Halliburton*, 618 F.3d 476 (5th Cir. 2010). Because the district court later entered a final order disposing of the remaining claims, and because the appeal was already pending, jurisdiction should be sustained and the merits reached.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
