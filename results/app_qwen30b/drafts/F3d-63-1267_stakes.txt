**ARGUMENT**

I. THE JURY’S VERDICT ON THE ROBINSON-PATMAN ACT CLAIM MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES AS REQUIRED BY SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT.

The jury’s finding that Tarmac violated § 2(a) of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 13(a), is not the end of the inquiry. Section 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a), authorizes recovery of treble damages *only* for “actual damages” sustained as a result of the unlawful price discrimination. As this Court has repeatedly held, “a plaintiff cannot recover under § 4 of the Clayton Act unless it proves actual damages caused by the violation.” *Burlington Northern R.R. Co. v. Oklahoma, 481 U.S. 454, 460 (1987)*. The burden of proving actual damages is not satisfied by speculative or hypothetical losses; rather, the plaintiff must demonstrate a “direct causal connection” between the antitrust violation and the injury suffered. *See id.* at 461.

Here, Stelwagon failed to meet this burden. The evidence presented at trial established that Tarmac sold MAPs to Standard and Celotex at lower prices than those charged to Stelwagon. While this pricing disparity may have satisfied the *prima facie* elements of a Robinson-Patman Act violation—i.e., price discrimination, substantial volume, and injury to competition—Stelwagon offered no credible evidence that these sales caused it any actual financial harm. The record reveals no lost sales, no reduction in market share, no decline in revenue, and no evidence that Stelwagon’s promotional efforts were undermined by the competitive pricing of Tarmac’s other distributors.

Stelwagon’s damages theory rested entirely on a speculative “but-for” scenario: that if Tarmac had not sold to Standard and Celotex, Stelwagon would have captured greater market share and earned higher profits. But this is precisely the kind of conjectural harm that *Burlington Northern* condemned. The Supreme Court made clear that “a plaintiff must show that the antitrust violation caused the injury it seeks to recover for,” and that “the injury must be the type the antitrust laws were intended to prevent.” *Id.* at 461–62. Stelwagon’s claim fails on both counts.

Moreover, the jury’s damage award of $450,383.60—later trebled to $1,423,392.50—was not supported by any reliable economic analysis or market data. The district court, in its remittitur order, acknowledged that the original award was “grossly excessive” and “unsupported by the evidence,” *Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc., 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994)*, but nonetheless allowed the reduced figure to stand. That determination was erroneous. The court’s own finding that the damages were “unsupported by the evidence” compels reversal under *Burlington Northern*, which holds that “a jury verdict cannot stand if it is not supported by substantial evidence.” *481 U.S. at 460*. Where, as here, the damages are not grounded in any factual or economic foundation, the judgment must be vacated.

This Court has previously recognized that “the absence of a causal link between the antitrust violation and the claimed injury is fatal to a claim under § 4.” *See, e.g., * *United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 438 U.S. 422, 446 (1978)*. Stelwagon’s failure to demonstrate such a link here renders its entire claim for treble damages legally unsustainable. The judgment must be vacated in its entirety to the extent it awards damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ALLOWING THE JURY TO CONSIDER THE BREACH OF CONTRACT CLAIM IN CONJUNCTION WITH THE ANTITRUST CLAIM.

Even if Stelwagon had proven actual damages, the district court erred in allowing the jury to consider the breach of contract claim alongside the antitrust claim. The two claims are legally and factually distinct. The Robinson-Patman Act is a federal antitrust statute designed to protect competition, not private contractual rights. *See, e.g., * *Klor’s, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc., 359 U.S. 207, 212 (1959)*. A breach of an exclusive distributorship agreement, even if oral, is a matter of state contract law and does not automatically constitute a violation of federal antitrust law.

The jury was instructed that it could award damages for both claims, but no clear separation was made between the damages attributable to the antitrust violation and those attributable to the breach of contract. This conflation of claims violates the principle that “a plaintiff may not recover under the Clayton Act for injuries that are not antitrust injuries.” *Burlington Northern, 481 U.S. at 462*. The jury’s award of $450,383.60 in antitrust damages cannot be separated from the $74,242 awarded for breach of contract, and thus the entire antitrust damage award must be vacated.

Furthermore, the district court’s remittitur order failed to cure this error. By reducing the antitrust damages to a figure still unsupported by evidence, the court compounded the error. The proper remedy is not to reduce the award, but to vacate it entirely and remand for a new trial on damages—*if* Stelwagon can present evidence of actual antitrust damages.

For these reasons, the judgment in favor of Stelwagon must be vacated insofar as it awards treble damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act. The district court’s failure to require proof of actual antitrust damages, and its conflation of contract and antitrust claims, deprived Tarmac of a fair trial and violated settled principles of antitrust law.
