**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. Congress has provided an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s exercise of delegated authority, and the subsequent sub-delegation to the DEA Administrator is fully authorized under the Administrative Procedure Act and longstanding precedent. The Toubys’ challenge fails both as a matter of law and as a matter of constitutional interpretation.

**I. Congress Has Provided an Intelligible Principle for Temporary Scheduling**

The non-delegation doctrine does not prohibit all delegations of legislative power, but only those that lack an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The principle is satisfied when Congress sets forth “a standard to guide the exercise of discretion,” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 377 (1989), even if that standard is broad or general. The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress has defined the purpose and provided criteria for decision-making, regardless of the complexity of the subject matter.

Section 811(h) satisfies this standard. It authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if he determines that “an imminent hazard to the public safety” exists. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is not vague or unbounded. The statute explicitly requires the Attorney General to consider three factors: (1) the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and (3) the risk it poses to public health. Id. § 811(h)(3). These are not mere suggestions—they are binding criteria that must be evaluated before any scheduling action. The Attorney General must also provide notice in the Federal Register and consider the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services. Id. § 811(h)(4). These procedural safeguards ensure that the decision is not arbitrary and that the delegatee acts within a defined framework.

The Toubys argue that the standard “imminent hazard to the public safety” is too amorphous. But the Supreme Court has rejected such challenges when the standard is accompanied by specific, measurable factors. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld the delegation of authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission to create guidelines, even though the Commission was tasked with “ensuring that the sentencing process is fair and consistent.” 488 U.S. at 377. The Court emphasized that “Congress may delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to act is directed to conform.” Id. at 377 (quoting *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409). Here, Congress did precisely that: it defined the purpose (protecting public safety), identified the relevant factors, and imposed procedural requirements. The standard is not “unintelligible” simply because it is flexible or responsive to emerging threats.

**II. The Sub-Delegation to the DEA Administrator Is Fully Authorized**

The Toubys further contend that the Attorney General exceeded his authority by delegating temporary scheduling power to the DEA Administrator. This argument is foreclosed by *Mistretta* and decades of administrative law. The Constitution permits Congress to delegate authority to executive agencies, and the Attorney General may, in turn, delegate to subordinate officials so long as the delegation is consistent with the enabling statute and the Administrative Procedure Act.

The Attorney General’s authority to delegate is rooted in 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which was enacted pursuant to 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), authorizing the Attorney General to “delegate to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice such functions as he may deem appropriate.” The 1987 amendment to this regulation explicitly assigned to the DEA Administrator the “functions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). This delegation is not only consistent with the statute but also with the principle that “Congress may not only delegate authority to the Executive, but may also authorize the Executive to delegate further.” *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 234 (2001).

The Toubys’ argument that the delegation is improper because the DEA is not a “legislative” body misses the point. The power to temporarily schedule is not legislative in the sense of creating new laws; it is an administrative function of implementing existing statutory standards. The DEA Administrator does not make law—he applies a statutory framework to a specific substance. The Court has long recognized that “the Executive Branch may carry out legislative functions when Congress has authorized it to do so.” *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 422 (1941). The DEA’s role is not to define criminality but to enforce it based on a standard set by Congress.

**III. The Preclusion of Judicial Review Does Not Bar Constitutional Challenge**

The Toubys argue that § 811(h)(6)’s provision that “no court shall have jurisdiction to review any order issued under this subsection” precludes any constitutional challenge. This is incorrect. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “the availability of judicial review is presumed” and that “Congress must manifest a clear and convincing intent to foreclose review of constitutional claims.” *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). No such intent appears here. The provision bars review of the *order* itself, not the constitutionality of the delegation. Indeed, the Court has held that “a statute may be unconstitutional even if it is not subject to judicial review of its application.” *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. at 422. The Toubys may challenge the delegation’s constitutionality because the issue is not whether the order is valid, but whether Congress may have delegated such power at all.
