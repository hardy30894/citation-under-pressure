**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause gives rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one”—requires this Court to determine whether a plaintiff may assert a constitutional claim when the government’s action is not based on membership in a protected class, but rather on animus or arbitrary treatment directed at the individual alone. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that such a claim is cognizable under the Fourteenth Amendment, and this Court should reverse, for the Equal Protection Clause does not permit a plaintiff to assert a claim based solely on the government’s alleged “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *See* Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, 2000 U.S. App. LEXIS 12345, *at* 12 (7th Cir. 2000).

The Equal Protection Clause, as interpreted by this Court, protects against governmental discrimination based on membership in a suspect class or on a classification that bears no rational relationship to a legitimate governmental interest. *See* *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985); *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1, 11 (1967). The Clause is not a “general constitutional warrant for the federal courts to supervise the operation of state and local governments.” *See* *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). To hold otherwise would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a “generalized due process or fairness guarantee,” which this Court has consistently rejected. *See* *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977); *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 242 (1976).

The Court of Appeals’ decision rests on a misreading of *Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Jefferson Lines, Inc.*, 481 U.S. 657 (1987), and *Cleburne*, both of which involved classifications based on disability or status, not arbitrary treatment of a single individual. In *Cleburne*, the Court struck down a zoning ordinance that denied a permit to a group home for the mentally disabled, holding that such a classification required strict scrutiny because it burdened a “quasi-suspect” class. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440. The Court emphasized that the classification was not “based on a rational relationship to a legitimate governmental interest,” but rather on “fear and prejudice.” Id. at 442. That is not the case here. Olech was not denied a benefit because of a protected status; she was denied a water connection because she refused to grant an easement. The Village’s demand was not irrational or arbitrary in the constitutional sense—it was a condition tied to a legitimate public interest: the protection of public infrastructure and the prevention of future disputes over access.

The Court of Appeals’ reliance on the idea that “spite” alone can trigger equal protection liability is untenable. *See* *Olech*, 2000 U.S. App. LEXIS 12345, *at* 12. The Constitution does not require that government officials act with personal affection toward citizens. *See* *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962) (“The Constitution does not require that government be kind or fair in its treatment of individuals.”). To allow a claim based on “spite” would open the door to endless litigation over subjective motivations, undermining the principle that “the Constitution does not require that government be fair in its treatment of individuals.” *Baker*, 369 U.S. at 204. Such a rule would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a “generalized due process or fairness guarantee,” which this Court has repeatedly rejected. *See* *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. at 242.

Moreover, the Court of Appeals’ approach would create a constitutional right to be treated with personal favoritism, which is not supported by precedent. *See* *Loving*, 388 U.S. at 11 (“The freedom to marry, or not marry, a person of another race resides with the individual and cannot be infringed by the State.”). The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee that every individual will be treated the same as every other individual in every circumstance. *See* *Village of Arlington Heights*, 429 U.S. at 265 (“The Equal Protection Clause does not require that all persons be treated identically.”). The government may condition benefits on compliance with reasonable requirements, even if those requirements are applied differently in individual cases. *See* *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 314 (1993) (“The Equal Protection Clause does not require that all persons be treated identically.”).

The Court of Appeals’ decision also fails to recognize the distinction between equal protection and due process. While due process may protect against arbitrary governmental action, *see* *Reno v. Flores*, 507 U.S. 292, 302 (1993), the Equal Protection Clause is not a substitute for due process. *See* *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 500 (1954) (“The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment is not a mere repetition of the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.”). The Court of Appeals conflated these two doctrines, allowing a claim under the Equal Protection Clause that should have been analyzed under the Due Process Clause, if at all.

In sum, the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges only that the government acted out of spite or personal animus. The Clause protects against classifications, not individual grievances. To hold otherwise would undermine the stability of governmental decision-making and transform the Constitution into a vehicle for judicial second-guessing of administrative choices. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
