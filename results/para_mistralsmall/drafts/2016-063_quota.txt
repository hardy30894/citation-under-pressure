**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Anthony W. Perry respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring his case to the Federal Circuit. The Board’s dismissal of his appeal for lack of jurisdiction was a mixed case over which the district court, not the Federal Circuit, has jurisdiction to review. This Court should reverse the transfer order and remand with instructions to transfer the case to the appropriate district court.

**I. The Board’s Dismissal of Perry’s Appeal Was a Mixed Case Subject to District Court Review.**

The Civil Service Reform Act (“CSRA”) provides that a federal employee who alleges discrimination in an appealable adverse action may seek review in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). Such cases are known as “mixed cases” because they involve both an appealable action and a discrimination claim. *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 47 (2012) (citing 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)). Here, Perry alleged that his retirement and suspension were coerced by discrimination, and the Board dismissed his appeal for lack of jurisdiction based on voluntariness. This dismissal did not remove the case from the mixed-case exception; it merely resolved a threshold jurisdictional issue. *See id.* at 604 (holding that a mixed case remains a mixed case even if the Board dismisses it on procedural grounds).

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), upon which the lower court relied, is inapposite. *Powell* involved a purely jurisdictional dismissal where the Board found the employee’s separation was voluntary and thus outside its jurisdiction. *Id.* at 597-98. Here, by contrast, Perry’s discrimination claim was inextricably intertwined with the voluntariness inquiry. The Board’s dismissal was not a jurisdictional bar but a merits-based conclusion that Perry’s settlement was voluntary, which necessarily implicated his discrimination allegations. *SeeConforto v. United States*, 713 F.3d 1113, 1118 (Fed. Cir. 2013) (distinguishing *Powell* where the jurisdictional issue was separate from the discrimination claim).

**II. The Supreme Court’s Decision in *Kloeckner* Controls and Requires District Court Review.**

In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, judicial review lies in district court. 568 U.S. at 604. The Court rejected the argument that such dismissals should be reviewed in the Federal Circuit, emphasizing that the mixed-case exception applies regardless of whether the Board reaches the merits. *Id.* at 603-04.

The Board and the D.C. Circuit attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that Perry’s dismissal was jurisdictional rather than procedural. But this distinction is unsupported by the statute or precedent. The CSRA defines mixed cases by reference to the nature of the claim, not the procedural posture of the Board’s dismissal. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). As the Federal Circuit has recognized, *Kloeckner* abrogated the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals for purposes of mixed-case review. *Conforto*, 713 F.3d at 1118 n.1.

Moreover, the Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* explicitly rejected the argument that the forum for review should turn on whether the Board’s dismissal was jurisdictional or procedural. 568 U.S. at 604. The Court held that the mixed-case exception applies whenever an employee alleges discrimination in an appealable action, regardless of the Board’s rationale for dismissal. *Id.* The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding in *Powell* is thus no longer good law.

**III. The Statutory Scheme and Policy Considerations Support District Court Review.**

The CSRA’s structure confirms that mixed cases belong in district court. Section 7703(b)(2) provides that discrimination claims “shall be filed in district court,” and the Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to apply to all mixed cases, including those dismissed by the Board. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 604. The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction, by contrast, is limited to cases where the Board reaches the merits of an appealable action. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A).

Policy considerations also favor district court review. District courts are better equipped to handle discrimination claims, which often involve complex factual inquiries and equitable remedies. *SeeBrown v. General Servs. Admin.*, 425 U.S. 820, 832 (1976) (noting the specialized role of district courts in employment discrimination cases). The Federal Circuit, by contrast, lacks expertise in discrimination law and is ill-suited to adjudicate such claims. *See id.*

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Reliance on *Powell* Is Erroneous and Should Be Rejected.**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Powell* is inconsistent with *Kloeckner* and the CSRA’s text. *Powell* held that a jurisdictional dismissal should be reviewed in the Federal Circuit, but the Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* rejected this approach for mixed cases. 568 U.S. at 604. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that Perry’s dismissal was jurisdictional rather than procedural is unavailing, as the Supreme Court expressly rejected this distinction. *Id.*

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is also inconsistent with the Federal Circuit’s own precedent. In *Conforto*, the Federal Circuit acknowledged that *Kloeckner* abrogated the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals for mixed cases. 713 F.3d at 1118 n.1. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding in *Powell* is thus no longer good law.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the D.C. Circuit’s transfer order and remand with instructions to transfer the case to the appropriate district court for review.
