The respondent, Eugene Woodard, has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not apply to clemency proceedings because they are not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. As established by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925), clemency is an act of "mercy" exercised by the executive as a matter of discretion, and is not subject to the same due process requirements as the judicial process.

The Court has consistently recognized that clemency decisions are not subject to judicial review. In United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871), the Court stated that the President's power to grant reprieves and pardons is "unlimited" and "cannot be controlled or modified by Congress." Similarly, in Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 603-04 (1896), the Court held that a pardon is a "private" act of the executive that is not subject to due process requirements. This principle is further supported by United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. 150, 161 (1833), where the Court stated that a pardon is "an act of grace" that is separate from the judicial process.

Moreover, the creation of a clemency procedure by the state does not create a protected liberty interest. As established in Murray v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. 272, 277 (1856), a statutory procedure does not necessarily create a constitutional right. The Court has also held that the executive's power to grant clemency is a constitutional duty that is not subject to judicial oversight, as seen in Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. 307, 311 (1856).

The respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is also without merit. The Ohio clemency procedure does not compel the respondent to waive his Fifth Amendment rights. As the Court held in Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 111-12 (1908), the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated when a person is not compelled to testify against themselves. In this case, the respondent is not required to participate in the clemency interview, and he is free to assert his Fifth Amendment privilege if he chooses to participate.

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals' remand for further proceedings is premature and unnecessary. As the Court held in Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496, 500-01 (1941), a federal court should not intervene in a state proceeding unless there is a clear constitutional violation. In this case, there is no such violation, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims was proper.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. Therefore, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims should be reinstated.
