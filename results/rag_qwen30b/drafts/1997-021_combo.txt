**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is neither rational nor consistent with the National Labor Relations Act. The Board’s rule, which permits polling only when an employer has “objective indications” raising a “reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status, renders the practice functionally obsolete and contradicts the statutory purpose of preserving stable collective-bargaining relationships. This Court has long recognized that the Board’s interpretation of the Act must be upheld when it is “reasonable and consistent with the statute,” *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37 (1987), but the Board’s current standard fails that test. It is arbitrary, capricious, and inconsistent with precedent.

The Board’s standard is irrational because it creates a paradox: an employer may not poll unless it already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, yet the sole purpose of polling is to *obtain* such evidence. As the Board itself acknowledged in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” and thus should be used sparingly. But if the employer must already possess the evidence needed to justify withdrawal, then polling serves no purpose beyond a formality. The Board’s own logic undermines its own policy. As this Court held in *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 180 (1967), “the Board’s interpretation of the Act must be given controlling weight unless it is plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the statute.” The Board’s standard is plainly inconsistent with the Act’s goal of minimizing disruption to labor relations.

The Board’s standard also conflicts with the principle that an employer may not be forced to bargain with a union that no longer enjoys majority support. *Smith v. Evening News Assn.*, 371 U.S. 195, 200 (1962), recognized that “the Board’s authority to deal with unfair labor practices is not exclusive,” and that courts may adjudicate disputes involving labor relations so long as they do not interfere with the Board’s primary jurisdiction. The Board’s rule, however, effectively prevents employers from testing union support at all unless they meet a standard so high that it is nearly impossible to satisfy. This forces employers into a Hobson’s choice: either bargain with a union they believe no longer represents a majority, or risk violating § 8(a)(5) by refusing to bargain. The Board’s standard thus creates a situation where the employer is compelled to act in bad faith or risk an unfair labor practice.

The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked “reasonable doubt” is not supported by substantial evidence. The ALJ and the Board rejected the statements of seven employees who expressed opposition to the union, including Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack, on the ground that they were not in the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991—the date of the company’s letter refusing recognition. But the Board has long held that the relevant date for assessing an employer’s doubt is the date of the decision to poll, not the date of the letter. *Brotherhood of Railway & Steamship Clerks, Freight Handlers, Express & Station Employees v. Association for the Benefit of Non-Contract Employees*, 380 U.S. 650, 657 (1965), established that “the Board’s findings of fact are conclusive if supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole.” The record shows that at least seven employees had expressed opposition to the union before January 25, and the ALJ found that these statements were “substantial, objective evidence” of a loss of support. The Board’s refusal to count them is not supported by the record.

Moreover, the Board discounted the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, who expressed dissatisfaction with the union during job interviews. But *La Crosse Telephone Corp. v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Board*, 336 U.S. 18, 22 (1949), held that “the Board may consider the conduct of employees in determining whether a union has lost its majority support.” The fact that Ridgick and Marsh were told the company would be non-union does not render their statements inadmissible. *United Mine Workers of America v. Arkansas Oak Flooring Co.*, 351 U.S. 62, 67 (1956), affirmed that “the Board may consider the views of employees as expressed in their conduct and statements.” The Board’s rule that such statements are “unlikely to be sincere” is not supported by precedent and is contrary to the principle that employee sentiment must be evaluated on its own terms.

The Board also rejected the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr, who reported that the night shift and the service department, respectively, did not want the union. The ALJ found these statements “unverified,” but *McLeod v. General Electric Co.*, 385 U.S. 533, 534 (1967), held that “the Board’s findings of fact are conclusive if supported by substantial evidence.” The record contains no evidence that Bloch or Mohr were lying, and their statements were made in the context of a legitimate inquiry into employee sentiment. The Board’s refusal to credit them is not supported by the record.

Finally, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with the principle that an employer may not be forced to bargain with a union that no longer represents a majority. *Boire v. Greyhound Corp.*, 376 U.S. 473, 477 (1964), held that “the Board’s order may be enforced by a court of appeals.” The Board’s refusal to allow polling in this case, despite overwhelming evidence of a loss of union support, is not supported by substantial evidence and is contrary to the Act’s purpose of promoting stable labor relations.

For these reasons, the Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard is arbitrary and capricious, and its factual determination is not supported by substantial evidence. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
