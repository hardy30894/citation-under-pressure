Under longstanding conspiracy doctrine, withdrawal before the limitations period is not an element the government must disprove in every case; it is an affirmative defense that the defendant must prove. This Court held in Smith v. United States, 568 U.S. 106, 113-14 (2013), that “withdrawal is an affirmative defense” and that “the burden of proving withdrawal rests on the defendant.” The Court reasoned that the government bears the burden to prove the defendant joined the conspiracy and that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, but once membership is shown, a defendant who claims he withdrew must come forward with proof of affirmative acts inconsistent with the object of the conspiracy. Id. at 110-14.

That allocation is consistent with the structure of conspiracy law. A conspiracy is complete upon agreement and an overt act where required; after that point, a conspirator remains liable for the conspiracy’s continuing existence and acts of co-conspirators until he affirmatively withdraws. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. 270, 274-75 (2003). Because withdrawal “terminates the defendant’s responsibility for postwithdrawal acts of his co-conspirators,” Smith, 568 U.S. at 110, it is properly treated as an excuse or avoidance defense, not as an element of the offense itself. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 206-10 (1977).

The Due Process Clause does not require the government to disprove affirmative defenses beyond a reasonable doubt unless the defense negates an element of the crime. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of each element, but Patterson makes clear that legislatures may place on the defendant the burden of persuasion on matters that do not negate an element. 432 U.S. at 206-07, 210. Withdrawal does not negate the defendant’s initial agreement to join the conspiracy; it only cuts off future liability. Smith, 568 U.S. at 113-14.

The statute of limitations does not alter that rule. The government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member during that time unless he withdrew earlier. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957). But once the prosecution establishes the conspiratorial agreement and the conspiracy’s existence within the limitations window, the defendant who claims he departed must prove withdrawal. Smith, 568 U.S. at 111-14. Otherwise, every limitations defense would collapse into an unworkable requirement that the government anticipate and negate any possible withdrawal evidence in the case.

Nor is there any constitutional problem in requiring the defendant to shoulder that burden. This Court has repeatedly upheld burden shifting on defenses that are peculiarly within the defendant’s knowledge. Patterson, 432 U.S. at 207-10; Dixon v. United States, 548 U.S. 1, 7-8 (2006). Withdrawal fits that model. It depends on the defendant’s own acts—communication to co-conspirators or disclosure to law enforcement—and those facts are ordinarily more available to the defendant than to the government. Smith, 568 U.S. at 110-11.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would also unsettle the settled presumption that a conspiracy continues until its objectives are achieved or abandoned. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910). The government need not prove the negative of every possible exit from the conspiracy. Once the prosecution proves membership and continuing activity during the charged period, the defendant may avoid liability for later acts only by proving withdrawal. Smith, 568 U.S. at 113-14.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
