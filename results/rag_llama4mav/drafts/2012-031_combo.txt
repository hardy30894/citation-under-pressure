The District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. To certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3), a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" to ensure that the requirements of Rule 23 are met, including the predominance of common questions of law or fact. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 318 (citing Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc., 259 F.3d 154, 166 (3d Cir. 2001)). This analysis requires a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations, which may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits. Id. at 317.

The District Court's certification decision was flawed because it failed to apply the correct legal standard in determining whether the class could establish antitrust impact and damages through common evidence. The court's finding that the class could establish antitrust impact through a single theory - that Comcast's clustering conduct deterred overbuilder competition - is not supported by the evidence. The relevant geographic market is a critical component in determining antitrust impact, and the District Court's acceptance of the Philadelphia DMA as the relevant geographic market without proper analysis was error. See United States v. Radio Corp. of America, 358 U.S. 334 (1959); United States v. First Nat. Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington, 376 U.S. 665 (1964).

In an antitrust case, the plaintiff must define the relevant market, which includes both the product market and the geographic market. Brown Shoe Co. v. United States, 370 U.S. 294, 324-325 (1962). The District Court's failure to rigorously analyze the evidence on this issue was clear error. See Seaboard Air Line Railroad Co. v. United States, 382 U.S. 154 (1965); Federal Maritime Commission v. Aktiebolaget Svenska Amerika Linien, 390 U.S. 238 (1968).

Furthermore, the District Court's acceptance of Dr. McClave's damages model without proper scrutiny was also error. The model must be grounded in admissible evidence and must be capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis. See Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe MacHinery Corp., 392 U.S. 481 (1968); Kiefer-Stewart Co. v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc., 340 U.S. 211 (1951). The District Court's failure to ensure that the damages model met these requirements was a clear error of law.

The Court of Appeals' affirmance of the District Court's certification decision was also error. The appellate court should have reviewed the District Court's decision de novo to ensure that the correct legal standard was applied. See Federal Communications Commission v. RCA Communications, Inc., 346 U.S. 86 (1953); White Motor Co. v. United States, 372 U.S. 253 (1963). The Court of Appeals' failure to do so was a misapplication of the law to the facts.

In conclusion, the District Court's certification decision was flawed due to its failure to apply the correct legal standard in determining whether the class could establish antitrust impact and damages through common evidence. The Court of Appeals' affirmance of this decision was also error. This Court should reverse the certification decision and remand the case for further proceedings. Leh v. General Petroleum Corp., 382 U.S. 54 (1965); United States v. Southwestern Cable Co., 392 U.S. 157 (1968).
