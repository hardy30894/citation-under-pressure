ARGUMENT

The award of an infringer’s entire profits on a product containing an infringing component contravenes the settled rule that a patentee must prove the portion of profits attributable to the patented feature. Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884) (“The burden of proof is upon the complainant, and … he must distinguish between the profits derived from the invention and those derived from other sources.”). This Court has long required apportionment in patent cases. Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1853); Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885); Tilghman v. Proctor, 125 U.S. 136, 145-46 (1888).

When the patented invention is but one element of a larger article, recovery is confined to the incremental value added by that element. McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co., 141 U.S. 459, 463-64 (1891); Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912). The same principle governs design patents. Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646 (1915). Allowing recovery of total profits on the finished article would confer a windfall unrelated to the patented design. Birdsall v. Coolidge, 93 U.S. 64, 69 (1876).

Equity likewise demands separation of the infringer’s gains traceable to the design from those traceable to other features. Keystone Mfg. Co. v. Adams, 151 U.S. 139, 147 (1894). The rule prevents the patent from operating as a tax on unpatented elements. Suffolk Mfg. Co. v. Hayden, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 315, 319 (1865). Because the record contains no evidence apportioning profits to the patented component alone, the judgment cannot stand.
