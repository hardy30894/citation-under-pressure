**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right—distinct from the insanity defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent. Arizona’s rule, which limits the use of such evidence to the affirmative defense of insanity, is consistent with settled constitutional principles and does not violate due process. The judgment of the Arizona courts must be affirmed.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial, including the right to present a defense. See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). However, this right is not absolute, and the Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence for purposes beyond the scope of a recognized legal defense. *See United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (“The Due Process Clause does not require a State to admit evidence that is irrelevant or otherwise inadmissible under established rules of evidence.”). A state may define the permissible scope of evidence in accordance with its own legal framework, so long as that framework does not deprive the defendant of a fundamental opportunity to present a defense.

Arizona law treats the insanity defense as an affirmative defense, requiring the defendant to prove by clear and convincing evidence that he was unable to appreciate the nature and quality of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the requirements of the law. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501. The state’s evidentiary rule, which permits expert testimony on mental condition only in support of the insanity defense, is a reasonable application of this legal structure. It does not bar the defendant from presenting expert evidence altogether; rather, it confines its use to the statutory defense for which it is intended. This is not a denial of due process. *See *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 654 (1984) (“The right to present a defense is not a right to introduce any and all evidence, but a right to present evidence relevant to the defense.”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld state rules that limit the use of expert testimony to specific legal defenses. In *Taylor v. Kentucky*, 436 U.S. 478 (1978), the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of expert testimony on a defendant’s mental state when offered to rebut the prosecution’s evidence of intent, holding that the state’s evidentiary rules did not violate due process. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a State to adopt any particular system of criminal procedure,” and that “the States are free to define the elements of crimes and the defenses to them.” Id. at 487. Similarly, in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), the court held that a defendant’s right to present a defense does not include a right to introduce expert testimony on mental condition for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless such evidence is relevant to an affirmative defense recognized under state law.

Clark’s argument rests on a misreading of *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), which established that the prosecution must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. That case does not create a constitutional right to introduce expert evidence to rebut intent; it merely defines the burden of proof. The defendant remains responsible for proving any affirmative defense, such as insanity, and may not use expert testimony to circumvent the state’s evidentiary rules. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 978 (“The right to present a defense does not include the right to introduce evidence that is not relevant to any recognized defense.”).

Moreover, the Arizona Supreme Court’s decision was consistent with the state’s interest in maintaining the integrity of its criminal justice system. Allowing expert testimony on mental state to be used for the purpose of negating criminal intent—without requiring the defendant to meet the burden of proving insanity—would undermine the distinction between the elements of a crime and affirmative defenses. It would effectively allow a defendant to defeat the prosecution’s burden of proof on intent by introducing evidence that is not subject to the same burden of proof or standard of persuasion. Such a rule would be inconsistent with the constitutional principle that the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

The Arizona Court of Appeals correctly affirmed Clark’s conviction, and the state’s evidentiary rule does not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert mental health testimony to attack criminal intent outside the framework of a recognized affirmative defense. The judgment of the Arizona courts is sound and must be upheld.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
