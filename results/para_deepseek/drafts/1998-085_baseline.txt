ARGUMENT

The text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), read against the common-law punitive-damages tradition, requires proof that the employer’s discriminatory practice was egregious or outrageous. A bare showing of intentional discrimination—even with an impermissible state of mind—does not by itself justify converting a Title VII remedial verdict into a punitive one.

**I. The Common-Law Standard That Congress Borrowed Includes an Outrageousness Requirement.**

Punitive damages are not compensation for ordinary intentional wrongdoing. At common law, exemplary damages were reserved for conduct that is willful, wanton, or oppressive. *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. Co. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858) (awarding punitive damages where the act is done “wilfully, or is the result of that reckless indifference to the rights of others which is equivalent to an intentional violation of them”). This Court has explained that punitive damages are “awarded against a person to punish him for his outrageous conduct” and that they may lie only where the defendant has acted “outrageously, because of the defendant’s evil motive or reckless indifference to the rights of others.” *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 41 n.8, 42 (1983) (quoting Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908). Thus, at common law, “malice” and “reckless indifference” are not free-floating mental states. They describe a degree of culpability that must be demonstrated through the outrageous character of the defendant’s conduct. Punitive damages are not favored, and they are reserved for especially blameworthy misconduct. *International Bhd. of Elec. Workers v. Foust*, 442 U.S. 42, 48 (1979).

**II. Section 1981a(b)(1) Adopts That Common-Law Standard.**

When Congress authorized punitive damages under Title VII in the Civil Rights Act of 1991, it used language with a settled common-law meaning: a discriminatory practice committed “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Congress is presumed to adopt the accepted meaning of words of art borrowed from the common law. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955). That accepted meaning is not satisfied by the intent necessary to establish a Title VII violation itself. Otherwise, “malice” and “reckless indifference” would add nothing to a statute that already requires proof of intentional discrimination, and every successfully proved Title VII case would automatically qualify for punitive damages. We are obliged to avoid that surplusage. *Menasche*, 348 U.S. at 538–39.

The legislative history confirms Congress’s intent. An interpretive memorandum on which Congress relied stated that punitive damages should be available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). The House Report likewise described § 1981a as imposing “an even higher standard” than that required for liability. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I) (1991). These contemporaneous materials are not isolated remarks; they reflect the common-law backdrop that Congress was incorporating. A plaintiff who proves only that an employer intentionally discriminated has proved liability, not outrageousness. The latter is a separate and demanding inquiry.

**III. The Egregiousness Inquiry Is Objective and Independent of the Employer’s State of Mind.**

The statutory question is whether the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice” with malice or reckless indifference. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That question is answered objectively by examining the nature and circumstances of the discriminatory practice: whether the employer departed from its own procedures, whether it gave pretextual or shifting explanations, whether the adverse action was severe, and whether the employer’s conduct manifested a high risk of violating clearly established federal rights. The D.C. Circuit correctly held that a plaintiff must show “egregious” or “outrageous” discrimination—conduct sufficiently reprehensible to warrant punishment and deterrence—not merely an intentional act plus a discriminatory state of mind. *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 958, 964, 966 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc).

Petitioner’s approach would collapse the distinction between compensatory and punitive remedies. Title VII liability already requires proof that sex was a motivating factor. If that intent, without more, satisfied § 1981a(b)(1), then every intentional discriminator would be exposed to punitive damages, and the statutory phrase “with malice or with reckless indifference” would be largely meaningless. *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 964. That cannot be the law. The egregiousness of the employer’s conduct is therefore an independent requirement, even though the employer’s state of mind may be relevant circumstantial evidence bearing on it.

**IV. *Smith v. Wade* Does Not Compel a Different Result.**

Petitioner relies heavily on *Smith v. Wade*, but that case interpreted 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and its own remedial history. *Smith*, 461 U.S. at 51–52. *Smith* did not involve Title VII or the carefully calibrated remedial scheme Congress enacted in 1991, complete with caps, legislative history, and a separate punitive-damages standard. At most, *Smith* confirms that actual evil motive is not required and that reckless indifference may suffice. It does not hold that every intentional wrongdoer is automatically subject to punitive damages. Indeed, *Smith* itself quoted the Restatement’s “outrageous conduct” formulation. *Id.* at 41 n.8, 42.

Here, the jury found intentional discrimination, but the evidence—involving a preselection and explanations that the jury could disbelieve—did not compel the conclusion that respondent’s conduct was so outrageous or egregious as to warrant punishment beyond the back-pay award. The district court therefore correctly denied punitive damages, and the court of appeals’ contrary holding should be reversed.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
