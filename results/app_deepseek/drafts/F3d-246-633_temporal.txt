## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot’s RLA Conspiracy Claim

Summary judgment is a drastic remedy that must be denied where, as here, the record reveals genuine factual disputes. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). All inferences must be drawn in favor of the non-moving party. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). The district court disregarded this principle.

The evidence—including the tape recording of the executive session—shows that UTU Vice-Chairman Earley actively argued against Smoot’s claim while purporting to represent him. This recording directly undercuts the union’s and CSX’s assertions that the PLB proceeding was fair. A union’s duty of fair representation under the Railway Labor Act requires it to act in complete good faith and without hostility. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 202–03 (1944). Yet Earley’s conduct, as captured on tape, demonstrates collusion with CSX and a breach of that duty. The close timing of the internal charges against Earley and the filing of this lawsuit further suggests a coordinated effort to stifle Smoot’s claims. *See Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 45 (1957) (holding that the RLA provides a remedy for union breaches of duty). Summary judgment was improper because a jury could reasonably infer that UTU and CSX conspired to deprive Smoot of his right to fair representation.

### II. The Wiretap Act Damages Award Must Be Reversed

The district court’s award of $436,308.74 in damages, fees, and costs is unsupported by the record and contrary to law.

**First**, Smoot’s recording of the executive session did not constitute an “interception” under 18 U.S.C. § 2510 *et seq.* The Act was designed to target clandestine eavesdropping, not the preservation of evidence by a participant in a quasi-judicial proceeding. *See Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 382 (1937) (recognizing limits on wiretap liability). Moreover, no pre-1970 case extends the Act to such circumstances. The executive session was an open meeting where Smoot’s tape recorders were left in plain sight; the PLB members simply failed to notice them. There was no surreptitious installation of a hidden device.

**Second**, the award of $250,000 in statutory damages is excessive and punitive in nature, unsupported by any evidence of actual harm. The Act authorizes statutory damages, but such awards must bear a rational relationship to the alleged violation. *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 565 (1886) (“[D]amages must be proved, not presumed.”). Here, no evidence showed that Smoot acted for commercial gain or with malicious intent. To the contrary, he recorded the session to expose what he believed was a denial of his rights.

**Third**, the punitive damages award of $100,000 violates due process. Punitive damages may not be grossly excessive or disproportionate to the gravity of the misconduct. *St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. Williams*, 251 U.S. 63, 67 (1919). Smoot’s conduct—recording a meeting in which he believed he was being wronged—falls far short of the flagrant, willful wrongdoing that justifies punitive sanctions. *Day v. Woodworth*, 13 How. 363, 371 (1852) (punitive damages require malice or wanton disregard). The district court made no finding that Smoot acted with such culpability.

**Finally**, the attorneys’ fees award of $86,308.74 was based on a flawed calculation and failed to account for the lack of any substantive violation. *See Missouri Pacific R. Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 525 (1885) (fees must be reasonable and proportionate). Without an underlying violation, no fees are recoverable.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse summary judgment on the RLA claim and vacate the Wiretap Act damages award.
