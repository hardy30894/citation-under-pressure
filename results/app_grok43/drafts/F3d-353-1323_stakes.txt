ARGUMENT

The district court erred in concluding that the commercial-activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), does not confer subject-matter jurisdiction. This Court reviews de novo a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act. See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 610 (1992).

I. Pakistan’s conduct constitutes commercial activity.

Section 1605(a)(2) withdraws immunity when the action is “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state.” The statutory phrase “commercial activity” turns on “whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in ‘trade and traffic or commerce.’” Saudi Arabia v. Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (quoting Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614). A foreign state acts commercially when it “acts, not as regulator of a market, but in the manner of a private player within it.” Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614.

Here the Government of Punjab dispatched a representative to the United States who offered Beg an alternative parcel in exchange for relinquishing his claim to the expropriated land. That transaction is indistinguishable from an ordinary private land exchange. The subsequent refusal of the Supreme Court of Pakistan to recognize title to the substitute parcel does not convert the antecedent offer into a sovereign act; the offer itself remains the type of bargain a private party would strike. Because the claim is “based upon” that commercial activity, the first clause of § 1605(a)(2) is satisfied.

II. The commercial activity caused a direct effect in the United States.

The same clause requires that the commercial activity have “an effect in the United States” that is “direct.” Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618. An effect is direct if it follows “as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s” activity. Id. The representative’s offer occurred on American soil and was communicated directly to Beg, who resides in the United States. Acceptance or rejection of the offer therefore produced an immediate consequence felt in this country. That consequence satisfies the direct-effect requirement.

III. The expropriation exception is not the exclusive avenue for relief.

Although 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(3) addresses certain expropriations, nothing in the statute or in Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 461 U.S. 480 (1983), precludes a plaintiff from invoking the commercial-activity exception when the foreign state’s later conduct is itself commercial. The district court therefore should have exercised jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2).

Conclusion

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
