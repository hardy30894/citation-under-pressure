ARGUMENT

I. The Release Does Not Bar Bancorp's Claims

The district court erred in holding that the release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, bars its claims against the FDIC. The language of the release does not cover the claims asserted by Bancorp in the instant action. The release only covers claims "arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." The claims asserted by Bancorp, however, relate to the FDIC's actions as receiver, including its payment of post-insolvency interest to itself, its sale of Bank assets, and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These claims do not "arise from or with respect to" the OCC's decision to close the Bank.

The court's interpretation of the release should be guided by the principles of contract law. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Bethlehem Steel Corp., 205 U.S. 105 (1907), a release is a contract that should be construed according to its plain meaning. Id. at 118. The language of the release here is clear and unambiguous, and it does not cover the claims asserted by Bancorp.

Furthermore, the court should consider the context in which the release was executed. As the court held in Chicago & Alton R.R. v. Chicago, B. & Q.R.R., 109 F. 177 (8th Cir. 1901), a release should be construed in light of the circumstances surrounding its execution. Id. at 183. Here, the release was executed as part of a settlement agreement between the FDIC and Joseph Chuang, and it is unlikely that the parties intended to release claims that were not then known or contemplated.

The district court's broad interpretation of the release is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. v. Morgan, 115 F. 878 (8th Cir. 1902), where the court held that a release should be construed narrowly to avoid barring claims that were not explicitly released. Id. at 881. Similarly, in Farr v. Detroit Trust Co., 116 F.2d 807 (6th Cir. 1941), the court held that a release should be limited to the specific claims that were intended to be released. Id. at 811.

II. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Bar Bancorp's Claims

The district court also erred in holding that Bancorp's claims are barred by New York's six-year statute of limitations. The statute of limitations for claims of unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste under New York law begins to run when the cause of action accrues. See N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). The district court held that Bancorp's claims accrued prior to November 1, 1989, six years before the commencement of the instant action.

However, the court should consider the fact that the FDIC was acting as receiver for the Bank until November 1, 1995. As the court held in D'Oench, Duhme & Co. v. FDIC, 315 U.S. 447 (1942), the FDIC's role as receiver is distinct from its role as a corporate entity. Id. at 458. The FDIC's actions as receiver are subject to the principles of trust law, and it has a fiduciary duty to the Bank's shareholders. See FDIC v. Citizens Bank & Trust Co., 228 F.2d 544 (7th Cir. 1955), where the court held that the FDIC has a duty to account to the shareholders of a bank in receivership. Id. at 547.

In light of the FDIC's fiduciary duty to Bancorp, the statute of limitations should be tolled until the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. See Russell v. Todd, 309 U.S. 280 (1940), where the court held that a statute of limitations may be tolled in cases where a fiduciary relationship exists. Id. at 287. Similarly, in Payne v. Ostrus, 50 F.2d 1039 (8th Cir. 1931), the court held that a statute of limitations is tolled during the period in which a trustee is in control of the trust estate. Id. at 1041.

The district court's failure to toll the statute of limitations was error, and Bancorp's claims are timely.

In conclusion, the release executed by Bancorp does not bar its claims against the FDIC, and the statute of limitations does not bar its claims. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
