**Argument**

The Eighth Amendment does not prohibit capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders. The text forbids only punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” and this Court has long held that the Constitution leaves to democratic judgment the definition of punishments within that category unless they transgress a settled principle of decency. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135–36 (1878); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 378 (1910). That inquiry has never turned on a judicially created rule excluding a class of offenders absent the most exacting historical and constitutional justification. See *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100–01 (1958); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962).

This Court’s precedents recognize that capital punishment itself is constitutionally permissible. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 134–36 (1878); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947). The question therefore is not whether death is categorically cruel and unusual, but whether the Constitution bars its application to a particular class of offenders. The answer under the historical understanding of the Eighth Amendment is no.

The controlling framework is whether there is a settled national consensus against the challenged punishment. In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100–01 (1958), the Court described the Eighth Amendment as reflecting “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” But that formulation does not authorize courts to displace legislative judgments absent an objective showing that the challenged practice is no longer tolerated. The Court has repeatedly looked to history, practice, and legislative action in applying the Amendment. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 373–82 (1910); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666–67 (1962); *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 531–34 (1968).

No such consensus existed when Virginia sentenced Atkins. To the contrary, states had continued to authorize capital punishment for mentally retarded defendants, and this Court had already rejected the contention that the Eighth Amendment categorically barred such executions. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989), the Court held that “executing the mentally retarded does not offend the Eighth Amendment in all cases,” because there was no national consensus and because “the mentally retarded do not have the same capacities to understand and process information, to communicate, to abstract from mistakes and learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, to control impulses, and to understand the reactions of others” did not compel a constitutional exemption. *Id.* at 340. That reasoning controls here. Even if a defendant’s mental retardation is a mitigating circumstance, the Constitution does not strip the sentencer of authority to impose death. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604–05 (1978), by its own terms addresses mitigation, not categorical immunity.

The Court’s Eighth Amendment cases also distinguish between individualized sentencing and categorical constitutional bars. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), and *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), reflect the modern requirement that capital punishment be administered rationally and non-arbitrarily, but they did not announce a prohibition on executing mentally retarded offenders. Because petitioner asks for a categorical exemption, he must satisfy the Court’s longstanding insistence on clear constitutional command or overwhelming objective evidence of evolving standards. *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 100–01; *Powell*, 392 U.S. at 532–34. He cannot.

Nor does the nature of mental retardation itself compel a constitutional ban. This Court has recognized that diminished mental capacity may affect culpability, but it has never equated intellectual disability with legal incompetence to be punished. See *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 484–85 (1895); *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (capacity concepts are context-specific); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 378–86 (1966) (competency to stand trial is distinct from punishment). The Constitution already requires that a defendant receive the full benefit of mitigation evidence and an individualized sentencing determination. *Lockett*, 438 U.S. at 604–05. That requirement was satisfied here.

Finally, the Court should be wary of converting the Eighth Amendment into a roving commission to second-guess state penal policy. The Amendment prohibits punishments that are barbaric by settled legal standards, not punishments that a later generation might prefer to reserve. *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 373–82; *Resweber*, 329 U.S. at 463–64; *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 100–01. Virginia’s decision to leave the judgment to the jury accords with the constitutional tradition of individualized capital sentencing. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247–52 (1949). Because the Constitution, as understood in the controlling precedents, does not forbid the execution of mentally retarded persons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
