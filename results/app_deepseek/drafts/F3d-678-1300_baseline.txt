## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Denying Judgment as a Matter of Law Because No Substantial Evidence Supports the Jury’s Finding of Invalidity Under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b)

JMOL is reviewed de novo, and the verdict must be supported by substantial evidence. *In re Google Tech. Holdings LLC*, 980 F.3d 858, 863 (Fed. Cir. 2020). The on-sale bar under § 102(b) requires proof by clear and convincing evidence that (1) the invention was the subject of a commercial offer for sale, and (2) the invention was ready for patenting before the critical date. *Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998). Facebook failed to satisfy the second prong.

**A. The Invention Was Not Ready for Patenting Before December 10, 2002**

Ready for patenting requires either actual reduction to practice or a sufficiently specific enabling disclosure. *Pfaff*, 525 U.S. at 67–68. The record shows that the Leader2Leader® product was still under development in early 2002. Leader’s founder testified that the project took “a couple of years . . . maybe three,” placing completion in the “2002ish time frame.” J.A. 24829. The white paper’s statement that the platform was “operational now with low user volumes,” J.A. 27207, does not establish that the claimed invention—the “underlying engine” with dynamic metadata association—was actually reduced to practice. A product that is merely “operational” for limited testing is not necessarily a completed invention. *See In re Cygnus Telecomms. Tech., LLC*, 536 F.3d 1343, 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (holding that a prototype that did not embody all claim limitations was not a reduction to practice). Here, the white paper described a system that aggregated communications, but the asserted claims require dynamic metadata association and tracking of user movement—features not shown to be operational in January 2002. No witness testified that the Digital Leaderboard® engine performed all claimed functions before the critical date. Thus, no substantial evidence supports a finding of actual reduction to practice.

Nor was there an enabling disclosure. The white paper attached a sample “Big Board” and mentioned a “Browser,” J.A. 27210, but it did not describe the metadata tagging or dynamic updating required by claim 9. A vague description of a system’s goals does not constitute an enabling disclosure. *See Pfaff*, 525 U.S. at 68 (requiring “a description that would enable a person skilled in the art to make the invention”). Because Facebook failed to prove readiness, the on-sale bar cannot apply.

**B. The Alleged Public Use Was Not a “Public Use” Under § 102(b)**

Public use requires that the invention be used in public and be accessible to the public. *Invitrogen Corp. v. Biocrest Mfg., L.P.*, 424 F.3d 1374, 1380 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Leader’s demonstrations to a few companies and the Wright Patterson Air Force Base were limited, non-enabling presentations. The white paper was a confidential offer, not a public disclosure. There is no evidence that any third party could practice the invention from those demonstrations. *See Bernhardt, LLC v. Collezione Europa USA, Inc.*, 386 F.3d 1371, 1379 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (holding that a single, non-enabling demonstration to a potential customer is not a public use). The jury’s contrary finding lacks substantial evidence.

### II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying a New Trial Because the Verdict Was Against the Clear Weight of the Evidence

A new trial is warranted when the verdict is against the great weight of the evidence. *Unisplay, S.A. v. Am. Elec. Sign Co.*, 69 F.3d 512, 517 (Fed. Cir. 1995). The evidence of invalidity was weak: the white paper was aspirational, the product was incomplete, and the witnesses gave equivocal testimony. The jury’s verdict cannot stand.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and hold the asserted claims not invalid, or alternatively remand for a new trial.
