ARGUMENT

The Qualifications Clauses of Article I fix the exclusive criteria for membership in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; art. I, § 3, cl. 3. A state may not enlarge them. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (the Constitution “fix[es]” the qualifications and “a House resolution” cannot add to them). The same principle bars state constitutional amendments that condition eligibility on prior service. 

The text itself demonstrates exclusivity. “No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. Parallel language governs senators. Id. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These provisions “are mandatory” and “exclusive.” Powell, 395 U.S. at 522. 

Uniformity among the states follows from the national character of the offices. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819) (“The government of the Union…is supreme.”). Allowing Arkansas to impose term limits would permit “a hodge-podge of qualifications” that the Framers rejected. Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1964) (Congressional districts must be equal because “the voice of every voter” must carry equal weight “in the national legislature”). 

History confirms the design. The Convention considered and defeated proposals to add property and religious qualifications. 2 The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787, at 248-51 (M. Farrand ed. 1911). The ratifying debates likewise treated the listed qualifications as complete. The Federalist No. 52, at 326 (J. Madison) (C. Rossiter ed. 1961). 

Precedent applying these principles is consistent. Ex parte Yarbrough, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884) (states may not “add to the qualifications” prescribed by federal law for federal elections); United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941) (federal power over congressional elections is “paramount”); Smith v. Allwright, 321 U.S. 649, 657 (1944) (state regulation of federal elections must yield to the Constitution); Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 217 (1962) (federal courts enforce “well developed and familiar” standards protecting national electoral rights); Gray v. Sanders, 372 U.S. 368, 380 (1963) (“one person, one vote” applies to federal elections); Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 562 (1964) (state laws cannot dilute the federal franchise). 

Amendment 73 therefore violates the Qualifications Clauses by adding an extra-constitutional eligibility requirement. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
