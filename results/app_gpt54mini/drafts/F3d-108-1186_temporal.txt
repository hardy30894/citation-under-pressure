**Argument**

The district court erred in refusing to clarify the jury’s ambiguous verdict before the jury was discharged. A trial court has a duty to ensure that a verdict is certain and intelligible before it is accepted. *McDonnell v. United States*, 288 U.S. 420, 431 (1933) (“It is the duty of the court to see that the verdict is certain, unqualified, and definite.”). That duty is especially important where, as here, the jury returned separate findings that can plausibly be read in more than one way and those readings produce materially different judgments.

Here, the jury found for Unit on liability and awarded $70,768.02 on Form 1, while separately awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3. Unit promptly asked the court to question the jury before discharge to determine whether the jury intended the Form 1 amount to be the gross damages and the Form 3 amount to be the offset, or whether the Form 1 amount already reflected the offset. The court refused. That refusal was error because “where the verdict is ambiguous or inconsistent, and the jury has not been discharged, the trial judge should send the jury back for further deliberation or ask for clarification.” *Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474, 486 (1935); see also *Baltimore & Carolina Line, Inc. v. Redman*, 295 U.S. 654, 660 (1935) (trial court may return jury for further consideration where verdict is incomplete or uncertain).

The ambiguity here was not academic. If the jury intended Form 1 to reflect Unit’s net recovery after subtracting Enron’s pipe-repair costs, then the separate Form 3 award duplicated the same loss. If, instead, the jury intended Form 1 to reflect Unit’s full invoice amount and Form 3 to reflect Enron’s distinct set-off, then the district court’s subtraction of Form 3 from Form 1 improperly reduced Unit’s recovery to zero. Either way, the verdict could not be entered as a final judgment without clarification. A verdict must be interpreted, if possible, in a manner that gives effect to the jury’s apparent intention, but not by substituting the court’s own assumption for the jury’s meaning. *McDonald v. Pless*, 238 U.S. 264, 267 (1915); *Phoenix Insurance Co. v. Doster*, 106 U.S. 30, 36 (1882).

The court’s refusal to clarify the verdict also compels reversal because it eliminated Unit’s right to the benefit of the jury’s actual determination. A judgment must conform to the verdict as rendered, not to the court’s reconstruction of what the verdict might have meant. *United States v. Brewer*, 139 U.S. 278, 289 (1891) (court may not alter the substance of a verdict under the guise of interpretation). The district court did precisely that when it treated the separate Form 1 and Form 3 findings as though the jury had already performed a single netting calculation, then entered judgment for Enron notwithstanding a liability finding for Unit.

Enron will likely argue that Unit waived any complaint by not objecting more specifically to the instructions. That contention fails for two reasons. First, Unit preserved the core problem: it objected that the verdict forms were “extremely difficult to understand,” and it sought clarification before discharge. Under the settled rule, a timely objection directed to the confusing verdict structure is enough to preserve the issue where the defect becomes apparent only after the jury’s return. *Stewart v. Wyoming Cattle Ranche Co.*, 128 U.S. 383, 388 (1888). Second, even apart from instruction error, the decisive issue is the court’s refusal to resolve an ambiguous verdict before the jury was discharged. That issue was preserved by Unit’s direct request for clarification at the only time it could be made effective. *Redman*, 295 U.S. at 660.

Nor can the judgment be sustained on the theory that the district court merely “interpreted” the verdict. Courts may interpret a verdict only where the intention of the jury is reasonably clear from the record; they may not speculate where the verdict is susceptible to competing, materially different constructions. *Cook v. United States*, 379 U.S. 645, 652 (1965) (verdict should be read in light of the record, but not rewritten by the court). Here, the verdict forms and instructions created at least two plausible readings, and the court chose the one that extinguished Unit’s recovery entirely. That was error.

Because the ambiguity in the verdict was discovered before discharge and Unit timely requested clarification, the proper remedy was to send the jury back for clarification, not to enter a judgment that may well contradict the jury’s intent. The judgment should therefore be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial or, at minimum, for entry of a judgment consistent with a clarified verdict. *Dimick*, 293 U.S. at 486; *Redman*, 295 U.S. at 660.
