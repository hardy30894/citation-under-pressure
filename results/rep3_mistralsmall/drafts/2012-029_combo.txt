**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit’s Decision in *Air Measurement* Correctly Recognizes That Federal Courts Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims That Require Resolution of Substantial Patent Issues.**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988) (citing 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)). This jurisdiction extends to state-law claims that "necessarily raise a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial." *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The Federal Circuit has correctly applied this principle to legal malpractice claims arising from patent litigation, holding that such claims fall within federal jurisdiction when they require resolution of a substantial patent issue. *See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007); *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281, 1287 (Fed. Cir. 2007).

The Texas Supreme Court erred in declining to follow this precedent. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that federal patent law is a field of national concern requiring uniform application. *See Sola Elec. Co. v. Jefferson Elec. Co.*, 317 U.S. 173, 176 (1942) ("Patents are creatures of federal law and their construction and application are matters for federal courts."); *United States v. Dubilier Condenser Corp.*, 289 U.S. 178, 199 (1933) ("The federal patent system is a carefully considered scheme for promoting invention and encouraging disclosure of advances in the useful arts."). Allowing state courts to adjudicate malpractice claims that hinge on the validity or infringement of patents would undermine this uniformity. *See Deepsouth Packing Co. v. Laitram Corp.*, 406 U.S. 518, 530 (1972) ("The federal patent laws are of such a character as to give rise to a federal right independent of the enforcement of a state-created right.").

**II. The Patent Issue in This Case Is Necessary, Disputed, and Substantial.**

Under *Grable*, federal jurisdiction exists where a state-law claim "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial." 545 U.S. at 314. Here, Minton’s malpractice claim turns entirely on whether his attorneys negligently failed to raise the experimental use exception in the underlying patent litigation. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (explaining the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar). This issue is not merely tangential—it is the core of Minton’s malpractice claim.

The Supreme Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction exists where a state-law claim "would be incomplete without resolution of the federal issue." *Franklin Nat’l Bank of Franklin Square v. New York*, 347 U.S. 373, 378 (1954). Here, Minton cannot prove his malpractice claim without establishing that the experimental use exception would have saved his patent from the on-sale bar. *See Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied) (holding that a legal malpractice plaintiff must prove a "case within a case"). Thus, the patent issue is not only substantial but also necessary to Minton’s claim.

**III. Federal Jurisdiction Will Not Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities.**

The Supreme Court has cautioned that federal jurisdiction should not be extended where it would "disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. However, in patent cases, Congress has already determined that federal courts are the proper forum for resolving patent disputes. *See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)*. The Federal Circuit has correctly held that this balance is not disturbed by allowing federal courts to adjudicate malpractice claims arising from patent litigation, as such claims are ancillary to the federal patent issues. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273; *Immunocept*, 504 F.3d at 1287.

The Texas Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion ignores the Supreme Court’s admonition that federal patent law requires uniform application. *See Sola Elec. Co.*, 317 U.S. at 176. Allowing state courts to adjudicate malpractice claims that hinge on patent issues risks inconsistent rulings and undermines the federal patent system. *See Deepsouth*, 406 U.S. at 530.

**IV. The Federal Circuit’s Precedent Is Binding and Should Be Followed.**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in declining to follow the Federal Circuit’s precedent. The Supreme Court has recognized that the Federal Circuit is the "specialized court of last resort" for patent cases. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 817. While state courts are not bound by the Federal Circuit’s decisions, they must respect its interpretation of federal patent law. *See id.* at 818 ("[T]he Federal Circuit’s interpretation of patent law is entitled to deference.").

The Supreme Court has also held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law, even when the claim is state-created. *See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)*. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Air Measurement* correctly applies this principle, and the Texas Supreme Court should have followed it.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Texas Supreme Court and hold that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim. The judgment below undermines the uniformity of federal patent law and conflicts with controlling Supreme Court precedent.
