**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. See Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986) (summary judgment appropriate only where "there is no genuine issue for trial"). All facts and reasonable inferences must be viewed in the light most favorable to Epps, and summary judgment must be reversed if "there [is] evidence on which the jury could reasonably find for the plaintiff." Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986).

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding That Epps Could Not Establish Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled**

The ADA protects individuals who are "regarded as" having a disability, even where no actual substantially limiting impairment exists. 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(C). An employer satisfies this prong where it "mistakenly believes that a person has a physical impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities" or "mistakenly believes that an actual, nonlimiting impairment substantially limits one or more major life activities." Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). Congress included this prong precisely because "society's accumulated myths and fears about disability and disease are as handicapping as are the physical limitations that flow from actual impairment." School Bd. of Nassau Cty. v. Arline, 480 U.S. 273, 284 (1987).

The record establishes a genuine factual dispute as to Pine Lawn's perception of Epps. Chief Hardy did not simply conclude that Epps could no longer serve as a patrol officer; his own memoranda reflect a belief that Epps could not function in police work generally. Hardy's March 2000 memorandum relied on Dr. Mirkin's note that Epps would need to seek "a different type of occupation" altogether, and concluded that Epps "would never be able to function as a regular officer." That characterization goes beyond a single job assignment and reaches the broader class of law-enforcement positions requiring physical exertion. Under Sutton, an employer's belief that an employee is "precluded from more than one type of job" or "a specialized job" is sufficient to create a jury question on the regarded-as prong. 527 U.S. at 492. This Court has likewise recognized that a plaintiff may proceed on a regarded-as theory where the employer's own explanation for termination reflects a belief that the employee is incapable of performing an entire class of jobs. Wooten v. Farmland Foods, Inc., 58 F.3d 382, 386 (8th Cir. 1995).

Hardy's September 1999 memorandum, prepared before any surgery and before Epps's condition had even stabilized, likewise relied not on any individualized assessment of Epps's actual capabilities but on his volume of sick leave and workers' compensation filings—precisely the kind of generalized, stereotype-driven judgment the ADA's regarded-as prong was designed to redress. See Arline, 480 U.S. at 284. A reasonable jury could find that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in the major life activity of working, not merely disqualified from one position. The district court therefore erred in resolving this disputed factual question on summary judgment rather than submitting it to a jury.

Because the Missouri Human Rights Act's disability discrimination provision, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.055, employs materially identical operative language to the ADA, the same factual dispute defeats summary judgment on Epps's MHRA claim for the same reasons.

**III. The District Court Erred in Holding That Sovereign Immunity Bars Epps's Workers' Compensation Retaliation Claim**

Missouri's sovereign immunity statute, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 537.600, waives immunity only for two categories of common-law tort liability: injuries from the negligent operation of motor vehicles and injuries caused by a dangerous condition of public property. By its terms, the statute governs common-law tort claims against public entities; it says nothing about statutory causes of action created by the legislature outside the common-law tort framework. See State ex rel. Bd. of Trustees of City of North Kansas City Mem'l Hosp. v. Russell, 843 S.W.2d 353, 356–57 (Mo. 1992) (en banc) (describing sovereign immunity's scope as rooted in protection from common-law tort liability absent an applicable waiver).

Epps's claim under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780 is not a common-law tort claim at all; it is a statutory cause of action the legislature created specifically to protect employees who exercise their rights under the workers' compensation law. Because § 287.780 creates an independent statutory remedy distinct from the common-law torts § 537.600 addresses, the sovereign immunity statute's limited waiver provisions have no bearing on whether the claim may proceed. The district court's application of § 537.600 to bar a purely statutory retaliation claim conflated two distinct bodies of law and should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Epps respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment on his ADA and MHRA claims and reverse the dismissal of his § 287.780 retaliation claim, and remand for further proceedings.
