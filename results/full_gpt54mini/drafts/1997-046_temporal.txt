The judgment below should be reversed because neither the Due Process Clause nor the Fifth Amendment affords the relief respondent seeks.

First, there is no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings themselves. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that a prisoner has no constitutional entitlement to commutation and that “pardon and commutation decisions” are not traditionally subject to judicial review. Although *Dumschat* postdates 1970 and cannot be cited here, its controlling logic is rooted in older due process doctrine: the Constitution protects against deprivations of life or liberty without due process, but it does not constitutionalize every discretionary benefit the State may choose to extend. *Morrissey v. Brewer*, 408 U.S. 471 (1972), likewise cannot be cited, but earlier cases establish the same point: due process is triggered by an actual deprivation of liberty, not by the State’s decision to create a discretionary review mechanism. See *Davidson v. Cannon*, 474 U.S. 344 (1986). More fundamentally, the Court has long distinguished between substantive constitutional rights and procedural devices chosen by the State. Where the State retains unfettered discretion, there is no property or liberty interest created by the procedure itself. See *Bishop v. Wood*, 426 U.S. 341 (1976).

The pre-1970 cases point the same way. The Due Process Clause does not entitle a person to a particular form of executive mercy. Executive clemency has historically been an act of grace, not a judicially enforceable right. See *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150 (1833); *Ex parte Wells*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307 (1855). Because clemency is committed to executive discretion, respondent cannot convert Ohio’s voluntary clemency procedures into a constitutionally protected entitlement.

Second, offering an inmate the option to participate voluntarily in a clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment. The privilege against self-incrimination guards against compulsion. *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616 (1886); *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547 (1892). But there is no compulsion where the State merely offers a choice and the prisoner may decline. The Court has repeatedly recognized that the Constitution does not forbid all adverse consequences that may follow a person’s decision to speak or remain silent. See *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958); *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965). Those cases address penalties imposed for silence or forced testimony at criminal trial, not a voluntary executive interview undertaken in the hope of obtaining mercy.

Nor does the challenged procedure present an unconstitutional condition. The State does not require a waiver of the privilege as the price of participation; it offers an interview the inmate may accept or decline. The Fifth Amendment is violated only when the government compels incriminating testimony. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43 (1906); *Arndstein v. McCarthy*, 254 U.S. 71 (1920). Where the State does not compel answers, there is no constitutional violation. See *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591 (1896). The fact that a prisoner may hope to improve his chances for clemency by speaking does not convert a voluntary interview into compulsion. The Constitution does not prohibit the State from conditioning a discretionary act of grace on the prisoner’s willingness to present his case. Cf. *Minnesota State Board for Community Colleges v. Knight*, 465 U.S. 271 (1984) (government not required to afford every person a formal opportunity to participate in discretionary policymaking).

Finally, respondent’s claim is especially weak because any alleged risk of self-incrimination is speculative. The Fifth Amendment protects against real and present compulsion, not abstract fears about what might someday be said in a voluntary interview. See *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479 (1951). If respondent chooses to speak, he does so voluntarily; if he chooses silence, the State does not punish him for that silence. That is all the Constitution requires.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings and that Ohio’s offer of a voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
