ARGUMENT

I. The Controlled Substances Act’s Temporary Scheduling Provision Supplies an Intelligible Principle and Therefore Does Not Effect an Unconstitutional Delegation.

Article I, Section 1 vests “All legislative Powers herein granted” in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. This Court has long recognized, however, that Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches “by laying down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The principle is satisfied when Congress “clearly delineates the general policy, the public agency which is to apply it, and the boundaries of this delegated authority.” Id. at 372-73 (quoting American Power & Light Co. v. SEC, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946)).

Section 811(h) meets this standard. It authorizes temporary scheduling only upon a finding that a substance poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), and requires consideration of three specific statutory factors drawn from § 811(c): history and current pattern of abuse, scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and risk to public health. Id. § 811(h)(3). The Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register, transmit the proposal to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and consider the Secretary’s comments. Id. § 811(h)(4). Temporary placement is limited to one year, extendable by six months. Id. § 811(h)(2). These constraints “meaningfully constrain” the delegatee, Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 378, and supply the requisite intelligible principle.

Petitioners’ reliance on the two 1935 decisions invalidating delegations is misplaced. Unlike the National Industrial Recovery Act provisions struck down in Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), § 811(h) contains “explicit standards” and “adequate safeguards.” Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 374. Every other delegation challenged in this Court since 1935 has been sustained. Id. at 373.

II. The Attorney General’s Subdelegation to the DEA Administrator Is Authorized by Statute and Consistent with Established Practice.

Congress expressly empowered the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General exercised that authority by regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning temporary scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator. Such subdelegation is routine and has been upheld when, as here, the statute permits it. See, e.g., Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, 315 U.S. 357, 366 (1942) (recognizing that express statutory authority to delegate removes any implied prohibition).

The bar on judicial review of temporary scheduling orders, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), does not preclude constitutional review of the delegation itself. Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). Petitioners’ facial challenge therefore fails on the merits, and the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
