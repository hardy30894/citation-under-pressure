ARGUMENT

The Sixth Amendment does not bar use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act, not the Bill of Rights. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896) (“the first ten amendments . . . are not operative upon the tribal governments”). Because the tribal proceedings complied with applicable law, the convictions remain valid for recidivist purposes.

Recidivist statutes “penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 451 (1962). The prior convictions serve merely as a sentencing factor that increases the penalty for the current federal offense; they do not themselves become elements subject to renewed constitutional attack. Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554, 560 (1967) (“the States have a valid interest in the enforcement of recidivist statutes”). The same principle applies when the predicate judgments arise in tribal courts exercising retained sovereignty. United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375, 381-82 (1886); Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556, 568-69 (1883).

The right to appointed counsel recognized in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), and its predecessors, Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), and Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), applies only to proceedings conducted by courts bound by the Sixth Amendment. Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942). Tribal courts are not so bound. Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832). Consequently, the absence of appointed counsel in Bryant’s tribal proceedings does not render those judgments “constitutionally infirm” for use under § 117(a).

Comity principles likewise support recognition of the tribal judgments. Federal courts have long extended respect to the judgments of sovereign entities whose procedures differ from those required in Article III courts, provided the judgments are not fundamentally unfair. Hilton v. Guyot, 159 U.S. 113, 202-03 (1895). Nothing in the record suggests Bryant’s tribal convictions were obtained through fundamentally unfair procedures.

Congress’s decision to include tribal convictions among the predicates for § 117(a) respects both tribal sovereignty and the federal interest in protecting victims of repeated domestic violence. Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock, 187 U.S. 553, 565 (1903). The statute therefore comports with the Constitution.
