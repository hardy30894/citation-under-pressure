**Argument**

Title VII’s punitive-damages provision turns on the employer’s **state of mind**, not on whether the underlying discrimination was independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” The statute authorizes punitive damages where the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Nothing in that text imposes a separate requirement that the conduct be especially heinous apart from the statutory mental state. This Court has repeatedly instructed that when Congress defines liability by reference to a mental-state element, courts should not add extra-textual qualifiers. See Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998) (looking to the statute’s text and rejecting a rule untethered to Congress’s chosen formulation).

A contrary “egregiousness” requirement cannot be reconciled with the settled Title VII framework. In **St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks**, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993), the Court explained that once the plaintiff proves a prima facie case and discredits the employer’s explanation, “the factfinder, if so persuaded, may infer discrimination.” The Court made clear that liability for intentional discrimination does not depend on additional proof beyond the statutory violation. Id. at 511-15. Congress then enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1991 to expand remedies for intentional discrimination, not to contract them. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994). Reading § 1981a(b)(1) to require some extra measure of “outrageousness” would reintroduce a restriction Congress did not enact.

The statutory history confirms the same point. In **City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.**, 453 U.S. 247, 260-61 (1981), the Court recognized that punitive damages traditionally serve to punish and deter wrongful conduct. Congress adopted that remedial concept in § 1981a(b)(1), but limited punitive damages to cases involving “malice” or “reckless indifference.” Those words already distinguish ordinary liability from aggravated culpability. A separate egregiousness screen would collapse the statutory terms into a judicially created super-requirement. That is not interpretation; it is amendment. See West Virginia University Hospitals, Inc. v. Casey, 499 U.S. 83, 98-99 (1991).

Nor does the ADA’s position find support in this Court’s punitive-damages precedents. In **Smith v. Wade**, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983), the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is motivated by “evil motive or intent,” or involves “reckless or callous indifference” to federally protected rights. The focus is on culpable mental state, not on an independent showing that the conduct was “outrageous” in some freestanding sense. Congress used similar language in § 1981a(b)(1), signaling an intent to codify a state-of-mind standard, not an amorphous severity threshold.

That reading also fits the broader structure of federal antidiscrimination law. In **Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins**, 490 U.S. 228, 251 (1989), the Court emphasized that Title VII reaches decisions motivated by sex stereotyping and other discriminatory intent even where the employer offers facially neutral explanations. Likewise, in **McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green**, 411 U.S. 792, 804-05 (1973), the Court described Title VII as concerned with whether discrimination actually motivated the employment decision. Once intentional discrimination is proven, § 1981a permits punitive damages if the employer acted with the requisite culpable awareness of federal rights. Nothing in those cases suggests an additional requirement that the acts be independently “egregious.”

The court of appeals’ contrary approach would also undermine uniform application of the statute. Employers could escape punitive damages in serious cases whenever a court deemed the conduct insufficiently “outrageous,” even when the employer knowingly or recklessly violated Title VII. That would displace Congress’s chosen standard with a vague judicial gloss. The better reading, and the only one faithful to the statute, is that the plaintiff must prove intentional discrimination plus malice or reckless indifference. See 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1); Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983).

Here, the jury had ample basis to find exactly that. The evidence showed deliberate preselection, shifting explanations, and a decisionmaker who ignored Kolstad’s qualifications. A rational jury could conclude that ADA acted in “reckless indifference to the federally protected rights” of a female applicant. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The court of appeals correctly recognized that punitive damages should be available on that record, and the judgment should be affirmed.
