The Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law, and its decision should be affirmed.

The buffer zone is content-neutral, as it applies to all individuals, regardless of their message or viewpoint. The law does not burden speech based on its content, but rather regulates the place and manner of speech to ensure public safety and facilitate access to healthcare facilities. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719-20 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute as content-neutral); Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (holding that a regulation is content-neutral if it is "justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech").

The buffer zone is also narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests. The state has a legitimate interest in protecting the safety and well-being of patients and staff at healthcare facilities, as well as ensuring that individuals can access these facilities without obstruction or harassment. See Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994) (recognizing the government's interest in protecting healthcare facilities and their patients); Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York, 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997) (upholding a floating buffer zone as a means of protecting patients and staff).

The buffer zone leaves open ample alternative channels of communication for individuals to express their views. Petitioners can still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signs, and pray outside the buffer zone. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 726-27 (noting that the Colorado statute left open alternative channels of communication); Ward, 491 U.S. at 802 (holding that a regulation is valid if it leaves open "ample alternative channels of communication").

Petitioners' argument that the buffer zone is not narrowly tailored because it burdens their ability to engage in effective "sidewalk counseling" is unpersuasive. The First Amendment does not guarantee a right to engage in speech in a particular manner or location. See Frisby v. Schultz, 487 U.S. 474, 487-88 (1988) (upholding an ordinance that prohibited picketing outside a residence, even though it limited the ability of protesters to communicate with their target audience).

The First Circuit's decision is consistent with this Court's precedent, including Hill v. Colorado, which upheld a similar buffer zone statute. See also McGuire v. Reilly, 386 F.3d 45 (1st Cir. 2004) (upholding an earlier version of the Massachusetts statute). Petitioners' attempt to distinguish Hill is unavailing, as the Colorado statute and the Massachusetts statute share the same purpose and structure.

Furthermore, the law of the case doctrine supports the First Circuit's decision. The court had previously upheld the facial constitutionality of the statute in McCullen I, and petitioners have not shown that there has been a significant change in controlling legal authority or that the earlier decision was blatantly erroneous. See United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011) (explaining the law of the case doctrine).

In addition, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying petitioners' motion to amend their complaint to challenge the Attorney General's guidance letter. The motion was untimely, and petitioners have not shown that they were prejudiced by the denial. See Hatch v. Dep't for Children, Youth & Their Families, 274 F.3d 12, 19 (1st Cir. 2001) (reviewing a district court's denial of a motion to amend for abuse of discretion).

In conclusion, the Massachusetts statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit's decision upholding the law should be affirmed, as it is consistent with this Court's precedent and the law of the case doctrine. See Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 340 (2010) (noting that the government has a legitimate interest in regulating speech to prevent corruption or the appearance of corruption); Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011) (holding that speech on public issues is entitled to special protection under the First Amendment, but noting that the government has a legitimate interest in regulating speech to protect the rights of others). The buffer zone is a reasonable means of achieving the government's interests, and it leaves open ample alternative channels of communication for individuals to express their views.
