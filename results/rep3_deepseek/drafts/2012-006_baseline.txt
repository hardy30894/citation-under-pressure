ARGUMENT

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the conspiracy existed within the applicable statute of limitations period. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1231 (7th Cir. 1981). When a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal from the conspiracy prior to the limitations period, the burden of persuasion shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *See United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d 1077, 1083 (6th Cir. 1991). The lower court erred by requiring petitioners Calvin Smith and John Raynor to prove their withdrawal, rather than requiring the government to disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt.

A. The Statute of Limitations Is an Element of the Conspiracy Offense

The government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1231. This is not a mere affirmative defense but an essential element of the offense. *See United States v. A-A-A Elec. Co.*, 788 F.2d 242, 245 (4th Cir. 1986). The government’s failure to produce evidence that the conspiracy existed within the limitations period requires acquittal. *United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d at 1083. Here, the evidence showed that the conspiracy’s leaders, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the limitations period. The government therefore had the burden to prove that each petitioner remained a member of the conspiracy after that dissolution. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (withdrawal terminates liability for later acts).

B. Withdrawal Negates an Element the Government Must Prove

Withdrawal from a conspiracy is not a traditional affirmative defense that shifts the burden of persuasion to the defendant. Instead, it negates the element of continuing membership. *United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d at 1083. Once the defendant produces some evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw and remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. *Id.*; *United States v. Steele*, 685 F.2d 793, 803-04 (3d Cir. 1982). This rule comports with the fundamental principle that the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

The lower court’s decision placed the burden on petitioners to prove withdrawal, effectively requiring them to disprove an element of the offense. This error is reversible. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1231. The government’s evidence of a single conspiracy was insufficient because the leaders’ split before the limitations period broke the conspiracy into separate agreements. *See United States v. Tarantino*, 846 F.2d 1384, 1393 (D.C. Cir. 1988) (finding that a single conspiracy requires a shared objective). Without evidence that petitioners joined or continued in the conspiracy after the split, the jury could not rationally find them guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.

C. The Lower Court’s Contrary Holding Conflicts with Settled Precedent

The lower court’s opinion—though not published in full here—appears to have held that the government’s burden was satisfied because petitioners failed to prove withdrawal. This directly contravenes well-established law. *See United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d at 1083; *United States v. Steele*, 685 F.2d at 803-04. The burden of persuasion on the limitations period remains with the government at all times. *United States v. A-A-A Elec. Co.*, 788 F.2d at 245. By requiring petitioners to prove withdrawal, the lower court effectively relieved the government of its constitutional obligation to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

The government’s theory that the conspiracy continued absent proof of withdrawal impermissibly shifts the burden. *See United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d at 1231. This Court should reverse and hold that when a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal before the statute of limitations period, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during that period.
