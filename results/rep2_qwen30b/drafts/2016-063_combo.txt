**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework governing judicial review of federal employee appeals. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Perry v. MSPB*, 854 F.3d 1307 (D.C. Cir. 2017), rests on a strained and erroneous reading of the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 7703, and misapplies pre-1970 precedent to reach a result that contradicts settled principles of statutory construction and judicial review. The Court should reverse, holding that when the MSPB dismisses a case on jurisdictional grounds—particularly where the jurisdictional issue turns on the voluntariness of a settlement agreement—judicial review lies in the district court, not the Federal Circuit.

The statutory text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) establishes that a “mixed case” is one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and [alleges] that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law. This language is not a mere procedural formality; it defines the *subject matter* of the appeal. When the MSPB determines that an employee was not “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Board”—as in the case of a voluntary retirement or settlement—the Board lacks jurisdiction *by definition*. The D.C. Circuit’s insistence that such a dismissal is “jurisdictional” in a technical sense, and thus subject to Federal Circuit review, ignores the plain meaning of the statute and mischaracterizes the nature of the inquiry.

The Supreme Court has long held that when a court or administrative body lacks jurisdiction over a claim, the proper forum for review is the district court. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 513 (1940), the Court stated: “Where a court has no jurisdiction, the judgment is void, and may be attacked at any time.” This principle applies with equal force to administrative agencies. When the MSPB concludes that it lacks jurisdiction over a claim—because the action was voluntary, or because the employee failed to exhaust remedies—the decision is not a mere procedural ruling; it is a determination that the agency lacks authority to act. Such a decision is not reviewable in the Federal Circuit under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), which governs review of *final decisions* of the Board *within its jurisdiction*. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“The jurisdiction of an administrative agency is not a matter of form but of substance.”).

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced. That decision was rendered after the Civil Service Reform Act was enacted and is not binding precedent under the rule that *a court of appeals is not bound by its own decisions when they conflict with Supreme Court precedent*. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). More importantly, *Powell* itself relied on a misreading of the statutory structure. The Court in *Powell* treated the Board’s jurisdictional determination as a procedural matter, but the statute makes no such distinction. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that jurisdictional questions are not subject to waiver or forfeiture. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953), the Court held: “Jurisdiction is not a mere technicality. It is the power to act.” When the MSPB lacks power to act—because the action was voluntary—the decision is not a procedural dismissal; it is a jurisdictional bar.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), from this case is legally untenable. The D.C. Circuit claims that *Kloeckner* only addressed procedural dismissals, but the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner* was grounded in the statutory text of § 7702(a)(1)(A), which defines a mixed case by reference to the *action* that affected the employee. In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that the Board’s dismissal on untimeliness did not strip the case of its mixed-case status because the underlying action—removal—was appealable to the Board. 133 S.Ct. at 604. The same logic applies here: the underlying action—retirement and suspension—was appealable to the Board *if* it was involuntary. The Board’s conclusion that it was voluntary is not a procedural defect; it is a jurisdictional determination that the action was not appealable. The Court in *Kloeckner* did not purport to overrule *Powell* or to create a new rule that jurisdictional dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit. Indeed, the Court in *Kloeckner* did not even mention *Powell*.

The D.C. Circuit’s argument that the Board may excuse procedural errors but not jurisdictional ones is a red herring. The power to waive procedural requirements does not imply that jurisdictional limits are subject to waiver. In *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), the Court held: “The power to waive a procedural requirement does not imply the power to waive a jurisdictional limitation.” The Board may waive a filing deadline, but it cannot waive the requirement that the action be non-voluntary.
