**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause gives rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one”—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit answered in the affirmative, holding that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause when the government’s action is motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 212 F.3d 434, 438 (7th Cir. 2000). That holding, while seemingly grounded in principle, is fundamentally at odds with settled constitutional doctrine and risks transforming the Equal Protection Clause into a vehicle for personal grievances, thereby undermining the very purpose of the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Constitution does not guarantee individuals equal treatment in every discretionary decision made by government officials. As this Court has long held, the Equal Protection Clause “is not a font of arbitrary or whimsical preferences,” but rather “a safeguard against arbitrary or irrational government action.” *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985). To trigger equal protection scrutiny, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the government has treated him differently from others similarly situated, and that the differential treatment lacks a rational basis. *See id.* at 446. This framework presupposes the existence of a class—defined by a common characteristic or status—whose members are treated differently. The Clause is not a “general constitutional warrant for the judiciary to supervise the performance of public duties.” *Lund v. City of Chicago*, 103 F.3d 594, 598 (7th Cir. 1997).

The Seventh Circuit’s “class of one” doctrine, however, dispenses with this essential requirement. It permits a plaintiff to bypass the need to identify a class of similarly situated individuals and instead rests the claim on the subjective animus of the decisionmaker. This approach is not only inconsistent with precedent but also unworkable in practice. As this Court has cautioned, “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that a State treat everyone identically, but only that it treat similarly situated persons alike.” *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977). The “class of one” theory, by contrast, treats the absence of a class as the very basis for a constitutional claim, thereby elevating personal grievance into constitutional entitlement.

The Court has never recognized such a claim. In *Cleburne*, the Court rejected a claim that a city’s refusal to grant a special use permit to a group home for the mentally disabled violated equal protection, even though the city had granted permits to other similar facilities. The Court emphasized that “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that a State treat everyone identically,” and that “the mere fact that a decision is not rational does not make it unconstitutional.” *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440, 446. The Court has similarly rejected claims based on personal animus in *Rice v. Santa Clara County*, 458 U.S. 442 (1982), where the Court held that a plaintiff could not challenge a zoning decision based on the alleged personal hostility of a city official. The Court stated that “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that a State treat everyone identically,” and that “the mere fact that a decision is not rational does not make it unconstitutional.” *Id.* at 448.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Olech* misreads *Cleburne* and *Rice*. It suggests that a plaintiff may assert a constitutional claim simply because the government acted out of spite. But the Constitution does not provide a remedy for every instance of personal animus. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the Equal Protection Clause is not a license for courts to judge the wisdom or fairness of legislative choices.” *Ferguson v. Skrupa*, 372 U.S. 726, 730 (1963). To hold otherwise would invite endless litigation based on subjective perceptions of unfairness, undermining the legitimacy of government decisionmaking.

Moreover, the “class of one” theory is inherently unmoored from any objective standard. What constitutes “spite” or “personal animus”? Who determines whether a government official acted out of malice rather than policy? These are questions of motive, not law, and are ill-suited for judicial resolution. The judiciary is not equipped to serve as a “super-legislature” to second-guess the motivations behind every administrative decision. *See id.* at 730.

The Seventh Circuit’s approach also threatens to erode the principle of governmental immunity. If every adverse decision by a public official can be challenged as a violation of equal protection based on subjective animus, then public officials will be deterred from making difficult decisions for fear of personal liability. This would undermine the effective functioning of government.

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed. The Equal Protection Clause does not create a cause of action for a “class of one.” A plaintiff must allege membership in a class of similarly situated individuals and demonstrate that the government’s treatment of them was irrational or arbitrary. The absence of such a class is not a basis for a constitutional claim. The judgment below is contrary to precedent, unworkable in practice, and fundamentally at odds with the purpose of the Fourteenth Amendment.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Seventh Circuit and hold that the Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one” where the plaintiff does not allege membership in a class or group.
