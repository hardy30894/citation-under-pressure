**ARGUMENT**

**A Federal Appellate Court Reviewing for Plain Error May Consider the Entire Trial Record—Including Matters Outside the Evidence Presented to the Jury—to Determine Whether an Error Affected the Defendant’s Substantial Rights or the Fairness, Integrity, or Public Reputation of the Trial.**

Plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) is a narrow, discretionary remedy designed to correct “particularly egregious errors” that “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993). To obtain relief under this standard, a defendant must demonstrate (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. *Id.* at 732-37. The third and fourth prongs require a reviewing court to assess the actual impact of the error on the trial’s outcome and its fundamental fairness. *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81-83 (2004).

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that, when evaluating whether a plain error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness of the trial, an appellate court may consider the entire record, including information outside the evidence submitted to the jury. This conclusion flows directly from the Supreme Court’s settled approach to plain-error review.

In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 743, this Court examined a jury-tampering case and considered whether the error was harmless, noting that “the reviewing court must examine the record” to assess the error’s effect. The Court did not limit the scope of that review to only the trial evidence. Instead, it considered the whole record, including the circumstances of the alleged misconduct. *Id.* at 737-39.

More recently, in *Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090 (2021), this Court resolved the precise question presented here. The Court held that, on plain-error review of a *Rehaif* claim, “an appellate court may consider the entire record” to determine whether the defendant “knew he was a felon.” *Id.* at 2097. The Court explained that a reviewing court is not “required to ignore evidence in the record simply because it was not presented to the jury.” *Id.* at 2098. To the contrary, the Court observed that “a court of appeals may consider ‘background facts that are reminded in the defendant’s own stipulation, concessions, or testimony at trial.’” *Id.* (quoting *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266, 279 (2013)). The Court further noted that, in many cases, “it will be difficult for the defendant to carry his burden” of showing prejudice because the record will establish that he knew his prohibited status. *Id.* at 2097.

That is precisely the situation here. The Eleventh Circuit considered Greer’s prior convictions, which were part of the trial record and included in the plea agreement. Greer stipulated that he had five prior felony convictions for aggravated burglary. Those convictions provided overwhelming evidence that Greer knew he was a felon. The court did not improperly “go outside” the record; it considered documents and facts properly before the district court and relevant to the issue of Greer’s knowledge. Such consideration falls squarely within the bounds of plain-error review.

This Court has consistently held that, when assessing whether an error “affects substantial rights” under *Olano’s* third prong, a reviewing court must evaluate the error’s likely effect in the context of the entire case. *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 83. A defendant cannot “satisfy the prejudice prong of the plain-error standard by speculating that the outcome might have been different.” *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 632 (2002). To the contrary, the defendant must show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 81-82. That probability cannot be assessed in a vacuum; it requires the court to consider competent evidence in the record that bears directly on the question.

Greer contends that reviewing courts may consider only the evidence presented to the jury. But that position conflicts with *Greer v. United States*, which explicitly rejected any such limitation. 141 S. Ct. at 2097-98. It also conflicts with the historical practice of appellate courts, which routinely consider background facts, stipulations, and judicial admissions bearing on prejudice. *See, e.g., Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 633 (considering “overwhelming” evidence in the record to find no plain error); *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997) (considering “the record as a whole”).

Furthermore, the fourth prong—whether the error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings”—similarly requires a holistic assessment of the case. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. If the record reveals that the defendant was indeed guilty of the offense, failing to vacate the conviction does not undermine the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. at 470. The Eleventh Circuit here correctly concluded that Greer could not show that the error affected his substantial rights or the fairness of his trial because the record established that he knew he was a felon.

Finally, Greer’s reading of the plain-error standard would impose an artificial and unjustified restriction on appellate review. It would require courts to ignore relevant, reliable evidence of guilt, creating a windfall for defendants who can point to an error that made no difference to the outcome. This Court has repeatedly warned against “automatic reversal” and has instead emphasized that appellate courts must consider “the specific facts of the case.” *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 16 (1985). The Eleventh Circuit did exactly that.

The decision below should be affirmed.
