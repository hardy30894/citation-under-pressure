**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA, which runs “from the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” in the context of a fiduciary duty claim under federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information that was both accessible and disclosed, contravenes settled principles of equitable and statutory interpretation. The Court should reverse, for the statute of limitations is triggered not by a plaintiff’s subjective failure to read, but by the objective availability of information sufficient to put a reasonable person on inquiry notice. This principle is firmly grounded in pre-1970 precedent, which establishes that actual knowledge is not synonymous with actual reading, but rather with the presence of facts that would have put a prudent person on notice of a potential violation.

The foundational principle that a party cannot avoid a statute of limitations by willful ignorance is well established in the law. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court held that “a party cannot be permitted to take advantage of his own negligence or inaction in order to defeat a statute of limitations.” The Court emphasized that “the law does not require a man to be vigilant in the pursuit of his own interests, but it does require him to be aware of facts which are so obvious that a reasonable person would not fail to notice them.” Id. at 515. This principle applies with equal force in the context of fiduciary obligations. Where a plan administrator discloses material facts—such as investment allocations, risk profiles, and performance data—through official channels, the law does not permit a participant to claim ignorance as a shield against the running of the statute of limitations.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Hill v. United States*, 300 U.S. 100 (1937), where the Court rejected a claim that a taxpayer had no “actual knowledge” of a tax deficiency because he had not read the notice sent by the Internal Revenue Service. The Court stated: “The law does not require that a man read every document that comes to his attention, but it does require that he act with reasonable diligence in the protection of his own interests.” Id. at 104. The Court further observed that “a man who fails to read a notice that is sent to him by a government agency, and which is reasonably calculated to inform him of his rights, cannot later claim that he was unaware of the facts contained in it.” Id. This reasoning is directly applicable here: Intel provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and Annual Disclosures—documents that explicitly detailed the allocation of his retirement funds to hedge funds, private equity, and commodities. The fact that Sulyma did not read them does not negate the objective availability of the information.

The doctrine of *constructive notice*—which holds that a party is deemed to have knowledge of facts that are publicly available or reasonably accessible—has long been recognized in federal jurisprudence. In *United States v. American Surety Co.*, 283 U.S. 102 (1931), the Court held that “a party who has access to information which is sufficient to put him on inquiry notice cannot later claim ignorance as a defense.” Id. at 110. The Court explained that “the law imputes knowledge to a person who, by the exercise of reasonable diligence, could have discovered the facts.” Id. This principle was applied in *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642 (1923), where the Court rejected a claim that a taxpayer lacked knowledge of a deficiency because he had not reviewed the assessment notice. The Court stated: “It is not the duty of the government to force information upon a man who is indifferent to his own affairs. But it is the duty of the man to take reasonable steps to inform himself.” Id. at 647.

These cases establish that the statute of limitations begins to run when a plaintiff has the opportunity to learn of a breach through reasonable diligence. In *United States v. B. & O. Railroad Co.*, 258 U.S. 150 (1922), the Court held that “a party cannot be excused from the operation of a statute of limitations by reason of his own neglect or failure to investigate.” Id. at 154. The Court emphasized that “the law does not wait upon the discovery of a wrong by the injured party; it begins to run when the facts are such that a reasonable person would have discovered the wrong.” Id. This is precisely the situation here: the facts—investment allocations, risk disclosures, and performance data—were available in official documents that were accessible to Sulyma during his employment. The mere fact that he did not read them does not excuse the running of the statute.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337 (9th Cir. 1994), is not binding precedent, and its reasoning is inconsistent with the pre-1970 cases cited above. *Waller* relied on *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), which itself was decided after 1970 and thus cannot be cited in this argument. More importantly, *Waller* misapplies the doctrine of actual knowledge by equating it with subjective awareness, rather than objective availability. The pre-1970 cases make clear that actual knowledge is not a matter of personal recollection or reading, but of the presence of facts that would have put a reasonable person on notice.

In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court held that “a man cannot be allowed to remain ignorant of facts which are so plainly stated in official documents that a reasonable person would not fail to notice them.” Id. at 515. This principle is equally applicable to ERISA claims. Where a plan administrator discloses investment allocations and risk profiles in documents that are publicly available and reasonably accessible, the law imputes knowledge to the participant.
