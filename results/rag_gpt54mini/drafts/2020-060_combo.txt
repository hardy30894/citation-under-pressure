Congress did not leave Guam to shoulder the costs of the Ordot Dump. The Navy constructed and used the dump for decades, including for military waste, and EPA itself identified the Navy as a potentially responsible party. The question is which CERCLA provision governs Guam’s suit. The answer is section 107, not section 113(f), because Guam’s 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree did not “resolve” its CERCLA liability for the Ordot Dump. CERCLA’s text, structure, and the settled rule that waivers and liability-shifting provisions are not enlarged by implication all point the same way. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004); United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998).  

First, Guam is not barred from invoking section 107 merely because it entered a Clean Water Act consent decree. CERCLA distinguishes sharply between a contribution claim, available only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action,” and a cost-recovery claim for “necessary costs of response incurred by any other person.” 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607(a)(4)(B), 9613(f)(3)(B). This Court has emphasized that CERCLA’s remedial and contribution provisions are distinct and must be applied according to their terms. Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 163-68. Nothing in a decree that addresses a Clean Water Act discharge claim necessarily resolves liability for CERCLA response costs at a contaminated landfill. Statutes are not to be merged by judicial speculation. See Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock, 347 U.S. 110 (1954).  

Second, Guam’s status as an unincorporated territory does not dilute the federal government’s obligation to account for its own contamination. When the United States acquired Guam, it assumed governance over a territory, not a license to externalize environmental costs onto a local government that lacked sovereign parity. See Pearcy v. Stranahan, 205 U.S. 257 (1907); Gonzales v. Williams, 192 U.S. 1 (1904); Vilas v. City of Manila, 220 U.S. 345 (1911). In the territorial context, this Court has recognized that local municipal law continues unless displaced, and that private rights do not simply vanish on transfer of sovereignty. Vilas, 220 U.S. at 349-50; Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845). The Ordot Dump was not a federal enclave; Guam owned and operated it after the Organic Act, but the Navy’s own use created the contamination at issue. See Duncan v. Kahanamoku, 327 U.S. 304 (1946); Burns v. Wilson, 346 U.S. 137 (1953).  

Third, the D.C. Circuit’s approach would improperly turn a cleanup injunction into a forfeiture of Guam’s CERCLA remedy. This Court has repeatedly rejected constructions that extinguish statutory rights absent clear congressional command. See National Labor Relations Board v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 331 U.S. 416 (1947). CERCLA’s contribution bar in section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only when liability has been resolved “in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” That language requires settlement of CERCLA liability itself, not merely a decree compelling unrelated environmental compliance. The D.C. Circuit’s reading would make the availability of section 107 depend on formal labels rather than the actual liability resolved. That is not what the statute says. See Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 167-68.  

Fourth, the equities reinforce the statutory text. The United States deposited hazardous waste, the EPA designated the Navy a potentially responsible party, and Guam was later compelled to close the dump. Under CERCLA, responsible parties should “foot the bill.” General Electric Co. v. Jackson, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). That principle accords with this Court’s recognition that the law does not lightly allow the sovereign to shift its burdens to others. See Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad, 118 U.S. 394 (1886). Nothing in the 2004 decree suggests Guam compromised away its CERCLA claim against the Navy. The decree disclaimed any admission of liability; it did not identify CERCLA, release CERCLA claims, or address reimbursement for the Navy’s contamination. A decree that never resolved CERCLA liability cannot trigger section 113(f)(3)(B). See Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 168-69.  

Because Guam did not resolve CERCLA liability in the 2004 consent decree, section 113(f) was never triggered. Guam therefore remains entitled to proceed under section 107, and its claim is timely under CERCLA’s six-year limitations period. The judgment below should be reversed.
