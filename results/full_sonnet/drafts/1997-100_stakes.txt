# ARGUMENT

**THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF A PRIOR-CONVICTION SENTENCING ALLEGATION WHEN THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTING THAT ALLEGATION WAS FOUND LEGALLY INSUFFICIENT**

**I. A Sentencing Proceeding That Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial Triggers Double Jeopardy Protection.**

The Fifth Amendment's guarantee against double jeopardy, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Among its core protections, the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969).

This Court has already held that these protections extend beyond the guilt phase to sentencing proceedings that function as a trial. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court examined a capital sentencing hearing and concluded that because the proceeding possessed the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence," double jeopardy barred the State from seeking the death penalty on retrial after a jury had effectively acquitted the defendant of that penalty. *Id.* at 439. Three years later, in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), the Court reaffirmed that the relevant inquiry is whether the proceeding has characteristics "that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." *Id.* at 209–10. Nothing in that functional inquiry turns on whether a human life is at stake; it turns on the *procedures* the State has chosen to employ.

**II. California's Prior-Conviction Proceeding Possesses Every Hallmark That Made the Sentencing Hearings in Bullington and Rumsey "Trials" for Double Jeopardy Purposes.**

California has not treated the determination of a prior-conviction allegation as an informal exercise of sentencing discretion akin to the parole-board-like inquiry in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949). To the contrary, California law affords defendants a jury trial of such allegations, Cal. Penal Code § 1025; requires the prosecution to prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt; affords the defendant the privilege against self-incrimination; applies the ordinary rules of evidence; and guarantees the right to confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses. These are precisely the procedural hallmarks—burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, adversarial presentation of evidence, and the panoply of trial-type protections—that led this Court to treat the capital sentencing proceedings in *Bullington* and *Rumsey* as trials rather than exercises of sentencing discretion. A proceeding conducted with a reasonable-doubt standard, jury trial rights, confrontation, and evidentiary rules "resembles a trial" no less when a prior conviction, rather than a death sentence, hangs in the balance. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209–10.

**III. Once the Reviewing Court Found the Evidence Legally Insufficient, Retrial Was Barred.**

The Court of Appeal held that the evidence introduced to prove the prior-conviction allegation was insufficient as a matter of law to establish that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. Where a trial-type proceeding results in a finding of insufficiency, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not permit the State a second opportunity to marshal the evidence it failed to muster the first time. This is simply the *Bullington* principle applied to its logical end: a defendant who has been "acquitted" of the sentencing allegation because the State's proof fell short may not be forced to run that gauntlet twice. To hold otherwise would allow the State, having lost once on the merits of the prior-conviction allegation, to refine its case and try again—the very evil the Double Jeopardy Clause exists to prevent.

**IV. This Court Has Not Confined Bullington to Capital Cases.**

The California Supreme Court read this Court's decision in *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), as confining *Bullington* to the capital context. But *Caspari* did not so hold. That case arose on habeas corpus under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and the Court's observation that "[b]oth *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding," *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392, was offered only to explain why extension of *Bullington* to noncapital sentencing was not "dictated by precedent" for *Teague* purposes—a distinct inquiry from whether the Constitution in fact requires that result. *Caspari* left the merits question open; it did not resolve it against double jeopardy protection, and *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), likewise did not purport to decide whether trial-like noncapital sentencing proceedings fall outside the Clause.

**V. The Underlying Purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause Apply with Equal Force Here.**

The Clause exists to prevent the State from using its superior resources to wear down a defendant through repeated attempts to secure a particular result, and to preserve the finality of proceedings that carry the solemnity of a trial. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. Monge already endured a trial-type proceeding—with a reasonable-doubt standard, confrontation, and evidentiary rules—on the prior-conviction allegation, and the State's proof was found wanting. Permitting a second attempt would subject him to precisely the multiple prosecutions the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids.

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
