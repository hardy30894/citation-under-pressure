**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-entered Before Its 1997 Effective Date**

Congressional enactments are presumed to operate only prospectively. As this Court held in *Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988), "congressional enactments . . . will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result." That presumption governs here. Nothing in the text of § 241(a)(5) states that it reaches aliens, like Petitioner, who re-entered the United States before its enactment in 1997. Where Congress wishes to reach past conduct, it says so plainly; its silence is telling.

This Court's two-step retroactivity framework confirms that silence should be read against retroactive application. In *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994), the Court explained that a reviewing court must first ask "whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute's proper reach," and if it has not, the court must ask "whether the new statute would have retroactive effect, i.e., whether it would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party's liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." If the statute would so operate, "our traditional presumption teaches that it does not govern absent clear congressional intent favoring such a result." *Id.* Congress made no such clear statement in § 241(a)(5). Because Petitioner's re-entry — the triggering conduct — occurred in 1982, fifteen years before the statute existed, the reinstatement provision should be construed not to reach him.

This Court applied the identical interpretive method in *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), refusing to read IIRIRA's repeal of discretionary relief under former § 212(c) to apply to aliens who had pleaded guilty before the repeal, absent a clear statement from Congress. The Court reasoned that "the presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence," *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 265, and that this deep-rooted principle applies with particular force in the immigration context, where individuals structure their conduct—and their families' lives—around existing law. The same logic compels construing § 241(a)(5) to leave undisturbed pre-1997 deportation orders against aliens whose subsequent conduct, including reliance on eligibility for adjustment of status, occurred long before the statute's passage.

**II. If the Statute Is Read to Apply to Petitioner, That Application Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even were the Court to conclude that § 241(a)(5) extends to pre-enactment re-entries, applying it to reinstate Petitioner's 1981 order — and thereby to strip him of the opportunity to seek adjustment of status based on his marriage — has an impermissible retroactive effect within the meaning of *Landgraf*.

This Court has long recognized that a statute has forbidden retroactive effect when it "attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting Society for Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (No. 13,156) (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)). Petitioner's re-entry in 1982, and his subsequent nineteen years of residence, marriage to a United States citizen, and application for adjustment of status, were "transactions or considerations already past" by the time § 241(a)(5) came into force in 1997. Applying the statute to bar him categorically from any relief attaches precisely the kind of "new disability" to that completed conduct that *Landgraf* forbids.

The retroactivity inquiry is not merely formal; it asks whether the new rule "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted . . . or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Before 1997, an alien in Petitioner's position who re-entered unlawfully nonetheless retained the statutory possibility of adjusting status upon marriage to a citizen. Section 241(a)(5), applied here, retroactively erases that possibility based on conduct — the re-entry — that occurred under a legal regime that did not categorically foreclose relief. That is the same kind of disruption of "settled expectations" this Court found unacceptable in *St. Cyr*, where aliens who pleaded guilty in reliance on the availability of § 212(c) relief could not, consistent with the presumption against retroactivity, be stripped of that possibility by a later-enacted statute. 533 U.S. at 321–23.

The Tenth Circuit's rationale — that Petitioner had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" because he was already unlawfully present — proves too much. The same could have been said of the alien in *St. Cyr*, who had likewise violated the law giving rise to his removability. Yet this Court held that unlawful conduct does not forfeit the protection of ordinary retroactivity principles when a later statute attaches new and more severe legal consequences to conduct already completed. *Id.* at 320–25. The same reasoning applies with full force to Petitioner: his continued presence following his 1982 re-entry was conduct completed under the law as it then stood, and § 241(a)(5) may not be applied to attach a categorical bar to relief that did not previously exist.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
