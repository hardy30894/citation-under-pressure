ARGUMENT

Under this Court’s settled retroactivity doctrine, a new rule of constitutional law that is substantive—rather than merely procedural—must be applied to cases on collateral review, even though the conviction or sentence became final before the rule was announced. The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), invalidating the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) as unconstitutionally vague, is substantive within the meaning of that doctrine. It must therefore apply retroactively to Gregory Welch’s sentence.

I. Substantive Rules of Constitutional Law Apply Retroactively on Collateral Review

In Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), this Court adopted Justice Harlan’s view that “unless they fall within one of two exceptions, new constitutional rules of criminal procedure will not be applicable to those cases which have become final before the new rules are announced.” Id. at 310 (plurality opinion). The first Teague exception preserves retroactive application for rules that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” Id. at 311. That exception applies with full force to rules altering the substantive reach of a criminal statute, not merely the procedures used to enforce it.

This Court explained the distinction in Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348 (2004): “Rules that allocate decisionmaking authority” between judge and jury “are prototypical procedural rules,” but “[n]ew substantive rules generally apply retroactively.” Id. at 351-52. Substantive rules include “decisions that narrow the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” as well as “constitutional determinations that place particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish.” Id. at 351-52 (citing Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998)). Such rules apply retroactively because they “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal or faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” Bousley, 523 U.S. at 620 (quoting United States v. Davis, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974)) (internal quotation marks omitted).

This Court reaffirmed and deepened that principle in Montgomery v. Louisiana, 136 S. Ct. 718 (2016), holding that “when a new substantive rule of constitutional law controls the outcome of a case, the Constitution requires state collateral review courts to give retroactive effect to that rule.” Id. at 729. Montgomery makes clear that the retroactive application of substantive rules is not a matter of judicial grace but a constitutional command flowing from the principle that “a court has no authority to leave in place a conviction or sentence that violates a substantive rule.” Id. at 731. A court, state or federal, may not evade this obligation by recharacterizing a substantive holding as merely procedural. Id. at 734.

II. Johnson Announced a Substantive Rule Because It Narrowed the Class of Persons Subject to ACCA’s Fifteen-Year Mandatory Minimum

Johnson held that the residual clause of ACCA—covering offenses that “otherwise involv[e] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii)—“is unconstitutionally vague,” because it requires courts to assess “a judicially imagined ‘ordinary case’ of a crime,” an inquiry that “both denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges.” 576 U.S. at 597. The consequence of that holding is not a change in the procedure by which courts determine whether a predicate offense qualifies; it is the elimination of an entire category of predicate offenses from ACCA’s reach. After Johnson, the residual clause simply cannot be used, by any court, applying any procedure, to classify an offense as a “violent felony.”

That is precisely the kind of rule Schriro identifies as substantive: one that “narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute,” 542 U.S. at 351, by placing an entire class of predicate offenses “beyond the State’s power to punish”—or, here, beyond Congress’s power to use as a basis for a mandatory fifteen-year sentence. Id. Just as a rule holding certain primary conduct outside the reach of a criminal statute “necessarily carr[ies] a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal,” Bousley, 523 U.S. at 620, Johnson necessarily carries a significant risk that offenders like Welch face “a punishment that the law cannot impose upon” them. Id. Welch was sentenced to a fifteen-year mandatory minimum based in part on the residual clause’s classification of his Florida robbery conviction as presenting a “serious potential risk of physical injury.” After Johnson, that clause no longer exists as a valid basis for any sentence. The substantive floor beneath his enhanced sentence has been removed by operation of the Constitution itself, not by any change in adjudicative procedure.

III. Retroactive Application Is Compelled Regardless of Any Procedural Overlay

That Johnson arose in the context of applying a categorical, offense-based test does not convert its holding into a procedural rule. The touchstone under Schriro is not the doctrinal vehicle through which a rule is announced, but its effect: does it “alter the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes”? 542 U.S. at 353. Johnson unmistakably does so. Because ACCA’s residual clause can no longer serve, in any case, as a valid predicate for the mandatory minimum, courts on collateral review “ha[ve] no authority to leave in place a conviction or sentence” resting on that now-void basis. Montgomery, 136 S. Ct. at 731. Johnson’s rule must therefore apply retroactively to Welch’s sentence.
