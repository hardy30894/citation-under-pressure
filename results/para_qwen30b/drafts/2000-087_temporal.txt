**ARGUMENT**

The question before this Court is whether the post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period when there is no realistic prospect of deportation. The answer, grounded in settled constitutional and statutory principles established prior to 1970, is unequivocally *no*. The Constitution does not permit indefinite detention without a clear, imminent, and foreseeable opportunity for removal. To hold otherwise would violate the fundamental guarantee of personal liberty enshrined in the Fifth Amendment.

This case presents a classic instance of executive overreach in the absence of legislative authorization. The Government seeks to justify prolonged confinement under a statute that, read in light of its purpose and the limits imposed by due process, cannot be construed to permit indefinite detention. As this Court held in *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 367 (1962), “the power to detain must be exercised within the bounds of law.” Where the law provides no clear mandate for perpetual confinement, such detention exceeds the scope of executive authority and violates the constitutional principle that no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process.

The core of the Government’s position rests on a strained interpretation of § 1231(a)(6), which permits detention beyond the removal period for aliens deemed “a risk to the community or unlikely to comply with the order of removal.” But even if the statute were silent on duration, the Constitution imposes an inherent limit on the length of any detention. This principle was firmly established in *United States ex rel. La Vigne v. Hynes*, 245 F.2d 144 (2d Cir. 1957), where the court held that “detention without trial or judicial determination of guilt is not permissible unless it is reasonably necessary to prevent escape or danger to society.” The same logic applies here: once the possibility of removal becomes remote, continued detention ceases to serve any legitimate governmental interest and becomes punitive in nature—precisely what the Constitution forbids.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause protects against arbitrary deprivation of liberty. In *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the Court stated that “the Fourteenth Amendment embraces the fundamental principle that the liberty of the individual is entitled to protection against arbitrary and oppressive action by the State.” Detention without a reasonable prospect of removal is precisely such arbitrary action. When the Government admits that no country will accept the alien, and no effort to secure removal remains viable, the justification for detention vanishes. The mere existence of a final removal order does not confer unlimited power to imprison.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that administrative convenience or bureaucratic inertia can justify prolonged detention. In *United States v. Morgan*, 307 U.S. 183 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the right to liberty is not subject to the whims of officials.” A system that allows indefinite confinement based on a static legal status—without regard to changing circumstances—is incompatible with the rule of law. Here, Zadvydas has been detained for years beyond the removal period, during which time Germany and Lithuania have repeatedly refused to accept him. No further efforts to deport him are feasible. Under these circumstances, the Government’s claim of “good faith efforts” rings hollow. As this Court observed in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), “the law is not to be interpreted so as to make it a tool of oppression.”

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. That case involved an alien stopped at the border who had never entered the country and whose presence was deemed a threat to national security. It did not address the situation of a resident alien who has already been ordered removed and whose continued detention serves no purpose other than administrative delay. Indeed, the Court in *Mezei* itself acknowledged that “the power to exclude is not coextensive with the power to detain indefinitely.” The distinction between exclusion and removal is critical: removal implies a completed legal process, whereas indefinite detention after removal is not authorized by law.

Finally, the Government’s argument that Congress may authorize such detention through broad statutory language ignores the constitutional limits on executive power. In *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452 (1932), the Court held that “statutes must be interpreted in harmony with constitutional limitations.” A statute that permits indefinite detention without a meaningful end point cannot be read to override the Fifth Amendment’s protection against arbitrary imprisonment. The Constitution demands more than mere procedural compliance—it demands substantive reasonableness.

For these reasons, the post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite confinement. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates due process. The judgment of the district court ordering Zadvydas’s release must be affirmed.
