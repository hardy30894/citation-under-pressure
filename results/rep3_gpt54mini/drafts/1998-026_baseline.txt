**Argument**

A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of the right to appeal, while contrary to Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32, does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief. Relief is available only where the omission caused prejudice. This Court has repeatedly held that “not every error” in a criminal proceeding warrants collateral relief; rather, the petitioner must show that the error had a substantial and injurious effect or influence on the proceeding. Brecht v. Abrahamson, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993); United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 166 (1982). That principle controls here.

Rule 32’s advisement requirement serves a simple purpose: to ensure that a defendant knows of the right to appeal. But where, as the District Court found after an evidentiary hearing, the defendant already knew of that right and chose not to appeal, the rule’s purpose has been fulfilled notwithstanding the court’s omission. The Third Circuit correctly treated the violation as subject to harmless-error analysis and held that petitioner suffered no prejudice because he was aware of his appellate rights. See Peguero v. United States, 108 F.3d 89 (3d Cir. 1997). That conclusion accords with this Court’s general harmless-error jurisprudence. Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 306-08 (1991).

Petitioner’s contrary rule—that any Rule 32 violation automatically requires vacatur—finds no support in this Court’s cases. This Court has distinguished between errors that are structural and require automatic reversal, and ordinary procedural errors that are subject to harmless-error review. Fulminante, 499 U.S. at 309-10; Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 8-15 (1999). A failure to advise a defendant of the right to appeal does not “defy analysis by harmless-error standards” in the way that denial of counsel or bias of the trial judge does. Fulminante, 499 U.S. at 309. Where the defendant in fact knew of the right and made a conscious decision not to pursue it, the omission is not a basis for collateral relief.

The Fifth and Tenth Circuits have likewise recognized that a Rule 32 omission does not warrant relief absent prejudice. See United States v. DeSoto, 839 F.2d 1450, 1453-54 (11th Cir. 1988); United States v. Allen, 983 F.2d 940, 942-43 (9th Cir. 1993). Those decisions reflect the settled rule that collateral relief is reserved for “fundamental defect[s] which inherently result[] in a complete miscarriage of justice.” Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A defendant who knew of the right to appeal cannot show such a miscarriage merely because the judge failed to recite the rule’s warning words.

Petitioner’s claim also fails because habeas relief is not available for a technical violation divorced from actual harm. In Reed v. Farley, 512 U.S. 339, 348-54 (1994), this Court held that a statutory violation does not warrant collateral relief absent a showing of prejudice or a fundamental defect. The same reasoning applies with equal force to a Rule 32 omission. The writ is not a vehicle for correcting every procedural misstep; it exists to redress unlawful confinement. Frady, 456 U.S. at 165; Hill, 368 U.S. at 428.

Petitioner cannot establish prejudice on this record. The District Court found that he knew of his right to appeal at sentencing, and the Court of Appeals accepted that finding. Once petitioner had actual knowledge, the judge’s failure to repeat the admonition did not deprive him of any meaningful opportunity to seek review. See Peguero, 108 F.3d 89. Because petitioner elected not to appeal despite that knowledge, any Rule 32 error was harmless. See Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a); Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946).

Finally, petitioner’s per se rule would create an unwarranted windfall. It would permit habeas relief even when the defendant was fully aware of the right to appeal and knowingly chose not to exercise it. That result would exceed Rule 32’s purpose and conflict with this Court’s insistence that collateral relief requires actual prejudice. Hill, 368 U.S. at 428; Reed, 512 U.S. at 353-54. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
