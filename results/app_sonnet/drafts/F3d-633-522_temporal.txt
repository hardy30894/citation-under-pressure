# ARGUMENT

## I. The Release Signed Under Compressed Deadlines Cannot Extinguish Statutory Worker-Protection Rights That Congress Intended to Be Inalienable Absent Genuine, Informed Consent.

The WARN Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109, exists to give workers the "advance notice" that Congress determined was necessary to protect them from the sudden shock of mass layoffs. Like the Fair Labor Standards Act and the Federal Employers' Liability Act, WARN is remedial legislation enacted "in the public interest to effectuate a legislative policy," and this Court's predecessors have long recognized that waivers of such rights are suspect precisely because an individual worker's private interest in signing a release for immediate cash often diverges from the public interest Congress sought to protect. In *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil*, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), the Supreme Court held that an employee could not bargain away his statutory right to liquidated damages under the FLSA through a private settlement, because permitting such waivers would undermine the very protections the statute was designed to secure. The Court reaffirmed this principle a year later in *D.A. Schulte, Inc. v. Gangi*, 328 U.S. 108 (1946), holding that even a bona fide, negotiated compromise of a FLSA claim could not bar a subsequent suit for the statutory amount absent the imprimatur of the Secretary of Labor or a court.

The same logic governs releases of statutory rights under the FELA. In *Boyd v. Grand Trunk Western Railroad Co.*, 338 U.S. 263 (1949), the Court struck down a contractual provision that would have restricted an employee's remedy under the Act, holding that private agreements cannot be permitted to defeat the statute's remedial design. By contrast, in *Callen v. Pennsylvania Railroad Co.*, 332 U.S. 625 (1948), the Court enforced a FELA release only because it found the release supported by adequate consideration, executed without fraud, and entered into with the employee's full appreciation of what he was surrendering. The DHL release satisfies none of those conditions. Drivers were given mere days—simultaneously with an unrelated job-rebid decision—to decide whether to sign away every claim "known or unknown," including their WARN Act rights, in exchange for benefits DHL was already contemplating providing. That is not the kind of deliberate, informed compromise *Callen* demands; it is the kind of one-sided extraction of statutory rights that *Brooklyn Savings Bank*, *Schulte*, and *Boyd* forbid.

## II. Even If WARN Claims Are Waivable, This Release Was Not Knowing and Voluntary Because It Was a Contract of Adhesion Procured Through Unequal Bargaining Power and Manufactured Time Pressure.

Contract doctrine long predating this litigation instructs that agreements procured through gross disparities in bargaining power and understanding are not entitled to ordinary enforcement. In *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.*, 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court explained that unconscionability includes "an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party." The same concern animated the New Jersey Supreme Court's decision in *Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors, Inc.*, 32 N.J. 358, 161 A.2d 69 (1960), which refused to enforce a standardized release where the weaker party had no realistic opportunity to negotiate its terms.

That is precisely what happened here. DHL announced the shutdown of its domestic operations on November 10, 2008; by December 5 it had a signed severance agreement, and workers were given only days—until December 11 for drivers electing the ten-week plan, and until December 22 for others—to decide whether to forfeit every legal claim they might have, including WARN Act claims for the very layoffs then in progress. This was not a negotiated exchange between equals; it was a take-it-or-leave-it release imposed on employees who had just watched their jobs disappear and who had no realistic opportunity to consult counsel meaningfully or weigh the value of what they were surrendering, notwithstanding the boilerplate seven-day revocation language. Under *Williams* and *Henningsen*, such adhesive terms, extracted under economic duress and artificial deadlines, cannot be read to accomplish the sweeping waiver DHL now claims.

## III. Any Ambiguity in the Release's Scope Must Be Resolved in Favor of Preserving the Statutory Remedy, Not Forfeiting It.

Finally, even were the Court to conclude the release was validly executed, its scope must be construed narrowly. Remedial labor legislation "must not be interpreted or applied in a narrow, grudging manner." *Tennessee Coal, Iron & R.R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123*, 321 U.S. 590, 597 (1944). That principle counsels against reading general release language—drafted unilaterally by DHL—to encompass a WARN Act claim that arose from the very transaction (the mass layoff) that prompted the release in the first place, particularly where workers had no genuine opportunity to appreciate that they were surrendering notice-pay claims tied to the shutdown itself.

For these reasons, the release cannot bar Appellants' WARN Act claims, and the district court's judgment should be reversed.
