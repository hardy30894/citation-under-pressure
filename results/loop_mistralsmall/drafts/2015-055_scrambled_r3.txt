**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Gregory Welch respectfully submits that the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133 (2010)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it places certain kinds of primary, personal conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 622 (1965) (citing *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961)). The rule announced in *Johnson* is such a rule.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the vagueness doctrine is rooted in the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. *Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972) (citing *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 387, 391 (1926)). In *Johnson*, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague because it fails to provide fair warning of the conduct it prohibits and invites arbitrary enforcement. 559 U.S. at 137. The Court explained that the residual clause’s “serious potential risk” standard required courts to imagine a “generic” version of the defendant’s crime and then assess the hypothetical risk posed by that imaginary crime. *Id.* at 139. This approach, the Court concluded, “produces [a] hopeless indeterminacy of scope.” *Id.* at 143.

The Court has long recognized that a statute that fails to provide fair warning of the conduct it prohibits violates due process. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (“No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of [a] penal statute.”). The *Johnson* rule thus places Welch’s prior conviction for Florida “strong arm” robbery beyond the reach of the ACCA’s residual clause because that clause, as applied to his conviction, was unconstitutionally vague.

Moreover, the Court has held that a new constitutional rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “requires the observance of those procedures that are implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Mapp*, 367 U.S. at 656 (quoting *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937)). The *Johnson* rule is such a rule. The vagueness doctrine is a fundamental principle of due process that ensures that criminal laws provide fair notice and prevent arbitrary enforcement. *Papachristou*, 405 U.S. at 162. The *Johnson* rule thus implicates the fundamental fairness required by the Due Process Clauses.

The Court has also held that a new constitutional rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “breaks new ground” or “implements a watershed rule of criminal procedure.” *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244, 251 (1969) (quoting *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 629). The *Johnson* rule is such a rule. It breaks new ground by clarifying that the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague and thus cannot be applied to predicate offenses. 559 U.S. at 143. The rule also implements a watershed rule of criminal procedure by ensuring that the vagueness doctrine is applied consistently to prevent arbitrary enforcement of criminal laws. *Papachristou*, 405 U.S. at 162.

Finally, the Court has held that a new constitutional rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “could prospectively alter the outcome of the case.” *Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 323 (1987). The *Johnson* rule could prospectively alter the outcome of Welch’s case because it would remove his prior conviction for Florida “strong arm” robbery from the ACCA’s residual clause, thereby reducing his sentence. 559 U.S. at 143.

In sum, the *Johnson* rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review because it places Welch’s prior conviction beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe, implicates fundamental fairness, breaks new ground, and could prospectively alter the outcome of his case. The Eleventh Circuit erred by failing to apply the *Johnson* rule retroactively to Welch’s collateral challenge. This Court should reverse the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
