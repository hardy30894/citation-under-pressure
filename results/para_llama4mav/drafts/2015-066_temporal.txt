The use of Michael Bryant, Jr.’s uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is inapplicable to tribal court proceedings, as established in cases such as Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), and Native American Church v. Navajo Tribal Council, 272 F.2d 131 (10th Cir. 1959). 

In enacting § 117(a), Congress exercised its plenary power to permit more vigorous federal prosecution of serious crimes. The statute does not rely on the validity of the tribal court convictions under the Sixth Amendment, but rather on their validity under the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) and tribal law. 

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), is misplaced, as it imposes a Sixth Amendment right to counsel on tribal courts, contrary to established precedent. The use of prior uncounseled convictions in a recidivist statute is analogous to the use of prior convictions in sentencing enhancements, which was discussed in cases such as Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948). In Gryger, the Court held that a recidivist statute does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment, as it "does not impose a penalty for the earlier offense, but rather increases the punishment for the latest offense." Id. at 732. 

Similarly, § 117(a) punishes Bryant for his 2011 offenses, not for his prior tribal court convictions. The use of those convictions as predicate offenses is a matter of legislative discretion, and Congress has chosen to permit their use in § 117(a) prosecutions. 

The Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split and undermines the purpose of § 117(a). The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) is consistent with the principles of federal Indian law and the plenary power of Congress to regulate Indian affairs, as established in cases such as United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375 (1886), and Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock, 187 U.S. 553 (1903). Therefore, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
