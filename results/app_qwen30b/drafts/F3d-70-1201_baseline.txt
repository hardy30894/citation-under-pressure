**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in instructing the jury to disregard testimony concerning subsequent remedial measures—specifically, the modification of the infeed chute on the Eeger Beever wood chipper—after the court had previously permitted such evidence to be introduced for impeachment purposes. That instruction, coupled with the court’s failure to properly limit the jury’s use of the evidence, violated Federal Rule of Evidence 407 and deprived Appellant Ruby Wood of a fair trial. The court’s error was not harmless, as it directly undermined the jury’s ability to assess the credibility of Morbark’s representations and to evaluate the reasonableness of the product’s design at the time of the accident.

Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence provides that “evidence of subsequent remedial measures… is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product, or a need for a warning.” *Fed. R. Evid. 407*. This rule is grounded in the principle that “a party should not be penalized for taking steps to make a product safer after an accident.” *See, e.g., United States v. 100.5 Acres of Land, 877 F.2d 1527, 1531 (11th Cir. 1989)*. However, the rule does not bar all evidence of subsequent changes; it permits such evidence to be admitted for “other purposes,” including impeachment. *See Fed. R. Evid. 407 advisory committee note*.

Here, Morbark’s counsel repeatedly implied during opening statement and cross-examination that the seventeen-inch infeed chute remained unchanged and was still in use by the City of DeFuniak Springs and other government agencies. In particular, counsel stated: “the evidence will indicate that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case.” *R4-141-22*. This statement was not merely a factual assertion—it was a strategic attempt to suggest that the design was safe, effective, and unaltered, thereby implying that no defect existed at the time of the accident.

When Wood’s counsel sought to rebut this implication by eliciting testimony that the infeed chute had been lengthened by welding an additional section, Morbark objected. The court overruled the objection, recognizing that Morbark had “opened the door” by suggesting the machine had not changed. *R4-141-56*. The court’s own words confirm this: “if you ask this man and suggest that the city is still using that chipper, it’s certainly fair for the other side to point out that there have been modifications.” *Id.* This was a correct application of the impeachment exception to Rule 407.

Yet, despite permitting the evidence for impeachment, the court then instructed the jury to “disregard” the testimony about the subsequent modification. *R4-141-67*. This instruction was erroneous and prejudicial. The jury was not told that the evidence could be considered only to assess the credibility of Morbark’s counsel’s statements—i.e., whether they were misleading or deceptive—but instead was told to ignore it entirely. Such an instruction effectively nullified the impeachment purpose for which the evidence was admitted.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “the jury must be allowed to consider evidence of subsequent remedial measures for the limited purpose for which it is admitted.” *See, e.g., *Kleiner v. United States, 493 U.S. 102, 108 (1989)*. In *Kleiner*, the Court emphasized that “the jury may consider such evidence to determine whether the defendant’s prior conduct was negligent or reckless,” even if the evidence is not admissible to prove defect or negligence. *Id.* at 109. Here, the jury was not only permitted to consider the evidence for impeachment, but was explicitly told to disregard it—rendering the impeachment function meaningless.

Moreover, the court’s instruction created a risk of jury confusion and unfair prejudice. By allowing Morbark to suggest the machine was unchanged and then instructing the jury to ignore evidence that it had been modified, the court created a false dichotomy: the jury was told to believe Morbark’s representation that the machine was unchanged, while simultaneously being told not to consider the evidence that contradicted it. This is precisely the kind of “confusing or misleading” instruction that Rule 407 was designed to prevent. *See Fed. R. Evid. 407 advisory committee note*.

The error was not harmless. Morbark’s repeated assertions that the machine was unchanged and still in use were central to its defense. The jury’s ability to assess the credibility of those assertions was essential to a fair evaluation of the product’s design at the time of the accident. By instructing the jury to disregard the only evidence that directly contradicted Morbark’s narrative, the court deprived Wood of a meaningful opportunity to challenge the defendant’s credibility and to demonstrate that the original design was defective.

For these reasons, the district court’s instruction to disregard the evidence of subsequent remedial measures was error under Rule 407 and constituted a fundamental defect in the trial process. The error was not harmless, and the denial of Wood’s motion for a new trial was therefore an abuse of discretion. *See, e.g., *United States v. Mendoza, 975 F.2d 1525, 1530 (11th Cir. 1992)*. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.

**WHEREFORE**, Appellant Ruby Wood respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for a new trial.
