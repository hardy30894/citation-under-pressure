**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Facilities Constitute a Public Forum Open to All Community Groups, Including Religious Ones.**

The District’s facilities are a public forum because the State has opened them for indiscriminate use by the general public during non-school hours. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951), the Supreme Court held that when a municipality “opens a forum for indiscriminate use by the public,” it may not “prohibit use by some but not by others on the basis of content.” The District’s policy of allowing community groups—including the Southern Harmonizers and the Center Moriches Free Public Library—to use its facilities demonstrates that it has created a public forum. *See id.* at 272-73 (finding a public forum where a city allowed public gatherings in a park without regard to subject matter).

**II. The District’s Denial of Access to Lamb’s Chapel Violates the First Amendment’s Command That There Shall Be No Law Abridging the Freedom of Speech.**

The District’s refusal to permit Lamb’s Chapel to show its films is a content-based restriction on speech, which is presumptively unconstitutional. In *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 293 (1951), the Court struck down a municipal ordinance that required a permit for religious services in public places, noting that such a restriction “must be justified by a compelling public interest.” The District’s policy, which singles out religious speech for exclusion, is no different. *See id.* at 294 (holding that a law “vest[ing] in an administrative official discretion to control in advance the right to speak” violates the First Amendment).

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not a Neutral Regulation of Time, Place, or Manner.**

The District argues that its policy is a neutral regulation of time, place, or manner, but this is incorrect. In *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948), the Court invalidated a municipal ordinance that banned the use of sound trucks except with a permit, noting that the law was not “content-neutral” because it allowed other speech while suppressing religious speech. Similarly, the District’s policy, which permits secular films but bans religious ones, is not neutral. *See id.* at 560-61 (finding that a law “which on its face is a prior restraint on speech” is unconstitutional).

**IV. The District’s Policy Violates the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause.**

The District’s policy discriminates against religious speech in violation of the Equal Protection Clause. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947), the Court held that a state may not “exclude some members of the community because of their faith” from public benefits. The District’s policy, which excludes religious groups from using its facilities, is precisely the kind of discrimination the Fourteenth Amendment forbids. *See id.* at 16 (holding that a state may not “aid one religion or prefer one religion over another”).

**V. The District’s Policy Does Not Serve a Compelling State Interest.**

Even if the District’s policy were content-neutral, it would still fail because it does not serve a compelling state interest. In *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945), the Court held that a state may not suppress speech unless it is “necessary to safeguard an interest of the highest order.” The District has not shown that its policy is necessary to prevent an Establishment Clause violation or any other compelling interest. *See id.* at 531 (finding that a law suppressing speech must be “narrowly drawn to achieve the asserted state interest”).

**VI. The District’s Policy Is Not Narrowly Tailored to Serve Any Legitimate Interest.**

The District’s policy is not narrowly tailored to serve any legitimate interest. In *Schneider v. Irvington*, 308 U.S. 147, 162 (1939), the Court held that a municipal ordinance banning all handbilling was unconstitutional because it was not “narrowly drawn to prevent littering.” Similarly, the District’s policy, which bans all religious films, is not narrowly tailored to serve any legitimate interest. *See id.* at 162-63 (finding that a law suppressing speech must be “narrowly drawn to achieve the asserted state interest”).

**VII. The District’s Policy Violates the First Amendment’s Establishment Clause Because It Discriminates Against Religion.**

The District’s policy violates the Establishment Clause because it discriminates against religion. In *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948), the Court held that a state may not “aid one religion or prefer one religion over another.” The District’s policy, which excludes religious groups from using its facilities, is precisely the kind of discrimination the Establishment Clause forbids. *See id.* at 210-11 (finding that a state may not “use its tax-supported property for the dissemination of religious doctrines”).

**VIII. The District’s Policy Violates the First Amendment’s Free Exercise Clause Because It Suppresses Religious Speech.**

The District’s policy violates the Free Exercise Clause because it suppresses religious speech. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940), the Court held that a state may not “prohibit the free exercise of religion” by suppressing religious speech. The District’s policy, which excludes religious groups from using its facilities, is precisely the kind of suppression the Free Exercise Clause forbids. *See id.* at 304 (finding that a state may not “restrict the dissemination of religious views”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter judgment for the petitioner.
