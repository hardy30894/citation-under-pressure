**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of Merit Systems Protection Board decisions to the Federal Circuit, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), except for “cases of discrimination” that “shall be filed in district court,” § 7703(b)(2). A case falls within the exception when an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the” Board “and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination” prohibited by enumerated statutes. § 7702(a)(1). Such “mixed cases” are defined by the employee’s allegations, not by the Board’s ultimate disposition. Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 50 (2012).

Kloeckner held that when the Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal on a procedural ground without reaching the merits of the discrimination claim, review lies in district court. Id. at 51. The Court explained that “the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction … is exclusive only when the MSPB’s decision ‘does not decide discrimination issues.’” Id. at 47 (quoting § 7703(b)(1)(A)). Because the employee had alleged both an appealable action and prohibited discrimination, the case remained a mixed case subject to district-court review regardless of the Board’s procedural ruling. Id. at 50.

The same reasoning governs jurisdictional dismissals. The statutory text turns on whether the employee “alleges” an action appealable to the Board and discrimination as its basis. § 7702(a)(1). Nothing in the text or in Kloeckner conditions that definition on the Board’s later conclusion that the action was voluntary and therefore outside its jurisdiction. Once the employee presents a colorable mixed case, “judicial review … shall be filed in district court.” § 7703(b)(2).

Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), drew an untenable distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. That distinction finds no support in the statute and was implicitly rejected by Kloeckner’s uniform treatment of pre-merits dismissals. The Court’s repeated references to “procedural grounds” merely described the facts before it; the holding rests on the employee’s allegations, not on the label the Board attaches to its dismissal. 568 U.S. at 50.

Requiring district-court review in both settings also serves the Act’s purpose of consolidating discrimination claims in a single forum experienced with such claims. Allowing the Board’s jurisdictional ruling to reroute the case to the Federal Circuit would produce the very “procedural ping-pong” Kloeckner condemned. Id. at 49.

The petition for review therefore belongs in district court.
