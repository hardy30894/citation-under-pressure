**ARGUMENT**

**I. Johnson v. United States Applies Retroactively on Collateral Review Because It Announced a New Substantive Rule of Constitutional Law.**

A new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it is substantive. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989) (plurality opinion). Substantive rules “alter[] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). Procedural rules, by contrast, “regulate only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability.” *Id.* at 353.

*Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), is substantive. The decision held that the Armed Career Criminal Act’s residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges” and is therefore void under the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 597. By invalidating that clause, *Johnson* “narrow[ed] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351, and thereby “place[d] particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish,” *id.* at 352. After *Johnson*, a defendant whose prior conviction qualified as a violent felony only under the residual clause is no longer subject to the Act’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. The rule therefore “prohibit[s] a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense.” *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989).

Because *Johnson* is substantive, it applies retroactively without regard to whether the conviction became final before or after the decision. *See Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620–21 (1998) (holding that a decision narrowing the scope of a federal statute applies retroactively on collateral review). The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply *Johnson* to Welch’s 1996 Florida robbery conviction therefore contravenes settled retroactivity doctrine.

**II. The Government’s Arguments Against Retroactivity Lack Merit.**

The government may contend that *Johnson* is merely procedural because it requires a new mode of categorical analysis. That characterization is incorrect. The vagueness holding did not prescribe new procedures for sentencing; it eliminated an entire statutory provision that had authorized enhanced punishment. *See Welch v. United States*, 578 U.S. 120, 129 (2016) (recognizing that *Johnson* “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act”). Any procedural consequences flow directly from the substantive change in the law’s coverage.

Nor does the fact that some robbery convictions might still qualify under the elements clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i), defeat retroactivity. The relevant inquiry is whether the residual clause itself has been removed from the statute, not whether every defendant previously sentenced under it will ultimately prevail. *See Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 352.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
