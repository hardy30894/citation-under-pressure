# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of Section 1113(2) Requires Only Knowledge of the Facts Underlying the Breach, Not a Litigant's Subjective Recollection of Having Absorbed Disclosed Information

Statutory construction begins, and often ends, with the text. As this Court explained long ago, "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 1113(2)'s three-year period runs from the date the plaintiff "had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." Nothing in that phrase requires that the plaintiff *remember*, at deposition years later, having read a particular document. It requires only that the underlying facts constituting the breach were known to him.

This construction accords with the longstanding rule that a person who receives a document cannot later disclaim its contents merely by declining to read it. As this Court held in *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. 45, 50 (1875), "[i]t will not do for a man to enter into a contract, and, when called upon to respond to its obligations, to say that he did not read it when he signed it, or did not know what it contained." The same logic governs here: Intel furnished Sulyma the fund fact sheets, disclosures, and plan descriptions that ERISA and its regulations required. Sulyma cannot escape the limitations consequence of that disclosure by professing, years later, that he does not recall reading them.

## II. Congress's Repeal of the Constructive-Knowledge Provision Did Not Displace the Background Principle That Disclosed Information Is Deemed Known

Respondent will argue that Congress's 1987 repeal of the separate "constructive knowledge" trigger shows that Congress meant to require subjective, remembered awareness. But repeal of one alternative trigger for accrual does not convert the remaining "actual knowledge" trigger into a memory test. Statutes must be read against the backdrop of settled common-law principles unless Congress speaks to the contrary. *Cf. United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543-44 (1940) (courts look to the statute "in the light of" its purpose and the evils it was designed to remedy). Congress eliminated only the *automatic* trigger arising from public filings unconnected to the plaintiff's own plan; it left intact the ordinary rule, reflected throughout the law of trusts and limitations, that knowledge of facts made available to a person through required disclosures is knowledge in fact.

## III. Limitations Doctrine Has Long Treated the Means of Knowledge as Equivalent to Knowledge Itself

This Court's limitations jurisprudence confirms that a plaintiff's failure to avail himself of information placed before him does not toll the running of a statute. In *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 143 (1879), the Court held that in assessing when a claim of fraud is discovered, "the means of knowledge are the same thing in effect as knowledge itself." *See also Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 348 (1874) (limitations period runs once the plaintiff had the opportunity to discover the relevant facts through reasonable diligence). Even the discovery rule this Court has recognized in equity looks to when a plaintiff, exercising diligence, could have learned the facts—not to whether he later swears he forgot having done so. *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 396-97 (1946); *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435, 448-49 (1918) (limitations begin when the cause of action "might reasonably have been discovered"). The Ninth Circuit's rule inverts this settled principle by permitting a plaintiff who was actually given the operative facts to defeat the statute simply by disclaiming recollection.

## IV. A Subjective-Recollection Standard Would Defeat the Purposes That Statutes of Limitations Serve

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that limitations periods exist to promote repose and evidentiary reliability, not to reward litigants who avoid or forget the information placed before them. "Statutes of limitation find their justification in necessity and convenience rather than in logic. They represent expedients, rather than principles." *Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson*, 325 U.S. 304, 314 (1945). Such statutes are "vital to the welfare of society and are favored in the law," *Order of Railroad Telegraphers v. Railway Express Agency*, 321 U.S. 342, 349 (1944), precisely because they force claims to be litigated while evidence remains fresh and memories reliable. A rule permitting a plaintiff's self-serving amnesia to override documented, timely disclosure would make the limitations period turn on the vagaries of individual memory rather than on objective facts capable of proof—the very evil limitations statutes are designed to avoid.

## Conclusion

Because Sulyma received the disclosures describing the alternative-investment strategy more than three years before filing suit, he had "actual knowledge of the breach or violation" under Section 1113(2) as that phrase has always been understood. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
