**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 authorizes punitive damages only upon a showing that the defendant “engaged in a discriminatory practice * * * with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That statutory text incorporates the common-law standard under which punitive damages are “not to be awarded for conduct that is merely negligent or even grossly negligent,” but only for conduct that is “outrageous” because of the defendant’s “evil motive or reckless indifference to the rights of others.” *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 46-48 (1983) (quoting Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979)). The requirement of egregious or outrageous conduct is therefore not independent of the defendant’s state of mind; it is the very measure of that state of mind.

This Court has consistently applied the same rule to federal civil-rights statutes authorizing punitive damages. In *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299 (1986), the Court reiterated that punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 are available only when the defendant’s conduct is “motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” *Id.* at 307 n.9 (quoting *Smith*, 461 U.S. at 56). The same limitation appears in *Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.*, 453 U.S. 247, 266-267 (1981), which held that punitive damages against municipalities are unavailable precisely because the common law conditioned such awards on “outrageous” misconduct.

The legislative history of § 1981a confirms that Congress intended no departure from this settled rule. The House Report stated that the 1991 Act “demands of plaintiff ‘an even higher standard’ than simple proof of intentional discrimination.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 72 (1991). Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum likewise explained that punitive damages would be available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991). These statements are consistent with the common-law tradition reflected in *Smith* and *Newport*.

The court of appeals therefore erred in holding that evidence sufficient to prove intentional discrimination automatically satisfies the punitive-damages standard. Because the record contains no evidence that the American Dental Association’s decision was accompanied by the requisite “evil motive or reckless indifference” rising to the level of outrageous conduct, the district court correctly refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed on this issue.
