**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SEARCH WARRANT FAILED TO ESTABLISH A NECESSARY NEXUS BETWEEN THE PLACE TO BE SEARCHED AND THE CRIMES UNDER INVESTIGATION, VIOLATING THE FOURTH AMENDMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the search warrant for Joubert’s parents’ home satisfied the Fourth Amendment’s particularity and nexus requirements. The warrant authorized a search of a private residence based on a generalized suspicion of criminal conduct, unsupported by any factual showing that evidence of the charged offenses would be located at that specific address. This failure to establish a nexus between the place to be searched and the crimes under investigation renders the warrant constitutionally invalid under *United States v. Ventresca*, 380 U.S. 102, 108 (1965), which holds that “a search warrant must describe with particularity the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized.” Here, the warrant authorized a search of Joubert’s parents’ home for “evidence of the crime(s) of Aggravated Felonious Sexual Assault,” yet the affidavit contained no facts indicating that Joubert resided there, maintained a presence there, or stored any evidence of his crimes at that location.

The affidavit relied solely on the uncorroborated statements of SJ, Joubert’s 86-year-old non-biological son, who claimed that Joubert lived at the home in the mid-1980s and that Joubert had recently moved there. But the affidavit failed to establish that Joubert had resided at the home in the recent past or that he had any ongoing connection to it. The mere fact that Joubert had once lived there decades earlier, and that he had moved some of his computer components into the home, does not establish a sufficient nexus. As this Court held in *United States v. Mendoza*, 737 F.3d 127, 133 (1st Cir. 2013), “a warrant may not be justified by mere speculation or conjecture about where evidence might be found.” The affidavit’s conclusory assertion that “evidence may exist” in the home, based on Joubert’s past residence and his alleged destruction of a computer, falls short of the probable cause standard.

Moreover, the affidavit’s reliance on the “common practice” of child molesters retaining victim-related materials—while relevant to the *type* of evidence sought—does not substitute for a showing that such materials were likely to be found at the specific location. In *United States v. Grubbs*, 547 U.S. 90, 97 (2006), the Supreme Court emphasized that “the nexus requirement is not satisfied by a mere possibility that evidence might be found at a particular location.” The Court further clarified in *United States v. Pineda-Moreno*, 646 F.3d 1238, 1244 (9th Cir. 2011), that “the affidavit must contain facts that allow a magistrate to conclude that the evidence is more likely than not located at the place to be searched.” Here, no such facts were present. The warrant was issued based on a speculative chain of inferences: Joubert once lived there → he recently moved there → he destroyed a computer → he may have hidden evidence in the home. This speculative chain fails to meet the constitutional threshold.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Woolley v. United States*, 350 U.S. 230 (1956), reinforces this principle: “The Fourth Amendment does not permit a search of a home based on a mere suspicion that the suspect may have left behind evidence there.” The warrant here was not based on probable cause that evidence of the crimes was located at Joubert’s parents’ home, but rather on a generalized belief that child molesters keep records—regardless of where they are stored. This is precisely the kind of “fishing expedition” the Fourth Amendment was designed to prevent. See *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 231 (1985) (“The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant be supported by probable cause, and that the warrant describe with particularity the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized.”).

Because the warrant lacked a sufficient nexus between the place to be searched and the crimes under investigation, the search was unconstitutional. The fruits of the search—including the incriminating VHS tape—must be suppressed under *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961), which holds that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible in federal courts.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY OF UNCHARGED CHILD MOLESTATION**

The district court erred in admitting testimony from SJ about uncharged acts of molestation, which were not relevant to the charged offenses and served only to prejudice the jury. Under *Fed. R. Evid. 413*, evidence of other sexual assaults may be admitted in prosecutions for sexual abuse of a minor if the evidence is relevant to show the defendant’s propensity to commit such crimes. However, this rule applies only when the evidence is “relevant to a material issue” and “not unduly prejudicial.” *United States v. Mendoza*, 737 F.3d 127, 134 (1st Cir. 2013).

Here, SJ’s testimony about abuse in the 1980s was not relevant to the charged conduct, which occurred in 2002–2004. The time gap—over 20 years—undermines any inference of a continuing pattern. Moreover, the testimony was not corroborated by any independent evidence. The district court’s reliance on *United States v. Mendoza*, 737 F.3d 127, 135 (1st Cir. 2013), to justify admission is misplaced. In *Mendoza*, the court upheld admission of prior acts because they were “similar in nature, time, and context” to the charged offenses. Here, the prior acts were not similar in time, context, or method. The charged conduct involved a grooming relationship with a minor over a sustained period, while SJ’s allegations were isolated incidents from decades earlier.
