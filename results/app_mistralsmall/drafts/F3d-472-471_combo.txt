**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS NEED FOR LEAVE.**

The FMLA requires an employee to provide "sufficient information" to apprise the employer of the need for leave, but it does not mandate the use of any particular words or formalities. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2612(e)(1); *cf. McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 803 n.14 (1973) (noting that Title VII does not require "magic words"). Burnett’s communications to Habitat—including his disclosure of his medical appointments, his prostate biopsy, and his doctor’s orders—were sufficient to put Habitat on notice that he required leave for medical treatment. *See* *Walsh v. United States Dep’t of Agric.*, 521 F. Supp. 116, 120 (D.D.C. 1981) (holding that an employee’s mention of a doctor’s appointment and need for surgery constituted sufficient notice under the FMLA).

The district court’s conclusion that Burnett failed to provide notice because he did not explicitly state he was invoking the FMLA is inconsistent with the statute’s purpose and precedent. *See* *Avitia v. Metro. Club of Chi.*, 49 F.3d 1219, 1226 (7th Cir. 1995) (noting that the FMLA’s notice requirement is "not a technical one"). The Seventh Circuit has held that an employee need not "invoke the FMLA by name" to trigger its protections. *See id.* (citing *Carafas v. Lawn*, 381 U.S. 613, 620 (1965), for the proposition that procedural requirements should not be construed to defeat substantive rights). Burnett’s repeated disclosures of his medical conditions and need for treatment were sufficient to satisfy the FMLA’s notice requirement.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S ADA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS DISABILITY.**

The ADA requires an employee to provide notice of a disability and the need for accommodation, but it does not require the use of specific language. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A); *cf. Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 432 (1971) (holding that employment practices must not discriminate on the basis of disability, even if facially neutral). Burnett’s disclosures to Habitat—including his mention of his "weak bladder," his doctor’s appointments, and his prostate biopsy—were sufficient to apprise Habitat of his disability and need for accommodation. *See* *Taylor v. Principal Fin. Group, Inc.*, 93 F.3d 155, 164 (7th Cir. 1996) (holding that an employee’s mention of a medical condition and need for leave constituted sufficient notice under the ADA).

The district court’s conclusion that Burnett failed to provide notice because he did not explicitly state he was disabled is inconsistent with the ADA’s purpose and precedent. *See* *School Bd. of Nassau Cty. v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 287 (1987) (holding that the ADA’s notice requirement is not a technical one). The Seventh Circuit has held that an employee need not "invoke the ADA by name" to trigger its protections. *See* *Taylor*, 93 F.3d at 164 (citing *McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 803 n.14). Burnett’s repeated disclosures of his medical conditions and need for treatment were sufficient to satisfy the ADA’s notice requirement.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S FMLA AND ADA CLAIMS BECAUSE HABITAT FAILED TO ENGAGE IN THE INTERACTIVE PROCESS.**

The ADA requires an employer to engage in an interactive process to determine the appropriate accommodation for an employee’s disability. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12111(9); *cf. Alexander v. Choate*, 469 U.S. 287, 295 (1985) (holding that the ADA requires reasonable accommodations for qualified individuals with disabilities). Habitat’s failure to engage in the interactive process after Burnett disclosed his disability and need for accommodation was a violation of the ADA. *See* *Taylor*, 93 F.3d at 165 (holding that an employer’s failure to engage in the interactive process constitutes a violation of the ADA).

Similarly, the FMLA requires an employer to provide leave for an employee’s serious health condition. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D); *cf. Cleveland Bd. of Educ. v. LaFleur*, 414 U.S. 632, 640 (1974) (holding that employment practices must not discriminate on the basis of pregnancy, a serious health condition). Habitat’s failure to provide Burnett with leave after he disclosed his need for medical treatment was a violation of the FMLA. *See* *Walsh*, 521 F. Supp. at 120 (holding that an employer’s failure to provide leave after an employee disclosed a need for medical treatment constitutes a violation of the FMLA).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on Burnett’s FMLA and ADA claims. Burnett provided sufficient notice of his need for leave and disability, and Habitat failed to engage in the interactive process. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
