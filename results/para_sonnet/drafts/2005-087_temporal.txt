**ARGUMENT**

**Arizona's Rule Channeling Mental-Disease Evidence to the Insanity Defense Does Not Violate Due Process**

**I. The States Possess Broad Authority to Define the Insanity Defense and to Structure the Use of Psychiatric Evidence.**

This Court has long recognized that "the science of psychiatry has become tremendously important in the disposition of criminal cases," but that its integration into the criminal law is a matter left to the States. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 800 (1952). In *Leland*, this Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process compelled adoption of any particular test or procedural framework for mental-state evidence. The Court explained that the choice of "a test of legal insanity" is "not a matter of law but a matter for psychiatrists," and that "the definition of insanity" as a defense "is one on which no rigid, mathematically precise formula could be devised or would be helpful, and which involves basic problems of responsibility for crime." *Id.* at 800-801. The Court thus declined to constitutionalize any single approach, holding that "Oregon's rule" survived because "the burden of proof is a matter of state procedure and not a violation of due process." *Id.* at 799.

*Leland*'s teaching applies with full force here. Arizona has not abolished the insanity defense; it has simply channeled psychiatric evidence to that defense rather than permitting it to be deployed a second time to negate the mens rea element directly. Just as Oregon was free to allocate the burden of proving insanity to the defendant, Arizona is free to determine the procedural channel through which mental-disease evidence enters a criminal trial.

**II. This Court Has Already Rejected the Claim That Due Process Requires Admission of Psychiatric Evidence to Negate Specific Intent Outside the Insanity Framework.**

The precise contention petitioner presses—that psychiatric evidence must be available to negate mens rea independent of, and without the burdens attached to, the insanity defense—was squarely addressed and rejected in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946). There, the defendant sought a jury instruction permitting the jury to consider evidence of a mental abnormality "not sufficient to establish insanity" as a basis for reducing murder to manslaughter, on a "partial responsibility" theory. *Id.* at 466. This Court held that no such right existed, explaining that the District Courts of the District of Columbia "have consistently followed a rule which excludes" such evidence for purposes other than the insanity defense, and that this Court would not "impose on local courts" a "novel doctrine" of partial responsibility "in the domain of local jurisprudence." *Id.* at 476. The Court emphasized that "the whole matter is one that raises questions of local policy" appropriately resolved by the courts and legislatures closest to the administration of criminal justice, not by imposition of a uniform constitutional rule. *Id.* at 476.

*Fisher* forecloses petitioner's argument. If the Constitution did not require the District of Columbia courts to permit psychiatric evidence to reduce a defendant's degree of guilt outside the insanity framework, it does not require Arizona to permit such evidence to negate intent outright. The logic is identical: both petitioner in *Fisher* and Clark here sought to use evidence of mental abnormality to defeat the mental element of the offense through a route independent of the insanity defense, and both effectively asked this Court to override a considered legislative and judicial choice about how psychiatric evidence should function in the criminal process.

**III. Due Process Requires Only Fundamental Fairness in the Trial as a Whole, Not the Availability of Every Evidentiary Avenue a Defendant Might Prefer.**

This Court's due process jurisprudence governing state evidentiary and procedural rules asks only whether the challenged rule offends "a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934), not whether the State has adopted the precise procedural structure a defendant would prefer. In *Snyder*, this Court upheld a state's discretion to exclude a defendant from a jury's view of the crime scene, reasoning that due process "does not assure to a defendant the privilege" of "recreat[ing] a procedure best suited in his own judgment to effectuate his defense," but only a proceeding fundamentally fair when viewed as a whole. *Id.* at 105, 122.

Arizona's rule easily satisfies this standard. Clark was permitted to introduce his psychiatric evidence—the trial court did not exclude it, but rather confined its legal significance to the insanity inquiry, on which Arizona, following *Leland*, was entitled to place the burden on the defendant. The State did not deprive Clark of a defense; it declined to multiply the uses of a single body of evidence in a manner this Court's precedents do not require.

**IV. The States' Traditional Primacy in Defining Substantive Criminal Law Confirms the Propriety of Arizona's Rule.**

Finally, this Court has consistently deferred to the States' authority over the substantive content of the criminal law, including the definition of criminal responsibility. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535-36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that "this Court has never articulated a general constitutional doctrine of mens rea" and that "the doctrines of criminal responsibility... are the products of state, and not federal, law"). Arizona's channeling rule is a legitimate exercise of that authority, consistent with *Leland*, *Fisher*, and *Snyder*.

For these reasons, Arizona's restriction of mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense does not violate the Due Process Clause.
