**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits specific personal jurisdiction when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction over Ford satisfies this standard because Ford purposely availed itself of the Montana market, and the claims—arising from a fatal crash in Montana—directly relate to Ford’s sustained contacts with that state.

**I. Ford Purposely Availed Itself of the Privilege of Conducting Activities in Montana**

A defendant purposefully avails itself of a forum when it “deliberately has engaged in significant activities within a State” or “has created ‘continuing obligations’ between itself and residents of the forum.” *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475–76 (1985). Ford advertises extensively in Montana, sells replacement parts and accessories through a network of dealerships and independent retailers, provides warranties and recalls to Montana residents, and offers financing and repair services. These activities are not random or attenuated; they reflect a deliberate effort to exploit the Montana market. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984) (regular circulation of magazine in forum established purposeful availment). Ford’s conduct “indicates that it has purposefully directed its activities at residents of the forum,” and therefore it “must present a compelling case that the presence of some other considerations would render jurisdiction unreasonable.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476–77.

**II. The Claims “Arise Out of or Relate to” Ford’s Forum Contacts**

The phrase “arise out of or relate to” is disjunctive and “contemplates that some relationships between the defendant and the forum will support specific jurisdiction without a causal showing.” *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 141 S. Ct. 1017, 1026 (2021). Here, the relationship between Ford’s forum activities and the claims is “close enough” because “Ford had systematically served a market in Montana for the very vehicles that allegedly malfunctioned and injured the plaintiffs.” *Id.* at 1022. The claims—design defect and failure to warn—concern the safety of Ford vehicles. Ford’s “continuous and systematic” contacts with Montana, including “advertising, selling, and servicing” its vehicles, mean that “the claims relate to Ford’s activities in the State.” *Id.* at 1028.

The accident occurred in Montana, and the injury occurred there. Ford’s failure to warn about tire tread separation is a nationwide design issue, but the injury manifested in Montana. Ford’s “selling of a product in [Montana]” is “the very activity that gives rise to the claim.” *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980). Even if the specific vehicle was not originally sold in Montana, Ford’s “stream of commerce” that regularly brings its vehicles into Montana, combined with its “additional conduct” of advertising and maintaining a service network, establishes “purposeful availment.” *Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987) (plurality opinion). Ford cannot claim surprise that a vehicle it designed and sold into the national market caused injury in a state where it actively markets and services its products.

**III. Montana’s Exercise of Jurisdiction Is Reasonable**

Ford has not shown that jurisdiction in Montana would be “unreasonable” or “unfair.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 477. Montana has a strong interest in adjudicating a dispute arising from a fatal crash on its highways. The estate’s witnesses, medical records, and evidence of damages are in Montana. Ford faces no unique burden; it “is a global automotive company that does business in all 50 States.” *Ford*, 141 S. Ct. at 1028. Fair play and substantial justice favor jurisdiction where the injury occurred and the defendant deliberately sought the forum’s market.

**IV. Ford’s Alternative Arguments Are Unavailing**

Ford argues that “none of the defendant’s contacts with that state caused the plaintiff’s claims.” But causation is not the sole touchstone. In *Keeton*, the Court upheld jurisdiction over a libel claim where the defendant’s forum contacts (circulation) were not the cause of the injury, yet “the combination of the defendant’s contacts with the forum and the plaintiff’s residence in the forum supported jurisdiction.” *Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 780. Similarly, here, Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana, coupled with the fact that the injury occurred within the state, satisfy the “relate to” standard. The “arise out of or relate to” test “does not require a causal link in the strict sense.” *Ford*, 141 S. Ct. at 1026 (internal quotation marks omitted). Ford’s contacts are “not merely random, fortuitous, or attenuated.” *Id.* at 1027.

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court properly exercised specific personal jurisdiction over Ford. The judgment should be affirmed.
