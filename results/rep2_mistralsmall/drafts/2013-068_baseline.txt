**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT MASSACHUSETTS’ 35-FOOT BUFFER ZONE IS A CONSTITUTIONALLY VALID TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

The First Circuit properly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone as a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral time, place, and manner regulation. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (explaining that such regulations are permissible if they are content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels for communication). The statute satisfies each of these requirements.

**A. The Massachusetts Buffer Zone Is Content-Neutral and Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The statute does not target speech based on its message or viewpoint. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-77 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014). It applies equally to all individuals—regardless of their message—who seek to enter or remain within the buffer zone. *See id.* at 177. The statute’s exemptions for clinic patients, employees, and law enforcement further demonstrate its neutrality. *See id.* at 177-78. Because the law does not single out any particular viewpoint or subject matter, it is content-neutral. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar buffer zone as content-neutral).

**B. The Buffer Zone Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve the Significant Governmental Interest of Public Safety and Patient Access.**

The Commonwealth has a substantial interest in ensuring safe access to healthcare facilities. *See id.* at 715 (recognizing the importance of protecting patients and employees from harassment and obstruction). The buffer zone directly advances this interest by preventing congestion and intimidation at clinic entrances. *See id.* at 720. The 35-foot distance is narrowly tailored; it is no broader than necessary to achieve the government’s objectives. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799 (explaining that a regulation need not be the least restrictive means, only that it is narrowly tailored). The statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication, including speech outside the buffer zone and the distribution of literature. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181.

**C. The Buffer Zone Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels for Communication.**

The plaintiffs retain meaningful opportunities to engage in speech. *See id.* at 181-82. They may speak, pray, and distribute literature outside the buffer zone. *See id.* The record reflects that the plaintiffs have successfully communicated with patients before and after they enter the buffer zone. *See id.* The First Circuit correctly concluded that the statute does not foreclose effective communication. *See id.*

**II. HILL V. COLORADO REMAINS GOOD LAW AND SHOULD NOT BE LIMITED OR OVERRULED.**

The First Circuit properly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, in upholding the Massachusetts buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. *Hill* remains controlling precedent and should not be limited or overruled.

**A. Hill v. Colorado Is Controlling Precedent.**

*Hill* directly addresses the constitutionality of buffer zones around healthcare facilities. *See* 530 U.S. at 706. The Supreme Court upheld Colorado’s 100-foot buffer zone as a valid time, place, and manner regulation. *See id.* at 720. The Massachusetts statute is materially indistinguishable. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. The First Circuit correctly applied *Hill* in this case. *See id.*

**B. Hill v. Colorado Should Not Be Limited or Overruled.**

The plaintiffs argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled in light of subsequent First Amendment decisions. *See Brief for Petitioners* at 40-45. However, *Hill* remains good law. *See, e.g., National Institute of Family and Life Advocates v. Becerra*, 138 S. Ct. 2361, 2374 (2018) (citing *Hill* with approval). The Supreme Court has not indicated any intent to overrule *Hill*, and its reasoning remains sound. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-20. The buffer zone in *Hill* was upheld as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored regulation that left open ample alternative channels for communication. *See id.* The same is true here.

**CONCLUSION**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone as a valid time, place, and manner regulation under the First Amendment. The statute is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Hill v. Colorado* remains good law and should not be limited or overruled. The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
