The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), allows Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down an intelligible principle to which the delegatee must conform. Id. at 372. The CSA provides such intelligible principles, including specific factors the Attorney General must consider when scheduling substances, such as the substance's potential for abuse, its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). These guidelines constrain the Attorney General's discretion and ensure that the delegation is not overly broad.

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is also constitutional. Although it grants the Attorney General significant authority, it is limited to situations where there is an "imminent hazard to the public safety." 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is sufficient to guide the Attorney General's decision-making and prevent arbitrary or capricious actions. See Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944) (upholding delegation where statute provided standard for delegatee to follow).

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also authorized. The CSA explicitly permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions to other officials, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and the Attorney General has done so through regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This sub-delegation is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505 (1974), which allowed the Attorney General to delegate authority to the DEA's predecessor agency.

The Toubys argue that the delegation is improper because it allows the DEA to create crimes without sufficient oversight. However, the CSA's intelligible principles and the Attorney General's oversight of the DEA mitigate this concern. See Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (noting that availability of judicial review can help ensure that delegation is not overly broad). Although 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6) precludes judicial review of the Attorney General's temporary scheduling decisions, this does not render the delegation unconstitutional. See Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (noting that availability of judicial review is presumed, but can be precluded by clear congressional intent).

The Supreme Court has upheld delegations in a variety of contexts, including those involving complex and technical fields like controlled substances. See Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation in banking regulation). The CSA's delegation to the Attorney General is similarly constitutional.

Other courts have considered the constitutionality of the CSA's delegation and have upheld it. See United States v. Emerson, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); United States v. Spain, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987). These decisions demonstrate that the CSA's delegation is consistent with established precedent.

In sum, the CSA's delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional because it provides intelligible principles to guide the Attorney General's decision-making. The sub-delegation to the DEA is also authorized and consistent with Supreme Court precedent. The Toubys' challenge to their convictions should be rejected.
