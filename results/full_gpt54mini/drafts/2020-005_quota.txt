The Montana Supreme Court’s decision should be affirmed because Ford’s forum contacts are not used to justify jurisdiction in the abstract; they are used to show that Ford purposefully cultivated the Montana market for the very type of product that allegedly injured respondent’s decedent. Specific jurisdiction turns on “an affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy,” not a rigid but-for causation test. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). The question is whether the suit “arise[s] out of or relate[s] to” the defendant’s forum contacts. Id. at 262; Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 472-73 (1985).

This Court has long held that due process permits jurisdiction when a defendant has purposefully availed itself of the forum and the litigation results from alleged injuries that are connected to those contacts. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945). The requisite connection need not be the sole causal source of the injury. In Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984), the Court distinguished general from specific jurisdiction but did not impose a strict causation requirement for the latter. Likewise, World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), emphasized that where a corporation “serves a market” in the forum, it may reasonably anticipate being haled into court there when its products cause injury there.

Ford did exactly that. Ford advertises, sells, and services the same model of vehicle and replacement parts in Montana. That sustained forum exploitation makes this case unlike Walden v. Fiore, 571 U.S. 277, 284-85 (2014), where the defendant’s conduct occurred entirely outside the forum and jurisdiction rested only on the plaintiffs’ residence. Here, Ford’s Montana activity is not “random,” “fortuitous,” or “attenuated.” Burger King, 471 U.S. at 475.

Nor does Bristol-Myers require dismissal. There, the Court rejected jurisdiction where nonresident plaintiffs sued in California over injuries from a drug they never obtained in California and where the defendant’s California contacts were unrelated to the plaintiffs’ claims. Bristol-Myers, 582 U.S. at 264. By contrast, respondent alleges a defect in a Ford Explorer, and Ford systematically sold, advertised, and serviced Explorers and Explorer parts in Montana. The “relate to” prong necessarily has independent content; otherwise, the Supreme Court would have said “arise out of.” Id. at 262; Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 141 S. Ct. 1017, 1026-28 (2021).

That conclusion is confirmed by precedent recognizing that a defendant may be sued where it has deliberately served the market for the very product at issue, even if the particular item entered the forum through a later resale. Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770, 773-74 (1984), upheld jurisdiction where the defendant had systematically exploited the forum market. Calder v. Jones, 465 U.S. 783, 789-90 (1984), likewise teaches that purposeful forum conduct giving rise to the suit satisfies due process. And Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 480 U.S. 102, 112-13 (1987), while divided on stream-of-commerce theory, did not displace the principle that deliberate market exploitation supports jurisdiction.

Ford’s contrary argument would collapse specific jurisdiction into strict proximate cause. But the Court has repeatedly rejected that cramped formulation. In Burger King, 471 U.S. at 472, the Court required only that a defendant’s forum conduct be such that it should “reasonably anticipate being haled into court” there. In J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro, 564 U.S. 873, 881-85 (2011), the controlling opinions turned on purposeful availment, not on a universal causation rule. And in Ford, 141 S. Ct. at 1026, the Court squarely held that the plaintiff need not show that the defendant’s forum contacts caused the particular accident; it is enough that the claims “relate to” those contacts.

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction therefore comports with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe, 326 U.S. at 316. Ford is a global manufacturer that markets the same vehicles and replacement parts in Montana and can readily defend there. World-Wide Volkswagen, 444 U.S. at 292, 297. Because Ford purposefully availed itself of the Montana market for the same type of vehicle and components involved in this suit, respondent’s claims sufficiently “relate to” Ford’s Montana contacts. The judgment should be affirmed.
