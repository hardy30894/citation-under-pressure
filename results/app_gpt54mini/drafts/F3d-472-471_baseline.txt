**Argument**

### I. The district court erred in holding that Burnett failed to give Habitat sufficient notice under the FMLA.

The Family and Medical Leave Act does not require an employee to use talismanic words or to prove a diagnosis before giving notice. It requires only that the employee provide enough information for the employer to reasonably understand that leave may be needed for an FMLA-qualifying reason. *29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1); 29 U.S.C. § 2613(a); Aubuchon v. Knauf Fiberglass, GmbH, 359 F.3d 950, 953-54 (7th Cir. 2004).* An employee need not “expressly assert rights under the FMLA” so long as the employer has enough information to make further inquiry. *Burnett v. LFW Inc., 472 F.3d 471, 478-79 (7th Cir. 2006); Byrne v. Avon Prods., Inc., 328 F.3d 379, 381-82 (7th Cir. 2003); 29 C.F.R. § 825.303(b).*

Under this Court’s precedents, notice is sufficient when the employee tells the employer enough facts to suggest a serious health condition and the need for leave. In *Byrne*, the Court explained that the employer must be alerted to a “probable basis” for FMLA leave, not given a formal legal claim. 328 F.3d at 381-82. Likewise, in *Stevenson v. Hyre Electric Co., 505 F.3d 720, 725-26 (7th Cir. 2007)*, the Court reiterated that notice may be informal and that the key question is whether the employer had reason to suspect a qualifying condition. Habitat had that information here.

Burnett told Polo in October 2003 that he had a “weak bladder” and was going to see a doctor. In December, after a week-long absence, he reported that he had undergone a physical, had two serious problems—high PSA and cholesterol—and had upcoming appointments with a urologist. On December 16, he told Habitat managers and a union representative that he had been “sick,” felt he was “getting sick or was sick,” and linked his condition to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. On January 7, he notified Habitat that he would undergo a prostate biopsy and gave a supervisor a document describing the biopsy procedure, including that elevated PSA is an indication for the test.

Taken together, those facts plainly were enough to trigger a duty to inquire further. A prostate biopsy, abnormal PSA results, recurring urinary symptoms, and repeated doctor visits are not minor ailments. Under the FMLA’s implementing regulations, “continuing treatment by a health care provider” and conditions involving “incapacity” may qualify as serious health conditions. *29 C.F.R. § 825.115(a), (b); Burnett, 472 F.3d at 479-80.* Burnett’s reports gave Habitat a concrete medical context and a clear signal that his absences were medically related.

The district court erred by requiring Burnett to spell out a diagnosis or prove that he had prostate cancer. That is not the law. In *Burnett*, this Court held that an employee’s description of symptoms and medical appointments can provide sufficient notice even without a formal diagnosis. 472 F.3d at 478-80. Employers are not entitled to sit back and demand medical precision from a lay employee who is in the midst of a developing condition. *Byrne, 328 F.3d at 381-82.* Habitat knew enough to act; indeed, Polo himself circulated an email stating that Burnett should be afforded sick leave for doctor’s appointments on January 6 and 8. That internal response confirms that Habitat understood Burnett was seeking leave for medical reasons.

Summary judgment therefore should not have been entered on the FMLA claim. The question is not whether Burnett had already proven the ultimate seriousness of his condition, but whether Habitat had sufficient notice to investigate and consider FMLA leave. *Burnett, 472 F.3d at 478-80.* On this record, a reasonable jury could conclude that it did.

### II. The district court also erred in granting summary judgment on Burnett’s ADA claim.

The ADA likewise does not require an employee to utter a precise statutory formula before the employer must consider accommodation. Once the employer knows of a disability or a medical condition that may require accommodation, it must engage in an interactive process and may not ignore the need for adjustment. *42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A); Beck v. University of Wisconsin Bd. of Regents, 75 F.3d 1130, 1135-37 (7th Cir. 1996); Hoffman v. Caterpillar, Inc., 256 F.3d 568, 572-73 (7th Cir. 2001).*

Here, Burnett gave Habitat repeated notice that he had a urinary/bladder condition, that he was undergoing testing, and that he needed medical appointments. He specifically told Polo that his “weak bladder” made a transfer undesirable because it would reduce restroom access. That is an accommodation-related statement, not merely a complaint. Habitat therefore knew both that Burnett faced a medical limitation and that work conditions affecting bathroom access mattered to him. Under *Beck*, the employer’s duty is triggered when the need for accommodation is apparent and the employee’s communication is sufficient to permit the interactive process to begin. 75 F.3d at 1135-37.

The district court’s contrary ruling improperly treated Burnett’s communications as inadequate because he did not name a disability in legal terms. But the ADA asks whether the employer had notice of a limitation and a possible need for accommodation, not whether the employee delivered a formal declaration. *Hoffman, 256 F.3d at 572-73; Beck, 75 F.3d at 1135-37.* The evidence here would permit a jury to find that Habitat knew enough to explore accommodation and instead responded with escalating discipline.

Because genuine disputes of material fact exist as to Habitat’s notice under both statutes, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
