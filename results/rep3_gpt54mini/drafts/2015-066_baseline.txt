**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not permit the Government to transform a constitutionally valid tribal-court misdemeanor conviction into a predicate for a federal felony when the prior conviction was uncounseled and resulted in imprisonment. The Sixth Amendment problem arises not in the tribal proceeding itself, but in the federal use of that conviction as an element of a new offense carrying felony punishment. See **In re Winship**, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); **United States v. Bryant**, 769 F.3d 671, 677-81 (9th Cir. 2014). A conviction obtained without counsel and resulting in jail is the very kind of unreliable adjudication the Sixth Amendment forbids the Government from using to sustain guilt in a later criminal prosecution. See **Alabama v. Shelton**, 535 U.S. 654, 661-62 (2002); **United States v. Ant**, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394-96 (9th Cir. 1989).

1. **Nichols addressed sentencing, not proof of an element of a felony.**  
The Government relies on **Nichols v. United States**, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), but Nichols held only that a valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used to enhance a sentence where the conviction itself did not violate the Sixth Amendment. Id. at 746-48. That distinction matters because the Court emphasized the difference between sentencing factors and offense elements: guilt must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt, while sentencing facts may be established under a lesser standard. Id. at 748; **Winship**, 397 U.S. at 364. Section 117(a) does not involve a discretionary sentencing enhancement. It makes prior convictions an element of the federal offense itself. Under Winship, the Government must prove each element with constitutionally reliable evidence. 397 U.S. at 364; see **Apprendi v. New Jersey**, 530 U.S. 466, 476-77 (2000). Nichols did not authorize the use of an uncounseled prior conviction to establish guilt for a new felony.

2. **Shelton confirms that imprisonment obtained without counsel is constitutionally defective.**  
In **Alabama v. Shelton**, the Court held that a suspended sentence activating incarceration cannot stand where the defendant lacked counsel in the misdemeanor case. 535 U.S. at 662-68. The Court reasoned that a defendant may not be “sent to prison” on the basis of an uncounseled conviction. Id. at 662. Bryant’s tribal convictions resulted in imprisonment without counsel. Even though tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment in the same way as state and federal courts, the federal Constitution forbids using those convictions as the basis for felony liability in a later prosecution. See **Ant**, 882 F.2d at 1394-96; **Bryant**, 769 F.3d at 677-81.

3. **Tribal-court convictions cannot be given greater federal effect than their constitutional reliability permits.**  
It is true that the Sixth Amendment does not directly apply to tribal proceedings. See **United States v. Wheeler**, 435 U.S. 313, 328-29 (1978); **Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.**, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). But Congress has not authorized federal courts to treat uncounseled, imprisoning tribal convictions as conclusive predicates for a federal felony. Section 117(a) must be construed against the background principle that criminal convictions used to establish guilt must be reliable. See **Winship**, 397 U.S. at 364; **Shelton**, 535 U.S. at 662. The Government’s rule would permit a conviction that could not constitutionally support incarceration in state court to support felony liability in federal court. That result is incompatible with the Sixth Amendment’s core concern for reliability. See **Gideon v. Wainwright**, 372 U.S. 335, 343-45 (1963); **Burgett v. Texas**, 389 U.S. 109, 114-15 (1967).

4. **The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule conflicts with the structure of recidivist statutes and this Court’s precedents.**  
Recidivist statutes punish the last offense, but only after the Government proves the predicate convictions in a constitutionally permissible manner. See **Nichols**, 511 U.S. at 747; **United States v. Rodriquez**, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). Section 117(a) does not create a free-standing exception to the Sixth Amendment merely because the prior convictions occurred in tribal court. If the predicate convictions were uncounseled and resulted in imprisonment, their use to establish an element of the federal offense violates the Constitution. See **Ant**, 882 F.2d at 1396; **Bryant**, 769 F.3d at 677-81.

For those reasons, the Court should reverse and hold that Bryant’s uncounseled tribal-court convictions could not constitutionally serve as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).
