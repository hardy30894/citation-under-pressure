# ARGUMENT

## I. A Private Individual Lacks Standing to Challenge a Federal Statute as Exceeding Congress's Enumerated Powers or Violating the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States, "or to the people," those powers not delegated to the United States. U.S. Const. amend. X. But this Court has already resolved whether a private litigant, unaccompanied by the State whose sovereignty is supposedly threatened, may invoke that reservation to defeat a federal prosecution. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that a federal agency's sale of electricity intruded on matters reserved to the States. This Court held that the companies could not raise the claim at all, explaining that "the appellants, absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." Id. at 144. That holding was not incidental to the Court's reasoning; it was an independent and sufficient ground for decision, and it controls here.

Tennessee Electric Power Co. reflects a structural feature of the Tenth Amendment, not a happenstance of that case's facts. The Amendment protects the States' residual sovereignty as governmental entities operating within the federal system; it is the States, not private citizens, whose prerogatives are said to be invaded when Congress legislates beyond its enumerated powers. A private defendant's interest in avoiding prosecution is fully vindicated by the panoply of individual rights the Constitution independently guarantees—due process, fair notice, and the like. The Tenth Amendment adds nothing to that individual protection; it exists to preserve "a healthy balance of power between the States and the Federal Government," Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 458 (1991), a balance the States themselves are equipped, and entitled, to defend.

This conclusion also follows from ordinary principles of standing. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that a litigant "generally must assert his own legal rights and interests, and cannot rest his claim to relief on the legal rights or interests of third parties." Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 499 (1975). Just as a citizen may not sue to vindicate the interests of a sovereign that has chosen not to object, see Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 485-86 (1923) (a taxpayer's "interest in the moneys of the Treasury . . . is comparatively minute and indeterminable" and insufficient to challenge federal spending as exceeding congressional power), a criminal defendant may not commandeer a State's sovereign prerogative to test the boundaries of federal power that the State has not itself asserted.

The overwhelming majority of the courts of appeals to consider the question agree. As the Third Circuit correctly recognized below, five circuits have denied private parties standing to raise Tenth Amendment challenges, while only two have permitted it. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). As the First Circuit explained, when a State declines to challenge a federal enactment, "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35. That is precisely the posture here: no Commonwealth has objected to § 229's application to Bond's conduct, and it is not for a criminal defendant to manufacture, on the Commonwealth's behalf, a controversy the Commonwealth itself has not raised.

## II. Nothing About This Prosecution Warrants Departure From That Rule.

Petitioner cannot escape Tennessee Electric Power Co. by characterizing it as dicta. As the First Circuit observed in rejecting an identical argument, this Court "dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits, and hence, the former holding is an alternative ground, rather than obiter dictum." Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 34; see California v. United States, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978). Because the standing determination in Tennessee Electric Power Co. implicated the federal courts' constitutional jurisdiction, it necessarily preceded and controls any discussion of the merits. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95, 101-02 (1998) (requiring resolution of jurisdictional questions before reaching the merits of a claim).

Nor does the prosecutorial context change the analysis. That Bond faces criminal liability rather than civil regulation does not transform the Tenth Amendment's protection of state sovereignty into a personal right she may assert. The federalism interest she invokes belongs to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, which has taken no position suggesting that § 229's application to Bond's poisoning campaign intrudes upon its reserved authority. Absent the State's participation, Bond's claim is exactly the kind of "generalized grievance" this Court's precedents forbid a private party from prosecuting alone. See Mellon, 262 U.S. at 488.

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
