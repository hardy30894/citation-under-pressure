## ARGUMENT

### I. The warrant lacked the constitutionally required nexus to Joubert’s parents’ home.

The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant be supported by probable cause and describe with particularity the place to be searched and the things to be seized. U.S. Const. amend. IV; *Agnello v. United States*, 269 U.S. 20, 30-31 (1925). Probable cause to search a place exists only when the affidavit establishes a fair probability that contraband or evidence will be found there. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 162 (1925); *Johnson v. United States*, 333 U.S. 10, 14-15 (1948). That showing must connect the evidence sought to the specific premises. *Nathanson v. United States*, 290 U.S. 41, 47 (1933); *Giordenello v. United States*, 357 U.S. 480, 485-86 (1958).

Here, the affidavit showed only that Joubert had previously lived elsewhere, that he had been accused of molestation, and that he had dismantled a computer tower in the presence of SJ. None of that established a fair probability that evidence of any crime would be found at his parents’ home. The affidavit did not say that Joubert kept the relevant computer, photographs, or tapes there; it did not describe any observation of contraband at the residence; and it did not identify any reliable source tying the items to that address. That is precisely the sort of inferential leap the Fourth Amendment forbids. *Agnello*, 269 U.S. at 30-31; *Nathanson*, 290 U.S. at 47; *Giordenello*, 357 U.S. at 485-86.

The government may point to the officer’s experience-based assertion that people engaged in molestation often retain documents and images. But “mere affirmance of belief or suspicion is not enough.” *Nathanson*, 290 U.S. at 47. An officer’s experience may help interpret facts already in the affidavit, but it cannot create probable cause where the facts do not establish a nexus to the place to be searched. *Johnson*, 333 U.S. at 14-15; *Carroll*, 267 U.S. at 162.

Because the warrant lacked a constitutionally sufficient nexus, the evidence seized from the home should have been suppressed. *Agnello*, 269 U.S. at 30-31; *Giordenello*, 357 U.S. at 485-86.

### II. The admission of uncharged molestation evidence was an abuse of discretion and violated basic fairness.

A criminal trial must be conducted with fairness, and evidence of other misconduct is inadmissible when offered merely to prove propensity. *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948); *Boyd v. United States*, 142 U.S. 450, 540-42 (1892). The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that convictions may not rest on “the evil intent of the accused” alone, but must be proved by competent evidence of the charged offense. *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76; *Ammons v. United States*, 341 U.S. 97, 101-02 (1951).

The government’s uncharged-molestation evidence here served the classic forbidden purpose: to portray Joubert as a sexual predator and thereby make it more likely the jury would believe the charged conduct occurred. Such evidence is especially dangerous where, as here, the charged counts themselves were hotly disputed and the government relied on emotionally charged testimony about alleged abuse of other children. *Boyd*, 142 U.S. at 540-42; *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76.

This Court has long recognized that trial courts must guard against evidence whose prejudicial force overwhelms its legitimate probative value. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236-37 (1941); *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 87-88 (1923). The uncharged allegations here did not directly prove the elements of the indicted offenses; they invited the jury to convict because Joubert was said to have done bad things before. That is not a permissible basis for guilt. *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76.

### III. The Commerce Clause does not support this application of the federal statutes to Joubert.

Congress’s commerce power is broad, but it is not boundless. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 189-90 (1824); *Hammer v. Dagenhart*, 247 U.S. 251, 276-77 (1918); *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 303-04 (1936). Federal criminal statutes cannot be sustained where the regulated conduct is purely local and the asserted connection to interstate commerce is too remote. *Hammer*, 247 U.S. at 276-77; *Carter Coal*, 298 U.S. at 303-04.

Joubert’s alleged exploitation of individual children in New Hampshire was local conduct. The government’s theory depends on the presence of computers, tapes, or photographs, but the statutes were applied here to punish conduct that was neither commercial nor interstate in character. That stretches the Commerce Clause beyond the limits recognized in *Gibbons*, *Hammer*, and *Carter Coal*. Federal power may regulate channels and instrumentalities of interstate commerce, but it does not follow that every local offense involving a camera, tape, or computer becomes federal. *Gibbons*, 22 U.S. at 189-90; *Hammer*, 247 U.S. at 276-77.

### IV. The 480-month sentence is substantively unreasonable.

A sentence must be tailored to the individual offense and offender. The Eighth Amendment also forbids punishments that are grossly disproportionate to the offense. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367-68 (1910); *Solem v. United States*, 263 U.S. 605, 621-22 (1924). Although courts have discretion in sentencing, that discretion is bounded by reason and the circumstances of the case. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247-49 (1949).

A forty-year sentence for the conduct proved here is excessive in light of the record, particularly where the sentence was driven by the government’s portrayal of Joubert as irredeemable rather than by individualized consideration of the charged counts. *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 367-68; *Solem*, 263 U.S. at 621-22. The Court should vacate the sentence and remand for resentencing.
