The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA grants the NLRB primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy, and courts should defer to the Board's expert judgment. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). The NLRB's standard for polling employees is consistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1062 (1989).

The NLRB reasonably limited the circumstances in which employers may conduct polls, as polling can be "potentially disruptive and unsettling" to employees. Id. at 1061. The Board's decision to maintain the same evidentiary standard for polling as for withdrawing recognition is not arbitrary, as both actions involve an employer's assessment of a union's majority status. Id. at 1062-63.

The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit correctly deferred to the NLRB's expertise in this area. The circuit court held that the NLRB's system has its faults, but so does the one created by the courts, and the only proper course is to defer to the Board. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987); Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978).

The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is supported by substantial evidence. The Board discounted the statements of several employees, including Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, as they were made during job interviews after being told the company would be non-union. Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978). The Board also questioned the reliability of reports by one employee of the antipathy of other employees toward their union. Westbrook Bowl, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989).

The NLRB's conclusion that Allentown Mack did not have sufficient objective evidence to justify polling is reasonable and supported by the record. The company's challenge to the NLRB's factual determination is without merit, and the Court should uphold the NLRB's order for Allentown Mack to recognize and bargain with Local 724.

In contrast to the dissenting opinion in the lower court, which advocated for a lower standard for polling, the NLRB's current standard is consistent with the NLRA. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have held that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support," but the NLRB's standard is not arbitrary or capricious. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982).

The Court should uphold the NLRB's decision and enforce its order, as the Board's standard for polling is rational and consistent with the NLRA, and its factual determination is supported by substantial evidence.
