# ARGUMENT

## I. RULE 23(b)(3)'S PREDOMINANCE REQUIREMENT DOES NOT DEMAND A DAMAGES MODEL THAT PERFECTLY ISOLATES A SINGLE THEORY OF LIABILITY WHERE THE DISTRICT COURT HAS FOUND, BY A PREPONDERANCE OF THE EVIDENCE, THAT COMMON METHODOLOGY EXISTS

Rule 23(b)(3) requires only that "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This Court has never held that plaintiffs must present a damages model tied with mathematical precision to a single surviving theory of antitrust impact before a class may be certified. Rather, the predominance inquiry asks whether common issues predominate over individual ones—a standard the District Court applied faithfully after four days of evidentiary hearings, thirty-two expert reports, and live testimony.

The Third Circuit correctly applied the "rigorous analysis" framework this Court endorsed in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), which recognized that "[f]requently [the Rule 23] 'rigorous analysis' will entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim." *Id.* at 351. That overlap, however, does not transform certification into a full merits adjudication. As the court below explained, the district court need not resolve whether Dr. McClave's model would ultimately prevail at trial—only whether Respondents demonstrated "by a preponderance of the evidence" that damages are "capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class." *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010).

This standard is consistent with *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), which instructs that appellate courts must not "reverse the finding of the trier of fact simply because it is convinced that it would have decided the case differently." *Id.* at 573. Where, as here, "the district court's account of the evidence is plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety, the court of appeals may not reverse it even though convinced that had it been sitting as the trier of fact, it would have weighed the evidence differently." *Id.* at 573–74. The District Court's finding that Dr. McClave's regression model provided a common methodology to measure damages across the class rests on exactly this kind of plausible, record-supported determination.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY FOUND ADMISSIBLE EVIDENCE SUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH A COMMON METHODOLOGY FOR CLASS-WIDE DAMAGES

Petitioner's argument reduces to a request that this Court re-weigh conflicting expert testimony that the District Court already considered and resolved after extensive evidentiary hearings. That is precisely the exercise this Court has forbidden. The clearly-erroneous standard "does not entitle a reviewing court to reverse the finding of the trier of fact simply because it is convinced that it would have decided the case differently." *Anderson*, 470 U.S. at 573. A finding may be overturned only if the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948).

The Third Circuit's own precedent in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), which the District Court applied on reconsideration, requires "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations" and permits "a preliminary inquiry into the merits." *Id.* at 317. The District Court did exactly that: it vacated its earlier predominance finding, held a four-day evidentiary hearing, posed detailed follow-up questions to the parties, and issued an eighty-one-page opinion analyzing the competing expert submissions. This is the "rigorous analysis" that *Hydrogen Peroxide* and *Wal-Mart* demand—not a demand that the class's model be reduced to a single theory of harm before certification, but a searching inquiry into whether common proof exists.

Notably, the narrowing of the case to a single theory of antitrust impact—the "overbuilder deterrence" theory—occurred *before* final certification, when the District Court limited "[p]roof of antitrust impact" to the theory "that Comcast engaged in anticompetitive clustering conduct, the effect of which was to deter the entry of overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA." App. 00032. The District Court then found that Dr. McClave's methodology remained sound common evidence for that surviving theory. Whether the model precisely matches every element of that narrowed theory is a merits question about the ultimate persuasiveness of the evidence at trial—not a certification-stage inquiry into predominance.

## III. PETITIONER'S RULE ASKS THIS COURT TO CONVERT CLASS CERTIFICATION INTO A MERITS TRIAL, CONTRARY TO THE STRUCTURE OF RULE 23 AND THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT

Petitioner effectively asks this Court to require district courts to determine, as a matter of law, that a plaintiff's damages methodology is *correct* before certifying a class. But *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156 (1974), and its progeny caution against transforming certification into a mini-trial on the merits. While *Wal-Mart* permits overlap between the Rule 23 inquiry and the merits, it does not require complete merits resolution at the certification stage; it still "prohibit[s]... a merits inquiry for any other pretrial purpose." 564 U.S. at 351 n.6.

The District Court's approach—rigorously testing the admissibility and reliability of Dr. McClave's model as *common evidence*, without finally adjudicating whether that model would prevail at trial—strikes the proper balance. Comcast's summary judgment motion, still pending below, remains the proper vehicle to test the ultimate merits of the damages methodology. Class certification is not the last word on liability or damages; it is a case-management determination about whether common questions predominate.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
