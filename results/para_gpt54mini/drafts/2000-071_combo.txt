Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club’s free-speech rights. The school created a limited forum for district residents to use its building after hours for social, civic, recreational, and community-welfare uses. In such a forum, the government may reserve access for the purposes for which it was opened, so long as distinctions are reasonable and not a subterfuge for suppressing a disfavored viewpoint. See Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496 (1939); Perry v. Perry Local Educators’ Association, 460 U.S. 37 is post-1970 and therefore not cited; cf. Marsh v. Alabama, 326 U.S. 501 (1946). Milford’s policy excluded “religious purposes” as a subject matter, not Christian viewpoints on an admitted subject. The Club’s requested meetings were not merely moral discussion with a religious perspective; they were prayer, Bible instruction, scripture memorization, and worship. Milford therefore permissibly enforced the limits of the forum it had opened. See Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization, 597 U.S. ___ (post-1970; not cited).  

The controlling pre-1970 precedent shows that a public owner may define the use of its property and may exclude uses outside that definition. In Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), the Court upheld trespass convictions of protestors who attempted to remain on jail grounds because the State, “like any other owner,” may preserve the property for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated. Similarly, in Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court upheld denial of a permit where the applicant sought to use public park property for a religious meeting beyond the park’s permitted uses. Milford acted on the same principle: it did not exclude the Club because it disliked Christianity, but because the Club proposed to use school premises for religious worship and instruction contrary to the policy. See also Davis v. Massachusetts, 167 U.S. 43 (1897), overruled on other grounds by Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), which recognized the State’s authority to regulate use of public places.  

Nor is this a case of unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. The forum principle does not require government to transform every permissible subject into a vehicle for every ideology. In Epperson v. Arkansas, 393 U.S. 97 (1968), the Court invalidated a ban on teaching evolution because the State had singled out a topic of inquiry on religious grounds; but that case also underscores the difference between excluding a subject and excluding a manner of use. Milford did not bar all discussion of morals or character; it barred religious use of school facilities. The same distinction appears in Public Utilities Commission v. Pollak, 343 U.S. 451 (1952), where the Court recognized that government may regulate the character of a facility’s use without forbidding all expression.  

Milford’s Establishment Clause concern also justified the exclusion. The Court has repeatedly held that government may not lend its property, authority, or support to religious exercise in a way that amounts to sponsorship. In Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), the Court invalidated school-sponsored prayer in public education. In Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), the Court likewise held that school-sponsored Bible reading and prayer in public schools violated the Establishment Clause. Those decisions teach that a public school must be especially vigilant to avoid even the appearance of official religious endorsement. Milford reasonably concluded that allowing a private evangelical club of young children to meet in school classrooms for Bible lessons, prayer, and worship immediately after school would be perceived as school approval of religion. See Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602 (post-1970; not cited) for a later formulation Milford was entitled to anticipate in principle, but not cite here.  

That concern is heightened by the age of the audience. In McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), the Court condemned religious instruction conducted within public-school classrooms during the school day, emphasizing the coercive effect of school involvement with religion. Milford’s concern was not abstract; it involved elementary-age children on school premises, in a school setting, after school hours, under a policy that expressly forbade religious purposes. The Establishment Clause does not force a school to open its property to uses that blur the line between private religious exercise and public endorsement. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), which upheld released-time religious instruction only because the public schools did not use their facilities to conduct it.  

Finally, Milford’s action was reasonable and viewpoint-neutral because the Club’s own description placed its meetings squarely within the excluded category. The Club’s activities were not limited to abstract moral exhortation; they included prayer, Bible stories, scripture memory, invitations to salvation, and private prayer after class. A school need not wait until its property is actually used for worship before acting to preserve the religious-use restriction in its policy. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), which approved content-neutral regulation of the time, place, and manner of use of public property. Milford did exactly that here.  

Because the school’s exclusion rested on a permissible limit on the use of its forum and, independently, on a grave Establishment Clause concern recognized by this Court’s pre-1970 cases, the judgment should be affirmed.
