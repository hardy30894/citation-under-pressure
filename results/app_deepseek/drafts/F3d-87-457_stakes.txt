ARGUMENT

I. The Disclosure Provisions Are Facially Overbroad and Violate the First Amendment

The compelled disclosure of all lobbying expenditures—including funds for research, communication, and media—sweeps far beyond any legitimate governmental interest. “[C]ompelled disclosure of affiliation with groups engaged in advocacy may constitute as effective a restraint on freedom of association” as direct prohibition. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958). Florida’s statute requires lobbyists to disclose not only direct payments to officials but also expenditures on “research,” “publications,” and “media advertising”—activities that often involve core political speech protected by the First Amendment. See *Talley v. California*, 362 U.S. 60, 64 (1960) (invalidating ordinance requiring handbills to identify sponsors because it “would tend to restrict freedom of expression”).

The government’s interest in preventing corruption or its appearance is legitimate, *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 67 (1976), but the disclosure provisions here are vastly overbroad. The statute requires disclosure without any linkage to potential quid pro quo corruption. It applies even when a lobbyist merely discusses policy with a legislator over coffee—a routine exercise of petitioning. Unlike the contribution disclosure upheld in *Buckley*, which was narrowly tied to campaign finance, Florida’s statute reaches all “lobbying,” defined to include any attempt to influence any official action. See Fla. Stat. § 11.045(1). Such breadth cannot survive a facial challenge, as the law “criminalizes a substantial amount of protected expressive activity.” *Houston v. Hill*, 482 U.S. 451, 459 (1987). Moreover, the statute’s administrative advisory-opinion mechanism does not cure its overbreadth; it merely invites case-by-case narrowing that cannot salvage a facially invalid law. *See Village of Schaumburg v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 444 U.S. 620, 634 (1980) (invalidating overly broad charitable solicitation ordinance despite availability of administrative guidance). Accordingly, the disclosure provisions are facially unconstitutional.

II. The Ban on Contingency-Fee Lobbying Violates the First Amendment

Florida’s prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying—i.e., paying a lobbyist based on success in influencing legislative or executive action—directly burdens core political speech. Lobbying is “the essence of … petition for the redress of grievances” and “occupies the core of the First Amendment.” *Eastern R.R. Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc.*, 365 U.S. 127, 137 (1961). A ban that conditions the ability to engage in such speech on the method of compensation is a content-based restriction because it penalizes speech based on the speaker’s financial incentive. *See* *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n*, 514 U.S. 334, 347 (1995) (striking down ban on anonymous advocacy, noting that government cannot “discriminate against certain speakers”).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “however reasonable the regulation of the conduct of litigation may be, it is not to be extended beyond the borders of the courtroom.” *Sperry v. Florida ex rel. Fla. Bar*, 373 U.S. 379, 384 (1963). The contingency-fee ban here targets conduct wholly outside the courtroom—influence on legislation. Unlike the ban on contingent fees for lawyers in litigation (which serves the state interest in preventing champerty and protecting clients), lobbying is not adversarial litigation; it is advocacy for policy outcomes. The asserted state interest—preventing corruption or its appearance—is not served by a wholesale prohibition of contingent fees. A lobbyist who is paid only if a bill passes has no greater incentive to engage in corrupt conduct than a salaried lobbyist; both seek the same outcome. The state has not demonstrated that contingent compensation creates any special risk of bribery. *Cf. Citizens Against Rent Control v. Berkeley*, 454 U.S. 290, 298 (1981) (striking down contribution limit for ballot measure committees because “the interest in preventing corruption … is not sufficient” to justify restriction). Moreover, the ban is a prior restraint on speech because it prohibits the very arrangement that enables many citizens to afford lobbying representation. *See Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 540 (1945) (striking down license requirement for union solicitation as prior restraint). Because the contingency-fee ban is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest, it violates the First Amendment.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and hold Chapter 93-121 facially invalid.
