**Argument**

ERISA’s text controls this case. Section 413 provides that an action for breach of fiduciary duty may not be commenced after “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). Congress chose “actual knowledge,” not constructive knowledge, and the phrase must be given its ordinary meaning. See *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d 1069, 1075-76 (9th Cir. 2018), rev’d, 140 S. Ct. 768 (2020); *Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch*, 489 U.S. 101, 112-13 (1989).

This Court has repeatedly held that ERISA limitations provisions turn on what the plaintiff knew, not on whether information was available somewhere in the record. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985), the court explained that the statute is triggered by knowledge of “the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [the plaintiff’s] knowledge of the law.” Likewise, in *Meagher v. International Association of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), the court held that a claimant may have knowledge of the transaction while still lacking knowledge that the conduct was unlawful under ERISA. And in *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520-21 (9th Cir. 1991), the court held that actual knowledge is measured from the earliest date on which the plaintiff knew of the breach. Those decisions confirm that ERISA’s clock begins when the plaintiff actually knows the pertinent facts of the breach.

That understanding is reinforced by the ordinary meaning of “knowledge.” A person does not “know” a fact merely because it appears in a document placed before him. Congress could have used a constructive-knowledge standard, as it once did in ERISA, but it deliberately repealed that language in 1987. *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1076-77. The result is a narrow trigger: actual awareness, not hypothetical awareness. See *Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co.*, 571 U.S. 99, 105-06 (2013) (enforcing ERISA limitations periods according to their terms).

Intel’s contrary rule would collapse “actual knowledge” into mere disclosure. But that is precisely what Congress rejected. ERISA fiduciaries already have powerful incentives to disclose information, and plan documents can be voluminous and technical. Yet the statute says actual knowledge, not notice. The difference matters. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, “sufficient information available” is not enough where the plaintiff testified he was “unaware” of the investments and “did not recall seeing” the documents at issue. *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1078-79.

Intel argues that because it disclosed the relevant facts on websites and in plan materials, Sulyma necessarily had actual knowledge more than three years before suit. But this Court’s ERISA cases do not permit a limitations defense to rest on constructive awareness. In *Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), the plaintiffs were told directly of the conduct at issue. In *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1342-43 (9th Cir. 1994), the court applied section 1113 where the plaintiffs knew the operative facts underlying the challenged transaction. Those cases are far removed from this one, where the participant testified that he did not actually know about the challenged investments.

Nor can Intel rely on *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996), which recognized ERISA’s “duty of care” and fiduciary obligations. That case addressed fiduciary standards, not limitations. Nor do *LaRue v. DeWolff, Boberg & Associates, Inc.*, 552 U.S. 248 (2008), *Mertens v. Hewitt Associates*, 508 U.S. 248 (1993), or *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson*, 525 U.S. 432 (1999), support rewriting section 1113(2). They concern ERISA remedies and substantive duties, not the meaning of “actual knowledge.”

Intel’s rule would also undermine the structure of section 1113. Congress set a six-year outer limit for fiduciary claims, with a shorter three-year period only when the plaintiff actually knows of the breach. 29 U.S.C. § 1113. If mere disclosure sufficed, the three-year period would become the default in most fiduciary cases, because plans routinely disseminate disclosures. That would defeat Congress’s chosen balance.

The judgment should be reversed. The question is not whether Intel made the information available. It is whether Sulyma actually knew the alleged breach more than three years before filing suit. On this record, the answer is no. See *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d 1069 (9th Cir. 2018), rev’d, 140 S. Ct. 768 (2020).
