**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and the Third Circuit correctly held that she may not. This Court has long recognized that the Tenth Amendment does not confer standing upon private individuals to litigate federalism concerns. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939), established that private parties, even when injured by federal action, lack standing to assert the Tenth Amendment’s limitations on federal power absent a showing of injury to the state itself. Bond’s claim is precisely the kind of generalized grievance that this Court has consistently rejected.

The Tenth Amendment, which reserves powers not delegated to the United States to the States or the people, is not a source of individual rights. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559, 115 S.Ct. 1624, 131 L.Ed.2d 626 (1995). It functions as a structural limitation on federal authority, designed to preserve the balance of power between the national government and the States. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156, 112 S.Ct. 2408, 120 L.Ed.2d 120 (1992). But the Constitution does not empower private citizens to enforce that balance. As this Court has repeatedly held, “the Tenth Amendment does not create rights enforceable by private parties.” *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 617, 120 S.Ct. 1740, 146 L.Ed.2d 658 (2000). To allow otherwise would transform the Tenth Amendment into a personal shield against federal law, contrary to its structural purpose.

Bond’s argument rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of standing doctrine. She claims that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers by enacting 18 U.S.C. § 229 to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. But even if that were true, the injury she alleges—being prosecuted under a federal statute—is not the kind of injury that confers standing to challenge the constitutional limits of federal power. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61, 112 S.Ct. 2130, 119 L.Ed.2d 351 (1992). The injury must be “concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent,” and must be “fairly traceable” to the challenged action and “likely to be redressed by a favorable decision.” Id. at 560. Bond’s injury—her conviction—is not traceable to a violation of federalism; it is traceable to her own conduct. Her claim that the statute is unconstitutional because it intrudes on state criminal jurisdiction is not a personal injury but a generalized grievance about the scope of federal power. Such a claim is not justiciable.

The Third Circuit correctly applied *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* to reject Bond’s standing. That case involved private utility companies challenging the federal government’s sale of electricity in competition with state-regulated utilities. The Court held that the companies lacked standing to assert a Tenth Amendment violation because the states themselves had not objected. *Id.* at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. The Court emphasized that “there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states,” and that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* This principle remains binding. The States, not private individuals, are the proper parties to assert federalism violations. *See also* *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201, 124 S.Ct. 1628, 158 L.Ed.2d 420 (2004) (recognizing that the Tenth Amendment “protects the States’ sovereignty, not the rights of individuals”).

Bond’s attempt to distinguish *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* by arguing that its holding was “dicta” is foreclosed by precedent. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005), held that the standing analysis in *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* was not mere dicta but an alternative ground for decision, given that the Court addressed both standing and the merits. Moreover, the standing issue in *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* concerned the very jurisdiction of federal courts to hear such claims—making it a threshold, dispositive issue. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998). The Court’s resolution of that issue is binding on lower courts.

Bond’s claim also fails on the merits. Even if she had standing, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s power under the Treaty Clause. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920), established that Congress may enact legislation to implement a valid treaty, even if such legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, ratified by the Senate with the requisite two-thirds majority. Congress’s enactment of § 229 to fulfill the United States’ international obligations is a proper exercise of the Necessary and Proper Clause. *Id.* at 433, 40 S.Ct. 382. The statute does not violate federalism because it is not a general criminal law but a targeted implementation of a treaty obligation that addresses a matter of global concern—chemical weapons proliferation.

Finally, Bond’s vagueness challenge fails.
