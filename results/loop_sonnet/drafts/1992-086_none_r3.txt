# ARGUMENT

Lamb's Chapel sought only what any other community organization would enjoy under the District's own practice: an opportunity to use the school auditorium after hours to share its views on family life with interested residents. The District refused solely because Pastor Steigerwald's proposed film series was, in its clerk's words, "church related." That refusal violates the First Amendment for three independent reasons. First, having opened its facilities to expressive use by outside groups, the District could not then exclude speech because of its religious viewpoint. Second, the District's selective exclusion of religious speech while tolerating comparable secular speech constitutes textbook viewpoint discrimination condemned by this Court's precedents. Third, neither the Establishment Clause nor the District's status as a public school insulates its actions from ordinary First Amendment scrutiny.

## I. Having Opened Its Facilities to Community Groups for Expressive Use, the District May Not Exclude Speech Because of Its Religious Content.

The degree of First Amendment protection afforded to speech on government property does not depend on whether that property is a street, a park, or a school auditorium; it depends on whether the government has opened the property to expressive use. Streets and parks receive the highest protection because they "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). The same principle applies wherever government voluntarily opens property to community expression, as the District did here by admitting civic clubs, the Southern Harmonizers, and a library-sponsored lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics. Having created such a forum, the District could not then draw a line excluding only religious speech from it.

That Lamb's Chapel's message took the form of a film does not diminish its protection. This Court has long recognized that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas," entitled to the same constitutional safeguards as other speech. *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). The District cannot escape its obligations by treating a film on family values differently from a live lecture on the identical subject simply because the film's perspective was religious. "A function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute," *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949)—yet it was precisely the District's discomfort with the religious content of Lamb's Chapel's message that produced its exclusion.

## II. Excluding Religious Speech While Tolerating Comparable Secular Speech Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly invalidated governmental denials of access to public facilities where the denial rested on the religious character of the speaker's message while comparable secular activity was freely permitted. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court struck down the denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses where other groups had used the same park and the denial rested on official disapproval of the applicants' religious views rather than any neutral standard. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), reached the identical result, condemning the discriminatory application of a park ordinance against a religious meeting while similar secular gatherings proceeded undisturbed. And in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), the Court held that officials could not exercise licensing discretion to exclude a speaker because they disapproved of the religious content of his message.

The record here presents the identical defect. The District permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform and allowed a lecture series touching on "spiritual" and "metaphysical" topics, yet categorically barred anything "church related"—the very phrase its clerk invoked, not once but three times, to reject Lamb's Chapel's applications. That is viewpoint discrimination of exactly the kind *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Kunz* forbid.

## III. The Establishment Clause Neither Requires Nor Permits the District's Discrimination Against Religious Speech.

Any suggestion that the Establishment Clause compelled this exclusion finds no footing in this Court's precedent. The Establishment Clause demands governmental neutrality toward religion; it does not license hostility toward it. As this Court has explained, the First Amendment "does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). Neutrality requires only that government neither advance nor inhibit religion through its own action, *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947); *School District of Abington Township v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963)—not that it treat religious speakers worse than secular ones once it has opened a forum to speech generally. Extending to Lamb's Chapel the same access already extended to secular community groups would not establish religion; it would simply honor the neutrality the Establishment Clause itself commands.

## IV. Public School Property Is Not an Enclave Exempt from First Amendment Scrutiny.

That the property belongs to a public school does not diminish this analysis. This Court has held that "state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism," and that neither students nor teachers "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). If constitutional protections for speech survive inside the schoolhouse for its own students and teachers, the District cannot invoke its custodial control over school facilities to silence a community organization's protected expression once it has opened those facilities to comparable secular use. The collective, associational character of Lamb's Chapel's proposed activity—a congregation sharing its views on family life with the broader community—is itself protected activity long safeguarded by this Court. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415 (1963).

## CONCLUSION

The District's exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Hague*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Kunz*, *Burstyn*, *Terminiello*, *Zorach*, *Everson*, *Schempp*, *Tinker*, or *Button*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
