**Argument**

I. **The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power.**

The nondelegation doctrine requires only that Congress supply an “intelligible principle” to guide executive action. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that Congress may obtain assistance from the other branches when it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989). Under that settled standard, the Controlled Substances Act easily survives.

Section 811(h) permits temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). Congress further constrained the Attorney General by requiring consideration of specified factors, limiting the action to one year with one possible six-month extension, and requiring notice in the Federal Register and consideration of comments from the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), (3), (4). Those constraints are far more definite than standards the Court has repeatedly upheld. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420–27 (1944), the Court sustained wartime price controls authorized by a standard of “generally fair and equitable” prices. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), it upheld authority guided by “safe and sound” banking practice. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld broad sentencing authority where Congress set general purposes, factors, and boundaries. 488 U.S. at 374–79.

The Third Circuit correctly recognized that the CSA is even more tightly cabined because the temporary-scheduling power operates in a narrow, emergency context and is backed by detailed statutory criteria. See *United States v. Touby*, 909 F.2d 759, 763–69 (3d Cir. 1990). The Court has made clear that “the notion that Congress must use specific terms or particularized findings in delegating power is not supported by our cases.” *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 531 U.S. 457, 474 (2001). Congress’s judgment that emergency drug scheduling must be flexible in light of rapidly changing designer drugs is precisely the kind of practical legislative choice the Court has approved. See *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 768; *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved open-ended delegations with no meaningful policy or criteria. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541–42. The CSA, by contrast, specifies the purpose, factors, procedures, duration, and subject matter of the temporary-scheduling authority. That is enough under modern nondelegation doctrine. See *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 371–79.

II. **Congress authorized the Attorney General to subdelegate temporary-scheduling authority to the DEA.**

The Attorney General may delegate functions assigned to the Department unless Congress has provided otherwise. See 28 U.S.C. § 510; *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514–23 (1974). Here, Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The temporary-scheduling authority is a function “under this subchapter,” and nothing in § 811(h) withdraws that general delegation authority. The Third Circuit therefore correctly held that the Attorney General could lawfully delegate to the DEA Administrator. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 769–72.

Petitioners argue that because temporary scheduling can have criminal consequences, it may not be delegated further. But the Court has rejected the proposition that criminal enforcement authority is categorically nondelegable. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517–22 (1911), the Court upheld regulations carrying criminal sanctions where Congress supplied the governing policy. Likewise, in *Loving v. United States*, 517 U.S. 748, 768 (1996), the Court held that the President could be authorized to prescribe aggravating factors in a capital case. The relevant question is authorization, and Congress provided it here in § 871(a). See *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 770–72.

III. **Judicial review is available to decide petitioners’ constitutional challenge.**

Although § 811(h)(6) states that temporary-scheduling orders are “not subject to judicial review,” that provision does not bar constitutional review. The Court presumes reviewability absent “clear and convincing” evidence of congressional intent to preclude it. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). The Third Circuit properly held that Congress did not foreclose constitutional challenges in criminal cases. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 762–63. Petitioners therefore received all the process constitutionally due; their challenge fails on the merits because Congress validly delegated the temporary-scheduling authority and validly authorized the Attorney General to subdelegate it to the DEA.
