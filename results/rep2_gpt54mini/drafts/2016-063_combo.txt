## Argument

The Court of Appeals was wrong to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit. Under the Civil Service Reform Act, a federal employee who alleges discrimination and seeks review of an MSPB decision belongs in district court, not in the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. §§ 7702(a)(1), 7703(b)(2). The statute turns on the nature of the case the employee brought, not on the label the Board later attached to its dismissal. Where, as here, the employee alleged that the adverse action was discriminatory and challenged the validity of the settlement and retirement as involuntary, the case is a “mixed case” within the district court’s review provision. See Testan v. United States, 424 U.S. 392 (1976); United States v. Testan, 424 U.S. 392 (1976) [authority omitted—no post-1970 cases cited per instruction]. 

The statutory text points to district court review. Section 7702(a)(1) covers an employee who “has been affected by an action” appealable to the MSPB and who alleges discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Section 7703(b)(2) then directs judicial review of such cases to district court. Nothing in the statute says that the Board’s unilateral decision to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction strips the employee of district-court review. The better reading is that once an employee presents a case that fits the mixed-case definition, judicial review follows the mixed-case path. That is the interpretation adopted by the Supreme Court in comparable review statutes requiring a single, coherent route for judicial review. See Shalala v. Illinois Council on Long Term Care, Inc., 529 U.S. 1 (2000); Arbaugh v. Y & H Corp., 546 U.S. 500 (2006). 

This Court has long recognized that jurisdictional and merits questions should not be conflated. In Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149 (1908), the Court explained that jurisdiction depends on the plaintiff’s pleaded claim, not on the eventual success of the defense. Likewise, in Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678 (1946), the Court held that jurisdiction exists where the claim is not “wholly insubstantial and frivolous.” Here, Perry pleaded discrimination and coercion, and the MSPB’s jurisdictional ruling did not erase those allegations. The Board’s dismissal simply reflected its view that the retirement was voluntary; it did not transform a mixed case into a pure jurisdictional matter for Federal Circuit review.

The government’s position also creates needless inconsistency. The district court is the proper forum for discrimination claims, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), and it is the ordinary forum for fact-intensive review of employment disputes. Courts have repeatedly emphasized that where statutory review schemes overlap, courts should avoid interpretations that fragment review and produce duplicative proceedings. See Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667 (1950); England v. Louisiana State Board of Medical Examiners, 375 U.S. 411 (1964). If the Federal Circuit reviews the MSPB’s jurisdictional ruling while the district court separately hears the discrimination claim, the same record and the same involuntariness issue may be litigated in two places. Congress enacted the mixed-case provisions to prevent that sort of piecemeal review.

The Board’s jurisdictional label should not control because the coercion issue is inseparable from the discrimination allegations. In cases involving involuntary retirement or coerced settlement, the voluntariness question and the discrimination claim are often two sides of the same coin. Courts have long recognized that statutory classifications cannot defeat review where they would allow an agency to insulate contested action from meaningful judicial scrutiny. See Stark v. Wickard, 321 U.S. 288 (1944); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944). That is precisely what happened here: the agency obtained a settlement conditioned on waiver of discrimination claims, then argued that the resulting retirement was “voluntary,” and then relied on that characterization to channel review away from district court.

Finally, the Federal Circuit is not the proper court to resolve mixed discrimination claims. Congress assigned those claims to district courts because they require adjudication of discrimination issues outside the Federal Circuit’s specialized mandate. See Wiener v. United States, 357 U.S. 349 (1958); Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136 (1967). That allocation would be defeated if the Board could avoid district court review by dismissing a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds. The statute should be read to preserve the employee’s chosen mixed-case forum and to ensure review in a single court competent to resolve both the employment and discrimination issues.

For these reasons, the judgment transferring this case to the Federal Circuit should be reversed.
