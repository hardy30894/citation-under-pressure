ARGUMENT

I. Selam was denied compulsory process.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that in all criminal prosecutions the accused shall enjoy the right to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor. This right is fundamental. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) ("The right to offer the testimony of witnesses, and to compel their attendance, if necessary, is in plain terms the right to present a defense."). Selam supplied his lay counsel a list of five character witnesses. Counsel located only three; the others refused to appear. Although the tribal judge and counsel advised Selam of his right to compel attendance, no subpoenas issued. The resulting absence of any defense witnesses other than Selam himself denied him the right to present a defense secured by the Indian Civil Rights Act.

II. Selam did not waive his confrontation claim.

A waiver of constitutional rights must be knowing and voluntary. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) ("waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege"). The Confrontation Clause likewise secures a fundamental right. Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. 400, 403 (1965) ("the right of confrontation... is 'a fundamental right'"). Selam raised compulsory-process error on tribal appeal but omitted confrontation. The tribal court of appeals denied even a hearing. Under these circumstances the omission cannot constitute an intentional relinquishment of a known right. Federal habeas review therefore remains available. Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 118 (1944) (habeas relief appropriate where state corrective process proves "ineffective").

The district court erred in denying relief on both grounds.
