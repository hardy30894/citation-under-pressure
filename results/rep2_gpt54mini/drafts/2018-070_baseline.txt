## Argument

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act “extend[s]” federal law to fixed structures on the Outer Continental Shelf, and it adopts adjacent state law only “to the extent that [state laws] are applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That text answers the Question Presented: state law comes in as surrogate federal law only when federal law is deficient on the point at issue. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach—applying state law whenever it concerns the subject matter and is not expressly preempted—rewrites the statute and conflicts with this Court’s and the Fifth Circuit’s OCSLA precedent.

First, the statute’s structure requires a federal-law-gap inquiry. OCSLA provides that the Constitution and laws of the United States apply to fixed platforms “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Section 1333(a)(2)(A) then adopts adjacent state law only as “the law of the United States” and only “to the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. That language does not create a free-standing invitation to apply any state rule touching the dispute. It incorporates state law only as surrogate federal law, and only when needed to fill a deficiency in federal law.

This Court’s OCSLA decisions point in that direction. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court explained that fixed platforms are treated as artificial islands, not vessels, and that federal maritime remedies do not apply of their own force to accidents occurring there. Id. at 355, 366. The Court emphasized that OCSLA’s design was to supply a body of law for the seabed and fixed structures on the Shelf. Id. at 355. Similarly, in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Court held that state courts may entertain OCSLA claims, but only because federal law supplies the governing rules for OCS accidents and state law comes in as surrogate federal law when appropriate. Id. at 479-84. In Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court again stressed that OCSLA’s “special relationship with the shore community” does not itself authorize displacement of federal law by state law. Id. at 219.

The Fifth Circuit’s OCSLA framework has likewise treated state law as surrogate federal law only where federal law leaves a genuine gap. In Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), the court held that state law applies on the Shelf only if it is “applicable,” not inconsistent with federal law, and if federal law does not address the issue. Id. at 1047. Later cases have described that approach as requiring a gap in federal law before state law may be assimilated. See Grand Isle Shipyard, Inc. v. Seacor Marine, L.L.C., 589 F.3d 778, 780-81 (5th Cir. 2009) (en banc). The Ninth Circuit rejected that settled understanding, but nothing in § 1333(a)(2)(A) supports replacing it with a mere not-inconsistent test.

Second, the FLSA does not supply the sort of “gap” the Ninth Circuit found, because Congress expressly allowed more protective state wage-and-hour laws to coexist with federal minimum standards. The FLSA’s savings clause preserves state laws that establish higher wage protections. See 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). This Court has recognized that the FLSA sets a floor, not a ceiling. See Williamson v. General Dynamics Corp., 208 F.3d 1144, 1149-50 (9th Cir. 2000). But that does not mean California law automatically governs on the Shelf. The relevant question under OCSLA is whether federal law leaves room for state law as surrogate federal law. Where Congress has already defined the operative federal rule, state law is not needed to fill a vacuum. See Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd. v. Newton, 945 F.3d 423, 432-35 (9th Cir. 2019) (O’Scannlain, J., dissenting from denial of rehearing en banc) (explaining the error in treating “not inconsistent” as the sole test).

Third, the Ninth Circuit’s rule collapses OCSLA’s carefully limited incorporation of state law into ordinary preemption analysis. But OCSLA is not a generic preemption statute. It makes state law federal only “to the extent” the federal scheme is incomplete. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). If mere compatibility were enough, Congress’s carefully chosen “to the extent that” and “applicable” language would do no work. Courts must give effect to every word Congress used. See Duncan v. Walker, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001).

Here, Newton’s claims arise from hours worked, meal periods, and wage payment rules on a fixed platform covered by OCSLA. The FLSA already governs wages and hours there, and Congress permitted additional state wage rules to operate only insofar as they may be assimilated under OCSLA’s limited federal-incorporation mechanism. California law therefore cannot apply merely because it is more protective and not expressly contradictory. The correct rule is the one Parker urges: state law applies on the OCS only when there is a gap in federal law that OCSLA can fill. The judgment below should be reversed.
