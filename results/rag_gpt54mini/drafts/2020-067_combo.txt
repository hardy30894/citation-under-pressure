**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because plain-error review is limited to the record, and petitioner cannot enlarge that record on appeal with material never presented to the jury or the trial court. This Court has long recognized that appellate correction of unpreserved error does not authorize factfinding in the first instance. In **Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969)**, the Court declined to decide an evidentiary-prejudice question because it would have required the Court to “explore the Maryland law of evidence” and “examine the record in detail,” explaining that it was not the kind of determination that should be made “unaided by prior consideration by the state courts.” Likewise, in **Eskridge v. Washington State Board of Prison Terms & Paroles, 357 U.S. 214 (1958)**, the Court emphasized that appellate review depends on the appellant’s ability to present the proceedings below through an adequate record. And **Garland v. Washington, 232 U.S. 642 (1914)** confirms that technical trial defects do not require reversal when the accused was otherwise “fully met” in the trial court; appellate review does not become a vehicle for relitigating matters outside the proceedings actually held.

That principle is especially strong under plain-error review. Plain error is reserved for errors that are plain and affect substantial rights, and even then relief is discretionary. **United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)**; **Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896)**. The party seeking reversal bears the burden to show prejudice. **Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189, 200 (1943)**. Where the issue is whether an error affected the outcome, the court must assess the effect of the error on the actual trial record, not on post hoc speculation. **Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946)**; **Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967)**. Nothing in those decisions authorizes an appellate court to comb through extra-record materials to manufacture prejudice.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would also conflict with the Court’s longstanding insistence that criminal judgments rest on the proceedings below. In **Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969)**, the Court reversed because the record did not show a knowing and voluntary guilty plea; the inquiry turned on what the record revealed, not on after-the-fact supplementation. In **Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967)**, the Court held that an uncounseled prior conviction could not be used to enhance punishment because the record itself showed constitutional infirmity. And in **Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948)**, the Court evaluated the sentencing claim on the sentencing record, not on extra-record reconstruction. These cases show that appellate courts review what was actually before the tribunal below.

Petitioner relies on the idea that an appellate court may look beyond the trial record to decide whether a plain error was prejudicial. But that approach finds no support in the Court’s precedents. **In re Belt, 159 U.S. 95 (1895)** recognized that the use of prior convictions at sentencing is governed by the record of those convictions. **Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155 (1900)** likewise treated sentencing error as a matter for correction based on the existing proceedings, not outside investigation. And **Greer County v. Texas, 197 U.S. 235 (1905)** and **Bogard v. Sweet, 209 U.S. 464 (1908)** both reflect the basic appellate principle that review proceeds from the record and judgment under review.

Here, the Eleventh Circuit did exactly what plain-error doctrine permits. It examined the trial record and petitioner’s own prior convictions to determine whether he could show a reasonable probability of a different outcome or that the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings was affected. That is the proper inquiry under **Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160**, and **Kotteakos, 328 U.S. at 765**. Petitioner’s requested rule would invert the burden on plain-error review, allowing a defendant to remain silent at trial and then obtain reversal by pointing to materials never put before the jury. **Johnson, 318 U.S. at 200** does not permit that result.

Finally, this Court should not disturb the judgment on a theory that would make appellate review depend on evidentiary materials outside the adversarial process below. The Court has repeatedly rejected such backward-looking speculation where the record does not itself demonstrate prejudice. **Benton, 395 U.S. at 794**; **Boykin, 395 U.S. at 242–44**; **Garland, 232 U.S. at 646–47**. The Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its prejudice analysis to the record and petitioner’s undisputed criminal history. That was proper, and the judgment should be affirmed.
