# ARGUMENT

## I. The Rule Announced in *Johnson* Is Substantive and Therefore Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review

This Court has long distinguished between constitutional rules that regulate the *manner* by which guilt is determined and those that define the very *scope* of conduct or persons the State may punish. In *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), the Court recognized that retroactivity turns on the purpose and effect of the new rule and on the extent to which the rule bears on the integrity of the fact-finding process, as opposed to bearing on the government's underlying power to convict or punish at all. A rule of the latter kind cannot be confined to future cases, because a State has no power—today or yesterday—to imprison a person for conduct the Constitution places beyond its reach. *See Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880) (a judgment rendered under a law the Constitution forbids is void, and the prisoner "is not lawfully imprisoned").

*Johnson v. United States* did not adopt a new trial procedure; it held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act's definition of "violent felony" is void for vagueness under the Due Process Clauses. That holding rests on the same due-process fair-warning principle this Court applied in *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347 (1964), where it explained that "[t]he basic principle that a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime has often been recognized by this Court." *Id.* at 350. As the Court there quoted from *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), due process is violated by "a criminal statute that fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice" of what it punishes. *Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 351. This Court reached the identical conclusion in *Giaccio v. Pennsylvania*, 382 U.S. 399 (1965), invalidating a statute for "vagueness and the absence of any standards sufficient to enable defendants to protect themselves against arbitrary and discriminatory" enforcement. *Id.* at 402–03.

Johnson's residual-clause holding is of the same character. It does not merely regulate how courts decide sentencing facts; it removes an entire category of conduct—crimes assessed only by "hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version" of the offense—from the universe of conduct that may lawfully trigger a fifteen-year mandatory minimum. A defendant sentenced under that clause was punished more severely than the substantive law permits, exactly the defect that *Siebold* and *Bouie* treat as void, not merely erroneous.

## II. Habeas Corpus Has Traditionally Been Available to Test Whether a Sentence Exceeds the Court's Lawful Power, Including Sentences Enhanced by Prior-Conviction Statutes

This Court's historic practice confirms that collateral review reaches precisely this kind of defect. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that habeas corpus lies to test whether a court had constitutional power to impose the sentence it imposed, because a sentence entered without that power is "void." The same principle governed *Reynolds v. Cochran*, 365 U.S. 525 (1961), where this Court entertained a habeas petition challenging confinement under a recidivist enhancement on the ground that the sentence "was not authorized by the Florida second-offender statute" and violated federal constitutional guarantees. *Id.* at 526. That case is Welch's case: a habeas petitioner attacking an enhanced sentence built on prior convictions that could not lawfully support it.

*Williams v. Kaiser*, 323 U.S. 471 (1945), likewise entertained a collateral habeas attack on a sentence entered upon a guilty plea, because the petitioner alleged he was "deprived of counsel contrary to the requirements of the due process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment," *id.* at 472—a constitutional defect discovered only after the judgment was final. And in *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), the Court reviewed on collateral attack a life sentence imposed under a recidivist statute predicated on prior convictions, confirming that this Court has never treated sentence-enhancement statutes as immune from later constitutional testing simply because the underlying convictions were already final. *See also Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963) (vindicating, through collateral proceedings, a constitutional right to counsel violated at a trial that had already produced a final judgment).

## III. Johnson's Rule Must Apply to Welch's Collateral Challenge

Welch's fifteen-year mandatory minimum rests entirely on the residual clause this Court held void in *Johnson*. Under the *Linkletter* framework, a rule announcing that a criminal statute failed to give "fair notice," *Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 351, and was therefore void for vagueness, *Giaccio*, 382 U.S. at 402, bears not on the fairness of Welch's fact-finding but on the government's underlying power to punish him at the level it did. As *Siebold*, *Zerbst*, *Reynolds*, and *Williams* establish, habeas corpus has always been the proper vehicle for testing whether a sentence exceeds a court's lawful authority. Welch's sentence does exactly that. *Johnson* therefore governs his case, and the judgment below should be reversed.
