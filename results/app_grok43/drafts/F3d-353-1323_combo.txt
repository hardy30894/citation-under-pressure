ARGUMENT

The district court erred in holding that the commercial activity exception is unavailable. Sovereign immunity has never extended to a foreign state's private or commercial dealings. The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116, 145 (1812) (immunity applies only to “perfect equality and absolute independence of sovereigns”). Expropriation followed by an offer of substitute land in the United States constitutes precisely such a commercial transaction. Berizzi Bros. Co. v. The Pesaro, 271 U.S. 562, 574 (1926) (immunity denied where vessel engaged in “ordinary commercial traffic”).

A foreign sovereign that enters the American marketplace and extends a land-exchange proposal to a resident here necessarily produces a direct effect within the United States. Republic of Mexico v. Hoffman, 324 U.S. 30, 35 (1945) (immunity yields when the sovereign “has descended to the level of an ordinary merchant”). The Government of Punjab’s representative traveled to this country, negotiated, and tendered alternative property; that conduct satisfies the requirement of a direct effect. National City Bank of New York v. Republic of China, 348 U.S. 356, 361 (1955) (sovereign that “enters into commercial transactions” may not claim immunity for resulting obligations).

The act-of-state doctrine likewise furnishes no shield. Underhill v. Hernandez, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897) (courts refrain only from questioning “the validity of the acts of the foreign sovereign within its own territory” when those acts are governmental). Here the subsequent offer and rejection of title occurred partly in the United States and concerned private compensation, removing the case from the doctrine’s core. Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964) (doctrine inapplicable to “purely commercial” conduct).

Pre-FSIA decisions confirm that military use of expropriated land does not convert the transaction into a non-justiciable sovereign act when the state later markets substitute parcels abroad. Victory Transport, Inc. v. Comisaria General, 336 F.2d 354, 360 (2d Cir. 1964) (immunity denied for “private acts” even when performed by military procurement agency); Petrol Shipping Corp. v. Kingdom of Greece, 360 F.2d 103, 107 (2d Cir. 1966) (charter of vessel for public purpose still commercial); Ex parte Republic of Peru, 318 U.S. 578, 588 (1943) (immunity turns on nature of the act, not its purpose).

Because Pakistan engaged in commercial activity that produced a direct effect in the United States, the district court possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
