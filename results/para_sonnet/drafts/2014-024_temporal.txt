# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 875(c) Requires Proof That Elonis Subjectively Intended His Statements To Be Understood As Threats.

Congress's use of the word "threat" in 18 U.S.C. §875(c), without more, does not dispense with the ordinary requirement that a serious federal crime carry a mental-state element matching the moral culpability the statute punishes. This Court has long held that the "contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Where Congress is silent on the mental element of an offense, courts must not assume that Congress meant to punish the "morally innocent." *Id.* at 275. A defendant who transmits words he does not intend or know will be understood as a threat, but which a reasonable person might nonetheless read that way, is the paradigm of the morally innocent actor Congress is not presumed to have intended to punish absent a clear statement.

The narrow exception to this rule, recognized in *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922), applies only to regulatory "public welfare" offenses carrying light penalties, where Congress has manifestly chosen to protect the public from hazards without regard to the intent of the actor. *Id.* at 252–54. Section 875(c) is nothing of the kind: it is a felony punishable by years of imprisonment, targeting core expressive conduct — communication. The *Balint* exception cannot be stretched to cover it.

Where a criminal statute regulating speech is ambiguous as to intent, moreover, this Court has consistently construed the mental-state requirement to demand a heightened showing precisely to avoid constitutional difficulty. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court confronted a statute punishing those who "willfully" deprive a person of constitutional rights and construed "willfully" to require that "the defendant have the specific intent to deprive a person of a federal right." *Id.* at 103 (plurality opinion). The Court took that course specifically because a lesser reading would leave the statute constitutionally vulnerable for reaching innocent conduct. The same logic compels reading §875(c) to require that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be understood as threats.

## II. The First Amendment Independently Requires A Subjective-Intent Standard For True Threats.

Threatening speech loses First Amendment protection only when it is a "true threat," a category this Court has treated with the same care it applies to other narrow categories of unprotected speech, such as the "fighting words" at issue in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942). But even within categories of arguably unprotected speech, this Court has refused to allow the government to punish speech based solely on how a reasonable listener might construe it, without regard to whether the speaker intended that meaning.

This Court's own review of a threat prosecution confirms the point. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), the Court reversed a conviction under a threat statute where the defendant, at a political rally, said that "the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.," holding that the statute "requires the Government to prove a true 'threat'" and that the language in context amounted only to "political hyperbole." *Id.* at 706, 708. The Court's analysis necessarily examined what the speaker meant and the setting in which he spoke, not merely what a detached listener might have thought upon reading a transcript. An objective-only standard—one that asks only whether a reasonable recipient would perceive a threat—collapses that inquiry and permits conviction for careless, hyperbolic, or artistic expression that the speaker never intended as a threat, precisely the outcome *Watts* forbids.

This concern for the chilling effect of imprecise standards on protected expression runs throughout this Court's First Amendment jurisprudence. In *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147 (1959), the Court struck down a strict-liability obscenity ordinance because eliminating scienter would compel booksellers "to restrict the public's access to forms of the printed word which the State could not constitutionally suppress directly," thereby "impos[ing] a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter." *Id.* at 153, 154. The same danger attends an objective "true threat" standard: speakers who cannot be sure how an ill-defined "reasonable person" will read excited, angry, or artistic language—rap lyrics, political invective, dark humor—will censor themselves rather than risk felony prosecution. As this Court explained in a related context, "[b]ecause First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive, government may regulate in the area only with narrow specificity." *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). And where, as here, the risk of erroneous suppression of protected speech is substantial, this Court has insisted that "the risk of error" be allocated to protect speech rather than punish it. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525–26 (1958).

## III. Conclusion

Both the presumption favoring mens rea in serious federal crimes and the First Amendment's demand for breathing room around protected expression converge on a single answer: a conviction under §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be understood as a threat. The judgment below, resting on a purely objective "reasonable speaker" standard, should be reversed.
