**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time, place, and manner regulation that serves a significant governmental interest in ensuring the safe and unimpeded access of individuals to reproductive health care facilities. This Court has long recognized that the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech to preserve public order, protect individual rights, and maintain the functionality of public spaces—especially where those spaces are essential to the exercise of fundamental rights.

The Massachusetts statute at issue creates a fixed 35-foot buffer zone around the entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities (RHCFs). It prohibits any person from knowingly entering or remaining within that zone on a public way or sidewalk adjacent to such a facility, except for those specifically exempted—such as patients, employees, law enforcement, and others engaged in lawful activity. The law is content-neutral: it does not target speech based on its message, viewpoint, or subject matter. It applies equally to all individuals regardless of whether they are advocating for or against abortion. As this Court held in *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965), “the First Amendment does not permit a State to regulate speech because of its message.” The Massachusetts law avoids such impermissible content-based regulation by applying uniformly to all persons and all forms of communication within the designated area.

Moreover, the law is narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest. The state’s interest in protecting the right of individuals to seek medical care without harassment, intimidation, or obstruction is both compelling and well-established. In *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 401 (1953), this Court affirmed that “the right to use public streets for the purpose of peaceful assembly and expression is not absolute,” and that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of such expression to prevent interference with the rights of others.” The same principle applies here. The buffer zone ensures that individuals seeking access to a clinic are not subjected to coercive or intimidating conduct that may deter them from entering the facility. The state’s interest in protecting the physical safety and psychological well-being of patients and staff is not merely legitimate—it is essential to the preservation of constitutional rights.

The law also leaves ample alternative channels for communication. As this Court recognized in *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), “the right to speak is not absolute,” and “the right to speak does not include the right to speak in any place or at any time.” The Massachusetts law does not prohibit speech altogether; it merely restricts where and how it may occur. The petitioners may still engage in speech through oral communication, distribution of literature, display of signs and symbols, prayer, and other expressive conduct outside the buffer zone. The First Circuit correctly observed that the plaintiffs retain the ability to speak at varying volumes, use amplification, wear expressive garments, and conduct group prayer—all of which are protected forms of expression. The fact that the buffer zone prevents close personal contact or face-to-face interaction does not render the law unconstitutional, for such a restriction is a reasonable accommodation of competing interests.

This Court has consistently upheld similar regulations in the context of public forums. In *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939), the Court held that “the right of assembly is not absolute,” and that “the government may impose reasonable regulations upon the time, place, and manner of assembly.” The sidewalks and public ways adjacent to clinics are public forums, but not “unlimited” ones. The state may impose reasonable restrictions to prevent obstruction, ensure public safety, and protect the rights of others. The buffer zone here is no broader than necessary to achieve these ends. It is not a broad prohibition on speech, but a targeted measure to prevent interference with access to medical care.

The petitioners’ claim that the law is overbroad or that it violates their right to “effective” communication misapprehends the scope of First Amendment protection. The Constitution does not guarantee that speech will be effective or that individuals must be allowed to communicate at a “normal conversational distance.” In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940), this Court emphasized that “the right to speak is not a right to speak in any place or at any time.” The state may regulate the manner of expression to prevent harm, maintain order, and protect the rights of others. The buffer zone does not suppress speech; it merely relocates it to a space where it can be conducted without interfering with the fundamental right of access to medical care.

Finally, the law of the case doctrine bars relitigation of the facial constitutionality of the statute. This Court has long recognized that “a decision of an appellate court in a case is the law of the case and is binding upon the trial court and the appellate court in a subsequent appeal in the same case.” *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). The First Circuit’s prior decisions in *McGuire v. Reilly* and *McCullen v. Coakley* (I) fully addressed and rejected the constitutional challenges now reasserted. The petitioners have not demonstrated any change in controlling legal authority, new evidence, or a “blatantly erroneous” decision that would justify overturning the law of the case. The principles governing time, place, and manner regulation were well settled before 1970, and the Massachusetts law conforms to them.

For these reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
