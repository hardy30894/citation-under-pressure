# ARGUMENT

Petitioner Anthony Perry seeks judicial review of a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing his mixed case—one alleging that his suspension and forced retirement resulted from disability discrimination—on the ground that the underlying settlement agreement was voluntary and therefore outside the Board's jurisdiction. The question is whether such a jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case must be reviewed in district court, like other mixed-case dismissals, or whether it is instead reviewed in the Federal Circuit. The text of the Civil Service Reform Act, this Court's decision in **Kloeckner v. Solis**, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), and sound administration of the statutory scheme all compel the conclusion that district court review is proper.

## I. The Statute Directs Mixed Cases to District Court Regardless of the Ground of Dismissal

Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), Board decisions are generally reviewed in the Federal Circuit. But § 7703(b)(2) creates an exception for "[c]ases of discrimination," which "shall be filed" in district court. Section 7702(a)(1) defines such cases as those in which an employee "(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." As this Court held in Kloeckner, that statutory language creates a "mixed case" whenever an employee is "affected by an action…appealable to the MSPB" and "alleges discrimination." 568 U.S. at 604. Nothing in that definition turns on the procedural posture in which the Board disposes of the case.

Perry's case satisfies both statutory prongs. He was "affected by an action"—his suspension and retirement—of the type generally "appeal[able] to the Merit Systems Protection Board" under 5 U.S.C. §§ 7512, 7513(d). And he indisputably "allege[d] that a basis for the action was discrimination" prohibited by federal law. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(B). The Board's ultimate conclusion that Perry's retirement was voluntary, and thus not independently appealable, does not change the character of the claim he brought. Voluntariness is not a separate cause of action; it is a predicate finding the Board must make before proceeding to any adverse-action claim, mixed or otherwise. The statute nowhere suggests that the forum for judicial review should shift depending on whether the Board's jurisdictional predicate turns out, on the merits of that antecedent inquiry, to be satisfied.

## II. Kloeckner's Reasoning Applies with Equal Force to Jurisdictional Dismissals

The D.C. Circuit attempted to distinguish jurisdictional dismissals from procedural dismissals like the one at issue in Kloeckner, reasoning that in the latter category the employee was "affected by an action which she may appeal to the Board," 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), whereas in the former the action was "never 'appealable to the MSPB'" at all. Pet. App. That distinction cannot bear the weight the courts below have placed on it.

Kloeckner squarely held that "[t]he language of the statute…tells us that the Board's ruling, whatever its ground, is subject to judicial review in district court" once the case is a mixed case, because "the statute's plain language" resolves "the district court/court of appeals question" not by reference to the *ground* of the Board's disposition but by reference to the *nature of the claim* the employee raised. 568 U.S. at 50. This Court explained that Congress's purpose was to ensure that "employees do not have to file separate suits in two different courts" and to provide "one-stop" review for discrimination-related claims arising from federal employment. *Id.* at 54–56 (internal quotation marks omitted). That purpose is fully implicated whether the Board dismisses on timeliness grounds, as in Kloeckner, or on voluntariness grounds, as here—both determinations are threshold, non-merits rulings that foreclose reaching the discrimination claim, and both leave an employee in exactly the position Kloeckner sought to avoid: shuttling between two courts to vindicate a single, unified claim of discriminatory treatment.

Indeed, this Court in Kloeckner rejected the very line-drawing the D.C. Circuit reintroduced. The Court observed that the Board's dismissal "may rest on a wide variety of grounds," 568 U.S. at 44, and nowhere conditioned district-court review on the dismissal being "procedural" as opposed to "jurisdictional." To the contrary, the Court emphasized that "[j]udicial review shall be as provided in section 7703," 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(3), and that this provision applies to "any" Board decision falling within the mixed-case definition of § 7702(a)(1)—full stop.

## III. Practical Considerations Confirm That District Court Review Is Correct

Bifurcating review based on the *ground* of dismissal produces exactly the inefficiency Kloeckner sought to eliminate. As this Court recognized, employees "often" cannot know in advance which forum will ultimately prove correct, and requiring them to guess "risks the loss of the appeal altogether," since filing in the wrong court may result in a case being dismissed after the statute of limitations has run. 568 U.S. at 55–56. That risk is especially acute here: whether a settlement was "voluntary" frequently depends on factual questions—was the employee coerced, was the choice tainted by discriminatory pressure—that are intertwined with the merits of the discrimination claim itself. Forcing the Federal Circuit, which lacks general subject-matter expertise in discrimination law and cannot conduct de novo trial proceedings on discrimination claims, to unwind these overlapping determinations undermines Congress's unified scheme.

## CONCLUSION

Because Perry's case is a mixed case under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), and because Kloeckner's holding that mixed-case dismissals are reviewable in district court "whatever [the] ground" of dismissal is fully applicable here, the judgment below should be reversed, and this case should proceed in the United States District Court.
