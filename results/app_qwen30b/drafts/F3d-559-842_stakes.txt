**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand Corporation on the Mouas’ claim for rescission under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), and the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (HOEPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b). The record establishes that Rand’s failure to provide timely, clear, and conspicuous disclosures—coupled with its use of a materially defective cancellation notice—rendered the three-day rescission period ineffective, thereby triggering the three-year rescission window under TILA’s equitable tolling doctrine. The Mouas’ right to rescind was not waived, and the district court’s conclusion to the contrary misapplies binding precedent.

**I. The Three-Day Rescission Period Was Invalidly Triggered Because Rand Failed to Provide Required HOEPA Disclosures in a Clear and Conspicuous Manner**

Under TILA, a borrower has the right to rescind a transaction secured by a dwelling within three business days after the transaction is consummated, or after the borrower receives the required disclosures, whichever is later. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a). For high-cost mortgages—those subject to HOEPA—this right is triggered only after the lender provides a “clear and conspicuous” notice of the right to cancel, along with a copy of the Truth-in-Lending disclosures, at least three business days before the transaction closes. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b); *Hunt v. First Union Mortgage Corp.*, 347 F.3d 1058, 1062 (8th Cir. 2003) (“HOEPA’s three-day advance disclosure requirement is mandatory and cannot be waived by the borrower.”).

Here, Rand failed to provide the Mouas with a three-day advance HOEPA disclosure. The record confirms that the Mouas did not receive the required disclosures until the closing on April 22, 2005—on the same day they were required to sign the cancellation notice. This violates the plain language of § 1639(b), which mandates that disclosures be delivered “at least three business days before the transaction.” The fact that Rand claims the Mouas waived the three-day period does not excuse its failure to comply with the statutory disclosure mandate. *See* *Barnes v. First Union Mortgage Corp.*, 337 F.3d 1006, 1010 (8th Cir. 2003) (“A borrower’s waiver of the three-day rescission period is not effective if the lender fails to provide the required disclosures.”).

Moreover, the notice Rand provided was not “clear and conspicuous.” The Notice of Right to Cancel was embedded in a multi-page document, and the section requiring the borrower to affirm that more than three business days had passed—thereby waiving the right to rescind—was not separately highlighted or clearly labeled. *See* *Barnes*, 337 F.3d at 1011 (“A notice is not ‘clear and conspicuous’ if it is buried in a mass of other information or if its meaning is obscured by confusing language.”). The Mouas, who are not financial experts, could not reasonably have understood that signing the “Confirmation” section—on the same day they received the disclosures—constituted a waiver of their statutory right to rescind.

**II. The Waiver of the Three-Day Rescission Period Was Not Knowing, Voluntary, or Informed**

Even if a waiver were permissible, the record shows it was neither knowing nor voluntary. The Mouas were not informed of the significance of the waiver. Manisy Moua testified she was told to copy a statement in her own handwriting without explanation. The statement, which read: “Because we are aware that the county has not been timely in returning the payoff statement… we request that this 3 day review period be waived,” was not presented as a waiver of a legal right but as a procedural request. The Mouas were not advised that waiving the three-day rescission period would permanently forfeit their right to cancel the transaction.

The Eighth Circuit has held that a waiver of rescission rights must be “knowing and voluntary,” and that “a borrower cannot be deemed to have waived a statutory right unless the lender has made the nature and consequences of the waiver clear.” *Hunt*, 347 F.3d at 1063. Here, Rand failed to meet this standard. The waiver was not in writing, was not signed in the presence of independent counsel, and was not accompanied by any explanation of the consequences. The district court’s conclusion that the waiver was valid ignores binding precedent and the reality of the Mouas’ limited financial literacy and the coercive pressure of the impending foreclosure.

**III. The Three-Year Rescission Period Applies Because the Three-Day Period Was Invalid**

When a lender fails to comply with TILA’s disclosure requirements, the three-day rescission period is void, and the borrower may rescind within three years of the transaction. *See* *Barnes*, 337 F.3d at 1012 (“Where a lender fails to provide the required disclosures, the three-day rescission period does not begin to run, and the borrower may rescind within three years.”). This equitable rule ensures that borrowers are not deprived of their statutory rights due to a lender’s failure to comply with the law.

Rand’s failure to provide timely, clear, and conspicuous disclosures—combined with the use of a misleading cancellation notice and an invalid waiver—renders the three-day rescission period inoperative. The Mouas discovered the defects in the transaction shortly after closing, when they reviewed the documents with their son. Their rescission request was made within three years of the closing date, on April 22, 2008—well within the statutory window.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in concluding that the Mouas waived their right to rescind. Rand failed to comply with TILA and HOEPA’s mandatory disclosure requirements, and the waiver of the three-day rescission period was neither knowing nor voluntary. Under binding Eighth Circuit precedent, the three-year rescission period applies. The judgment of the district court is therefore reversed, and the case is remanded for entry of judgment in favor of the Mouas.
