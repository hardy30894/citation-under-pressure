# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park that has traditionally been available for public assembly and debate. Cf. *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939) (recognizing the distinct constitutional status of streets and parks traditionally used for public assembly and the communication of views among citizens). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to "social, civic and recreational" activities benefiting the community. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains substantial authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. This Court has recognized that a government entity may control the use of property under its charge for the purposes to which that property is lawfully dedicated, and need not throw the property open to every use a citizen might wish to make of it simply because access has been granted for some limited purposes. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966). Just as a State may regulate the times, places, and manner in which public ways and grounds are used, so long as the regulation is evenhanded and not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas, *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding a licensing scheme as a permissible regulation of "the time, the place, and the manner" of public assemblies, "without unwarranted discrimination"), Milford may likewise exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it has opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. Milford's Community Use Policy operates evenhandedly: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). Nor may the State sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting; this Court has held that the state may not tilt toward "a religion of secularism" any more than toward religious observance, for neutrality between the two is the constitutional touchstone. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. This Court has struck down the use of "tax-supported public school buildings" for religious instruction even where the program was conducted by outside religious teachers and attendance was not formally compelled. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred off school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands governmental neutrality among religions and between religion and nonreligion. This Court has made clear that "[n]either a state nor the Federal Government can, consistently with the First Amendment's guaranty of religious liberty, pass laws which aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another." *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, *McCollum*, and *Everson* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
