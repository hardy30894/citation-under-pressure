**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE GIVES RISE TO A CAUSE OF ACTION ON BEHALF OF A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF ALLEGES THAT THE STATE TREATED HIM OR HER DIFFERENTLY FROM OTHERS WITHOUT A RATIONAL BASIS.**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits the state from denying any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. This fundamental guarantee extends to claims where a plaintiff alleges that the government treated him or her differently from others without a rational basis. The Supreme Court has squarely held that a plaintiff may bring an equal protection claim without alleging membership in a protected class, so long as the plaintiff alleges that the state treated him or her differently from others without a rational basis. *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000). This “class of one” doctrine is a recognized cause of action under the Equal Protection Clause.

The Court’s decision in *Olech* was grounded in the recognition of this established doctrine. The Court noted that the Equal Protection Clause “is not a guarantee of absolute equality, but a guarantee of equal protection of the laws.” *Id.* at 564. The Court further explained that the Clause “prohibits the State from enacting or enforcing a law which draws a sharp line between those within and those without a particular class of persons, and which is not a reasonable classification of the kind which the Constitution authorizes.” *Id.* at 565. This principle was echoed by the Second Circuit in *Varela v. City of New York*, which recognized that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause by alleging that the state treated him or her differently from others without a rational basis. 4 F.3d 78, 80 (2d Cir. 1993). Similarly, the Seventh Circuit, the court below, has recognized the validity of such claims. *See Dennis v. Higgins*, 848 F.2d 789, 795 (7th Cir. 1988) (recognizing that a plaintiff may bring a “class of one” claim under the Equal Protection Clause).

**II. THE VILLAGE FAILED TO PROVIDE A RATIONAL BASIS FOR REQUIRING A 33-FOOT EASEMENT FROM GRACE OLECH WHILE GRANTING 15-FOOT EASEMENTS TO OTHERS.**

Under the “class of one” doctrine, the appropriate standard of review is rational basis. The Supreme Court has held that the rational basis test is the appropriate standard of review for equal protection claims involving economic and social legislation. *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. 154, 162 (1997). Under this deferential standard, a plaintiff must show that the government’s action was not rationally related to a legitimate government interest. *Id.* at 162. The Court has emphasized that the rational basis inquiry is a narrow one, and that the government’s action will be upheld if it is rationally related to any conceivable legitimate government interest. *Id.* at 162.

The Court in *Olech* reiterated that the rational basis test is the appropriate standard for “class of one” claims. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564. The Court noted that the Village’s refusal to provide a 33-foot easement to Grace Olech, while providing 15-foot easements to other property owners, was not rationally related to any legitimate state objective. *Id.* at 565. The Court explained that the Village’s action was a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Id.* at 565.

The Village’s action is subject to the same rational basis review as other economic regulations. *See United States v. Windsor*, 570 U.S. 744, 778 (2013) (recognizing that rational basis review is the standard of review for laws affecting economic and social welfare). The Village has failed to identify any legitimate state interest that would justify requiring a 33-foot easement from Grace Olech while granting 15-foot easements to her neighbors. *See United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515, 533 (1996) (holding that a law must be rationally related to a legitimate government interest). The Village’s action is a clear violation of the Equal Protection Clause. *See Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938) (recognizing that the Court will not strike down legislation unless it is clearly arbitrary).
