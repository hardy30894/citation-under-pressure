I. The Release Does Not Bar Bancorp’s Claims.

A release is a contract whose scope is determined by its plain language. See Seiden Assocs., Inc. v. ANC Holdings, Inc., 959 F.2d 425, 428 (2d Cir. 1992). The June 6, 1988 release discharges only claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language does not encompass claims arising from the FDIC’s subsequent payment of post-insolvency interest, sale of assets, or charging of expenses to the receivership estate. See County of Suffolk v. Alcorn, 266 F.3d 131, 142 (2d Cir. 2001) (release limited to matters expressly identified). Releases are construed narrowly against the drafter and do not bar later-arising claims outside their stated subject. See Consol. Edison, Inc. v. Northeast Utils., 426 F.3d 524, 528 (2d Cir. 2005); see also Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Epstein, 516 U.S. 367, 381 (1996).

The district court’s contrary reading would nullify the release’s limiting clause, violating the rule that every word must be given effect. See United States v. Chem. Found., Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926). Because the claims pleaded in the First Amended Complaint concern post-closure conduct, the release supplies no bar. See Bank of Am. Nat’l Trust & Sav. Ass’n v. Gillaizeau, 766 F.2d 709, 713 (2d Cir. 1985).

II. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Bar Bancorp’s Claims.

New York’s six-year limitations period for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), begins to run when the claim accrues. See Golden Pac. Bancorp v. FDIC, 273 F.3d 509, 518 (2d Cir. 2001). A claim against a receiver for improper distribution or accounting accrues only upon termination of the receivership. See United States v. Byrum, 408 U.S. 125, 138 (1972); see also FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471, 483 (1994). The receivership ended November 1, 1995; suit was filed the preceding day. Accrual therefore occurred within the statutory period. See Conopco, Inc. v. Roll Int’l, 231 F.3d 82, 89 (2d Cir. 2000).

The FDIC’s earlier payments of interest and expenses did not trigger accrual because Bancorp could not sue until the receivership concluded and the final accounting was rendered. See Stone v. Williams, 970 F.2d 1043, 1049 (2d Cir. 1992). Equitable tolling principles further confirm timeliness where the defendant controlled the timing of termination. See Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990).

The judgment should be reversed.
