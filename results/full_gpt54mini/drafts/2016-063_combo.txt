**Argument**

The decision below should be reversed because the Civil Service Reform Act sends mixed cases to district court, not the Federal Circuit, and nothing in the statute turns that forum choice on whether the MSPB labels its dismissal “jurisdictional.” The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that jurisdictional labels do not control where Congress has prescribed a different path for judicial review. See *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288 (1936); *Rock Island, Ark. & La. R. Co. v. United States*, 254 U.S. 141 (1920); *United States v. American Railway Express Co.*, 265 U.S. 425 (1924).

A mixed case is one in which an employee alleges discrimination and challenges an action that may be appealed to the MSPB. 5 U.S.C. §§ 7702(a)(1), 7703(b)(2). Perry did exactly that. He challenged a forced retirement and suspension and alleged race, age, and disability discrimination, as well as retaliation. Because the Board’s asserted lack of jurisdiction depended on the involuntariness of the settlement and retirement, the discrimination claim and the adverse-action question were inextricably linked. When federal law gives one court authority to resolve the whole controversy, piecemeal review should be avoided. See *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238 (1933); *Siler v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.*, 213 U.S. 175 (1909); *United States v. American Railway Express Co.*, 265 U.S. 425 (1924).

The Federal Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the settled principle that courts should decide a case on the broadest ground Congress has made available, not by fragmenting a single controversy into separate forums. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Court recognized the practical need for effective judicial review of federal rights; in *Giles v. Harris*, 189 U.S. 475 (1903), the Court likewise emphasized that jurisdictional doctrine must not defeat the enforcement of federal protections. The mixed-case provisions embody that same design. District court review permits one tribunal to address both the discrimination allegations and the validity of the adverse employment action. See *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238 (1933); *Siler v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.*, 213 U.S. 175 (1909).

The government’s forum rule would also undermine the well-established doctrine that administrative labels cannot deprive a litigant of judicial review where Congress has provided it. The Court has repeatedly rejected efforts to make review turn on formal characterization rather than substance. See *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22 (1932); *St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 38 (1936); *Morgan v. United States*, 298 U.S. 468 (1936). Here, the substance of Perry’s claim is that the agency imposed an adverse action infected by discrimination and then insulated the dispute by recasting the matter as a jurisdictional dismissal. That should not move the case to the Federal Circuit.

The D.C. Circuit relied on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), but that decision should not control because it reads the CSRA to create a jurisdictional trap inconsistent with the statute’s text and structure. The proper approach is the one this Court has long taken when a statute gives rise to overlapping issues: the court with authority over the federal claim should resolve the case in full. See *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238 (1933); *Siler v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.*, 213 U.S. 175 (1909).

Perry’s case belongs in district court because that is where Congress directed review of mixed cases. The contrary rule would force employees to guess, at the outset, whether the MSPB will later accept or reject jurisdiction and would allow the Board’s own ruling to determine the reviewing court. Nothing in the statute suggests Congress intended such circularity. Courts instead presume a coherent judicial scheme that avoids unnecessary duplication and uncertainty. See *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288 (1936); *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22 (1932).

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for review in district court.
