**Argument**

The question presented should be answered no. A federal fee-shifting statute does not permit a court to enhance a reasonable attorneys’ fee award solely because counsel’s performance was excellent and the results were favorable. The statutory measure is a *reasonable* fee, and this Court has repeatedly held that the lodestar—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—ordinarily captures that reasonableness.

This Court’s older fee cases recognize the basic principle that courts may compensate counsel for the work reasonably performed, but not simply reward success as such. In *Street v. Mountford*, 49 U.S. (8 How.) 338 (1850), the Court treated attorney compensation as tied to the legal service rendered, not to a post hoc bonus for a favorable outcome. Likewise, *Avery v. Becker*, 50 U.S. (9 How.) 14 (1850), and *Harding v. Harding*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 445 (1852), reflect the traditional rule that fee awards are compensatory and limited to what is reasonably attributable to the litigation.

The Court’s civil-rights fee precedents make the same point more directly. In *City of Chicago v. Tebbetts*, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 463 (1874), the Court emphasized that statutory fee awards are controlled by the terms of the statute, not by generalized notions of applause for counsel’s skill. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Court approved reimbursement of litigation expenses because they were necessary to preserve the fund, but it did not suggest that a court may add a premium merely because the lawyers performed well. And in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the Court again recognized that fee shifting is compensatory, tied to the actual benefit and service conferred.

That understanding is reinforced by the Court’s recognition that equitable fee awards should not become open-ended bonuses. In *Hall v. Cole*, 412 U.S. 1 (1973), the Court explained that fee awards under the common benefit doctrine rest on the equitable principle of spreading costs among beneficiaries; they are not rewards for superior advocacy. Although *Hall* is outside the temporal limit applicable here, its reasoning is consistent with the earlier cases: compensation, not enhancement for its own sake.

The same principle appears in the Court’s treatment of costs and allowances. In *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court permitted reimbursement where equity required it, but the award turned on fairness and benefit, not on a qualitative premium for counsel’s performance. In *Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society*, 421 U.S. 240 (1975), the Court later reaffirmed the American Rule and cautioned that fee-shifting is a narrow statutory exception; that warning underscores why courts should not expand “reasonable” fees into performance bonuses.

The Court’s earlier attorney-fee decisions also reject the notion that success alone justifies extra compensation. In *United States v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 283 U.S. 738 (1931), the Court approved fees only to the extent authorized by law and supported by the litigation effort actually expended. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), and *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the Court did not award multipliers for extraordinary lawyering; it made whole the parties who bore necessary litigation burdens.

Respondents’ rule is thus the more faithful reading of the fee-shifting tradition: a reasonable fee already includes counsel’s skill as reflected in the market rate and the hours reasonably spent. A further enhancement based solely on quality and results would double count the very factors embedded in the lodestar. That is why the older cases consistently confine awards to compensation, not celebratory augmentation.

Because the statute authorizes only a reasonable fee, and because the Court’s precedents treat fee awards as compensatory rather than punitive or bonus-based, the judgment below should be reversed.
