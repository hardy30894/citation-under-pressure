## Argument

### I. The Massachusetts statute is a valid, content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation.

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Act because it regulates access to a sensitive location, not speech because of its message. The Supreme Court has long held that the government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions in public fora so long as they are content neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Heffron v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647-49 (1981); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984).

The Act easily satisfies that standard. Massachusetts adopted a fixed buffer zone to protect access to reproductive health clinics, patients, and staff. The Court has repeatedly recognized that the government has a substantial interest in ensuring the free flow of traffic and the safe access of persons to public facilities. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 559, 562-64 (1965); *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 484-85 (1988). It has also upheld location-based restrictions directed to preserving access and order in sensitive settings. *Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768-70 (1994); *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 376-81 (1997).

The First Circuit’s analysis was especially consistent with *Madsen* and *Schenck*, which approved injunctions and restrictions around abortion clinics because the government’s interest in preventing obstruction, intimidation, and interference justified tailored limits on speech in the immediate vicinity of clinic entrances. *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 769-70; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376-81. Here, the Massachusetts law is less burdensome than the floating buffer invalidated in *Schenck* because it is fixed, marked, and limited to 35 feet. The law thus leaves protesters free to speak on sidewalks and public ways outside the zone, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and converse at a short distance away. That is precisely the kind of ample alternative channel that *Ward* requires. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791; *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 726-30 (2000).

The petitioners’ as-applied arguments do not change the constitutional calculus. The record showed that the plaintiffs continued to communicate effectively outside the three clinics through speech, signs, literature, and prayer. The Constitution does not require the government to preserve a speaker’s preferred manner of communication when reasonable alternatives remain. *Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 654-55; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 797-99. Nor does the First Amendment guarantee a right to stand at a particular distance from a captive audience in a sensitive setting. *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 484-85; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376.

The Court should also reject the suggestion that the law is content based merely because it applies to speech near reproductive health facilities. A regulation is not content based when it turns on location and the character of the place, not on agreement or disagreement with a message. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719-20; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. Massachusetts did not single out pro-life viewpoints; it regulated all persons within the zone, subject to neutral exemptions for patients, staff, emergency personnel, and passersby.

### II. Hill remains controlling and should not be overruled.

If *Hill* governs, the judgment should be affirmed. In *Hill*, the Court sustained a Colorado statute that created a floating buffer zone around persons entering healthcare facilities. 530 U.S. at 707-08, 726-30. The Court concluded that the law was content neutral, served significant interests in protecting access and privacy, and left open alternative channels for communication. *Id.* at 719-30. The Massachusetts law is narrower than the statute upheld in *Hill* because it creates a fixed zone, not a floating one.

Petitioners ask the Court to limit or overrule *Hill*, but stare decisis strongly counsels against such a step. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that precedent should not be discarded absent special justification. *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991); *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984). *Hill* has been applied as part of the settled First Amendment framework governing access to clinics and other sensitive locations. *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-70; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376-81. Petitioners identify no unworkable rule or intervening authority requiring departure from that decision. To the contrary, the Court’s modern cases continue to distinguish between content-based laws and neutral place regulations. *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155, 163-64 (2015), did not undermine the principle that laws directed to access, conduct, and location may be content neutral when justified without reference to speech content. *Id.* at 171-72.

In sum, the First Circuit correctly applied settled precedent. Massachusetts enacted a modest, neutral buffer zone to protect access to clinics while leaving open abundant avenues for protest and persuasion. Under *Ward*, *Clark*, *Heffron*, *Madsen*, *Schenck*, and *Hill*, the statute is constitutional, and there is no basis to overrule *Hill*.
