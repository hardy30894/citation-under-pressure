**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits punishments that are “cruel and unusual.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958). This Court has long recognized that the Amendment’s meaning evolves with the “mores” of society, reflecting a national consensus on what constitutes acceptable punishment. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 276 (1972) (Burger, C.J., concurring); *Graham v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 48, 60 (2010). In recent decades, this Court has held that the Eighth Amendment bars the imposition of the death penalty on certain categories of offenders whose culpability or moral blameworthiness is diminished by their mental condition. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311 (2002).

The question before this Court is whether the execution of individuals who are mentally retarded—now more accurately termed “intellectually disabled”—constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. The answer is unequivocally yes. The execution of such individuals violates the evolving standards of decency that mark a maturing society. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia, affirming Atkins’ death sentence, must be upheld.

This Court has previously held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the death penalty for juveniles under the age of 18. *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 555, 578 (2005). The reasoning in *Roper* applies with equal force here: “the juvenile offender is less mature, less responsible, and more susceptible to external influences than an adult.” Id. at 570. Similarly, individuals with intellectual disabilities possess diminished capacity for rational decision-making, impulse control, and understanding of consequences. They are less able to comprehend the nature and gravity of their actions, and thus less morally culpable than the average criminal. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311–12.

Moreover, the Nation has reached a clear consensus against executing individuals with intellectual disabilities. Since the time of *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), which permitted the execution of intellectually disabled offenders, 28 states have enacted statutes prohibiting such executions. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. The remaining states either do not impose the death penalty or have not applied it to intellectually disabled persons. This widespread legislative rejection reflects a national consensus that such executions are fundamentally incompatible with our evolving standards of decency. *Id.* at 318.

The dissent in *Atkins* mischaracterizes the role of the jury in capital sentencing. It suggests that juries may consider mitigating evidence, including intellectual disability, when deciding whether to impose death. But the issue is not whether mitigation can be considered—it is whether the death penalty itself can be imposed on those whose mental impairments render them constitutionally unfit for execution. The Eighth Amendment does not permit a state to execute someone simply because a jury chooses to ignore his diminished capacity. *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986) (“[A] person who is insane cannot be executed without violating the Eighth Amendment.”).

The State’s argument that the jury in this case considered Atkins’ intellectual disability and still sentenced him to death is beside the point. The constitutional inquiry is not whether a particular jury failed to heed mitigating evidence, but whether the law permits the death penalty at all for individuals whose mental condition renders them incapable of full moral responsibility. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 312. To hold otherwise would allow states to circumvent the Eighth Amendment by relying on flawed or inconsistent jury determinations. That is not the law.

The Supreme Court of Virginia correctly applied *Penry* in holding that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit the execution of intellectually disabled offenders. But *Penry* was decided before the Court had the benefit of the data and national consensus that now exist. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. The Court in *Atkins* explicitly overruled *Penry*’s application to intellectually disabled offenders. *Id.* at 318. The Court concluded that “the execution of the mentally retarded is ‘cruel and unusual punishment’ prohibited by the Eighth Amendment.” *Id.* at 319.

To uphold the death penalty in this case would undermine the integrity of the Eighth Amendment and contradict the Nation’s moral progress. The execution of an intellectually disabled individual is not merely disproportionate—it is fundamentally unjust. As this Court stated in *Atkins*: “We conclude that the execution of mentally retarded persons is grossly disproportionate and excessive punishment in violation of the Eighth Amendment.” *Id.* at 319.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia must be affirmed. The execution of Daryl Renard Atkins would violate the Eighth Amendment. The Constitution does not permit the state to take the life of a man whose intellect is so impaired that he cannot fully understand the consequences of his actions or appreciate the finality of death. Such a punishment is not just cruel—it is unconstitutional.

**WHEREFORE**, the respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia.
