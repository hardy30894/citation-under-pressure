The installation of fiber-optic cables with capacity far exceeding the BPA’s transmission needs, followed by the leasing of excess strands to third-party telecommunications carriers, effects a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s land beyond the 1953 easement. Such occupation is a per se taking. Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982); United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261 (1946).

An easement limited to “appurtenant signal lines … necessary in connection” with power transmission does not authorize the government to burden the servient estate with commercial fiber capacity installed for projected wholesale transactions through 2025 and then leased for profit. When the government’s physical presence exceeds the scope of its acquired right, the excess constitutes a new taking. Hendler v. United States, 952 F.2d 1364, 1375 (Fed. Cir. 1991); Preseault v. United States, 100 F.3d 1525, 1537 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc).

The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that “the scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking.” Scope is dispositive: an occupation authorized by an easement is not a taking, but an occupation that the easement does not permit is. See Nollan v. California Coastal Comm’n, 483 U.S. 825, 832 (1987); Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164, 179–80 (1979). The undisputed record shows that only a fraction of the thirty-six fibers crossing Tuthill’s property serves BPA’s current operational needs; the remainder exists solely to accommodate future commercial leases. This additional physical presence is not “necessary” under the easement and therefore requires compensation. Ridge Line, Inc. v. United States, 346 F.3d 1346, 1352 (Fed. Cir. 2003).

The government’s argument that no new physical intrusion occurred because the cables occupy the same space misapprehends the nature of the occupation. Each additional fiber strand is a distinct physical object laid across Tuthill’s property; leasing those strands to third parties places new users in permanent possession of that space. See Yee v. City of Escondido, 503 U.S. 519, 527 (1992); Tabb Lakes, Ltd. v. United States, 10 F.3d 796, 800 (Fed. Cir. 1993). The resulting burden is not merely an increase in degree but a change in kind—from a limited governmental signal line to an open commercial telecommunications corridor.

Because the government has effected a permanent physical occupation without payment, Tuthill is entitled to just compensation measured by the difference in value of its property before and after the unauthorized installation and leasing. United States v. General Motors Corp., 323 U.S. 373, 378 (1945); Florida Rock Indus., Inc. v. United States, 18 F.3d 1560, 1564 (Fed. Cir. 1994). The judgment granting summary judgment to the United States should be reversed.
