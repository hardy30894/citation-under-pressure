**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit’s objective standard for “true threats” under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) cannot be reconciled with the First Amendment. In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Court defined true threats as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” *Id.* at 359. This formulation requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended the communication to be understood as a threat.

The objective “reasonable speaker” test applied below, drawn from *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), measures only whether a hypothetical listener would perceive a threat. That inquiry omits the speaker’s own mental state and therefore reaches protected speech that the speaker did not intend to threaten. *Black* rejected precisely this approach when it invalidated a Virginia statute that permitted conviction on a showing that the speaker “should have known” the cross-burning would intimidate. 538 U.S. at 360.

Statutory text reinforces the constitutional requirement. Section 875(c) punishes one who “transmits” a communication “containing any threat.” The verb “threaten” has long been understood to demand purposeful or knowing conduct. *See Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam) (distinguishing “political hyperbole” from a true threat on the ground that the speaker did not intend to threaten). Absent clear congressional direction to the contrary, courts presume that criminal statutes require proof of mens rea as to every element that separates lawful from unlawful conduct. *See Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994).

The government’s contrary reading would criminalize a vast range of protected expression—satire, rap lyrics, and heated political rhetoric—whenever a listener might reasonably feel threatened. Such a regime is incompatible with the “breathing space” the First Amendment demands. *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 272 (1964). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions rest on an impermissible basis and must be reversed.
