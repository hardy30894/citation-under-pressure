## ARGUMENT

Title VII’s punitive damages provision, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), permits recovery only when the employer acts “with malice or with reckless indifference” to federally protected rights. That standard incorporates the common-law requirement that the defendant’s conduct be independently egregious or outrageous—not simply intentional discrimination. Reading the statute otherwise would conflate liability with the heightened blameworthiness necessary for punishment.

### I. THE COMMON LAW DEMANDS EGREGIOUS CONDUCT FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES

At common law, punitive damages require more than intentional wrongdoing. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting) (characterizing the majority’s standard as “reckless or callous” but noting that most States require “evil motive or actual malice”). This Court has consistently described punitive damages as reserved for conduct that is “reprehensible,” *BMW of North America v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 575 (1996), “outrageous,” *Pacific Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip*, 499 U.S. 1, 21 (1991), or “grossly negligent and wanton,” *Browning-Ferris Indus. v. Kelco Disposal*, 492 U.S. 257, 275 (1989). Congress borrowed Section 1981a(b)(1) from this common-law tradition, and the Court presumes that Congress intends to adopt the established meaning of common-law terms. *Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 307 (1992).

Every unanimous Court to consider punitive damages under analogous statutes has required conduct rising above bare intentional discrimination. *See, e.g., TXO Prod. Corp. v. Alliance Resources*, 509 U.S. 443, 462 (1993) (plurality opinion) (emphasizing “the character of the defendant’s conduct”). The Civil Rights Act of 1991’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended the same limit. Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum—signed by seven other sponsors—stated that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (1991). That view is consistent with the House Report, which required “an even higher standard than the conscious disregard of the rights of others.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 74 (1991).

### II. THE STATUTORY TEXT REQUIRES A CONDUCT-BASED INQUIRY

Section 1981a(b)(1) demands that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice “*with* malice or *with* reckless indifference.” The preposition “with” ties the mental state to the *conduct*, not merely the fact of discrimination. An employer who intentionally discriminates—even knowing that the act is unlawful—does not necessarily act *with* malice. Malice requires an “evil motive,” and reckless indifference requires a “conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustified risk” that is “grossly” above ordinary negligence. *See Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 836 (1994) (defining deliberate indifference in Eighth Amendment context). 

Every circuit to address the issue before the 1991 Act required egregious conduct for punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, which Congress used as a model. *See, e.g., Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy-Province of Detroit*, 816 F.2d 1104, 1109 (6th Cir. 1987) (requiring “egregious” discrimination); *McKnight v. GMC*, 908 F.2d 104, 115 (7th Cir. 1990) (requiring “willful, wanton, or malicious” conduct). The one circuit that applied a lower standard, *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987), was later repudiated in *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (stating that punitive damages under § 1981a require a “higher hurdle” than intentional discrimination). 

### III. INTERPRETING THE STATUTE TO REQUIRE EGREGIOUS CONDUCT COMPORTS WITH DUE PROCESS

This Court has recognized that punitive damages pose due process concerns when imposed for conduct that is not “sufficiently reprehensible.” *Gore*, 517 U.S. at 575. If every intentional discrimination claim automatically could support punitive damages, employers would face punishment for conduct that is already fully redressable through back pay, compensatory damages, and injunctive relief. That would undermine the “goal of reasoned, nonarbitrary decisionmaking” that punitive damages standards must serve. *Honda Motor Co. v. Oberg*, 512 U.S. 415, 432 (1994). 

### IV. CONCLUSION

The court of appeals correctly held that an employer’s conduct must be egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind, to sustain punitive damages under Title VII. The District Court properly denied punitive damages here because Kolstad offered no evidence of conduct beyond intentional discrimination. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
