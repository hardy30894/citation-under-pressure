**ARGUMENT**

I. The district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because substantial evidence does not support the jury’s verdict that Leader’s Leader2Leader® product was on sale or publicly used more than one year before the critical date.

A. The on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) requires that the invention be the subject of a commercial offer for sale or a public use that places the invention in the public domain. *Elizabeth v. Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 134 (1878) (“The use must be public, and not confined to the inventor and those to whom he may choose to exhibit the machine.”). The Federal Circuit has consistently held that the on-sale bar applies only when the invention is “ready for patenting” and “the subject of a commercial offer for sale.” *Monsanto Co. v. McFarling*, 488 F.3d 1316, 1326 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (citing *Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998)). Here, the asserted claims were not reduced to practice until after the critical date, and Leader’s Leader2Leader® product was merely a prototype, not a commercial product. *See Macbeth-Evans Glass Co. v. General Elec. Co.*, 246 F. 695, 700 (6th Cir. 1917) (“A mere experiment with a view to perfecting the device is not a public use.”).

B. The district court improperly relied on Leader’s white paper to the Wright Patterson Air Force Base as evidence of a commercial offer for sale. A white paper does not constitute a commercial offer for sale under § 102(b). *See Deering Milliken, Inc. v. Temp–Rez, Inc.*, 436 F.2d 753, 758 (2d Cir. 1970) (holding that a proposal to license technology did not constitute a commercial offer for sale). The white paper was merely a proposal to license the Leader2Leader® product, not an offer to sell the patented invention itself. *See id.* (“The mere submission of a proposal to license does not constitute an offer to sell.”).

C. The district court erred in finding that Leader’s demonstrations of the Leader2Leader® product to potential customers constituted public use. Public use requires that the invention be used in its patented form by someone other than the inventor in a manner that places the invention in the public domain. *Elizabeth*, 97 U.S. at 134. Leader’s demonstrations were limited to the inventor and a small group of potential customers under confidentiality agreements, and the product was not in its patented form. *See Smith & Griggs Mfg. Co. v. Sprague*, 123 U.S. 249, 267 (1887) (“The use must be such as to give the public the right to use the invention.”).

II. The district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because the asserted claims were not anticipated by the Leader2Leader® product.

A. Anticipation under § 102(b) requires that every limitation of the claimed invention be found in a single prior art reference. *Graham v. John Deere Co.*, 383 U.S. 1, 17 (1966) (“[A] patent is invalid for anticipation only if each and every element of the claimed invention is found in a single prior art reference.”). The Leader2Leader® product did not disclose all the limitations of the asserted claims, particularly the dynamic updating of metadata and the tracking of user movement between user environments. *See Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Co. v. Supermarket Equip. Corp.*, 340 U.S. 147, 153 (1950) (“[A]nticipation requires that the prior art reference disclose every element of the claimed invention.”).

B. The district court improperly relied on Leader’s founder’s testimony that the Leader2Leader® product was covered by the asserted claims. A patent owner’s subjective belief that a product practices the patent does not establish anticipation. *See Expanded Metal Co. v. Bradford*, 214 U.S. 366, 385 (1909) (“The question is not what the inventor believed, but what the patent discloses and what the prior art teaches.”).

III. The district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because the asserted claims were not obvious under 35 U.S.C. § 103.

A. Obviousness requires a showing that the claimed invention would have been obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the art at the time the invention was made. *Graham*, 383 U.S. at 17. The district court improperly relied on hindsight reconstruction to find that the asserted claims would have been obvious. *See United States v. Adams*, 383 U.S. 39, 52 (1966) (“[C]ourts should not resort to hindsight by ignoring what was not known at the time of the invention.”).

B. The district court erred in finding that the asserted claims would have been obvious in light of the Leader2Leader® product and other prior art. The combination of prior art references does not teach or suggest all the limitations of the asserted claims, particularly the dynamic updating of metadata and the tracking of user movement between user environments. *See Great Atlantic*, 340 U.S. at 153 (“[A] combination of familiar elements according to known methods is likely to be obvious when it does no more than yield predictable results.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law. The jury’s verdict that the asserted claims were invalid under § 102(b) is not supported by substantial evidence, and the district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law or, in the alternative, for a new trial on the invalidity issues.
