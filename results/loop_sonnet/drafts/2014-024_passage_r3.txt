**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long held that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement, not to dispense with it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of regulatory "public welfare" offenses recognized in *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). *Balint* upheld dispensing with scienter only in circumstances where, as this Court explained, a defendant "deals with others and his mere negligence may be dangerous," 258 U.S. at 252, placing the risk of mistake on an actor engaged in a regulated activity. A prosecution for speech transmitted over the Internet is far removed from that regulatory context; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty or would punish conduct undertaken without a wrongful purpose. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court construed a federal civil-rights statute's "willfully" requirement to demand that the defendant act with a specific intent to deprive a person of rights made definite by law, in order to avoid rendering the statute unconstitutionally vague as applied to defendants who did not know their conduct was unlawful. *Id.* at 101–104 (plurality opinion). Likewise, this Court has instructed that criminal statutes touching on liberty must be construed narrowly, resolving doubt against extending criminal liability by inference absent a clear indication that Congress intended to reach such conduct. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176–177 (1952). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." This Court has explained that "there are certain well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech" that fall outside constitutional protection, and that such utterances are "no essential part of any exposition of ideas." *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). Because the category is narrow and must be defined with precision, this Court's own application of the true-threat concept confirms that the inquiry cannot rest on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous or hyperbolic language. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam), the Court held that "we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by petitioner fits within that statutory term," even though the literal words—a statement that if made to carry a rifle the first man in his sights would be the President—could have been understood by some listeners as menacing. The Court's focus was on the character of the speech itself and the speaker's evident meaning, not simply on audience perception.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression—dark humor, hyperbole, rap lyrics, venting—because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has recognized that "First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive." *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Where scienter requirements are absent, "the ordinance tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter." *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 153 (1959). And as this Court has explained, one who bears the risk of being found to have acted unlawfully "necessarily must steer far wider of the unlawful zone" than the Constitution actually requires. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before punishment may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951) (requiring proof that speech was intended to produce unlawful ends before liability may be imposed).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense—punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952); *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–102 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
