**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE PROCEDURAL DEFAULT OF CARPENTER’S SUFFICIENCY-OF-THE-EVIDENCE CLAIM CANNOT BE EXCUSED BECAUSE HIS CLAIM OF INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL CANNOT SERVE AS “CAUSE” TO CURE THAT DEFAULT, GIVEN THAT THE IAC CLAIM ITSELF IS PROCEDURALLY DEFAULTED.**

A state prisoner may not obtain federal habeas relief for a claim that has been procedurally defaulted unless the petitioner demonstrates both "cause" for the default and "actual prejudice" resulting from the alleged constitutional violation. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b)(1)(B). "Cause" requires an objective factor external to the petitioner that impeded efforts to comply with the state procedural rule. *Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes*, 504 U.S. 1, 8 (1992). Here, Carpenter’s claim of ineffective assistance of counsel (IAC) is itself procedurally defaulted. Consequently, Carpenter cannot use this defaulted claim to establish "cause" to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.

The Supreme Court has squarely held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to establish "cause" to excuse a procedural default if the IAC claim itself is procedurally defaulted. *United States v. Nix*, 493 U.S. 115, 125 (1990). The Court explained that "a petitioner may not use a procedural default to excuse a procedural default." *Id.* at 125. This rule prevents a circularity in the habeas process where a petitioner asks the court to resolve the default of a claim in order to determine if that claim provides cause to excuse a different default. *Id.* at 124-25. Because Carpenter’s IAC claim was dismissed by the Ohio Court of Appeals as untimely and affirmed by the Ohio Supreme Court, it is procedurally defaulted. *See* Ohio Rev. Code § 2953.21. Therefore, Carpenter cannot invoke this defaulted claim to establish the "cause" necessary to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency claim.

Furthermore, Carpenter’s reliance on the "cause" exception is misplaced because the alleged IAC does not constitute an objective factor external to him. *Keeney*, 504 U.S. at 8. The "cause" exception is designed to address situations where a petitioner is prevented from complying with a state procedural rule by some external impediment, such as a breakdown in the functioning of the state courts. *Id.* at 8-9. Here, Carpenter’s failure to raise the sufficiency claim on direct appeal was due to his own choice to plead guilty and his own failure to timely seek relief in state court. The alleged IAC by his appellate counsel does not constitute an external impediment; rather, it is a claim arising from the very procedural default Carpenter seeks to excuse. The Sixth Circuit has consistently applied *Nix* to bar a petitioner from using a procedurally defaulted IAC claim to establish cause for another default. *See, e.g., United States v. Brown*, 311 F.3d 730, 734 (6th Cir. 2002).

For these reasons, Carpenter has failed to demonstrate the "cause" required to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. Accordingly, the judgment of the district court should be reversed.
