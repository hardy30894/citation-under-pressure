**Argument**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that navigability for title turns on whether each relevant river segment was navigable in fact at the time Montana entered the Union. That rule follows directly from the equal-footing doctrine and this Court’s repeated instruction that title to submerged lands vests, if at all, “at the time of statehood.” *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 132 S. Ct. 1215, 1228 (2012); *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 34 (1997); *Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Mississippi*, 484 U.S. 469, 475 (1988). The inquiry is not whether the river is navigable today, nor whether later dams, channel alterations, or downstream improvements created present-day navigability. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75-76 (1931); *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922).

This Court has long treated navigability for title as a historical inquiry. In *The Daniel Ball*, the Court explained that waters are navigable in fact when they are used, or susceptible of being used, “in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce.” 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). For title purposes, that question is applied as of the moment of statehood. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-76; *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926). In *Utah*, the Court emphasized that “[t]he question remains one of fact, to be determined in each case upon all the circumstances,” 283 U.S. at 75, and then examined the river’s condition at the time Utah entered the Union. Likewise, in *Holt State Bank*, the Court looked to whether the water body was navigable “at the time of statehood,” 270 U.S. at 56.

Petitioner’s attempt to broaden the inquiry to some later or generalized stretch of river is incompatible with the rule of equal footing. The doctrine gives a new State title only to “lands beneath waters then navigable,” *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935), because the federal government holds submerged lands in trust until statehood and then transfers title only if the waters are navigable in fact at that time. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-76; *Alaska*, 521 U.S. at 34. That is why this Court in *Montana v. United States* described the federal navigational servitude as extending to “navigable rivers acquired by states upon their entrance into the Union.” 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). Title and servitude alike depend on the condition of the river at statehood. *Id.*; *Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co. v. City of Chicago*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941).

Nor does the Constitution permit a trial court to infer title from navigability of an entire river system without segment-specific proof. This Court has rejected any notion that a whole river is either navigable or not in gross. In *United States v. Utah*, the Court required an assessment of “the particular stream” and noted that navigability may vary by segment. 283 U.S. at 80-81. Later, in *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, the Court reaffirmed that navigability must be assessed with attention to the “particular segment” and its physical condition. 311 U.S. 377, 408-09 (1940). In *PPL Montana*, the Court again explained that “title navigability must be determined on a segment-by-segment basis.” 132 S. Ct. at 1233.

That rule makes sense because title vests only in those riverbeds over which the United States would have conveyed ownership to the State at statehood. *United States v. Idaho*, 533 U.S. 262, 274 (2001); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57-58 (1894). If one segment was not navigable in fact at statehood, the State acquired no title to that bed merely because some other segment upstream or downstream was navigable. *PPL Montana*, 132 S. Ct. at 1233-34.

Finally, the Montana court’s reliance on historical evidence was proper because the relevant question is historical. This Court has repeatedly accepted historical sources, including maps, logs, and testimony about contemporaneous use, to determine navigability at statehood. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76-77; *Missouri v. Nebraska*, 196 U.S. 23, 31-32 (1904); *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 698-99 (1899). The trial court therefore did exactly what this Court’s precedents require: it determined whether each disputed river segment was navigable in fact in 1889, when Montana entered the Union. That is the constitutional test for title.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
