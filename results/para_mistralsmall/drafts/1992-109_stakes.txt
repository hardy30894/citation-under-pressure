**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires a Trial Court to Apply a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Standard When Assessing a Defendant’s Competency to Waive Counsel or Enter a Guilty Plea.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects an accused from conviction and punishment absent a knowing, voluntary, and intelligent waiver of fundamental constitutional rights. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 396 (1993) (citing *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938)). This protection extends to waivers of the right to counsel and the right to plead not guilty. *Id.* at 396-97. However, the standard for determining whether a defendant is competent to waive these rights is not the same as the standard for determining competency to stand trial. *Id.* at 399-400.

A defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (quoting *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam)). This is a minimal threshold. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 400. By contrast, competency to waive counsel or plead guilty requires a higher degree of mental functioning—specifically, the capacity for a “reasoned choice” among the available alternatives. *Id.* at 400-01 (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981)).

This Court has long recognized that the decision to waive counsel or plead guilty is “among the most important a defendant can make.” *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 401 (quoting *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 834 (1975)). The “reasoned choice” standard ensures that a defendant’s waiver is not the product of confusion, impulsivity, or mental impairment. *Id.* at 401. It demands that the defendant possess the capacity to understand the nature of the charges, the possible penalties, and the consequences of waiving counsel or pleading guilty. *Id.* (citing *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242-43 (1969)).

**II. The Trial Court Failed to Apply the Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Standard, Violating Due Process.**

Here, the trial court accepted Moran’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas without conducting any inquiry into whether he possessed the capacity for a “reasoned choice.” The record reflects substantial evidence that Moran was not competent to make such a decision. He had recently attempted suicide, was taking multiple medications, and responded to the court in monosyllables. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 266-67 (9th Cir. 1992). The court’s cursory inquiry—merely asking whether Moran was taking medication and accepting his monosyllabic responses—was constitutionally insufficient. *Id.* at 267.

The trial court’s failure to apply the heightened standard violated due process. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399-400. Due process requires a trial court to initiate a competency hearing, sua sponte, whenever it has a “good faith doubt” about the defendant’s competency to waive rights. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987) (citing *Seiling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973)). A good faith doubt arises when there is “substantial evidence of incompetence.” *Id.* (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1982)). Here, the evidence of Moran’s incompetence was overwhelming.

**III. The Post-Conviction Hearing Did Not Cure the Error.**

The state post-conviction court applied the wrong legal standard—competency to stand trial rather than “reasoned choice”—and relied on psychiatric evaluations that assessed only the former. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 270-72. The federal habeas court must review the state court’s legal conclusions de novo. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. Because the state court applied the incorrect standard, its factual findings are not entitled to deference. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963).

Moreover, the psychiatric evaluations did not address whether Moran could make a “reasoned choice.” *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 272-74. They focused solely on his ability to consult with counsel and understand the proceedings—insufficient to determine competency to waive rights. *Id.* The trial court’s failure to apply the heightened standard, combined with the state court’s erroneous post-conviction review, deprived Moran of due process.

**CONCLUSION**

The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when assessing a defendant’s competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. The trial court here failed to do so, and the error was not cured by the post-conviction proceedings. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
