**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONGRESS LACKED CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY TO ENACT 18 U.S.C. § 1951**

Appellant Dominic Pearson contends that § 1951 of Title 18, the Hobbs Act, exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers under the Commerce Clause. This Court should reverse his conviction on that ground.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that Congress may regulate under the Commerce Clause only activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 612 (2000) (“[T]he proper test requires an analysis of whether the regulated activity ‘substantially affects’ interstate commerce.”). Section 1951 criminalizes interference with commerce “by robbery or extortion.” 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). To survive constitutional scrutiny, the statute must be limited to conduct that, in the aggregate, substantially affects interstate commerce.

Here, the government presented no evidence that the robbery of Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas—an intrastate restaurant—had any substantial effect on interstate commerce. The only evidence offered was that the restaurant purchased goods from out-of-state suppliers. *See* Trial Tr. at 120 (Mr. Peterson testifying that the restaurant buys goods from both Kansas and out-of-state companies). This Court has rejected such attenuated connections as insufficient to support federal jurisdiction. *See United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 561–62 (1995) (holding that mere possession of a firearm in a school zone did not substantially affect interstate commerce, even though education is tied to commerce); *see also Morrison*, 529 U.S. at 613 (finding that gender-motivated violence did not substantially affect interstate commerce).

Moreover, the government did not allege that the stolen funds were destined for interstate commerce or that the robbery disrupted any commercial transaction. The evidence showed only a local theft of cash. Under *Lopez* and *Morrison*, such conduct falls outside Congress’s commerce power. The district court therefore erred in denying appellant’s motion to dismiss the indictment based on the unconstitutionality of § 1951.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING APPELLANT’S TARDY ALIBI DEFENSE**

Appellant also challenges the district court’s exclusion of his proffered alibi evidence. The Fifth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to present a defense, *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 51 (1987), and due process requires that criminal trials be fundamentally fair. *See In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). However, a defendant does not have an absolute right to present late or untimely evidence. *United States v. Cook*, 949 F.2d 289, 293 (10th Cir. 1991).

Here, appellant sought to introduce evidence that he was having his watch repaired at the time of the robbery. The district court excluded this evidence as untimely under Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1, which requires notice of an alibi defense at least ten days before trial. The court found that appellant failed to show good cause for the delay. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1(d).

This Court reviews the exclusion of evidence for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Pena*, 949 F.2d 751, 756 (10th Cir. 1991). The district court did not abuse its discretion. Appellant did not explain why he could not have timely disclosed the alibi until the eve of trial. The court’s ruling was consistent with the purposes of Rule 12.1—to prevent surprise and ensure fair preparation. *See United States v. James*, 528 F.2d 999, 1007 (5th Cir. 1976). Accordingly, the exclusion of the alibi evidence was proper.

**III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT CONVICTION**

Appellant next argues that the evidence was insufficient to support his conviction for violating § 1951 and for felony murder. This Court reviews the sufficiency of the evidence de novo, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government and asking whether any rational jury could have found the essential elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

To convict under § 1951, the government must prove that the defendant obstructed, delayed, or affected commerce by robbery. 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). As discussed above, the government failed to prove that the robbery had any substantial effect on interstate commerce. Even if it had, the evidence tying appellant to the robbery was weak. The only direct evidence came from accomplices who had pleaded guilty and stood to benefit from their testimony. *See* Trial Tr. at 50–70 (Courtney Martin’s testimony). Their credibility was undermined by inconsistencies and the fact that they implicated appellant while minimizing their own roles.

Moreover, the identification evidence was unreliable. Angela Starks initially failed to identify appellant in a photo array and later saw his picture on television before making an in-court identification. *See* Trial Tr. at 100–05. This Court has cautioned against admitting identifications tainted by suggestive procedures. *United States v. Angulo-Lopez*, 791 F.2d 1394, 1398 (10th Cir. 1986). Given the weakness of the government’s case, no rational jury could have found appellant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON FELONY MURDER**

Appellant contends that the district court’s felony-murder instruction misstated the law. Under Kansas law, felony murder requires proof that the killing occurred during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony. *State v. Rueckert*, 252 Kan. 109, 116 (1993). The district court instructed the jury that appellant could be convicted of felony murder if the killing occurred during a robbery, even if the killing was accidental.

This instruction was erroneous because it relieved the government of proving that the underlying felony was inherently dangerous. *See id.* at 117 (requiring that the felony be inherently dangerous to human life). The robbery of a restaurant, while serious, is not inherently dangerous to the same extent as, for example, burglary of a dwelling at night. *Cf. State v. Ordway*, 261 Kan. 776, 789 (1997) (holding that burglary is inherently dangerous). The district court’s instruction therefore permitted conviction based on an incorrect legal standard.

**V. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN REFUSING TO INSTRUCT ON LESSER INCLUDED OFFENSES**

Finally, appellant argues that the district court erred in refusing to instruct the jury on lesser included offenses, such as misdemeanor theft. This Court reviews the refusal to give a lesser included offense instruction for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Heinlein*, 799 F.2d 1326, 1329 (10th Cir. 1986).

A lesser included offense instruction is required only if the evidence would permit a jury rationally to find the defendant guilty of the lesser offense while acquitting him of the greater. *Schmuck v. United States*, 489 U.S. 705, 716 n.8 (1989). Here, the evidence showed that appellant participated in a robbery that resulted in a death. There was no evidence to support a finding that he committed only a theft. The district court therefore did not err in refusing the instruction.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, appellant’s conviction should be reversed. The Hobbs Act exceeds Congress’s commerce power as applied to this case, and the district court erred in excluding appellant’s alibi evidence, instructing the jury on felony murder, and refusing to instruct on lesser included offenses. The evidence was also insufficient to support the conviction.
