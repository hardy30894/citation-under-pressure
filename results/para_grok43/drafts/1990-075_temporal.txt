The Controlled Substances Act's temporary scheduling provisions do not transgress the constitutional prohibition on delegation of legislative power. Article I, Section 1 vests "All legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress, yet this Court has long recognized that Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Act supplies such a principle by enumerating eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, 21 U.S.C. § 811(c), requiring a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services that binds the Attorney General whenever the Secretary recommends against scheduling, id. § 811(b), and limiting temporary scheduling to substances that pose "an imminent hazard to the public safety" after consideration of the history, pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse together with the risk to public health, id. § 811(h)(1), (3).

These constraints are materially more definite than those sustained in earlier statutes regulating economic activity. In Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), the Court upheld a tariff provision that authorized the President to suspend statutory duties upon finding that foreign governments imposed "reciprocally unequal and unreasonable" duties on American products. Id. at 680. The same tolerance for practical delegation appears in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), where Congress empowered an administrator to fix maximum prices that "will effectuate the purposes of this Act" to stabilize wartime prices, subject only to enumerated considerations of production costs, profits, and supply. Id. at 420, 426. The temporary-scheduling criteria are no less intelligible.

Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), do not compel a different result. Both involved statutes that conferred "virtually unfettered" authority without any "criterion to govern the President's course." Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 415. Here, by contrast, the statute itself prescribes the precise factors, the mandatory consultation with the Secretary, and the temporal limits on temporary orders. The existence of these standards distinguishes the delegation from the "unfettered" grants invalidated in 1935.

The Attorney General's subdelegation of temporary-scheduling authority to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration is likewise consistent with settled principles. Congress may authorize an executive officer to assign duties to subordinates when the statute so provides, as it does here. See United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1926) (upholding delegation by the President to the Alien Property Custodian of authority to sell seized property). The regulation effectuating that assignment, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely implements the statutory grant and does not enlarge the power Congress conferred.

Because the Act supplies an intelligible principle and the subdelegation rests on express statutory authority, the convictions should be affirmed.
