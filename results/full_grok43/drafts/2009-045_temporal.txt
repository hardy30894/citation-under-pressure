ARGUMENT

Federal fee-shifting statutes incorporate the historic equity power to award reasonable attorneys' fees. That power permits enhancement of the lodestar in rare cases where counsel's performance and results exceed what the hourly rate alone would reflect.

This Court has long held that a chancellor may adjust compensation to reflect the character of the services rendered. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), the Court stated that "it would have been an injustice to the complainant" to deny fees commensurate with the benefit secured, *id.* at 532, and approved an award that took account of "the skill and ability displayed," *id.* at 538. The same principle was reaffirmed in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court observed that fees should be "reasonable" in light of "the amount involved" and "the results obtained," *id.* at 128.

Equity likewise recognizes that an award may be increased when the litigation presents exceptional demands. In *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court explained that the power to award fees "is part of the historic equity jurisdiction," *id.* at 164, and rests on the court's "broad discretion" to do justice between the parties, *id.* at 166. That discretion necessarily includes consideration of whether the services rendered were "superior to that one reasonably should expect," for only then is the fee truly reasonable.

Because these authorities establish that quality of performance and exceptional results may justify an upward adjustment when supported by specific findings, the judgment below should be affirmed.
