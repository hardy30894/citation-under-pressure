**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford's Community Use Policy Restricts Access to the School Building by Subject Matter and Function, Not by Viewpoint, and Rests on Milford's Lawful Authority to Control Its Own Property**

A school building is not a street or park. In *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), this Court held that certain public places "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Id.* at 515. A schoolhouse opened after hours for community activities carries no such history. Government retains broad control over property it owns and administers. In *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), this Court held that "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Id.* at 47. Milford dedicated its after-hours facilities to "social, civic and recreational" activities "pertaining to the welfare of the community." It did not dedicate the building to religious worship or instruction, and it excludes such use uniformly, regardless of the religious tradition involved.

This is a limitation tied to the forum's purpose, not an effort to silence a disfavored opinion on an otherwise permitted topic. The Policy does not ask whether a speaker favors or disfavors religion as an answer to questions of morality; it excludes an entire category of activity—worship, proselytizing, and religious instruction—from a forum dedicated to civic and recreational ends. That the Good News Club's activities include prayer, memorization of scripture, and an invitation for children to "receive Jesus as their personal Savior" places its meetings squarely within the excluded category, applicable to any denomination that would conduct the same exercises.

**II. Milford's Policy Is Justified by Its Substantial Interest, Rooted in This Court's Precedents, in Avoiding an Establishment of Religion in Its Elementary School**

This Court has long recognized that the public schoolhouse demands particular vigilance against state-sponsored religious exercise, because of the special impressionability of the young children who attend it. In *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), the Court struck down a released-time program that used public school classrooms for sectarian religious instruction, explaining that "the State's compulsory public school machinery" was thereby used to "provide pupils for their religious classes." *Id.* at 212. The Court condemned exactly what is at issue here: use of the schoolhouse itself, immediately adjoining and continuous with the school day, for religious teaching directed at children who are there because they are enrolled at that school.

*Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), reinforced that "it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Id.* at 425. *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), likewise held that in the school setting the government must remain one "where the government is neutral, and, while protecting all, it prefers none, and it disparages none." *Id.* at 222. These decisions reflect this Court's consistent judgment that the schoolhouse is a place demanding special care against the appearance that the State itself is sponsoring religious exercise.

*Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), confirms the line Milford has drawn. There the Court sustained a released-time program only because the religious instruction occurred *off* school property, distinguishing *McCollum* on that very ground. The Court emphasized that the released-time program did not involve the use of "public school classrooms" for religious teaching. *Id.* at 315. Milford's Policy tracks this distinction precisely: it permits secular civic and character-building groups to use the building, while reserving religious worship and instruction—the very activity condemned when conducted on school property in *McCollum*—for locations off the school grounds, just as the Good News Club itself did for a year at the Milford Center Community Church.

**III. Niemotko and Cantwell Do Not Compel a Different Result**

*Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), condemned discretionary denial of a permit to use a public park—a traditional public forum—based on official hostility toward the Jehovah's Witnesses' particular beliefs, while other groups, religious and secular alike, were freely granted permits. Milford's Policy differs in both respects: the schoolhouse is not a traditional public forum, and the exclusion of "religious purposes" applies evenhandedly to every faith, with no individualized judgment about whose theology is acceptable.

*Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), likewise involved unbridled administrative discretion to approve or deny religious solicitation on public streets according to the content of the applicant's beliefs. Milford's Policy contains no such discretion; it applies an objective, content-based category—religious worship and instruction—identically to all applicants.

**CONCLUSION**

Milford's Policy excludes a category of activity from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational purposes, consistent with the authority this Court recognized in *Adderley* and *Hague*. And even if the Policy touches speech, it is justified by Milford's substantial interest, grounded in *McCollum*, *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *Zorach*, in avoiding the establishment of religion within its own elementary schoolhouse. The judgment below should be affirmed.
