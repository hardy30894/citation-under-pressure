**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees, and its factual application of that standard here, are both arbitrary and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s judgment and hold that an employer may poll its employees when it possesses “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support”—even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. Alternatively, the Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.

**I. The Board’s Polling Standard Is Arbitrary and Contradicts the Act’s Purposes.**

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987). When an employer has a “doubt, ‘reasonably grounded’ and ‘based on objective considerations,’” that the union has lost majority support, it may lawfully withdraw recognition or poll employees. *Broadway v. Bogan*, 459 U.S. 994, 994 (1982) (White, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari). The Board, however, requires an employer to satisfy the *same* evidentiary standard for polling as for withdrawal—a standard that, as applied, demands proof equivalent to a loss of majority status. That requirement renders polling a “useless” tool, as three courts of appeals have recognized. *See A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). An employer with a sincere doubt about majority status cannot poll to resolve that doubt unless it already possesses enough evidence to justify abandoning the union entirely—a Catch-22 that serves no legitimate statutory purpose.

The Board defends its standard as a means to promote stability in collective-bargaining relationships. But the Act’s primary objective is “to encourage the practice and procedure of collective bargaining” by ensuring that the union actually represents a majority of employees. 29 U.S.C. § 151. An employer who bargains with a union that lacks majority support violates § 8(a)(2) of the Act. *See Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 177 (1967). The Board’s standard forces employers to choose between withdrawing recognition (risking an unfair labor practice if the doubt is deemed insufficient) and continuing to bargain with a union that may no longer command majority support (risking a different violation). Polling, conducted with procedural safeguards, offers a middle ground that the Board has irrationally foreclosed. The Board’s own statements—that RM elections are the “preferred” method—cannot justify a standard that makes polling *less* accessible than an election. *Contra Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). This anomalous result is arbitrary and not entitled to deference. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (Board’s discretion is not unlimited; it must act rationally and consistent with the Act).

Accordingly, this Court should adopt the standard of the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits: an employer may poll employees if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence would not alone justify withdrawal. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 867. Such a standard respects the employer’s need to determine union majority status while protecting employees through required procedural safeguards like secret ballots and notice to the union. *See Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967). The Board’s contrary rule is inconsistent with the Act.

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

Even under the Board’s own standard, the record compels the conclusion that Allentown Mack reasonably doubted the union’s majority status. The Board found that only seven employees made statements repudiating the union, but it improperly discounted credible evidence from other employees and failed to consider the totality of circumstances.

The Board excluded statements made during job interviews because the employer had indicated a non-union workplace. But that rationale assumes insincerity without record support. The employees—Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh—expressed dissatisfaction with the union *in their own words*, not in response to coercion. The Board’s precedent, *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888 (1978), does not justify disregarding such evidence in the successor-employer context, where employees have no reason to feign anti-union sentiment. Similarly, the Board dismissed statements from union steward Ron Mohr that “the union would lose” as “off-the-cuff,” and Kermit Bloch’s report that the night shift did not want the union as hearsay. But these statements were unrebutted and consistent with the overwhelming objective evidence of lost support, including a 19-to-13 secret-ballot vote against the union—a result that itself proves the union lacked majority support.

The Board’s refusal to consider the poll’s outcome as evidence of *prior* doubt is circular: the poll was conducted precisely to test doubt, and its result confirms that the doubt was reasonable. Under the substantial evidence standard, the Board cannot ignore “overwhelming unrefuted evidence” of the union’s diminished support. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board’s contrary finding). Here, Allentown Mack had statements from employees representing nearly one-third of the unit, plus the concedely fair poll. No reasonable factfinder could conclude that the employer lacked a good-faith doubt. *Broadway*, 459 U.S. at 994 (doubt must be “reasonably grounded”).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s standard for polling is arbitrary and contrary to the NLRA. Alternatively, the Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
