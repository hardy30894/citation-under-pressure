# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CATEGORICALLY BAR CAPITAL PUNISHMENT OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS; IT ENTRUSTS THAT JUDGMENT TO INDIVIDUALIZED SENTENCING

This Court has long recognized that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). That standard is measured by objective indicia—principally the judgments of legislatures and juries—not by this Court's independent moral assessment. *Id.* Petitioner asks this Court to declare a categorical constitutional rule where neither legislative enactments nor jury verdicts demonstrate the sort of settled national consensus that has previously justified the Court's intervention. Absent such consensus, the Amendment leaves the question where it has always resided: with the sentencing body that hears the evidence of the individual defendant's capacity and culpability.

This Court's proportionality jurisprudence confirms that the Clause is "progressive" and "not fastened to the obsolete," *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910), but progressivity cuts against, not toward, a rigid categorical rule fixed by this Court rather than by the considered judgments of the several States. *Weems* struck down a punishment grossly disproportionate to a trivial offense; it did not purport to remove from legislatures and juries the individualized weighing of an offender's mental capacity against the gravity of a capital crime such as murder. The proper vehicle for that weighing is precisely what Virginia provided Atkins: a capital sentencing proceeding in which the defense presented a forensic psychologist's testimony on his intellectual functioning, and the jury weighed that evidence against the aggravating circumstances of an armed abduction and murder.

## II. ROBINSON v. CALIFORNIA DOES NOT SUPPORT A CATEGORICAL BAR, AND POWELL v. TEXAS CONFIRMS THAT THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CONSTITUTIONALIZE PARTICULAR THEORIES OF DIMINISHED CAPACITY

Petitioner's argument draws implicit support from *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), which held that a State could not punish the "mere status" of narcotic addiction absent any criminal act. But Atkins was not punished for a status; he was sentenced for the conduct of abduction, robbery, and murder, after a jury considered his intellectual capacity as a mitigating circumstance bearing on his culpability for that conduct. *Robinson*'s status/conduct distinction is simply inapplicable to a capital sentencing scheme that already channels evidence of diminished mental capacity into the individualized weighing process.

More directly on point is *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), in which this Court declined to extend *Robinson* to bar punishment of a chronic alcoholic for public drunkenness, notwithstanding evidence that his affliction rendered his conduct compulsive. The plurality explained that "[t]his Court has never held that anything in the Constitution requires the criminal law to conform to the theories of any particular school of psychiatric thought." *Powell*, 392 U.S. at 536. The same principle governs here. Just as *Powell* left the relationship between mental condition and criminal responsibility to the States' criminal law rather than freezing it into constitutional command, the Eighth Amendment likewise leaves the relationship between mental retardation and capital culpability to state sentencing law—provided, as Virginia's scheme requires, that the sentencing jury may consider and weigh that evidence.

## III. LELAND v. OREGON CONFIRMS THAT DETERMINATIONS OF MENTAL CAPACITY AND CRIMINAL RESPONSIBILITY ARE FOR THE STATES AND THEIR JURIES, NOT FOR A CATEGORICAL FEDERAL RULE

This Court's decision in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the claim that due process compelled a different allocation of the burden or a different substantive test of responsibility. The Court there recognized that the choice of how to weigh evidence of impaired mental capacity against criminal responsibility is a matter of state law, entrusted to the factfinder applying the State's chosen standard. The same deference is due here. Virginia's capital sentencing statute permits the defendant to introduce, and the jury to weigh, evidence of mental retardation as mitigation. That Atkins' jury heard the same psychologist's testimony twice and twice rejected the claim that his intellectual limitations warranted a sentence less than death is not a constitutional defect; it is the system functioning exactly as *Leland* contemplated—an individualized assessment left to the factfinder rather than a categorical rule imposed from above.

## IV. NEITHER THE HISTORICAL PEDIGREE OF THE CLAUSE NOR ITS APPLICATION IN THIS COURT'S EARLY DECISIONS SUPPORTS A PER SE EXEMPTION

This Court's earliest Eighth Amendment decisions confirm that the Clause was directed principally at barbaric methods of execution, not at categorical exemptions turning on an offender's mental capacity. See *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (Clause forbids only punishments that are "cruel in their infliction," not particular classes of otherwise lawful capital sentences); *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135–36 (1878) (upholding execution by shooting, and reasoning that the Clause reaches only "torture" and unnecessary cruelty in the mode of punishment). No decision of this Court predating the modern era recognized, or even suggested, a freestanding categorical bar on executing offenders with diminished intellectual capacity; that question was, and remains, committed to individualized sentencing.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
