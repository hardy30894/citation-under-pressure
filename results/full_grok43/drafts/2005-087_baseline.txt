ARGUMENT

Arizona’s rule confining mental-health evidence to the insanity defense comports with the Fourteenth Amendment. A state may allocate the burden of proving insanity to the defendant, Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), and may “channel” evidence relevant to that affirmative defense without thereby relieving the prosecution of its burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 207-08 (1977). Due process “does not require a State to adopt one procedure over another,” id. at 207, provided the chosen scheme preserves the prosecution’s obligation to establish mens rea.

This Court has repeatedly upheld analogous restrictions. In Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), the Court sustained a statute barring voluntary-intoxication evidence offered to negate intent, explaining that a state may “exclude evidence through the application of evidentiary rules that themselves are not arbitrary,” id. at 53 (plurality opinion). The same principle governs here. Arizona’s limitation is not arbitrary; it prevents the risk that “the insanity defense will be swallowed up” by a mens-rea negation theory that would shift the practical burden of persuasion onto the State. See id. at 56-57.

Petitioner’s proposed right would require every jurisdiction to permit two independent avenues for introducing the same body of expert testimony—an outcome never mandated by this Court’s precedents. The Due Process Clause “does not permit the States to dispense with” proof of mens rea, but it leaves them “free to determine the allocation of burdens” and the “admissibility of evidence” bearing on those burdens. Id. at 58 (plurality opinion). Arizona has done so rationally by requiring defendants who wish to rely on mental disease to carry the affirmative-defense burden rather than to rebut an element.

Because the Arizona rule neither shifts nor lightens the prosecution’s burden of proving intent, it violates no Fourteenth Amendment guarantee. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
