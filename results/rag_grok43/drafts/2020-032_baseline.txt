The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s policies during litigation rendered the First Amendment claims moot, notwithstanding the request for nominal damages. A federal court’s “constitutional obligation” is to decide only “actual controversies,” and once the challenged conduct ceases, “no live controversy remains.” *Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth*, 529 U.S. 217, 227 (2000). Here, the College eliminated the speech zones and the challenged code provisions, “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies,” and there is “no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” The students’ graduation further eliminated any prospect of future enforcement against them.

Nominal damages cannot revive an otherwise moot claim when the complaint alleges no “actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the ‘abstract value’ of the constitutional rights that may have been violated.” *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (citing *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978)). The First Amended Complaint never identified monetary loss, emotional distress, or reputational harm; it rested solely on the “irreparable harm” inherent in the alleged violation itself. Under these circumstances, an award of nominal damages would constitute an advisory opinion on a historical dispute, contrary to Article III.

The campus-speech precedents confirm that once the challenged restrictions are removed, no ongoing case or controversy exists. *Healy v. James*, 408 U.S. 169, 180 (1972); *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981). Because the policies no longer constrain the plaintiffs and no compensable injury was pleaded, the action is moot.
