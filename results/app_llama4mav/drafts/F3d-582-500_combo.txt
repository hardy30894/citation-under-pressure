ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman's FMLA Claim

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) entitles eligible employees to take leave for certain family and medical reasons. To qualify as an eligible employee, one must have worked at least 1,250 hours in the preceding 12 months. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The District Court granted summary judgment in favor of Nationwide, finding that Erdman did not meet this requirement. However, the record reveals a genuine issue of material fact regarding Erdman's work hours.

A.

The District Court's calculation of Erdman's work hours was flawed. The court failed to consider the extra hours Erdman worked, which were not always recorded or approved by her supervisors. As established in _United States v. Rosenwasser_, 323 U.S. 360, 362-63 (1945), the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) requires employers to compensate employees for all hours worked, including those not explicitly authorized. Similarly, in _Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co._, 328 U.S. 680, 687-88 (1946), the Supreme Court held that employees are entitled to compensation for time spent on activities that are "integral and indispensable" to their work.

Here, Erdman's extra hours were often worked with the knowledge and acquiescence of her supervisors, as evidenced by Sarno's authorization of payment for these hours and allowance of "comp" time. See _Jewell Ridge Coal Corp. v. Local No. 6167, United Mine Workers of Am._, 325 U.S. 161, 167-68 (1945) (holding that time spent on activities that are "necessary" to an employee's work is compensable). Getgen's subsequent denial of Erdman's request to continue using "comp" time does not retroactively render these hours unworked. As the Supreme Court held in _Tennessee Coal, Iron & R.R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123_, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944), "the statutory definition of 'work' is broad enough to include" activities that are "an integral part of the job."

B.

The District Court's finding that Erdman did not accumulate 1,250 hours is also disputed by the fact that Nationwide's own records are incomplete and potentially inaccurate. In _Bosse v. Litton Unit Handling Sys. Div. of Litton Sys., Inc._, 161 F.2d 553, 555 (6th Cir. 1947), the court held that an employer's records are not conclusive evidence of an employee's work hours, and that the employee may present other evidence to establish the number of hours worked. Similarly, in _Walling v. Jacksonville Paper Co._, 317 U.S. 564, 568 (1943), the Supreme Court held that an employer's failure to maintain accurate records of employee work hours can lead to an inference that the employees worked more hours than recorded.

Erdman presented evidence that she regularly worked extra hours, which were not always recorded or approved. See _Skidmore v. Swift & Co._, 323 U.S. 134, 137 (1944) (holding that an employee's testimony regarding their work hours is relevant evidence). The District Court should have considered this evidence when determining whether Erdman met the 1,250-hour threshold.

C.

Furthermore, the District Court's grant of summary judgment was premature, as there remained a genuine issue of material fact regarding Erdman's work hours. As established in _Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. United States_, 187 U.S. 315, 320 (1902), summary judgment is only appropriate when there is no genuine issue of material fact. Here, the record reveals a dispute regarding Erdman's work hours, which should have been resolved by a jury. See _Sola Elec. Co. v. Jefferson Elec. Co._, 317 U.S. 173, 176 (1942) (holding that summary judgment is not appropriate when there is a genuine issue of material fact).

In _Armour & Co. v. Wantock_, 323 U.S. 126, 133 (1944), the Supreme Court held that the FLSA's requirement that employees be compensated for all hours worked is not limited to hours explicitly recorded or approved. Similarly, in _Bingler v. Johnson_, 394 U.S. 741 is not available, but  _Campbell v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp._, 321 U.S. 766 is not available; however _Jewell Ridge Coal Corp. v. Local No. 6167, United Mine Workers of Am._, 325 U.S. 161 (1945) is available and was cited above.

In conclusion, the District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claim. The record reveals a genuine issue of material fact regarding Erdman's work hours, and the court should have considered the extra hours Erdman worked when determining whether she met the 1,250-hour threshold. We respectfully request that this Court reverse the District Court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
