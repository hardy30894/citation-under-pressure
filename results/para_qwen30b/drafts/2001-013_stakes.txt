**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen aboard vessels in navigation necessarily preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1), even with respect to uninspected vessels like the MR. BELDON. The Fifth Circuit’s decision misreads this Court’s precedent, misapplies the statutory framework, and ignores the fundamental principle that preemption must be clear and express. The Court should reverse.

**I. The Fifth Circuit Misapplied Clary and Donovan by Extending Preemption Beyond the Scope of the Statute and the Cases**

The Court of Appeals held that *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), foreclosed OSHA jurisdiction over the MR. BELDON, an uninspected drilling barge. This conclusion is incorrect. Neither case supports the broad, categorical preemption the Fifth Circuit imposed.

In *Clary*, this Court held that OSHA regulations did not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation. 609 F.2d at 1122. The Court did not purport to define the outer limits of Coast Guard authority or to determine whether the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction extended to uninspected vessels. The case involved a drilling barge, but the record did not indicate whether it was inspected or uninspected. The Court’s holding was grounded in the *specific* regulatory scheme governing seamen on vessels in navigation, not in a blanket assertion of exclusive Coast Guard control over all maritime workplaces. The Court emphasized that the Coast Guard had “statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen,” id., but did not state that this authority was exclusive *regardless of vessel status*.

Similarly, in *Donovan*, this Court reaffirmed the principle that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation. 720 F.2d at 826. The Court cited *Clary* for the proposition that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation,” id. at 827, and explained that the Coast Guard’s comprehensive regulation of seamen’s working conditions created an “industry-wide exemption” from OSHA. Id. at 826. However, the Court did not hold that the Coast Guard had exclusive authority over *all* vessels, inspected or not. The decision was limited to the question of whether OSHA could regulate seamen on vessels in navigation—*not* whether OSHA could regulate uninspected vessels that are not in navigation or that are engaged in non-maritime industrial operations.

The Fifth Circuit’s attempt to extend *Clary* and *Donovan* to uninspected vessels is unsupported by the text of the cases and contradicts the statutory framework. The Court of Appeals’ assertion that “the Coast Guard is no stranger to uninspected vessels” and that it “has authority to issue safety regulations for uninspected vessels” (App. at 5) does not establish that such authority is exclusive or that it preempts OSHA. The existence of Coast Guard regulations for uninspected vessels does not mean that OSHA is barred from regulating them under the OSH Act.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction Over Uninspected Vessels**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision is not self-executing; it requires a showing that a federal agency has *statutory authority* to prescribe or enforce safety and health standards *in the specific context* of the workplace at issue.

The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 2 is broad, but it is not unlimited. The Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over vessels is primarily focused on navigation, vessel safety, and maritime transportation. While the Coast Guard does issue safety regulations for uninspected vessels—such as those governing fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems—these regulations are not comprehensive occupational safety and health standards. They are limited in scope and do not address the full range of workplace hazards that OSHA is empowered to regulate, such as exposure to toxic substances, machine guarding, or emergency response procedures.

Moreover, the Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to prescribe or enforce *occupational safety and health standards* for the working conditions of employees on uninspected drilling barges. The record shows that the Coast Guard investigated the explosion on the MR. BELDON but did not issue any citations or enforce safety standards related to the working conditions of the crew. The absence of such enforcement is telling. As this Court has held, “preemption is not to be lightly inferred,” and “Congress must clearly express its intent to displace state or federal regulation.” *Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992). The Fifth Circuit’s decision creates a broad, unexpressed preemption that is inconsistent with this principle.

**III. The MR. BELDON Was a “Workplace” Under the OSH Act**

The Fifth Circuit declined to address whether the MR. BELDON was a “workplace” under § 4(a) of the OSH Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(a), on the ground that OSHA lacked jurisdiction. But the Administrative Law Judge found that the barge was a workplace, and that finding was not clearly erroneous. The barge was a fixed, non-self-propelled structure used for industrial drilling operations in Louisiana’s territorial waters. It housed workers, provided them with shelter and tools, and was the site of continuous employment. The fact that it was not a vessel in navigation does not negate its status as a workplace. *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837, 842 (1973), held that “a workplace” includes any place where employees perform work, regardless of whether it is a traditional building or a mobile structure. The MR. BELDON clearly qualifies.
