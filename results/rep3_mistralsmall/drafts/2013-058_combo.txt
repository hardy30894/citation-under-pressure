**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT-FUND EXEMPTION DOES NOT REQUIRE THAT THE FUNDS BE “RESERVED FOR RETIREMENT” IN THE DEBTOR’S HANDS**

The Seventh Circuit erred by engrafting a temporal requirement onto § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) that Congress did not impose. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The exemption applies to “retirement funds . . . in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” the Internal Revenue Code. *Id.* Nothing in the statute conditions the exemption on the funds being “reserved for use after [the debtor’s] retirement.” *Cf. United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. 482, 486 (1868)* (holding that statutes must be construed according to their plain terms unless a contrary intent is clear). The Seventh Circuit’s requirement that the funds be “savings reserved for use after their owners stop working” is a judicial addition unsupported by the statutory text. *See id.*

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly cautioned against judicial rewriting of exemptions. *See, e.g., United States v. La Franchi, 261 U.S. 509, 513 (1923)* (“Exemptions are to be strictly construed in favor of the estate and against the claimant.”). The Seventh Circuit’s novel “reserved for retirement” test conflicts with this principle. *See id.* The exemption turns on the *status* of the fund or account—not its *use* by the debtor at the time of bankruptcy. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12).

II. **THE INHERITED IRA QUALIFIES AS “RETIREMENT FUNDS” BECAUSE IT IS AN IRA EXEMPT FROM TAXATION UNDER THE INTERNAL REVENUE CODE**

The Seventh Circuit held that an inherited IRA is not exempt because it “does not have the economic attributes of a retirement vehicle.” *Clark v. Rameker (In re Clark)*, 714 F.3d 559, 564 (7th Cir. 2013). This reasoning misapprehends the statute. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts funds held in an account that is *itself* exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code—not funds that are *functionally* retirement savings in the debtor’s hands. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12); *see also* 26 U.S.C. § 408(e)(1) (providing that an IRA is exempt from taxation). The Seventh Circuit’s focus on the debtor’s ability to withdraw funds prematurely conflates the *tax status* of the account with its *economic function*. *Cf. United States v. Merriam, 36 U.S. 646, 653 (1837)* (“The intention of the lawmaker is to be gathered from the words used, not from any supposed spirit or policy of the law.”).

This Court has long held that exemptions are determined by the *nature* of the asset, not its *use*. *See, e.g., United States v. Childs, 266 U.S. 304, 309 (1924)* (exemption turns on the *character* of the property, not its *application*). An inherited IRA is an IRA for purposes of § 408 of the Internal Revenue Code, and thus qualifies for the exemption under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); *see also* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12).

III. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S HOMSTEAD EXEMPTION ANALOGY IS INAPPLICABLE**

The Seventh Circuit analogized the inherited IRA exemption to the homestead exemption, reasoning that a debtor cannot claim an inherited home as exempt unless it was the debtor’s domicile for two years before bankruptcy. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565–66. This analogy fails. The homestead exemption in § 522(b)(3)(A) explicitly requires that the property be the debtor’s *domicile*. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). By contrast, § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) contains no such temporal or functional limitation. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *Barker v. Comstock*, 768 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1985), is misplaced because *Barker* interpreted a state-law exemption that expressly required the property to be the debtor’s homestead. *See id.* at 196. The federal exemptions at issue here impose no such requirement. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12).

IV. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD DISENCOURAGE RETIREMENT SAVINGS**

The Seventh Circuit expressed concern that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage parents to save more to make larger bequests. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 566. This policy argument is irrelevant to statutory interpretation. *See* 2 *Sutherland Statutory Construction* § 45.10 (1943) (“The motives of the legislature in passing a statute are not to be considered in construing it.”). Moreover, this Court has rejected the notion that courts should rewrite statutes to achieve perceived policy goals. *See, e.g., United States v. Shreveport Ry. & Nav. Co., 267 U.S. 303, 307 (1925)* (“Courts are not at liberty to add to or take from the terms of a statute to suit the equities of a particular case.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision misreads the Bankruptcy Code by imposing a temporal and functional requirement not found in the statute. The inherited IRA qualifies for exemption under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is an IRA exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code. The judgment below should be affirmed.
