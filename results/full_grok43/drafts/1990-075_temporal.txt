The Controlled Substances Act's delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General satisfies the constitutional requirement that Congress lay down "an intelligible principle" to which the delegatee "is directed to conform." J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The statute supplies such a principle by enumerating eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, requiring a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services that binds the Attorney General when negative, and limiting temporary scheduling to substances posing an imminent hazard to public safety after consideration of three enumerated criteria. These constraints are at least as definite as those sustained in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-693 (1892), and Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904).

The same intelligible principle governs the Attorney General's exercise of temporary authority. Because the statute channels that authority within "well-known and generally acceptable standards," the delegation is valid. See Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364, 386 (1907); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-517 (1911).

The Act also permits the Attorney General to subdelegate the temporary scheduling power to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration. Congress has long authorized the head of an executive department to delegate duties "to such officers or employees as he may designate." United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1926). The Attorney General's regulation effecting that subdelegation therefore rests on statutory authority and does not offend the separation of powers.

The temporary scheduling provision is accordingly constitutional, and the convictions should be affirmed.
