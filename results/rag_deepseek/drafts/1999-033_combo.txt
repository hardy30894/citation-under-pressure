**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Provide a Cause of Action for a “Class of One” in the Absence of Membership in a Protected Class or Group.**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” By its text and this Court’s unbroken precedent, that command is directed at governmental classifications that arbitrarily burden discrete groups or target individuals based on invidious discrimination. Petitioner Village of Willowbrook respectfully submits that the court below erred in recognizing a cause of action where respondent Grace Olech alleged only that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from other property owners, without any claim that she was a member of a protected class or that the Village’s action was motivated by discriminatory animus rooted in group-based prejudice.

**A. The Equal Protection Clause Has Historically Required That a Plaintiff Allege Membership in a Class or Demonstrate Invidious, Intentional Discrimination.**

From the earliest days of this Court’s interpretation of the Equal Protection Clause, the focus has been on laws or governmental actions that create arbitrary classifications. The Clause does not guarantee equal treatment in every “son of a transaction” but instead guards against systematic, intentional discrimination. As this Court has long held, the Equal Protection Clause is not a “catch-all” remedy for every perceived slight by a government actor. *See, e.g., City of Joplin v. Southwest Missouri Light Co.*, 191 U.S. 150, 158 (1903) (recognizing that the Clause prohibits “arbitrary” governmental action, but still requiring a showing of a “discriminatory purpose” tied to a classification).

Indeed, in *Norwood v. Baker*, 172 U.S. 269, 291-292 (1898), this Court emphasized that the Equal Protection Clause is concerned with “the protection of persons and property from arbitrary and discriminatory legislation.” The Clause, however, does not “permit a court to revise the action of a municipal corporation in the exercise of its judgment and discretion,” absent a showing that the action was “so arbitrary as to be a denial of equal protection.” *Id.* at 293. That showing has always been tied to a classification or group-based animus.

**B. A “Class of One” Theory Is Inconsistent with the Core Purpose of the Equal Protection Clause.**

This Court has repeatedly held that a plaintiff alleging an equal protection violation must show that he or she has been intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated *and* that the differential treatment was based on an “impermissible consideration,” such as race, religion, or other suspect or quasi-suspect classifications. *See, e.g., Skaneateles Water Works Co. v. Skaneateles*, 184 U.S. 354, 360 (1902) (holding that a municipal decision that “merely diminished the value” of a water company’s property, without any discriminatory purpose against a “class,” did not violate the Equal Protection Clause). This Court cautioned that the Equal Protection Clause was not intended to “furnish a remedy for every grievance which a citizen may conceive he has suffered at the hands of a municipal government.” *Id.*

The “class of one” theory advanced by respondent—that any inconsistent application of a government regulation, if motivated by “spite,” violates the Equal Protection Clause—would open the door to constitutionalizing every routine administrative decision. This Court has soundly rejected such a broad reading of the Clause. *See, e.g., Consumers' Co. v. Hatch*, 224 U.S. 148, 156 (1912) (refusing to find an equal protection violation in a water utility’s differential treatment of customers, where “no classification was made” and no “invidious discrimination” was shown).

**C. The Requirement of a Classification or Group-Based Animus Is Well-Settled in This Court’s Precedents.**

This Court has consistently required that an equal protection claim be based on a demonstration that the plaintiff was the victim of intentional discrimination *because of his or her membership in a particular class*. *See, e.g., Boise Artesian Hot & Cold Water Co. v. Boise City*, 230 U.S. 84, 91 (1913) (rejecting an equal protection claim where the plaintiff did not allege that it was “arbitrarily singled out from others” by reason of membership in a “class”).

Similarly, in *Rogers Park Water Co. v. Fergus*, 180 U.S. 624, 631 (1901), this Court explained that the Equal Protection Clause protects against “unreasonable classification,” not against every instance of alleged unfairness by a government actor. The Court reasoned that a plaintiff must demonstrate that “the legislature has by its action imposed a burden upon a *class* of persons to which the complainant belongs, which burden is not imposed upon others similarly situated.” *Id.* (emphasis added). Where no such class is alleged, the claim fails.

**D. The Lower Court’s “Spite” Standard Is an Impermissible Expansion of the Equal Protection Clause.**

The Seventh Circuit’s holding that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was “motivated solely by a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him” finds no support in this Court’s precedents. This Court has never recognized a constitutional cause of action based on “spite” alone. To the contrary, in *Hetrick v. Village of Lindsey*, 265 U.S. 384, 387 (1924), this Court held that even where a municipality’s action appeared “harsh or unjust,” such action did not violate the Equal Protection Clause if it was not based on an “unreasonable classification.” The Court stated that “mere inequality of result” is not enough; “the inequality must be of a kind which the Constitution forbids.” *Id.* at 388.

Moreover, in *Superior Water, Light & Power Co. v. City of Superior*, 263 U.S. 125, 133-134 (1923), this Court flatly rejected the notion that a public entity’s “arbitrary” decision to treat one party differently from another, without more, violates the Equal Protection Clause. The Court observed that “the mere fact that the city exercised its discretion in a manner that was disadvantageous to the plaintiff” does not “give rise to a constitutional claim.” *Id.* at 134.

**II. Respondent Has Failed to Allege Any Victimization Based on Membership in a Protected Class or Any Invidious Discriminatory Purpose.**

Respondent’s complaint alleges only that the Village demanded a 33-foot easement from her while requiring only a 15-foot easement from others. She does not claim that she is a member of any racial, ethnic, or other suspect class. She does not claim that the Village acted with any discriminatory animus based on such a classification. Her theory—that the Village acted out of “spite”—cannot transform a routine dispute about property rights into a constitutional claim. As this Court has long made clear, the Equal Protection Clause “does not take away from the States the power to classify, if the classification be reasonable.” *Ohio Public Service Co. v. Ohio ex rel. Fritz*, 274 U.S. 12, 24 (1927). The Village’s decision to demand a larger easement from respondent, even if erroneous or ill-motivated, is precisely the kind of discretionary decision that the Equal Protection Clause leaves to the States.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Seventh Circuit and hold that a plaintiff alleging a “class of one” equal protection claim must, at a minimum, allege membership in a protected class or intentional discrimination based on an invidious classification. Because respondent alleged neither, her claim fails to state a cause of action under the Equal Protection Clause.
