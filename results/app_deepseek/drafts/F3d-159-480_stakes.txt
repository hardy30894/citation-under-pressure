## ARGUMENT

### A. Congress Lacked Constitutional Authority to Enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951 as Applied to Mr. Pearson’s Conduct

The district court erred in denying Mr. Pearson’s motion to dismiss the indictment because the Hobbs Act, as applied to this purely local robbery, exceeds Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause. The government’s evidence—that Mr. Goodcents is part of a national chain, purchases goods from out-of-state, and that the stolen money *would have been* used to buy out-of-state products—establishes only an attenuated and speculative connection to interstate commerce. That is insufficient to satisfy the “substantial effects” test required by *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558–59 (1995).

Under *Lopez*, Congress may regulate only three categories: (1) the channels of interstate commerce; (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce; and (3) activities having a substantial relation to interstate commerce. *Id.* at 558–59. The robbery of a local sub shop is not an economic activity that substantially affects interstate commerce. The government’s theory—that any robbery of a business that purchases out-of-state goods necessarily affects commerce—would permit federal jurisdiction over virtually every robbery of a commercial establishment, thereby transforming the Hobbs Act into a general police power. *Lopez* rejected such reasoning, holding that “the Constitution creates a Federal Government of enumerated powers.” *Id.* at 552.

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bolton*, 68 F.3d 396 (10th Cir. 1995), is distinguishable. In *Bolton*, the evidence showed that the robbed convenience store continuously replenished its inventory from out-of-state suppliers and that the stolen cash directly depleted assets used for those ongoing interstate purchases. *Id.* at 399. Here, the government presented no evidence that the stolen $2,500 was actually used to purchase out-of-state goods or that the robbery disrupted any specific interstate transaction. The owner’s testimony that the money “would have been” used for out-of-state purchases is speculative and insufficient to establish a substantial effect. *See United States v. Robinson*, 119 F.3d 1205, 1210 (5th Cir. 1997) (holding that evidence that a robbed business purchased out-of-state goods, without more, fails to show a substantial effect on interstate commerce under *Lopez*).

Moreover, the Hobbs Act’s jurisdictional element—requiring that the robbery “in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce,” 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a)—does not automatically satisfy the Commerce Clause. The Supreme Court has instructed that jurisdictional elements must be interpreted to require a substantial effect when the regulated activity is non-economic. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561. The robbery here was a violent, intrastate crime with no demonstrated impact on the flow of goods across state lines. The killing of Ms. Montgomery, while tragic, is a state-law offense. Allowing federal prosecution under these circumstances would “obliterate the distinction between what is national and what is local.” *Id.* at 557 (quoting *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 554 (1935)).

Because the government failed to prove that Mr. Pearson’s conduct substantially affected interstate commerce, the Hobbs Act as applied here exceeds Congress’s constitutional authority. The convictions must be reversed.
