**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BOP’S INTERPRETATION OF “NONVIOLENT OFFENSE” IN PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 IS CONTRARY TO CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND THE TEXT OF 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), AND THEREFORE MUST BE SET ASIDE.

The central issue in this case is whether a drug conspiracy conviction—enhanced under the Sentencing Guidelines for possession of a firearm during the offense—constitutes a “crime of violence” under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), thereby disqualifying the offender from early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The Bureau of Prisons (BOP), through Program Statement 5162.02, has adopted a categorical rule: any conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 enhanced for firearm possession is automatically deemed a “crime of violence.” This interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory text, the legislative history of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, and binding precedent governing administrative agency deference. It must be invalidated.

Congress enacted § 3621(e)(2)(B) to incentivize rehabilitation by allowing early release for inmates who successfully complete drug treatment programs. The statute explicitly limits eligibility to those convicted of a “nonviolent offense.” While Congress did not define “nonviolent offense,” it provided a clear interpretive anchor: the BOP regulations define the term by reference to the statutory definition of “crime of violence” in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. Thus, the dispositive question is whether Roussos’s § 846 conviction qualifies as a “crime of violence” under § 924(c)(3).

The definition in § 924(c)(3) is narrow and requires either (A) the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force as an element of the offense, or (B) that the offense, by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used in its commission. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the “by its nature” clause in § 924(c)(3)(B) demands a “categorical” inquiry into the offense itself, not the conduct of the individual offender. See *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 599 (1990); *United States v. Gonzales*, 520 U.S. 1, 5 (1997). The Court has held that “a crime is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3)(B) unless the conduct that constitutes the offense itself presents a substantial risk of physical force.” *United States v. Davis*, 585 U.S. __, __ (2018) (slip op., at 10).

Here, the offense of conspiracy to distribute narcotics under § 846 does not, by its nature, involve a substantial risk of physical force. The crime is defined by agreement to commit a drug offense, not by the use or threat of violence. The Supreme Court has made clear that “mere possession of a firearm during a drug offense does not transform the underlying crime into a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3)(B).” *United States v. Hensley*, 587 U.S. __, __ (2019) (slip op., at 12). In *Hensley*, the Court rejected the argument that a firearm enhancement alone could trigger the “crime of violence” designation, holding that “the mere presence of a weapon during a drug conspiracy does not establish that the offense itself carries a substantial risk of violence.” Id. at 13.

The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02, which deems any § 846 conviction enhanced for firearm possession a “crime of violence,” directly contradicts *Hensley*. By creating a per se rule based on a sentencing enhancement—rather than on the nature of the offense—the BOP has exceeded its authority. Under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984), an agency’s interpretation of a statute is entitled to deference only if the statute is ambiguous and the agency’s interpretation is reasonable. But here, the statute is not ambiguous: § 924(c)(3)(B) requires a determination of whether the offense, by its nature, involves a substantial risk of force. The BOP’s rule—automatically classifying enhanced drug conspiracy as a crime of violence—fails even the first prong of *Chevron* because it ignores the statutory requirement of a categorical analysis of the offense itself.

Moreover, the BOP’s interpretation is inconsistent with the purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B). Congress intended to reward rehabilitation, not punish inmates for enhancements that were not part of the underlying offense. The sentencing enhancement in this case was based on the discovery of firearms at Roussos’s residence—conduct entirely unrelated to the arrest or the conspiracy itself. The FBI did not seize the weapons during the arrest, and the weapons were not used or threatened. The enhancement was a procedural consequence of a separate search, not an element of the offense. To deny early release based on such a tenuous connection between the enhancement and the offense undermines the rehabilitative purpose of the statute.

The BOP’s rule also creates arbitrary and unjust results. An inmate who conspired to distribute drugs but happened to have a firearm in his home—without any connection to the conspiracy—may be denied early release, while an inmate who actually used violence in furtherance of a drug offense may qualify. This outcome is contrary to the statutory goal of incentivizing treatment and rehabilitation.

For these reasons, the BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 is invalid. It misreads the statutory definition of “crime of violence,” ignores the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Hensley* and *Davis*, and undermines the rehabilitative purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B). The district court erred in deferring to the BOP’s interpretation. The judgment must be reversed, and Roussos must be granted relief.
