ARGUMENT

I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR STATE COURT JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE AGE DISCRIMINATION IN EMPLOYMENT ACT (ADEA)

The ADEA prohibits employers from discriminating against employees on the basis of age, but it exempts certain individuals, including "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question presented is whether Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within this exemption.

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" and thus are excluded from the ADEA's protection. As the First Circuit observed in EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), "policymaking" is a part of the function of judging, as judges create law by filling the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. Missouri's appointed judges exercise significant policymaking responsibility, particularly in their supervisory roles. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar, and for resolving disputes within the judicial hierarchy. See Mo. Const. art. V, § 4; In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc).

The legislative history of the ADEA's exemptions supports the conclusion that appointed state court judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." Although the conference report accompanying the 1972 amendments to the ADEA states that the exemption should be construed narrowly, it also indicates that the exemption applies to "persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The state judiciary is a critical "agency" of state government, and appointed judges are policymakers within the meaning of the ADEA.

II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR STATE COURT JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT

The Equal Protection Clause requires that a state's actions be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312-314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is rationally related to several legitimate government interests, including the need to ensure that judges are physically and mentally capable of performing their duties, to reduce the risk of delay and inefficiency in the administration of justice, and to promote a judiciary that is representative of the population it serves. See Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988).

The rational basis test is a deferential standard of review, and the state's mandatory retirement requirement easily satisfies it. The state's interest in ensuring a capable and representative judiciary is legitimate, and the mandatory retirement requirement is a rational means of achieving that interest.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed. Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.
