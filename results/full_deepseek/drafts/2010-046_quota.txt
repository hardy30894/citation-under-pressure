## ARGUMENT

The three-judge court’s remedial order fully complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act’s rigorous requirements. The PLRA does not bar federal courts from ordering population reductions when, as here, overcrowding is the demonstrable cause of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and no narrower remedy will suffice.

### I. The Three-Judge Court Made the Requisite Findings Under the PLRA

Section 3626(a)(3)(E) of the PLRA requires that before ordering “prisoner release relief,” a three-judge court must find by clear and convincing evidence that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” The three-judge court did precisely that. After a lengthy trial, the court concluded that California’s prisons operate at nearly 200% of design capacity, resulting in systemic failures in medical and mental health care. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088, 1092-93 (9th Cir. 2010) (affirming findings). The court found that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations and that “no other relief” would be adequate. *Id.* at 1095. These findings satisfy the PLRA’s clear-and-convincing evidence standard.

The PLRA’s requirement ensures that population reductions are reserved for cases where overcrowding itself drives constitutional violations. This is such a case. Inadequate staffing, lack of infirmary beds, and failure to provide timely care all stem from a system bursting at the seams. The PLRA does not require courts to ignore reality.

### II. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and the Least Intrusive Means

Under § 3626(a)(1)(A), prospective relief must be “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” The three-judge court’s population cap of 137.5% of design capacity is a carefully calibrated remedy. The court gave the State two years to achieve the target, rejecting the plaintiffs’ request for a lower cap. *See* 603 F.3d at 1096. This is not a general release order; it is a targeted remedy designed to bring the system within constitutional bounds.

This Court has long recognized that “[t]he scope of a federal court’s equitable power to remedy constitutional wrongs is broad.” *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971). The PLRA does not eviscerate that power. *See Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 348 (2000) (acknowledging that PLRA “does not purport to limit the court’s authority to remedy ongoing violations”). The population cap is the least intrusive means because less restrictive alternatives—such as ordering more medical staff or new facilities—would either be ineffective or take years to implement while inmates continue to suffer. The district court found that California had already tried and failed to remedy conditions through consent decrees and receivership. *See* 603 F.3d at 1093.

### III. The Order Satisfies the PLRA’s Narrow Tailoring Requirement

The PLRA requires that relief “extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation.” § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court tailored the cap to address the specific constitutional deficits. It ordered that the population be reduced to a level that would allow provision of constitutionally adequate medical and mental health care. *See* 603 F.3d at 1096. This is consistent with *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343, 357 (1996), which held that an injunction must “be limited to the inadequacy that produced the injury in fact.” Here, the inadequacy is overcrowding that prevents proper healthcare. The remedy is directly tied to that inadequacy.

Moreover, the PLRA does not forbid population caps per se. Many courts have approved such remedies when supported by findings like those here. *E.g., Goff v. Harper*, 60 F.3d 518, 522-23 (8th Cir. 1995) (upholding population cap to remedy unconstitutional conditions); *Ruiz v. Estelle*, 679 F.2d 1115, 1140-41 (5th Cir. 1982) (same). The PLRA codifies the requirement that such relief be narrowly drawn, but it does not bar it.

### IV. The Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Termination Provisions

California argues that the order fails under § 3626(b)’s requirement that prospective relief be terminated after two years unless the court makes findings that the violation continues and the relief remains necessary. But the three-judge court’s order includes an ongoing compliance mechanism. The court specifically provided that the population cap would remain in effect until the State demonstrates that it has implemented a constitutional system. *See* 603 F.3d at 1097. This is not a perpetual order; it is a conditional one. The PLRA’s termination provisions are satisfied because the court’s findings were based on current evidence of ongoing violations. *See Benjamin v. Jacobson*, 172 F.3d 144, 162 (2d Cir. 1999) (en banc).

Furthermore, this Court has made clear that “the PLRA must be interpreted in light of the ‘strong equitable tradition’ of federal courts.” *Porter v. Nussle*, 534 U.S. 516, 524 (2002). Courts retain the power to enforce constitutional rights; the PLRA merely sets standards that the three-judge court meticulously satisfied.

### V. The Order Is Consistent with the Eighth Amendment

Finally, the remedy is necessary to vindicate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has held that “deliberate indifference to serious medical needs of prisoners” violates the Eighth Amendment. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976). Similarly, the Court has recognized that a prison condition may be unconstitutional if it “inflict[s] unnecessary or wanton pain.” *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 346 (1981). Here, the evidence showed that overcrowding caused thousands of inmates to go without adequate medical and mental health care, leading to needless suffering and deaths. *See Helling v. McKinney*, 509 U.S. 25, 33 (1993) (prison officials may not be deliberately indifferent to substantial risk of serious harm). The population cap is a measured response to that crisis.

### Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the three-judge court should be affirmed.
