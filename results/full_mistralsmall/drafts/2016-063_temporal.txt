**ARGUMENT**

I.  THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN HOLDING THAT A JURISDICTIONAL DISMISSAL OF A MIXED CASE MUST BE REVIEWED IN THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT.

A.  The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 (“CSRA”) did not repeal the longstanding rule that district courts have jurisdiction over employment-discrimination claims arising under federal statutes.  In McClanahan v. United States, 292 F.2d 605, 608 (5th Cir. 1961), this Court held that a federal employee’s claim of discrimination in violation of 42 U.S.C. § 1981 must be heard in district court, notwithstanding the existence of an administrative remedy.  That holding rested on the principle that “where a right is created by statute and a remedy for its violation is provided by statute, the remedy provided is exclusive only when the statute in clear terms so states.” Id.  The CSRA contains no such clear statement.  Thus, under McClanahan, Perry’s discrimination claim must be heard in district court.

B.  The jurisdictional scheme of the CSRA does not divest district courts of jurisdiction over mixed cases dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.  In Leedom v. Kyne, 358 U.S. 184, 188 (1958), the Supreme Court held that a district court has jurisdiction to review an administrative determination that an election was not “affected by any error or mistake” under the National Labor Relations Act, notwithstanding the Act’s provision for review in the courts of appeals.  The Court reasoned that the district court’s jurisdiction was necessary to ensure that employees’ statutory rights were not impaired by an erroneous administrative decision. Id.  Similarly, Perry’s discrimination claim implicates statutory rights that would be impaired if the Board’s jurisdictional dismissal were unreviewable in district court.  Thus, under Leedom, district courts retain jurisdiction over mixed cases dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

C.  The Federal Circuit is not the exclusive forum for reviewing Board decisions.  In United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19, 26 (1947), the Supreme Court held that the federal courts of appeals do not have exclusive jurisdiction over claims arising under federal statutes, absent a clear statutory directive.  The CSRA contains no such directive.  Therefore, the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over Board decisions is not exclusive, and district courts may review mixed cases dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

II.  THE BOARD’S DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S CLAIM FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION WAS NOT ENTITLED TO DEFERRAL TO THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT.

A.  The Board’s jurisdictional determination is not entitled to deference under the principles of administrative law established in Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951).  In that case, the Supreme Court held that courts must independently review administrative determinations of law, giving deference only to findings of fact supported by substantial evidence. Id.  The Board’s conclusion that Perry’s retirement was voluntary is a legal determination subject to plenary review, not a finding of fact.  Thus, the Federal Circuit erred in deferring to the Board’s jurisdictional dismissal.

B.  The Board’s jurisdictional dismissal conflicts with the remedial purposes of the anti-discrimination statutes.  In Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R., 323 U.S. 192, 203 (1944), the Supreme Court held that courts must construe anti-discrimination statutes broadly to effectuate their remedial purposes.  The Board’s dismissal of Perry’s discrimination claim frustrates that purpose by insulating the agency’s alleged discrimination from judicial review.  Thus, the dismissal must be reviewed de novo in district court to ensure that Perry’s statutory rights are vindicated.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the decision below and hold that district courts have jurisdiction to review Board decisions dismissing mixed cases for lack of jurisdiction.
