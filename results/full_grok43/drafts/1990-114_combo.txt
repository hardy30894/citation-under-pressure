I.

Missouri's constitutional requirement that state judges retire at age seventy does not conflict with the Age Discrimination in Employment Act. The statute's definition of "employee" excludes appointees on the policymaking level. State judges exercise the core powers of a coordinate branch of government. Principles of federalism require a clear statement before Congress may regulate the tenure of state judicial officers. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819) (federal power "limited to certain enumerated objects"); Collector v. Day, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 113, 124 (1871) (federal taxation may not "impair the ability of the states" to maintain their governments); Coyle v. Smith, 221 U.S. 559, 565 (1911) (the Constitution "presupposes the continued existence of the states" as independent sovereigns). Absent such a statement, the Act leaves untouched the states' traditional authority over their judiciaries. Texas v. White, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 700, 725 (1869).

II.

The equal-protection challenge likewise fails. Age classifications in state law are measured by the rational-basis standard. Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885) (Fourteenth Amendment "only inhibits discriminating and partial legislation"); Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886) (law must operate alike on all persons "under like circumstances"); Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co., 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911) (classification valid if "any state of facts reasonably can be conceived" to justify it). Missouri's rule rests on the judgment that judicial service imposes unique demands that age may impair. Such a line is permissible even if drawn with imperfect precision. Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc., 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) ("a law need not be logically consistent in every respect"); McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961) (legislature may proceed "one step at a time"); Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs, 330 U.S. 552, 563 (1947) (state may select officers by criteria that "eliminate the risk of divided loyalty"); Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948) (state may conclude that certain occupations require "peculiar" qualifications). The provision therefore satisfies the Fourteenth Amendment.

III.

Because the ADEA does not reach these judges and because the retirement rule rests on a rational basis, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
