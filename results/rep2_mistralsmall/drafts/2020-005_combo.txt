**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Permits Specific Jurisdiction Where a Defendant’s Forum Activities Are Sufficiently Related to the Claims, Even If the Claims Did Not Arise from Those Activities.**

The Due Process Clause does not require that a defendant’s forum contacts *cause* the plaintiff’s claims; it requires only that the claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945) (holding that jurisdiction is proper where the suit “aris[es] out of or [is] connected with” the defendant’s activities in the forum). This Court has long recognized that the “minimum contacts” inquiry focuses on the defendant’s purposeful availment of the forum’s benefits, not the causal nexus between the contacts and the injury. *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (finding jurisdiction where the insurer “deliberately” reached into California to solicit business and mailed a policy there).

Ford’s extensive marketing and distribution of its vehicles and parts in Montana—including advertising, parts sales, and service support—establish sufficient contacts to support jurisdiction. *Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 447 (1952) (finding jurisdiction where a corporation’s “continuous corporate operations” in the forum were “so substantial and of such a nature as to justify suit against it on causes of action arising from dealings entirely distinct from those activities”). Ford’s systematic efforts to cultivate the Montana market render it amenable to suit there, regardless of whether the specific vehicle at issue was sold in Montana. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958) (noting that jurisdiction exists where a defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum”).

**II. Ford’s Nationwide Distribution System Supports Jurisdiction in Montana.**

Ford’s national distribution network—including sales, service, and parts distribution—demonstrates its deliberate exploitation of the Montana market. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (plurality opinion) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant “deliberately” served the forum market). The fact that the vehicle at issue was first sold outside Montana does not defeat jurisdiction, as Ford’s ongoing business relationships in Montana (e.g., parts sales, advertising, and service networks) provide a sufficient basis for specific jurisdiction. *Kulko v. Superior Court*, 436 U.S. 84, 96 (1978) (holding that jurisdiction may exist where a defendant “purposefully directs” activities toward the forum, even if the injury occurs elsewhere).

**III. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the “Relate To” Standard.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly concluded that Ford’s forum activities “relate to” the claims, as the defective design and failure-to-warn claims implicate Ford’s nationwide safety practices, which Ford purposefully directed toward Montana consumers. *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 781 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant’s “regular circulation” of magazines in the forum gave rise to the claims). Ford’s extensive advertising and parts distribution in Montana demonstrate its intent to serve that market, satisfying the due process standard. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (holding that jurisdiction exists where the defendant “purposefully avails itself of the forum’s laws”).

**IV. Ford’s Reliance on *Goodyear* and *Daimler* Is Misplaced.**

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle was not designed or manufactured in Montana misreads *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915 (2011), and *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014). Those cases addressed *general* jurisdiction, not *specific* jurisdiction. *Goodyear*, 564 U.S. at 924 (distinguishing between general and specific jurisdiction). Here, the claims arise from Ford’s Montana activities, not its global operations. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984) (noting that specific jurisdiction requires a connection between the forum contacts and the claims).

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly upheld jurisdiction over Ford, as Ford’s purposeful conduct in Montana—including advertising, parts sales, and service networks—supports specific jurisdiction over claims that “relate to” those activities. The judgment should be affirmed.
