The district court plainly erred by admitting Agent Jones’s testimony. Plain error review applies because no contemporaneous objection was lodged. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936).

Expert opinion is admissible only when the subject matter lies beyond the common knowledge of lay jurors and the testimony rests on a scientifically recognized basis. Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). Agent Jones’s assertions—that Hill’s statements “did not make sense,” displayed “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element,” and were deceptive because they invoked faith—directly addressed the credibility of a witness. Such opinions invade the exclusive province of the jury. United States v. Spaulding, 293 U.S. 498, 506 (1935) (“The question of credibility is for the jury, not for the expert.”).

Because the testimony supplied the jury with an expert’s conclusion on the very issue it was empaneled to decide, its admission constituted error that affected Hill’s substantial rights. The government’s case turned on whether the jury credited Hill’s account; the improper expert endorsement therefore seriously affected the fairness of the proceeding. Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160.

Reversal is required.
