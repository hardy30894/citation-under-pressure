# ARGUMENT

Section 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act delegates authority to the Attorney General within permissible constitutional limits, and the Attorney General's redelegation of that authority to the Drug Enforcement Administration was expressly authorized by Congress. Petitioners' convictions should therefore be affirmed.

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard Governing Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but that provision has never been read to bar Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering a complex regulatory scheme. This Court fixed the controlling test more than sixty years ago: legislative action is "not a forbidden delegation of legislative power" so long as Congress "lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Measured against that standard, § 811(h) is unremarkable.

**A. Congress supplied specific, judicially enforceable standards.** Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It identified the factors he must weigh before acting — the substance's history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk it poses to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary to avoid an "imminent hazard to the public safety," § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). This is precisely the sort of governing standard whose *absence* doomed the statutes petitioners invoke. In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the Court struck down a delegation because the statute failed "to govern the President's course" with any criterion whatsoever. In *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), the Court likewise invalidated a delegation resting on no ascertainable standard at all. Those decisions turned on the total absence of guiding principles — not on the fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action. Section 811(h) supplies exactly what those statutes lacked.

**B. This Court has repeatedly sustained comparably broad — or broader — delegations where administrative flexibility was necessary.** Congress may rely on executive discretion where the pace of change in the regulated subject outstrips the capacity of the ordinary legislative process. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix wartime commodity prices under a general "fair and equitable" standard, recognizing the practical necessity of administrative speed in a fast-moving field. The same logic controls here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new "designer drugs" faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond, which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment proceedings. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), the Court approved the open-ended "public interest, convenience, or necessity" standard governing broadcast licensing, and in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), it approved the similarly general "fair and equitable" standard governing utility reorganizations. Section 811(h)'s detailed, multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more confining than either of those.

**C. The absence of exhaustive technical detail is not fatal where the regulated field itself supplies workable standards.** In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a conservatorship statute containing minimal express standards because the regulated field possessed "well-known and generally acceptable standards" on which the delegatee could draw — a description equally apt for the pharmacological and enforcement criteria Congress specified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture "excessive profits," a standard with no fixed numerical content, because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to supply detail Congress could not practically anticipate. The same institutional necessity justifies Congress's choice here: identifying dangerous new chemical compounds before they proliferate is a technical, fast-moving task for which the Attorney General, not Congress itself, is best equipped.

**D. Judicial review of the constitutionality of the delegation remains available.** This Court has treated the availability of review as relevant to the propriety of a delegation. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952). Section 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling decision, but it does not — and could not — foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself. That structural safeguard, which protects "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution," *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), remains fully intact and was available to, and exercised by, petitioners below.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a more direct reason: Congress expressly authorized the very redelegation at issue. Section 871(a) of the Act empowers the Attorney General to "delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization permitting the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue raises no separation-of-powers concern. *Cf. United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (sustaining delegated executive authority to establish enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted in § 871(a), and the redelegation was therefore proper.

For these reasons, the delegation to the Attorney General under § 811(h), and his subsequent redelegation to the DEA, were constitutional and statutorily authorized. Petitioners' convictions should be affirmed.
