# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR STATE JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA

### A. The Plain State Sovereignty Principle Requires a Clear Statement Before Federal Law Displaces a State's Authority to Set Qualifications for Its Own Judges

This Court has long recognized that "the States retain substantial sovereign powers under our constitutional scheme, powers with which Congress does not readily interfere." *See Will v. Michigan Department of State Police*, 491 U.S. 58, 65 (1989). Among the most fundamental of those retained powers is the authority to determine the qualifications of those who govern the State, particularly the judges who exercise its sovereign judicial power. As Justice Story observed in *Sturges v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 122, 193 (1819), the structure of federalism presumes that the States retain control over matters "of purely domestic concern."

The decision of how to structure a state judiciary—including how long judges may serve—goes to the heart of a State's ability to govern itself. This Court has therefore required that when Congress seeks to intrude upon such a core attribute of state sovereignty, it must make its intention "unmistakably clear in the language of the statute." *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985). This clear-statement rule finds further support in *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), where this Court held that "[i]n traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision."

The Eighth Circuit correctly applied this principle below, holding that because "the tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance to a state, and one that traditionally has been left to each state to regulate," the ADEA should not be read to conflict with Missouri's constitutional retirement provision "unless we find that Congress clearly has expressed its intention to bring state judges into the ambit of the federal statute." *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 599–600 (8th Cir. 1990). No such clear statement exists here.

### B. Missouri's Appointed Judges Are "Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level" Under 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The ADEA expressly excludes from its definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). This exclusion squarely covers Missouri's judges.

Judging is not merely mechanical application of settled rules. As the Eighth Circuit recognized, "[t]he reality of judging is that it is not always—and perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law to specific fact patterns as they arise." *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604. The First Circuit reached the identical conclusion in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), explaining that "'[p]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents."

This conclusion is reinforced by the significant administrative and supervisory responsibilities Missouri judges exercise. The Chief Justice, Chief Judges, and Presiding Judges possess general supervisory powers, and the Missouri Supreme Court promulgates binding rules governing the conduct of the entire judiciary and bar. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 605 (citing *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc)).

Nor should the placement of the "policymaking" exception among categories primarily addressing executive and legislative appointees narrow its scope. As the Eighth Circuit observed, reading the exception so narrowly would mean "none of the four categories of exemptions from the coverage of the ADEA would apply to employees in the judicial branch in those states where judges are appointed"—a result Congress could not have intended given its complete exemption of *elected* judges. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement provisions for public employees are subject only to rational basis review. This Court settled that question in *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (1976) (per curiam), upholding a mandatory retirement age for state police officers and holding that "[t]his Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental." *Id.* at 313. The same deferential standard governs here.

Multiple courts have identified rational bases for judicial retirement provisions like Missouri's. The Seventh Circuit in *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623 (7th Cir. 1979), held it rational to treat "judges differently from other officials on the ground that the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode," and further noted that "[m]andatory retirement . . . reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges." *Id.* at 627, 629. The Third Circuit likewise recognized in *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565 (3d Cir. 1980), that such a provision "eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges." *Id.* at 572.

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court's reasoning in *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver General*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141 (1988), is equally persuasive: "[a] line drawn at age seventy eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence pursuant to an evaluation process." *Id.* at 146. That court further observed the provision "makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary." *Id.*

Petitioners may disagree with these policy judgments, but as the Eighth Circuit correctly concluded, "[o]ne might disagree with some or all of these rationales on either empirical or philosophical grounds and yet be unable properly to conclude that a state which thinks otherwise lacks a rational basis." *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 609. Under this Court's deferential standard, that is fatal to petitioners' equal protection claim.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
