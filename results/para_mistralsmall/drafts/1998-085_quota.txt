**ARGUMENT**

**I. PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS,” INDEPENDENT OF THE EMPLOYER’S STATE OF MIND.**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where the employer acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This language tracks the standard for punitive damages in tort law, which requires more than mere intentional discrimination—it requires “egregious” or “outrageous” misconduct. *BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 576 (1996) (punitive damages require conduct “so reprehensible that it warrants the imposition of further sanctions to achieve punishment and deterrence”); *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 1343, 1350 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc) (punitive damages under Title VII require “egregious” or “outrageous” misconduct), *rev’d on other grounds*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999).

This Court has long held that punitive damages are not available for mere intentional torts but only for conduct that is “vile, base, or contemptible.” *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (quoting *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851)). The same principle applies in the Title VII context. *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 1350 (citing *Smith v. Wade* for the proposition that punitive damages require “egregious” misconduct). Thus, even if an employer acts with “malice” or “reckless indifference,” punitive damages are unavailable unless the conduct is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” *Id.* (citing *Harless v. Duck*, 62 F.3d 1001, 1006 (10th Cir. 1995) (punitive damages require “egregious” misconduct); *Bryant v. Aiken Reg’l Med. Ctrs., Inc.*, 844 F. Supp. 421, 426 (D.S.C. 1994) (same)).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED PUNITIVE DAMAGES BECAUSE THE EVIDENCE DID NOT ESTABLISH “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” MISCONDUCT.**

The evidence in this case falls far short of the “egregious” or “outrageous” threshold. The jury found that ADA discriminated against Kolstad because of her sex, but discrimination alone—even intentional discrimination—does not suffice for punitive damages. *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 1350 (citing *Harless*, 62 F.3d at 1006). The evidence showed that ADA’s decision-makers acted on legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons—Kolstad’s lack of recent legislative experience and her value in her current role—even if those reasons were pretextual. *Id.* at 1348. Pretextual discrimination, without more, is insufficient to support punitive damages. *Id.* at 1350 (citing *Harless*, 62 F.3d at 1006).

Moreover, the conduct here does not rise to the level of “egregious” or “outrageous” misconduct. The record contains no evidence of repeated or pervasive discrimination, no evidence of a corporate policy of discrimination, and no evidence of intentional retaliation or harassment. *Id.* (citing *Bryant*, 844 F. Supp. at 426 (punitive damages require “egregious” misconduct, such as repeated or pervasive discrimination)). Instead, the evidence shows a single, isolated discriminatory decision—one that, while unlawful, does not warrant punitive sanctions. *Id.* (citing *Harless*, 62 F.3d at 1006 (punitive damages require “egregious” misconduct, not mere discrimination)).

**III. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 CIVIL RIGHTS ACT CONFIRMS THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES REQUIRE “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” MISCONDUCT.**

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 makes clear that Congress intended punitive damages to be available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). This standard is consistent with the common law of punitive damages, which requires conduct that is “vile, base, or contemptible.” *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56. The House Report likewise states that punitive damages under § 1981a require an “even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 12 (1991). Thus, even if ADA acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference,” punitive damages are unavailable unless the conduct is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 1350 (citing *Harless*, 62 F.3d at 1006).

**IV. THE MAJORITY’S APPROACH WOULD IMPROPERLY EXPAND PUNITIVE DAMAGES TO MERE INTENTIONAL DISCRIMINATION.**

The majority’s approach—permitting punitive damages whenever an employer acts with “malice” or “reckless indifference”—would effectively eliminate the “egregious” or “outrageous” requirement. *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 1350 (citing *Harless*, 62 F.3d at 1006). This would contravene the plain text of § 1981a(b)(1), which requires both a culpable state of mind *and* “egregious” or “outrageous” misconduct. *Id.* (citing *BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. at 576). It would also ignore the legislative history, which makes clear that punitive damages are reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The evidence did not establish “egregious” or “outrageous” misconduct, and punitive damages are unavailable under Title VII where the employer’s conduct, while intentional, does not rise to that level.
