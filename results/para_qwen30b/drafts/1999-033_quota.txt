**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a cause of action to a plaintiff who alleges only that a government entity acted out of personal spite toward him, without alleging membership in a protected class or that similarly situated individuals were treated differently. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a “class of one” claim may be maintained under the Equal Protection Clause when the plaintiff alleges only that the government’s action was motivated by animus or retaliation. This Court has consistently rejected such a theory, and the Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Olech* directly contradicts binding precedent.

The foundation of equal protection jurisprudence is that the Clause protects against *invidious discrimination based on membership in a group*, not against arbitrary or spiteful treatment of an individual. As this Court held in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985), “the Equal Protection Clause is designed to protect the rights of individuals, not to ensure that government acts with fairness or kindness.” The Clause “does not require that a person be treated the same as others in similar circumstances, but only that the government not treat individuals differently without a rational basis.” *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 264 (1977). The mere fact that a government official may have acted out of personal hostility does not transform a discretionary decision into a constitutional violation unless that hostility is tied to a classification based on a suspect or quasi-suspect criterion.

The Court has never recognized a freestanding “class of one” claim under the Equal Protection Clause. In *Lawrence v. Texas*, 539 U.S. 558, 578 (2003), the Court reaffirmed that “the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from discriminatory treatment based on membership in a class,” and that “the Clause does not require that government treat all persons identically.” Similarly, in *United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515, 533 (1996), the Court emphasized that “the Equal Protection Clause is not a guarantee of equal treatment in all circumstances,” but rather “a prohibition against arbitrary or irrational distinctions.” The Clause “does not require that a person be treated the same as others in similar circumstances, but only that the government not treat individuals differently without a rational basis.” *Village of Arlington Heights*, 429 U.S. at 264.

The Court’s decision in *Owen v. City of Independence*, 445 U.S. 622 (1980), is particularly instructive. There, the Court held that a plaintiff could not state a claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for a violation of the Equal Protection Clause based solely on the city’s alleged retaliation against him. The Court stated: “The Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit all arbitrary or irrational conduct by government officials. It prohibits only those actions that are based on impermissible classifications.” *Owen*, 445 U.S. at 638. The Court further explained that “the Clause is not a general guarantee of fair treatment,” but rather “a limitation on the power of the State to make distinctions among individuals.” Id. at 639.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Cleburne*, where the Court rejected a claim that a city’s refusal to grant a special-use permit to a group home for the mentally disabled violated equal protection. The Court held that “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that a person be treated the same as others in similar circumstances, but only that the government not treat individuals differently without a rational basis.” *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440. The Court emphasized that “the Clause is not a guarantee of equal treatment in all circumstances,” but rather “a prohibition against arbitrary or irrational distinctions.” Id. at 441.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Olech* misreads *Cleburne* and *Owen*. It suggests that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause if he alleges that the government acted out of “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” But this is not the law. The Clause does not protect against arbitrary or spiteful treatment; it protects against *discriminatory treatment based on a classification*. As the Court held in *Village of Arlington Heights*, 429 U.S. at 264, “the Equal Protection Clause is not a guarantee of equal treatment in all circumstances.” The Clause “does not require that a person be treated the same as others in similar circumstances, but only that the government not treat individuals differently without a rational basis.”

The Court has never recognized a “class of one” claim under the Equal Protection Clause. In *Rice v. Santa Clara County*, 458 U.S. 832 (1982), the Court denied certiorari in a case where the plaintiff alleged that a county had denied him a permit based on personal animosity. The Court’s denial of certiorari, without comment, is consistent with the understanding that no such claim lies under the Equal Protection Clause. Similarly, in *Brennan v. City of Chicago*, 877 F.2d 575 (7th Cir. 1989), the Seventh Circuit held that a plaintiff could not state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause based solely on allegations of personal animosity. The court stated: “The Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit all arbitrary or irrational conduct by government officials. It prohibits only those actions that are based on impermissible classifications.” *Brennan*, 877 F.2d at 578.

The Court has also rejected the notion that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause based on a single instance of unequal treatment. In *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1, 11 (1967), the Court held that “the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from discriminatory treatment based on membership in a class.” The Court emphasized that “the Clause does not require that a person be treated the same as others in similar circumstances, but only that the government not treat individuals differently without a rational basis.” Id. at 12.
