**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Relief Because the Conflict of Interest Created When District Attorney Hill Testified as a Material Witness While Continuing to Serve as Chief Prosecutor Deprived Riddle of a Fundamentally Fair Trial.**

The Due Process Clause guarantees a criminal defendant a fair trial conducted by a prosecution whose only interest is the pursuit of justice, not personal vindication. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78 (1935). In Berger, the Supreme Court explained that a prosecutor's "interest, therefore, in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done." Id. at 88. That principle rests on the recognition that the prosecutor exercises a public trust, and any personal stake that distorts that trust corrupts the proceeding regardless of the prosecutor's subjective good faith. See Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton et Fils S.A., 481 U.S. 787, 803-04 (1987) (holding that due process forbids prosecution by counsel who has a personal interest in the underlying dispute, because such an arrangement compromises the prosecutor's obligation of disinterested judgment). The same concern for institutional impartiality animated the Court's holding in Marshall v. Jerrico, Inc., 446 U.S. 238, 249-50 (1980), that enforcement of the law must remain free of "the temptation" created when the enforcer's personal interest becomes entangled with the outcome of the case he is prosecuting.

Here, District Attorney Hill did not merely prosecute Riddle; he became a witness against him. Riddle testified that Hill personally threatened him with a promise to "do his damnest [sic] to make sure he got the death penalty." To rebut that testimony, Hill took the stand himself, placing his own credibility as a sworn witness squarely before the jury. He then resumed his role as chief prosecutor, and his co-counsel exploited Hill's testimony in closing argument to brand Riddle a liar — inviting the jury to credit the State's case because its own chief advocate had testified against the defendant. This is precisely the merger of advocate and witness that Berger, Young, and Marshall condemn: the prosecution's institutional interest in impartial justice was displaced by Hill's personal interest in defending his own veracity and enforcing the very threat Riddle attributed to him. A prosecutor cannot simultaneously be sworn testimony offered against the accused and the advocate who argues that testimony to the jury without transforming the trial from an adversarial contest into a vehicle for personal vindication. See Young, 481 U.S. at 804 (a prosecutor with a personal stake "calls into question... whether the prosecution... is pursued in the public interest"). Because the conflict here went to the heart of Riddle's credibility on a contested capital charge, the trial cannot be said to have been the fair proceeding the Constitution demands.

**II. Alternatively, the District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Riddle's Request for an Evidentiary Hearing.**

Even if this Court declines to resolve the due-process claim on the existing record, Riddle was entitled to develop it through an evidentiary hearing. A habeas petitioner is entitled to a hearing where the facts alleged, if proven, would entitle him to relief and the material facts were not adequately developed in the state court proceeding. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963). Congress did not eliminate this principle when it enacted 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2); it merely conditioned a hearing on petitioner's diligence in the state forum. Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 420, 437 (2000) (holding that § 2254(e)(2) bars a hearing only where the petitioner's own lack of diligence caused the failure to develop the factual record, because "a person is not 'at fault' when his diligent efforts to perform an act are thwarted"). Riddle diligently sought state habeas relief, notifying the district court of his intent to exhaust and filing his state application; the failure to develop full evidence regarding Hill's conflict and its effect on the jury cannot be attributed to any lack of diligence on Riddle's part. The full scope of Hill's communications with Riddle, the extent of coordination between Hill's testimony and the State's closing argument, and the prejudicial impact of that testimony on the jury's credibility determination were never explored below.

Nor can the claim be resolved as a matter of law without such development. Where, as here, a claim depends on the interaction between witness credibility, prosecutorial strategy, and prejudice to the defense, an evidentiary hearing is the only mechanism through which the habeas court can test the petitioner's allegations against the record actually made at trial. See Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 695-96 (1984) (requiring courts to consider "the totality of the evidence" in assessing prejudice, a task that here requires findings never made below).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Riddle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court, vacate the grant of summary judgment, and remand with instructions either to grant the writ or, at minimum, to conduct an evidentiary hearing on the due-process claim arising from Danny Hill's dual role as witness and prosecutor.
