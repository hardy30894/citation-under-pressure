ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, this provision only pre-empts OSHA jurisdiction where another federal agency has exercised its statutory authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917); United States v. Locke, 163 U.S. 267 (1896).

The Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is not as broad as the Court of Appeals suggested. While the Coast Guard has authority to regulate certain aspects of vessel safety, such as fire extinguishers and life preservers, 46 U.S.C. § 4102, its authority is not comprehensive. See The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558 (1874); The Belfast, 74 U.S. 624 (1868). In particular, the Coast Guard's regulations do not cover all aspects of occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels. See Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1 (1824).

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals' reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. While these cases held that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, they did not address the specific issue of whether the Coast Guard's jurisdiction is exclusive as to uninspected vessels. See The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158 (1903); The Arizona v. Anelich, 298 U.S. 110 (1936).

In fact, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the Coast Guard's authority is not exclusive, and that other federal agencies may regulate certain aspects of vessel safety. See United States v. Coombs, 37 U.S. 72 (1838); The Plymouth, 70 U.S. 20 (1865). Moreover, the Court has held that federal statutes should be construed to avoid conflicts between different regulatory schemes. See Missouri Pacific Ry. Co. v. Larabee Flour Mills Co., 211 U.S. 612 (1909); Erie R. Co. v. New York, 233 U.S. 671 (1914).

In this case, the Coast Guard's investigation of the accident on Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 did not pre-empt OSHA's jurisdiction to issue citations under the Act. See The City of Norwalk, 55 F. 98 (1893); The Silvia, 171 U.S. 462 (1898). The Act and the Coast Guard's regulations can coexist, and OSHA's jurisdiction is not pre-empted by the Coast Guard's authority.

In conclusion, OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels under the Act. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction precluded OSHA's regulation. The decision should be reversed, and the citations issued by OSHA should be reinstated.
