I. The Reinstatement Provision Does Not Apply to Pre-1997 Reentries.

Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), authorizes reinstatement of a prior removal order against an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally.” The provision took effect on April 1, 1997. See Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 309(a), 110 Stat. 3009-546, 3009-625. Petitioner reentered in 1982 and remained continuously thereafter. Because the statute contains no express command that it reach conduct completed before its effective date, it does not apply here. See Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994) (“[A] requirement that Congress first make its intention clear helps ensure that Congress itself has determined that the benefits of retroactivity outweigh the potential for disruption or unfairness.”).

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary reading—that the statute reaches any alien “who reenters” after 1997 regardless of when the reentry occurred—contradicts the settled presumption against retroactive operation. See id. at 265 (“[T]he presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence.”). No language in § 241(a)(5) or its legislative history supplies the “clear intent” required to overcome that presumption. See INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001) (“[C]lear statement” required before a statute may be applied to “attach new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.”).

II. Application to Pre-1997 Reentries Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive.

Even if Congress intended § 241(a)(5) to reach pre-enactment reentries, the statute may not be so applied. A law operates retroactively when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270. Petitioner’s 1982 reentry and subsequent continuous residence created settled expectations that he could seek adjustment of status under then-existing law. See St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321 (holding that elimination of discretionary relief “attaches a new disability” to pre-enactment guilty pleas). The reinstatement bar eliminates that avenue entirely, imposing “a new disability” on conduct that occurred fifteen years earlier. Id.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on the absence of a “protectable expectation” misapplies the retroactivity inquiry. The relevant question is not whether the alien possessed an enforceable right, but whether the new statute “would have an retroactive effect” by changing the legal consequences of past conduct. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70. Because § 241(a)(5) does so here, it cannot be applied to Petitioner’s 1982 reentry.
