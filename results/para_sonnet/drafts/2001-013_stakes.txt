**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 4(b)(1) Vests the Coast Guard, Not OSHA, With Exclusive Authority Over the Working Conditions of Seamen Aboard Vessels in Navigation**

Congress provided that the Occupational Safety and Health Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard possesses exactly that authority. Under 14 U.S.C. § 2, the Coast Guard "shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States." This Court's precedents confirm that this grant is exclusive with respect to seamen's working conditions. In *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), the Fifth Circuit held that OSHA regulations "do not apply to the working conditions of seamen on vessels operating on the high seas." *Id.* at 1121-22. That holding was reaffirmed in *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), which explained that "the Coast Guard's comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen's working conditions" creates "an industry-wide exemption [from OSHA] for seamen serving on vessels operating on navigable waters." *Id.* at 826. Rig 52 was a drilling barge crewed by seamen performing maritime work on navigable waters—precisely the circumstance *Clary* and *Donovan* place beyond OSHA's reach.

**II. The Coast Guard's Authority Over Uninspected Vessels Is No Less Exclusive Than Its Authority Over Inspected Vessels**

Petitioner's attempt to carve uninspected vessels out of this exemption finds no footing in the statute. The Coast Guard is "expressly authorized to issue safety regulations for uninspected vessels," including regulations governing fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. It has exercised that authority extensively. See 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.*; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 *et seq.* Nothing in *Clary* turned on whether the vessel there was inspected—indeed, "there is no indication from *Clary* that the barge in that case was inspected," and "the broad language of *Clary* does not turn on any such distinction." The category "uninspected" describes the degree of Coast Guard *discretion* in prescribing detailed standards, not the scope of its *jurisdiction*. Congress did not confine the Coast Guard's statutory mandate under 14 U.S.C. § 2—which covers "all matters not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department"—to only those vessels the Coast Guard happens to inspect. To read § 4(b)(1) as petitioner urges would make OSHA's authority turn on the happenstance of a vessel's inspection classification rather than on the Coast Guard's underlying statutory jurisdiction, a distinction the statute nowhere draws.

**III. Concurrent Regulation of Uninspected Vessels Would Recreate the Fragmentation Section 4(b)(1) Was Designed to Eliminate**

The structure of § 4(b)(1) reflects a considered congressional judgment that "redundant control programs offer little except confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise." *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 827. This concern is especially acute in the maritime context, where vessels move fluidly between jurisdictions and even between inspected and uninspected status depending on their activity. Permitting OSHA to regulate uninspected vessels while the Coast Guard regulates inspected ones would produce the very "anomaly . . . of steaming in and out of OSHA coverage" that *Donovan* warned against. *Id.* at 829. As the court there recognized, "a single, uniform set of rules should govern the maritime workplace," and "[b]ecause of OSHA's geographic limitations . . . this cannot be those of OSHA . . . [thus] it must be those of the Coast Guard." *Id.*

This need for uniformity is not new to maritime regulation. This Court has long insisted that matters within the maritime domain be governed by rules that are "uniform in its application and interpretation" throughout the Nation's navigable waters, and has held that local or overlapping regulation is invalid if it "works material prejudice to the characteristic features of the general maritime law or interferes with the proper harmony and uniformity of that law." *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917). Subjecting uninspected barges like Rig 52 to a patchwork of OSHA citations, superimposed on Coast Guard investigation and regulation of the same casualty, would generate precisely the "material prejudice" to maritime uniformity that *Jensen* forbids and that Congress sought to avoid by enacting § 4(b)(1).

**IV. Conclusion**

Because the Coast Guard exercises comprehensive statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen aboard vessels in navigation—inspected and uninspected alike—§ 4(b)(1) forecloses OSHA's citation of Rig 52. The judgment of the Fifth Circuit vacating that citation should be affirmed.
