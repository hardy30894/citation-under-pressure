The Sixth Amendment does not apply to Indian tribes. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 382 (1896). Tribal courts therefore may try offenses without appointed counsel, and their judgments remain valid exercises of retained sovereignty. Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556, 568-569 (1883); United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375, 381-382 (1886). Congress has long recognized this distinction when it authorizes federal prosecutions that rest on prior tribal adjudications. 

A prior valid conviction may serve as an element of a later offense without reopening the earlier proceeding. The right to counsel attaches only in the case then before the court. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938). When the earlier conviction occurred in a forum to which the Sixth Amendment never extended, its use in a subsequent federal prosecution does not violate that Amendment. Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 461-462 (1942). 

The same principle governs incorporation of the right to jury trial. Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, 156 (1968). A conviction obtained without a jury in a jurisdiction where the Sixth Amendment does not operate may nevertheless be proved in a later federal case. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 18 (1967). Section 117(a) merely requires proof of the fact of two prior domestic-violence convictions; it does not retry those offenses or impose new punishment for them. 

Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963), confirmed that the right to counsel is fundamental when the Sixth Amendment applies. It did not hold that every sovereign must supply counsel or that every uncounseled judgment is a nullity everywhere. Because tribal proceedings lie outside the Amendment’s reach, the convictions at issue here retain their full force as predicates. 

The statute therefore comports with the Constitution.
