**Argument**

The district court erred in denying suppression because the police lacked the particularized, objective basis required to frisk Michael Soares. A pat-frisk during a traffic stop is permissible only when officers can point to specific, articulable facts creating reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968); *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 327 (2009). The government bears the burden of justifying the intrusion. *United States v. Ruidíaz*, 529 F.3d 25, 28 (1st Cir. 2008); *United States v. Fernandez*, 600 F.3d 56, 60 (1st Cir. 2010).

Here, the stop began as a routine traffic stop for a headlights violation. A traffic stop does not automatically authorize a frisk of a passenger. *Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997); *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. at 327-28. Although officers may order occupants out of a lawfully stopped vehicle, *Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 111 n.6 (1977), and *Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. at 414-15, removal from the car is not the same as reasonable suspicion to search the person. *Johnson*, 555 U.S. at 327; *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27.

The government relied chiefly on four facts: late hour, a high-crime neighborhood, movement inside the car, and Soares’s agitation and profanity. Those facts, whether taken singly or together, do not amount to reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that presence in a high-crime area is relevant but not sufficient. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000). Nervousness or agitation is likewise an unreliable indicator, especially during a police confrontation. *United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d 38, 39-41 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204-05 (2002). And “furtive movements,” without more, do not automatically create reasonable suspicion. *United States v. Cordero*, 42 F.3d 697, 699-700 (1st Cir. 1994); *United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d at 41.

That is especially true here because the movements the officers observed were ambiguous. The driver moved toward his door, the front-seat passenger toward the console, and no movement was seen in the back seat where Soares sat. The officers did not observe a weapon, a bulge, a threatening gesture, or any conduct directed toward concealment of a gun. In comparable circumstances, this Court has rejected frisk authority where the government could point only to generalized concern, vague motion, or the mere possibility of danger. *United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d at 40-41; *United States v. Trullo*, 809 F.2d 108, 113 (1st Cir. 1987).

Soares’s verbal protest and refusal to remain perfectly still also do not transform the encounter into an armed-danger case. The Fourth Amendment permits officers to protect themselves, but it does not permit a frisk whenever a person is displeased with police conduct. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 24, 30; *United States v. George*, 732 F.3d 296, 299-300 (1st Cir. 2013). If agitation alone were enough, nearly every tense roadside encounter would justify a weapons search. The Constitution requires more: individualized suspicion tied to a real danger. *Johnson*, 555 U.S. at 327; *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27.

Nor does the fact that multiple officers were present and one passenger consented to a frisk supply the missing suspicion as to Soares. Reasonable suspicion must be individualized. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 91 (1979). The frisk of one occupant cannot bootstrap a frisk of another absent specific facts as to that person. *Ybarra*, 444 U.S. at 91-92; *United States v. Woodrum*, 202 F.3d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 2000). Here, the police had no such individualized basis when they directed Soares out of the car and attempted to pat him down. The record shows only that Reid “felt really uneasy” about Soares’s demeanor. Subjective unease is not the objective standard the Fourth Amendment requires. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21-22; *United States v. Ruidíaz*, 529 F.3d at 28.

Because the frisk was unlawful, the firearm discovered as its direct product must be suppressed. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-88 (1963). The denial of the suppression motion should therefore be reversed, the conviction vacated, and the case remanded.
