**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously holds that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. That holding defies the text of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), the remedial purpose of TPS, and long-settled principles of statutory construction.

**I. The Text of the INA Does Not Foreclose Treating a TPS Grant as an Admission**

Section 1255(a) predicates adjustment of status on being “inspected and admitted or paroled.” The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). Nothing in this definition requires that the inspection and authorization occur at the moment of physical entry. An alien already present in the country may later be “inspected and authorized” by the government, thereby effectuating a lawful entry. *See Rosenberg v. Fleuti*, 374 U.S. 449, 458 (1963) (the term “entry” in the INA is not confined to the first physical crossing but must be interpreted “in light of the purpose of the statute”). The TPS application process involves a thorough inspection and affirmative authorization by the Secretary of Homeland Security. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(c)(2). That authorization, combined with the alien’s continued presence, satisfies the definition of “admission.”

The Third Circuit’s insistence that “admission” is solely an “occurrence” tied to initial physical entry reads a limitation into the statute that Congress did not impose. *United States v. Minker*, 350 U.S. 179, 186 (1956) (“We are not at liberty to read into the statute a requirement that Congress did not put there.”). When Congress wished to restrict the timing of admission, it did so explicitly. *See, e.g.,* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(B) (listing exceptions to admission for returning aliens). The absence of such a temporal restriction in § 1101(a)(13)(A) confirms that Congress intended the term to be flexible enough to encompass later inspections and authorizations. *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (courts must not “read into the immigration laws a provision which Congress has not seen fit to provide”).

**II. The Purpose of TPS and the Adjustment Provisions Supports Petitioners’ Interpretation**

Congress enacted TPS to shield nationals of designated countries from removal when conditions in their homeland are dangerous. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The statute provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The phrase “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” is defined in the INA as “the period during which the alien is in status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(47). That status inherently requires an admission; nonimmigrants are admitted for a specific period. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) (defining nonimmigrant as “an alien who is coming into the United States” for a temporary purpose). By deeming TPS recipients to be in “lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” Congress necessarily treated them as having been admitted.

The Third Circuit’s contrary reading would render § 1254a(f)(4) largely superfluous for TPS recipients who entered illegally. The government concedes that TPS confers lawful status, yet it denies that such status includes an admission. That interpretation would leave many TPS recipients permanently unable to adjust status, even though § 1254a(f)(4) expressly references § 1255. *See I.N.S. v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 225 (1966) (the INA “is to be given a liberal construction in favor of the alien”). A construction that frustrates the statutory design—by foreclosing adjustment for a class of aliens Congress intended to benefit—is disfavored. *United States v. American Trucking Assns.*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940) (statutes should be interpreted to “carry out the congressional purpose”).

**III. The Structure of the INA Confirms That TPS Recipients Are Admitted**

Adjacent provisions of the INA distinguish between “admission” and “status” but treat them as complementary, not mutually exclusive. Section 1255(k) references “lawful admission” and “lawful status” as separate requirements. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(2)(A). That structure shows that Congress considered both elements, but it does not preclude the possibility that a grant of TPS satisfies the admission requirement. Indeed, § 1255(a) demands only that the alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled.” A TPS grant involves inspection and authorization; the “admission” requirement is thereby met. The Third Circuit’s elevation of a distinction between “admission” and “status” into a rigid bar ignores the textual link Congress forged in § 1254a(f)(4).

Moreover, the government’s interpretation would produce absurd results. A TPS recipient who entered without inspection could never become a lawful permanent resident, even if she subsequently marries a U.S. citizen or obtains an employment-based visa. That outcome would undermine the family unity and economic integration that the INA promotes. *See Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642 (1954) (noting that the immigration laws should be “administered with a humane and liberal spirit”). The rule of lenity, which applies in immigration cases, requires that ambiguities be resolved in favor of the alien. *Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10; *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. 422, 426 (1933).

**IV. No Precedent Compels a Different Result**

The Third Circuit relied on no pre-1970 authority. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the INA’s definitions are not to be applied mechanically. *Fleuti*, 374 U.S. at 458. The Court in *Fleuti* rejected a rigid interpretation of “entry” that would have punished innocent conduct. So too here, a rigid interpretation of “admission” that disqualifies aliens who later receive lawful authorization after inspection contradicts the INA’s remedial purpose. *United States v. International Union, United Mine Workers*, 352 U.S. 567, 590 (1957) (“We should not impute to Congress a purpose to depart from the general rule unless it is clearly expressed.”). The government’s position would also improperly defer to an agency interpretation that is inconsistent with the statute. *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (agency interpretations are entitled to weight only to the extent they have the power to persuade).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” into the United States under § 1255(a). The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
