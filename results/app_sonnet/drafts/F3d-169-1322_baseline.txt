**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard the district court was required to apply. Summary judgment is proper unless the non-movant offers evidence sufficient to create a genuine issue for trial. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986) (Rule 56 “mandates the entry of summary judgment ... against a party who fails to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party’s case”). A factual dispute precludes summary judgment only when it “might affect the outcome of the suit under the governing law.” *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). The district court correctly applied these standards in holding that Little Rock and Hall were not joint employers and in limiting the Burtons’ liability to statutory damages.

**II. The District Court Correctly Held That Little Rock and Hall Were Not Joint Employers Under the AWPA’s Economic-Reality Test**

The AWPA borrows the “economic reality” test developed under the Fair Labor Standards Act to determine employer status. *Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb*, 331 U.S. 722, 730 (1947) (employment status turns on “economic reality” rather than “technical concepts”). This Court has applied that same framework to AWPA joint-employment claims, examining factors such as control over the manner and conditions of work, the power to hire and fire, and the degree of permanency of the relationship between the alleged employer and the labor contractor’s crew. *Antenor v. D & S Farms*, 88 F.3d 925, 929-33 (11th Cir. 1996); *Aimable v. Long and Scott Farms*, 20 F.3d 434, 439-40 (11th Cir. 1994).

Applying those factors, Little Rock and Hall exercised no control over Luxama’s crew. Luxama, not Little Rock, recruited, transported, supervised, and paid the workers; Little Rock’s role was limited to supplying seed, boxes, and a trailer, and marketing the finished crop—an arm’s-length commercial relationship with the Burtons, not an employment relationship with the pickers. As in *Aimable*, where this Court held that a grower’s incidental oversight of a contractor’s crew did not establish joint employment, 20 F.3d at 444, Little Rock’s and Hall’s attenuated involvement with the harvest cannot satisfy the economic-reality test. The district court therefore correctly granted summary judgment to Little Rock and Hall on the joint-employer issue. *Charles v. Burton*, No. 92-150-VAL (M.D. Ga.).

**III. The District Court Correctly Limited the Farm Workers’ Recovery Against the Burtons to Statutory Damages**

The AWPA’s private-right-of-action provision authorizes a court to award either actual damages or statutory damages “for each violation,” 29 U.S.C. § 1854(c)(1), but a plaintiff seeking actual damages must still establish that the claimed injury was caused by the defendant’s violation of the statute. The Burtons do not dispute that they failed to verify Luxama’s registration as required by 29 U.S.C. § 1842, but the appellants offered no evidence linking that recordkeeping failure to the vehicle-insurance violation that produced the accident. Those are separate statutory duties: § 1841(b) imposes the insurance obligation on the labor contractor himself, while § 1842 requires only that a person “utilizing” the contractor’s services take reasonable steps to confirm registration.

Just as the summary-judgment standard requires more than speculation to create a triable issue, *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 248, the appellants’ theory that verification of Luxama’s paperwork would have prevented the accident is speculative. Luxama’s own default in paying his Department of Labor fine—not the Burtons’ failure to check his file—caused the lapse in insurance coverage that left the workers unprotected. Under these circumstances, the district court properly exercised its discretion under § 1854(c)(1) to confine the workers’ remedy to the statutory damages Congress made available precisely for technical or recordkeeping violations unaccompanied by proof of causally connected harm.

**IV. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be affirmed in all respects: Little Rock and Hall were properly found not to be joint employers under the economic-reality test established in *Rutherford*, *Antenor*, and *Aimable*, and the Burtons’ liability was properly limited to statutory damages under 29 U.S.C. § 1854(c)(1).
