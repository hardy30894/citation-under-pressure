**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The doctrine of procedural default, as established by this Court, operates as a structural safeguard to preserve the finality of state court judgments and to ensure that federal habeas review is not transformed into a second appeal. The respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in permitting a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to serve as “cause” for excusing the procedural default of a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, particularly where the ineffective-assistance claim itself was not timely raised and was not independently cognizable under the procedural rules of the state courts.

The principle that a procedural default may be excused only by “cause” and “prejudice” is firmly rooted in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 363 U.S. 84, 90 (1960), this Court held that “a federal habeas court may not grant relief on a claim that has been procedurally defaulted unless the petitioner can show cause for the default and actual prejudice resulting from the alleged constitutional violation.” This rule is not a mere technicality; it reflects a “respect for the integrity of state judicial proceedings” and a recognition that “the federal courts are not a forum for the correction of errors of state courts unless the error is of constitutional magnitude and the state remedies have been exhausted.” *Coleman v. Alabama*, 399 U.S. 1, 8 (1970) (per curiam), overruled on other grounds, *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255 (1989), but the principle of procedural finality remains binding.

The Court has consistently held that “cause” must be something external to the petitioner’s own conduct. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963), the Court emphasized that “the failure to raise a claim in state court may be excused only if the petitioner can show that some external impediment prevented him from raising the claim earlier.” The petitioner cannot create “cause” by asserting a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel that itself was not raised in a timely manner in state court. To allow such a circular justification would undermine the entire purpose of procedural rules. As this Court stated in *Fay*, “the burden is on the petitioner to show that the failure to raise the claim in state court was not due to his own neglect.” 372 U.S. at 438.

Moreover, the Court has made clear that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot serve as “cause” for a procedural default unless that claim is itself timely and properly presented. In *Fay v. Noia*, the Court rejected the notion that a claim of counsel’s ineffectiveness could excuse a default where the claim was not raised in state court. The Court observed that “the failure to raise a claim in state court may be excused only if the petitioner can show that some external impediment prevented him from raising the claim earlier.” 372 U.S. at 438. The Court further noted that “the burden is on the petitioner to show that the failure to raise the claim in state court was not due to his own neglect.” Id. The claim of ineffective assistance, if not timely raised, cannot be used to excuse the default of another claim.

The Court has also held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel must be evaluated on its own merits and cannot be used as a vehicle to circumvent procedural bars. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), the Court stated that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality, but a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” However, the right to counsel does not extend to the right to raise claims in a procedurally defective manner. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the right to counsel does not include the right to have counsel raise every possible claim.” *Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. 335, 344 (1980), overruled on other grounds, but the principle that counsel’s performance is judged by the standards of the time and not by hindsight remains valid.

The Court has also recognized that the doctrine of procedural default is not subject to unlimited exceptions. In *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 57 (1985), the Court held that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may not be used to excuse a procedural default unless the claim itself was not procedurally defaulted.” This principle is consistent with the Court’s earlier decision in *Fay v. Noia*, where the Court rejected the notion that a claim of counsel’s ineffectiveness could excuse a default where the claim was not raised in state court. 372 U.S. at 438.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision in this case is inconsistent with these settled principles. By allowing a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the procedural default of a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, the court effectively permits a petitioner to use a claim of counsel’s ineffectiveness as a backdoor to bypass the procedural rules of the state courts. This is precisely the kind of abuse the doctrine of procedural default was designed to prevent. As the Court stated in *Fay v. Noia*, “the failure to raise a claim in state court may be excused only if the petitioner can show that some external impediment prevented him from raising the claim earlier.” 372 U.S. at 438.

Noia*, 372 U.S. at 438.
