**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedurally Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance-of-Counsel Claim May Serve as Cause to Excuse the Default of Another Claim Without Independently Satisfying the Cause-and-Prejudice Standard**

**I. This Court's cause-and-prejudice doctrine exists to protect state interests that are fully vindicated when the ineffective-assistance claim has been presented, even untimely, to the state courts.**

The cause-and-prejudice standard of *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), was designed to ensure that state procedural rules are not "eviscerate[d]" and that federal courts respect the "state's finality" interests. *Id.* at 90–91. Those interests are structural, not talismanic. Where, as here, a petitioner has actually presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the state courts—as Carpenter did in his application to reopen his direct appeal—the state has already been given the "opportunity to pass upon and correct" the alleged constitutional violation. *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971). The state courts' rejection of that claim on timeliness grounds does not diminish the fact that the state judicial system confronted the substance of counsel's ineffectiveness. Comity, the animating concern of *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509 (1982), is satisfied once the state courts have had that fair opportunity, regardless of the procedural vehicle by which the claim ultimately founders.

**II. Attorney error that violates the Sixth Amendment is, by this Court's own reasoning in *Murray v. Carrier*, an external factor not fairly attributable to the petitioner, and it does not lose that character merely because the claim asserting the error is untimely.**

In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), this Court held that "[a]ttorney error that constitutes ineffective assistance of counsel is cause" for a procedural default because such error is an "objective factor external to the defense" that "impede[s] counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule." *Id.* at 488. The rationale is that a defendant cannot be charged with the default of a claim when the very reason it was defaulted is his lawyer's constitutionally deficient performance—performance for which, under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), the defendant himself bears no responsibility, since the Sixth Amendment guarantees him counsel whose performance does not fall "below an objective standard of reasonableness." *Id.* at 688. The untimeliness of the state-court application vindicating that Sixth Amendment right does not convert the underlying constitutional violation into something the petitioner himself caused. The default of the *vehicle* for raising the claim does not erase the *external* nature of the error the claim describes.

**III. Petitioner's rule would create an infinite regress inconsistent with this Court's framework in *Engle v. Isaac* and *Reed v. Ross*, under which the "cause" inquiry looks to whether some objective impediment prevented compliance with state procedure, not to whether that impediment is itself procedurally pristine.**

In *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107 (1982), this Court explained that the cause requirement is satisfied by showing "some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule." *Id.* at 133. Similarly, in *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1 (1984), the Court recognized that a claim may be treated as cause where "the constitutional claim is so novel that its legal basis is not reasonably available to counsel," *id.* at 16, without requiring that the novelty claim itself be free of procedural taint. Petitioner's position—that the ineffective-assistance claim must itself clear an independent cause-and-prejudice hurdle before it can excuse the default of the underlying claim—would require every "cause" showing to be preceded by its own showing of cause, and that showing by another, ad infinitum. Nothing in *Carrier*, *Engle*, or *Sykes* contemplates such a regress; each asks only whether an external impediment existed, not whether the petitioner can prove, through successive procedural gauntlets, that his lawyer's failure was in turn excusable.

**IV. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's presentation of his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio courts, coupled with the demonstrated prejudice from counsel's failure to raise a meritorious sufficiency claim, satisfies *Carrier*'s cause-and-prejudice standard in full.**

Because Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim was raised—unsuccessfully, but substantively—in the Ohio Court of Appeals and Ohio Supreme Court, the state judicial system was given its due opportunity to address the constitutional violation, satisfying the exhaustion principle of *Picard*, 404 U.S. at 275, and the comity principle of *Rose*, 455 U.S. at 518–19. The Sixth Circuit went on to find, consistent with *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694, that Carpenter suffered prejudice from counsel's omission because there existed "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." That finding satisfies *Carrier*'s two-part standard as applied to the sufficiency claim, and no further layer of independent cause-and-prejudice analysis directed at the ineffective-assistance claim itself is required by this Court's precedents.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit should be affirmed.
