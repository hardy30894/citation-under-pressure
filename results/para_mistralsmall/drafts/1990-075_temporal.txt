**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Controlled Substances Act Does Not Unconstitutionally Delegate Legislative Power to the Attorney General.**

The Constitution vests "[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted ... in a Congress of the United States." U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. Yet this Court has long recognized that Congress may delegate authority to other branches so long as it supplies an "intelligible principle" to guide the delegate’s exercise of that authority. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The delegation here easily satisfies that test.

In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), the Court struck down a delegation because the statute gave the President "no guidance whatever" in deciding whether to prohibit the interstate shipment of petroleum. *Id.* at 415. By contrast, the Controlled Substances Act directs the Attorney General to consider specific factors—including the substance’s potential for abuse, its accepted medical use, and its risk to public health—before temporarily scheduling a drug. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3) (1988). These standards are far more precise than those upheld in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944), where the Court sustained a delegation that allowed the Price Administrator to fix commodity prices "in the light of the prices prevailing in the same or similar commodities during the base period." The Act’s criteria are also more detailed than those in *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932), where the Court upheld a delegation to the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate railroad rates "just and reasonable."

The temporary scheduling provision further ensures that the Attorney General’s discretion is cabined by the statute’s structure. The Act limits temporary scheduling to one year (with a possible six-month extension), 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), and requires notice and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, *id.* § 811(h)(4). These procedural safeguards distinguish this delegation from the unconstitutional one in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 542 (1935), where the Court invalidated a delegation that lacked any temporal or procedural constraints.

**II. The Attorney General’s Delegation to the DEA Is Authorized by Law.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 693–94 (1892), the Court upheld a statute that expressly authorized the President to delegate his tariff-adjustment power to executive officers. Similarly, the Controlled Substances Act does not prohibit subdelegation; indeed, it implicitly contemplates it by vesting authority in the "Attorney General," an office that necessarily acts through subordinate officials. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding a statute delegating authority to the Secretary of Agriculture to issue regulations, which the Secretary then subdelegated to subordinate officers).

The DEA’s role here is analogous to that of the Postmaster General in *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 395 (1880), where the Court upheld a statute authorizing the Postmaster General to appoint deputies to perform statutorily defined duties. The temporary scheduling power, like the duties in *Siebold*, is ministerial and constrained by the statute’s terms. The DEA’s exercise of this authority is thus a valid exercise of executive power, not an unlawful delegation.

**III. The Nondelegation Doctrine Does Not Bar Criminalization by Regulation.**

The Toubys argue that the nondelegation doctrine is stricter when the delegated power creates crimes. But this Court has never drawn such a distinction. In *United States v. Eaton*, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892), the Court upheld a statute that made it a crime to violate regulations issued by the Secretary of the Treasury under a broad delegation of authority. The Court explained that the statute provided "ample warning" of the conduct prohibited, even if the precise contours of the crime were defined by regulation. *Id.* at 687. Similarly, the Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision gives fair notice of the conduct it criminalizes—manufacturing or conspiring to manufacture a substance the Attorney General has determined poses an imminent hazard to public safety.

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining* and *Schechter Poultry* is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations that were entirely unguided; here, the Act supplies detailed standards. The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations that, like this one, involve technical or rapidly evolving fields. *See, e.g., FTC v. Gratz*, 253 U.S. 421, 427 (1920) (upholding a delegation to the FTC to prevent "unfair methods of competition" in commerce).

**Conclusion**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. It supplies an intelligible principle, imposes procedural safeguards, and is consistent with this Court’s longstanding recognition of Congress’s authority to delegate authority to the executive branch. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is likewise lawful. The judgments below should be affirmed.
