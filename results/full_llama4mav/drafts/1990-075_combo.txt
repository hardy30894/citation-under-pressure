The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as established by the Supreme Court, permits Congress to delegate authority to another branch of government as long as it provides an "intelligible principle" to guide the delegatee's actions (J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). 

The CSA satisfies this requirement by specifying the factors the Attorney General must consider when scheduling a substance, including its potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health (21 U.S.C. § 811(c)). This guidance is sufficient to ensure that the Attorney General's actions are not arbitrary or capricious. The Supreme Court has upheld delegations with similar guidance, such as in United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506 (1911), where the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make rules regarding forest reserves.

The CSA also provides additional checks on the Attorney General's authority, such as the requirement to obtain a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services (21 U.S.C. § 811(b)). This ensures that the Attorney General's decisions are informed by expert opinion and are not solely based on law enforcement considerations. The Supreme Court has recognized the importance of such checks in cases like Mahon v. Justice, 127 U.S. 700 (1888), where the Court upheld a delegation to a state official subject to similar constraints.

Furthermore, the Attorney General's delegation of authority to the DEA is authorized by Congress, which has permitted the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the CSA (21 U.S.C. § 871(a)). The Supreme Court has long recognized the validity of sub-delegations, as in United States v. Chemical Foundation, 272 U.S. 1 (1926), where the Court upheld a sub-delegation by the President to an administrative official.

The Toubys argue that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the Attorney General to create crimes. However, the Supreme Court has upheld delegations that involve the creation of crimes, such as in United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co., 287 U.S. 77 (1932), where the Court upheld a delegation to the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate rates. The key factor is not whether the delegation involves the creation of crimes, but whether it is guided by an intelligible principle.

In addition, the Supreme Court has emphasized that the non-delegation doctrine is not a rigid or inflexible principle, but rather a flexible standard that takes into account the complexities of modern governance (Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944)). The CSA's delegation to the Attorney General is a reasonable response to the rapidly changing nature of the drug trade, and is guided by clear and specific principles.

The Court should also consider the historical context in which the non-delegation doctrine was developed. As the Supreme Court noted in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), the doctrine is rooted in the separation of powers and the need to prevent any one branch of government from abusing its authority. However, the Court has also recognized that the doctrine must be applied in a practical and flexible manner, taking into account the needs of modern governance (Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245 (1947)).

In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), the Supreme Court struck down delegations that lacked clear guidance or intelligible principles. However, the CSA's delegation to the Attorney General is distinguishable from these cases, as it provides specific factors to be considered and requires the Attorney General to obtain expert evaluation.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the CSA's delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional. The statute provides sufficient guidance and checks on the Attorney General's authority, and is consistent with the principles established by the Supreme Court in cases such as J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, United States v. Grimaud, and Fahey v. Mallonee.
