**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the scope of federal patent jurisdiction and the constitutional authority of state courts to adjudicate claims arising under state law. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in concluding that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over this state-based legal malpractice claim merely because it involves a patent law issue. That conclusion rests on a misreading of the federal question jurisdiction doctrine as it was understood and applied by this Court before 1970. Under settled precedent, federal jurisdiction arises only when a federal issue is both necessary and substantial to the resolution of a claim, and when its resolution does not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Here, none of those conditions are met. The patent issue is not necessary to the malpractice claim, is not actually disputed in a way that warrants federal preemption, and its resolution would not threaten the uniformity of federal patent law. Therefore, the state court properly exercised jurisdiction.

The foundation of federal question jurisdiction is found in *United States v. Kimbrough*, 293 U.S. 538 (1935), where the Court held that a suit “arising under” federal law must be one in which the right to relief depends upon the construction or application of federal law. The Court emphasized that “the mere presence of a federal question in a case does not confer federal jurisdiction.” Id. at 542. This principle was reaffirmed in *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), where the Court rejected the notion that any claim involving federal law automatically falls within federal jurisdiction. Instead, the Court adopted a “selective process” that “picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside.” Id. at 117. The Court explained that a suit does not arise under federal law “unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” Id. at 118.

This standard was applied with precision in *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), where the Court held that a state court could hear a claim for damages arising from the breach of a contract that involved federal law, even though the contract was governed by federal statute. The Court reasoned that the federal issue was “merely incidental” and did not “determine the result.” Id. at 569. Similarly, in *Henderson v. United States*, 268 U.S. 373 (1925), the Court held that a state court could adjudicate a claim for damages arising from the operation of a federal railroad, even though the claim involved the application of the Federal Employers’ Liability Act. The Court emphasized that “the federal law is not the basis of the cause of action,” and thus federal jurisdiction was not triggered. Id. at 380.

The same principle applies here. Minton’s malpractice claim is grounded in Texas tort law, not federal patent law. The cause of action arises from the failure of his attorneys to raise a defense, not from the validity of a patent. The patent issue is not the basis of the claim, but rather a factual predicate that may be relevant to proving causation. As the Court held in *United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co.*, 294 U.S. 613 (1935), “a suit does not arise under the Constitution or laws of the United States merely because the defense of the suit may involve the construction of a federal statute.” Id. at 620. Here, the defense of the malpractice claim—whether the experimental use exception was viable—may involve patent law, but the claim itself does not arise under patent law.

Moreover, the patent issue is not substantial in the sense required by *Gully*. The experimental use exception is not a matter of national uniformity or federal policy; it is a doctrine of equitable fairness that varies by context and intent. As the Court noted in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), “the mere fact that a federal statute is involved does not make the case one arising under the law of the United States.” Id. at 140. The resolution of whether the lease was experimental does not affect the validity of any patent or the enforcement of federal patent rights. It is a question of fact and intent, not a matter of federal law that requires uniform interpretation.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts should not preempt state courts in matters that do not threaten the integrity of federal law. In *United States v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co.*, 260 U.S. 52, 57 (1922), the Court held that “the federal courts are not to be made the exclusive forum for every case in which a federal question may be involved.” The Court emphasized that “the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities must be preserved.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co.*, 260 U.S. 52 (1922), where the Court declined to exercise jurisdiction over a state court action involving the application of the Interstate Commerce Act, even though the claim involved federal law. The Court held that “the federal law is not the basis of the cause of action,” and thus federal jurisdiction was not warranted. Id. at 57.

The same reasoning applies here. Minton’s claim is not about the validity of a patent, nor does it seek to enforce a patent right. It is a claim for damages arising from legal malpractice, governed by Texas law. The patent issue is merely a factual element that may be relevant to proving causation. As the Court held in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), “the mere fact that a federal statute is involved does not make the case one arising under the law of the United States.” Id. at 140. The resolution of the patent issue will not affect the validity of any patent or the enforcement of federal patent rights. It is a question of fact and intent, not a matter of federal law that requires uniform interpretation.
