ARGUMENT

**I. Section 811(h) Contains an Intelligible Principle and Is a Constitutional Delegation**

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from enlisting the coordinate branches. Congress may delegate authority if it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Court has recognized that “Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372.

Section 811(h) is not a broad grant; it is a narrow, emergency supplement to the permanent scheduling apparatus. It authorizes temporary scheduling only when a substance poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). It then ties that determination to three specific factors: the drug’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)–(6)). It requires notice in the Federal Register and transmission of that notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be considered. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). It limits the temporary order to one year, with one possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). These are precisely the sorts of “intelligible principles” the Court has consistently upheld. *See Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374–79 (Sentencing Commission standards); *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424–25 (1944) (“fair and equitable” prices); *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 773–75 (1948) (“excessive profits”).

The nondelegation doctrine has been enforced only twice, both times against the National Industrial Recovery Act, which supplied no policy and no criteria. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935); *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 541–42 (1935). Here Congress supplied both. The Attorney General does not define criminal conduct in the abstract; he makes factual findings under statutory criteria, and Congress has already attached the criminal penalty to substances that meet those criteria. That is a permissible executive function. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521–22 (1911) (upholding criminal penalties for violating administratively adopted rules implementing legislative policy).

The Toubys’ contrary argument proves too much. If every delegation that could lead to criminal enforcement were unconstitutional, much of the modern administrative state would be suspect. The Court, however, applies the same “intelligible principle” test regardless of the sanctions at issue. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 n.7. And the Court has expressly declined to adopt a stricter nondelgation standard for statutes that authorize agencies to impose penalties. *Skinner v. Mid-America Pipeline Co.*, 490 U.S. 212, 220–21 (1989). Section 811(h) easily passes.

**II. The Act Authorized the Attorney General To Subdelegate to the DEA**

The Toubys’ second argument—that the Attorney General could not transfer temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator—is foreclosed by the plain text of the Controlled Substances Act. Congress expressly provided that “[a]ll powers and functions vested by this subchapter in the Attorney General” may be exercised by “such officers, employees, or agencies of the Department of Justice as the Attorney General may direct.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That is exactly what the Attorney General did: he issued 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), delegating to the DEA Administrator the functions vested in him under the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.

This case is therefore unlike *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 508–10 (1974), where the Court refused to permit subdelegation because Congress had specifically vested the power in “the Attorney General,” demonstrating an intent to confine the power to the officer himself. Here, Congress used a statute that expressly authorizes delegation. Courts have accordingly sustained the Attorney General’s delegation of CSA scheduling authority to the DEA. *United States v. Gordon*, 580 F.2d 827, 834 (5th Cir.), *cert. denied*, 440 U.S. 936 (1979).

The subdelegation did not enlarge the power Congress granted. The DEA Administrator exercised temporary scheduling under the same statutory standards, the same notice requirements, and the same durational limits that would have applied had the Attorney General acted personally. The regulation was a lawful exercise of the Attorney General’s statutory authority.

**Conclusion**

Congress supplied an intelligible principle--“imminent hazard to the public safety” plus specific factors--before delegating temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA followed Congress’s explicit grant in 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
