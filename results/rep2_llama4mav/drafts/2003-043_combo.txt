The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing the partners or obtaining a judgment against them is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961); see also Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960) (acknowledging that an individual partner is a separate taxpayer).

The government's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. As the Ninth Circuit held in Coson, an assessment against a partnership does not automatically create a lien against the property of individual partners. 286 F.2d at 462-63. The court in Coson relied on the fact that the assessment did not identify the individual partners, and therefore did not meet the requirements for a valid assessment under § 6203. Id. at 458; see also United States v. O'Connor, 291 F.2d 520 (2d Cir. 1961) (holding that an assessment against a partnership did not create a lien against the property of individual partners).

Other courts have reached similar conclusions. In United States v. Kaufman, 267 F. Supp. 351 (S.D.N.Y. 1967), the court held that the government could not collect a tax debt from individual partners without first obtaining a judgment against them. The court relied on the fact that the partners were separate "taxpayers" under the Code, and that the assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. Id. at 354; see also United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995) (distinguishable because the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners).

The government's reliance on state partnership law is also misplaced. While state law may make partners jointly and severally liable for the debts of the partnership, it does not permit the government to collect a tax debt from individual partners without first obtaining a judgment against them. As the Ninth Circuit held, under California law, a creditor must first obtain a judgment against a partner before collecting a partnership debt from the partner's assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); see also United States v. Hack, 33 F. Supp. 496 (D. Cal. 1940) (holding that a partner's liability for a partnership tax debt is not automatically enforceable against the partner's assets).

The government's argument is also inconsistent with the principles established in United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), and United States v. Motsinger, 123 F.2d 585 (4th Cir. 1941). In Updike, the Supreme Court held that the government's ability to collect a tax debt from a transferee of a taxpayer's assets was limited by the statute of limitations. 281 U.S. at 494. Similarly, in Motsinger, the Fourth Circuit held that the government could not collect a tax debt from a partner without first assessing the partner or obtaining a judgment against them. 123 F.2d at 586; see also Commissioner v. Stern, 357 U.S. 39 (1958) (holding that a transferee's liability for a taxpayer's tax debt is determined by state law).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the government must assess individual partners or obtain a judgment against them before collecting a partnership tax debt from the partners' assets. The government's failure to do so in this case renders its claim against the respondents unenforceable, and the bankruptcy court's disallowance of the claim was proper. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
