The District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. Under Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a class action may be maintained only if "the court finds that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating the controversy." The predominance requirement is not met here because the District Court failed to ensure that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.

The need for a class-wide damages methodology is well-established. As the Supreme Court held in Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, damages "must be capable of measurement on a classwide basis" to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3). 133 S. Ct. 1426, 1433 (2013) (citing Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 311). The Court in Hydrogen Peroxide emphasized that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" to determine whether the plaintiffs have met their burden of proof on the predominance requirement. 552 F.3d at 318. This includes examining the methodology for calculating damages to ensure it is "capable of measurement on a classwide basis." Id. at 311.

The District Court's acceptance of Dr. McClave's damages model was flawed because it failed to account for the various theories of antitrust impact alleged by the plaintiffs. As the Supreme Court noted in Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, a damages model must be tied to the specific theory of liability. 133 S. Ct. at 1434. Here, the District Court narrowed the class's theories of impact to a single theory, but Dr. McClave's model was not adjusted accordingly. This failure to match the damages model to the theory of liability renders the model unreliable for class-wide damages calculation.

Furthermore, the District Court's decision to certify the class despite the lack of a reliable damages model is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156 (1974). In Eisen, the Court held that a class action may not be maintained under Rule 23(b)(3) if individualized damages calculations are required. Id. at 164. The need for individualized damages calculations is precisely the problem here, as Dr. McClave's model is not capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis.

Id. at 28. Similarly, in McLaughlin v. Am. Tobacco Co., 522 F.3d 215 (2d Cir. 2008), the Second Circuit emphasized that a class action may not be maintained under Rule 23(b)(3) if individualized damages calculations are required. Id. at 231.

In Newton, the court held that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" to determine whether the plaintiffs have met their burden of proof on the predominance requirement. Id. at 166. This includes examining the methodology for calculating damages to ensure it is reliable and capable of measurement on a class-wide basis.

A class-wide damages methodology must be tied to a well-defined geographic market.
