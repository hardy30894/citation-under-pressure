**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B), DOES NOT APPLY RETROACTIVELY TO REINSTATE DEPORTATION ORDERS ISSUED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT, AND ITS APPLICATION HERE IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE**

The central question in this case is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), intended for Section 241(a)(5)—now codified as 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B)—to apply to aliens who re-entered the United States *before* the statute’s effective date in 1997. The Tenth Circuit held that it does, and that this application is not impermissibly retroactive. That conclusion is contrary to settled principles of statutory construction and constitutional law.

Congress enacted the reinstatement statute as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), which became effective on April 1, 1997. The statute provides that “[i]f an alien who has been ordered removed… reenters the United States… the prior order of removal shall be reinstated without further hearing.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B). The provision was designed to streamline removal proceedings for certain repeat offenders and to deter unlawful re-entry. But it does not, by its terms, purport to apply to orders issued before its enactment.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a statute is presumed not to have retroactive effect unless Congress clearly expresses such an intent.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994). This presumption is especially strong when the statute “alters the legal consequences of past conduct.” *Id.* at 265. Here, the reinstatement statute fundamentally changes the legal consequences of an alien’s prior unlawful re-entry—conduct that occurred in 1982, long before the statute existed. At that time, Fernandez-Vargas was subject to a deportation order issued in 1981. Under the law then in effect, he could have applied for relief, including adjustment of status, if he had met the statutory requirements. The fact that he later married a U.S. citizen and applied for permanent residence in 2001 demonstrates that he reasonably believed he had a chance to remain in the country legally.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Congress intended the statute to apply retroactively rests on a strained interpretation of legislative history and a flawed understanding of the statute’s purpose. The court relied on the fact that the statute “denies relief” to aliens who re-enter after deportation. But that is not a basis for retroactivity. The denial of relief is a *consequence* of the statute’s operation, not a *manifestation* of congressional intent to apply it to pre-enactment conduct. As the Court emphasized in *Landgraf*, “the mere fact that a statute alters the legal consequences of past conduct does not justify retroactive application absent clear congressional direction.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly held that “a statute that imposes new burdens or penalties on conduct that occurred before its enactment is impermissibly retroactive.” *United States v. Carlton*, 512 U.S. 26, 31 (1994). Here, the reinstatement statute imposes a new, harsh penalty—automatic deportation without a hearing—on conduct (re-entry in 1982) that was not subject to such a penalty at the time. The alien had no notice that his re-entry would trigger a permanent bar to relief. He had no “protectable expectation” of being able to adjust his status, the Tenth Circuit says, but that expectation was reasonable under the law as it existed in 1982. The fact that Congress later changed the law does not justify stripping an alien of a right he reasonably relied upon.

The Court has recognized that “the Constitution’s Due Process Clause prohibits the retroactive application of laws that impair rights established by prior law.” *Brentwood Academy v. Tennessee Secondary School Athletic Ass’n*, 531 U.S. 288, 295 (2001). In *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965), the Court held that “a criminal statute cannot be applied retroactively to punish conduct that was lawful when committed.” While this case involves civil immigration law, the principle remains the same: “the Constitution forbids the imposition of new legal consequences on past conduct without fair warning.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270.

Fernandez-Vargas did not commit any new offense in 2001. He applied for adjustment of status under a law that permitted such relief. The fact that the reinstatement statute now bars that relief does not make the prior conduct unlawful or justify the application of a new penalty. To hold otherwise would allow Congress to retroactively strip an alien of a right he reasonably expected to have, in violation of due process.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *Canales-Vargas* is misplaced. That case involved a different factual and legal context—specifically, the application of asylum law to threats made nearly fifteen years prior. The court in *Canales-Vargas* did not address the retroactive application of a statutory provision that alters the legal consequences of past conduct. Its discussion of “age of threats” is irrelevant here, where the issue is not the credibility of a fear of persecution, but the constitutionality of applying a new law to conduct that occurred before the law existed.

**II. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, AS APPLIED HERE, VIOLATES DUE PROCESS BY DENYING FERNANDEZ-VARGAS A FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO SEEK RELIEF**

The reinstatement statute, as applied to Fernandez-Vargas, denies him a meaningful opportunity to seek relief. He applied for adjustment of status in good faith, relying on the law as it existed at the time. The statute now bars that relief, not because he failed to meet a statutory standard, but because he re-entered the country before the statute was enacted.
