# ARGUMENT

## I. The Statute of Limitations for Collecting Unpaid Taxes From a Person Requires an Assessment or Judgment Against That Person.

Congress structured the assessment and collection provisions of the Internal Revenue Code around the individual "taxpayer." Section 6501(a) requires the government to assess a tax "against the taxpayer" within three years of the filing of a return, and only a timely assessment extends the collection period to ten years under § 6502(a). A "taxpayer" is statutorily defined as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and "person" separately includes both a "partnership" and an "individual." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14). Because a partnership and its partners are distinct statutory "taxpayers," an assessment against one is not, by operation of law, an assessment against the other.

This Court's decisions confirm that procedural prerequisites to collection—assessment, notice, judgment—are not mere formalities but jurisdictional predicates to the government's collection remedies. The Ninth Circuit's own precedent illustrates the point. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the court reviewed a district court holding that a federal tax lien could not attach to a partner's individual property where the assessment named only the partnership and "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers." *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (quoting *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 675 (S.D. Cal. 1958)). Although the Ninth Circuit ultimately affirmed on a different, narrower ground, it did not disturb the district court's core recognition that "such a lien does not exist against a particular individual's property pursuant to §§ 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203." *Coson*, 169 F. Supp. at 676. The same logic governs here: without an assessment identifying Petitioners individually, no extended ten-year collection period arose as to them.

*Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not aid the government. There, a partner who had *voluntarily paid* an assessed partnership tax sought a refund on the theory that he personally had never been assessed. The court held only that a person who is substantively liable for a debt under state law "may not recover a refund of taxes he paid because ... the assessment did not name him." *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959), *aff'd*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). *Young* never held that the government could have *compelled* payment absent an individual assessment or judgment; indeed, the district court expressly acknowledged that to collect from an unnamed partner, the IRS must "resort to court action" or assess the individual. *Id.* at 76,050. Respondents here never voluntarily paid; they contested liability precisely because no individual assessment or judgment was ever obtained.

## II. Decisions From Other Circuits Confirm That an Assessment Against the Entity Does Not Substitute for Individual Process Against the Partners.

The Seventh Circuit's decision in *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), on which the government relies, is fully consistent with Respondents' position, not the government's. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed *both* the partnership and the individual partners, extending the six-year limitations period as to both. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the partners' limitations period was tolled during the partnership's bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562–63. *Wright* thus presupposes—rather than dispenses with—the necessity of an individual assessment; it never addresses whether an assessment against the partnership alone suffices to bind the partners.

Similarly, the Tenth Circuit's decision in *Goldston v. United States (In re Goldston)*, 104 F.3d 1198 (10th Cir. 1997), confirms that absent a timely assessment, the government's only alternative is to bring a collection action within the basic three-year period of § 6501(a). No such action was brought here against Respondents individually, and the three-year window has long since closed.

The government's citation to *United States v. Western Products, Ltd.*, 168 F. Supp. 2d 84, 91 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), fares no better, for that case rested on New York partnership law, which is materially different from California's partnership statute. Under California law, "[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner," and a partnership judgment "may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). No such judgment—federal or state—was ever obtained against Respondents.

## III. Standard Bankruptcy Principles Confirm That the Government's Claims Were Properly Disallowed.

Under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), a bankruptcy court must disallow any claim that is "unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor" under applicable law. Consistent with *Neilson v. Chang (In re First T.D. & Inv., Inc.)*, 253 F.3d 520, 526 (9th Cir. 2001), and *In re Gruntz*, 202 F.3d 1074, 1084 n.9 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc), the bankruptcy court's legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, and the underlying question—whether an enforceable claim exists against Respondents individually—is purely legal. Because the government obtained no timely assessment or judgment against Respondents personally, *see In re Chang*, 163 F.3d 1138, 1140 (9th Cir. 1998) (reviewing legal conclusions de novo), its claims were correctly disallowed.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
