**Argument**

**I. The Prison Litigation Reform Act Does Not Prohibit a Court from Ordering Population Reductions to Remedy Eighth Amendment Violations.**

The PLRA was enacted to curb frivolous prisoner suits, not to prevent courts from remedying constitutional violations. 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(e). The statute explicitly states that it "shall not apply to any action brought by a prisoner to challenge the constitutionality of conditions of confinement." *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978). The population reduction order at issue here is a direct challenge to the unconstitutional conditions of confinement. Furthermore, the PLRA’s exhaustion requirement does not apply to injunctive relief. *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343, 351 (1996). The State’s argument that the PLRA bars this order is foreclosed by these clear statutory limits.

**II. The State Has Waived Any Eleventh Amendment Immunity and Exhaustion Defenses by Consenting to the Suit and the Receivership.**

The State has waived any Eleventh Amendment immunity and exhaustion defenses by consenting to the jurisdiction of the district court. The State did not appeal the order appointing the Receiver and joined motions to modify the original stipulated orders. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 3589 F.3d 3589, 3591 (9th Cir. 2009). By consenting to the receivership and the underlying litigation, the State relinquished its ability to invoke immunity or procedural defenses that would otherwise bar the court’s remedial authority.

**III. The Population Reduction Order Is a Necessary Remedy for the Eighth Amendment Violation.**

The order is necessary to prevent the Eighth Amendment violation of cruel and unusual punishment. The district court found that serious overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the violations. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 3589 F.3d at 3590. Overcrowding leads to inadequate medical care and increased suicide risks, which constitute deliberate indifference to serious medical needs. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976). Reducing the inmate population to 137.5 percent of design capacity is a reasonable remedy to alleviate these conditions. *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987). The State’s refusal to comply with this order would perpetuate the constitutional violations.
