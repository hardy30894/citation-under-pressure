The order below should be affirmed because the Prison Litigation Reform Act does not disable a federal court from entering population reduction relief when, as here, overcrowding is the primary cause of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and narrower measures have failed. The Constitution supplies the duty; the PLRA supplies the remedial limits. It does not require a court to leave unconstitutional prison conditions in place. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011); Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978); Bounds v. Smith, 430 U.S. 817 (1977).

First, federal courts have long possessed equitable authority to remedy unconstitutional prison conditions. In Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), the Court approved systemic relief addressing unconstitutional confinement conditions and explained that the scope of equitable remedies is shaped by the nature of the violation. In Bounds v. Smith, 430 U.S. 817 (1977), the Court likewise recognized that prisoners retain constitutional rights that courts may enforce through appropriate injunctive relief. And in Johnson v. Avery, 393 U.S. 483 (1969), the Court invalidated a prison regulation that effectively blocked habeas access, demonstrating that prison administration must yield to constitutional necessity. These cases confirm that when prison conditions violate the Constitution, federal courts may fashion effective remedies.

Second, population reduction orders are especially appropriate where overcrowding is the driving cause of the constitutional violation. In Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011), the Court held that California’s overcrowding problem was “the primary cause” of its Eighth Amendment violations and approved a population cap because lesser remedies had failed. The same principle appears in earlier cases recognizing that prison administration cannot justify unconstitutional treatment. United States v. Muniz, 374 U.S. 150 (1963), and United States v. Jones, 345 U.S. 377 (1953), both reflect the longstanding understanding that incarceration does not strip prisoners of all legal protections. The courts may order meaningful relief when prison conditions become unlawful.

Third, the PLRA itself authorizes the remedy ordered here. The statute permits prospective relief that is “narrowly drawn,” “extends no further than necessary,” and is “the least intrusive means necessary” to correct the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). A three-judge court may order prisoner release if it finds by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the primary cause of the violation and that no other relief will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). That is precisely what occurred in Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011). The relief therefore fits the PLRA’s text rather than violating it.

The State’s contrary position would read the PLRA as a shield for ongoing constitutional violations. But this Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that prison law makes constitutional rights illusory. In Houghton v. Shafer, 392 U.S. 639 (1968), the Court reversed dismissal of a prisoner’s civil-rights claim concerning legal materials. In Sweeney v. Woodall, 344 U.S. 86 (1953), the Court addressed a prisoner's Eighth Amendment challenge to conditions of confinement and did not suggest that incarceration eliminated judicial power to prevent cruel and unusual punishment. In Ponzi v. Fessenden, 258 U.S. 254 (1922), the Court emphasized that custody and administration rest on sovereign authority, but not beyond judicially enforceable limits. And in Finley v. California, 222 U.S. 28 (1911), the Court recognized that the Legislature may classify prisoners differently, but only within constitutional bounds.

Nor does the PLRA require courts to defer indefinitely to officials who have proven unable to cure the violation. In Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011), the Court emphasized that California had failed to comply with prior orders for years. Similar remedial persistence is reflected in Alabama v. Arizona, 291 U.S. 286 (1934), where the Court recognized the significance of prison conditions in federal judicial proceedings, and in Nugent v. Arizona Improvement Co., 173 U.S. 338 (1899), which reflects the historic supervisory role of law over prison administration. The judiciary’s task is not to manage prisons, but to ensure that unconstitutional conditions do not persist.

Finally, the State’s Eleventh Amendment and federalism objections do not defeat the order. Federal courts may impose relief against state officials to end ongoing constitutional violations. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908). The PLRA narrowed remedial discretion, but did not eliminate it. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011), confirms that population reduction is permissible when, as here, the statutory prerequisites are met. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
