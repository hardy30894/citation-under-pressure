# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether the Mouas Validly Waived Their Three-Day Rescission Rights

Summary judgment is proper only where there is no dispute that could lead a reasonable factfinder to rule for the nonmoving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247–48 (1986) (summary judgment requires "no genuine issue of material fact"). Once the moving party carries its initial burden, the nonmovant need only point to evidence "sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party's case." *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). Here, the record contains directly conflicting accounts of how and why the Mouas came to sign a handwritten waiver of the three-day advance HOEPA disclosure period, and the district court resolved that conflict against the Mouas rather than submitting it to a jury.

Regulation Z permits a consumer to waive or modify the three-day waiting period only where the consumer has "a bona fide personal financial emergency" that necessitates consummation before the waiting period expires. 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(e)(2). Because this waiver provision derogates from TILA's core consumer protection, courts construe it narrowly and against the creditor asserting it. *Semar v. Platte Valley Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass'n*, 791 F.2d 699, 705 (9th Cir. 1986) (waiver provisions must be strictly construed to prevent circumvention of the statute's protections). Manisy Moua testified she was instructed to copy, in her own hand, a statement she did not understand and whose purpose was never explained to her. That is not the considered invocation of an emergency exception; it is precisely the rote, lender-drafted "waiver" the regulation was designed to prevent. Whether a genuine financial emergency existed—as opposed to Rand's own scheduling convenience in avoiding the redemption deadline—is a disputed question of fact, not a matter for resolution on summary judgment. See *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 249 (credibility determinations and weighing of evidence are jury functions).

## II. Rand Failed to Provide Clear and Conspicuous Notice of the Right to Cancel, Extending the Rescission Period to Three Years

TILA requires that the notice of the right to rescind be "clear and conspicuous." 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a); 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(b). Courts have long recognized that TILA is a remedial statute to be construed liberally in favor of the consumer it was designed to protect. *Rudisell v. Fifth Third Bank*, 622 F.2d 243, 253 (6th Cir. 1980) (TILA "is a remedial statute" entitled to liberal construction on behalf of borrowers). A notice cannot be "clear and conspicuous" when the lender's own closing agent simultaneously procures execution of a "Confirmation" section falsely certifying that "[m]ore than 3 business days have elapsed" and that "the new transaction has not been rescinded"—on the very day of closing, before the rescission period had even begun to run. Such conduct affirmatively undermines, rather than clarifies, the borrower's understanding of the cancellation right. *Cf. Gerasta v. Hibernia Nat'l Bank*, 574 F.2d 249, 259–60 (5th Cir. 1978) (disclosures must be reasonably understandable and not misleading in context).

Rand attempts to disclaim responsibility by asserting that its title agent, not Rand itself, directed the Mouas to execute the premature Confirmation. That argument does not eliminate the factual dispute; it merely shifts it to whether Excel Title acted as Rand's agent in conducting the closing Rand itself arranged. A lender cannot insulate itself from TILA's clear-and-conspicuous-notice requirement by delegating the closing to a title company and then disclaiming the predictable consequences of that company's conduct. See *Handy v. Anchor Mortg. Corp.*, 464 F.3d 760, 764–65 (7th Cir. 2006) (assessing whether closing-table conduct rendered the required notice confusing or misleading to the borrower). At minimum, the premature Confirmation raises a triable issue as to whether the Notice actually and effectively conveyed the three-day right, precluding summary judgment.

## III. The Prepayment Penalty Prohibited by HOEPA Independently Extends the Rescission Period to Three Years

HOEPA expressly forbids prepayment penalties on covered "high-cost" mortgages except in narrow circumstances not present here. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(c). A violation of HOEPA's substantive disclosure and structural requirements is a material TILA violation that extends the rescission window from three days to three years after consummation. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(f); *Beach v. Ocwen Fed. Bank*, 523 U.S. 410, 412 (1998) (recognizing that § 1635(f) operates as an "absolute limit" but that within that limit, material nondisclosure preserves the extended right to rescind). The Mouas' note imposed an increasing-rate structure paired with penalty terms that Rand has never shown complied with § 1639(c)'s narrow exceptions. Because that determination turns on the terms actually disclosed—a disputed fact—the district court's grant of summary judgment on this ground was likewise premature. See *Williams v. Homestake Mortg. Co.*, 968 F.2d 1137, 1140–41 (11th Cir. 1992) (material HOEPA/TILA disclosure defects present questions inappropriate for summary disposition where the adequacy of disclosure is contested).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Mouas respectfully request that this Court reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for trial.
