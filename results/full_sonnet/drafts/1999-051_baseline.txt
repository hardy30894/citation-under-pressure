**ARGUMENT**

**A Habeas Petitioner's Ineffective-Assistance Claim May Serve as Cause for a Procedural Default Even Though That Claim Was Itself Rejected on State Procedural Grounds**

This Court has long recognized that "cause" excusing a procedural default may be established by showing that appellate counsel's constitutionally deficient performance prevented a claim from being raised on direct appeal. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). The rationale is straightforward: because "the right to effective assistance of counsel . . . may in a particular case be violated by even an isolated error," counsel's ineffectiveness is "constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel" and, once established, "is cause for a procedural default." *Id.* at 488, 494. This Court has never suggested that the ineffective-assistance claim asserted as cause must itself have escaped every state procedural bar. It has required only that the claim be "presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause." *Id.* at 489.

Carpenter met that requirement. He presented his ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim to the Ohio Court of Appeals through an application to reopen his direct appeal, and he sought further review in the Ohio Supreme Court. That the state courts declined to reach the merits because they deemed the application untimely does not mean the claim was never "presented." The exhaustion doctrine "requires only that state prisoners give state courts a fair opportunity to act on their claims," *O'Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 844 (1999), and this Court has consistently distinguished the question whether a claim was fairly presented from the question whether the state court, once presented with it, chose to adjudicate it. *See Castille v. Peoples*, 489 U.S. 346, 351 (1989) (fair presentation requires only that "the substance" of the claim be presented). Carpenter's claim was fairly presented; the Ohio courts simply resolved it against him on procedural grounds, just as a federal habeas claim can be resolved against a petitioner on procedural default grounds even though it, too, was "presented."

**Petitioner's Rule Would Require an Endless Chain of Cause-for-Cause Showings and Would Gut the Purpose of the Cause-and-Prejudice Standard**

The premise underlying the cause-and-prejudice exception is that a habeas petitioner should not be charged with the default of a claim he was legally and practically unable to raise. *See Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1, 13 (1984) (recognizing that "it is asking too much of the same trial counsel who failed to perceive" a constitutional claim "to expect him to raise the issue on appeal"). Ineffective assistance of appellate counsel is the paradigmatic instance of an "objective factor external to the defense" that "impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule." *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488. That is precisely why this Court held in *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 754 (1991), that "[a]ttorney ignorance or inadvertence is not 'cause'" unless it amounts to constitutionally deficient performance—but where it does, "[i]neffective assistance of counsel . . . is cause for a procedural default."

Petitioner's position—that the ineffective-assistance claim asserted as cause must itself survive an independent cause-and-prejudice inquiry—would convert a doctrine designed to excuse defaults attributable to constitutionally deficient counsel into a doctrine that punishes petitioners for the very same deficiency. If a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause unless the petitioner shows cause for *that* default, and if the showing of *that* cause in turn requires yet another layer of exhausted, non-defaulted constitutional violation, habeas petitioners will be trapped in an infinite regress that this Court's precedents do not contemplate. Nothing in *Murray*, *Carrier*, or *Coleman* imposes such a requirement; each speaks of the ineffective-assistance claim being "presented," not of its surviving state procedural bars intact. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489.

**The Sixth Circuit Correctly Found Cause and Prejudice on These Facts**

Applying these principles, the Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim—having been presented to, and rejected on procedural grounds by, the Ohio courts—could nonetheless serve as cause for the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. Carpenter's appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting his aggravated murder and aggravated robbery convictions, an omission that satisfies *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), because it fell "below an objective standard of reasonableness" and because "there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." The Sixth Circuit's finding of prejudice from that omission—an evaluation this Court should not disturb—confirms that Carpenter's default of the sufficiency claim was directly attributable to constitutionally deficient representation, not to any strategic choice or lack of diligence on his part.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
