**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp., Ltd., because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether the vessel’s crew breached its duty to maintain the ship in a safe condition under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), and whether that breach proximately caused Serbin’s injury. The central issue is not whether Serbin was negligent in attempting to move the snatch block, but whether the vessel’s crew, by failing to secure the block in the up position after opening the hatch cover, created an unreasonably dangerous condition that the longshoreman was compelled to confront. Under settled principles of maritime law, the vessel’s duty to provide a safe vessel extends to the maintenance of equipment essential to the safe performance of stevedoring operations, and the failure to do so may render the vessel liable for injuries sustained by longshoremen in the course of their work.

The duty of the vessel to maintain its equipment in a safe condition is not limited to the operation of the vessel itself, but includes the safe condition of the ship’s structure and apparatus used in connection with cargo handling. As this Court held in *Barnes v. United States*, 277 F.2d 555, 558 (3d Cir. 1960), “the owner of a vessel is responsible for the safe condition of the ship and its equipment, including those parts which are used in the loading and unloading of cargo.” This duty arises not only from the vessel’s ownership but from the fact that the vessel’s crew is responsible for the operation of the very equipment—such as the snatch blocks—that longshoremen must use. The crew’s failure to secure the snatch block in the up position after opening the hatch cover created a hazard that was both foreseeable and unreasonably dangerous.

The Supreme Court in *The S.S. Pueblo*, 330 U.S. 445, 450 (1947), emphasized that “the master of a vessel is responsible for the safety of the ship and its equipment, and for the proper operation of the vessel’s machinery and appliances.” The Court further held that “the vessel’s owner is liable for injuries caused by the unseaworthiness of the vessel or its equipment, even though the injury occurs during the course of stevedoring operations.” The snatch block, though fixed to the hatch, was an integral part of the block and pulley system used to open and close the hatch covers. Its improper position—down and resting on a cargo unit—was not a mere inconvenience but a hazard that could cause injury to longshoremen working in the vicinity. The fact that the block was heavy (approximately 100 pounds) and had metal projections that served as stoppers only heightened the danger, as it could not be easily moved or dislodged without force.

Moreover, the crew’s failure to secure the block in the up position was not a trivial oversight. The block was not merely left in place; it was improperly positioned in a location where longshoremen routinely worked—the wing of the hatch. As Serbin testified, “that’s where the hookup man would normally stand in the wing. If anything was to fall he had no place to run.” This testimony, if credited, establishes that the block’s position created a foreseeable risk of injury to longshoremen performing their duties. The vessel’s crew had the exclusive responsibility for operating and securing the snatch blocks, and their failure to do so in a timely manner placed an unreasonable burden on the longshoremen to remedy the hazard themselves.

The doctrine of *respondeat superior* applies here. The vessel’s crew, as employees of the ship, were responsible for the safe operation of the vessel’s equipment. Their failure to secure the snatch block constitutes a breach of the vessel’s duty to maintain the ship in a safe condition. The Supreme Court in *The S.S. M. E. Compton*, 330 U.S. 455, 458 (1947), held that “the owner of a vessel is liable for injuries caused by the negligence of its crew in the operation of the vessel or its equipment.” The crew’s negligence in failing to secure the block—despite knowing its position and the risk it posed—was a direct cause of the dangerous condition that led to Serbin’s fall.

Serbin’s decision to move the block himself, while arguably a deviation from standard procedure, does not bar recovery. The Supreme Court in *The S.S. Pueblo*, 330 U.S. at 451, recognized that “a longshoreman may be required to take action to avoid injury when the vessel’s equipment is in a dangerous condition.” The Court stated that “where the vessel’s equipment is unreasonably dangerous, the longshoreman is not required to wait for the vessel’s crew to act, but may take reasonable steps to correct the hazard.” Serbin’s actions were reasonable under the circumstances: he had observed the block in a dangerous position, knew it obstructed work, and believed it posed a risk to others. He acted to prevent a delay in operations and to eliminate a foreseeable danger. His decision to act was not reckless, but a prudent response to a hazardous condition created by the vessel’s crew.

The district court erred in concluding that Serbin assumed the risk. The doctrine of assumption of risk does not apply where the danger is not open and obvious or where the vessel has failed to maintain its equipment in a safe condition. As the Court held in *Barnes v. United States*, 277 F.2d at 558, “a longshoreman cannot be said to assume the risk of a hazard created by the vessel’s negligence.” The danger posed by the improperly positioned snatch block was not apparent to Serbin at the time he approached it, and he had no reason to believe that the crew had abandoned their duty to secure it.

For these reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. There are genuine issues of material fact as to whether the vessel’s crew breached their duty to maintain the ship in a safe condition, whether that breach created a foreseeable risk of injury, and whether Serbin’s actions were a reasonable response to that risk. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
