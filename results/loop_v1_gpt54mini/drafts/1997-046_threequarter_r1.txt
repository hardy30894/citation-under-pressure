Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected life or liberty interest, and a voluntary interview does not compel self-incrimination.

First, an inmate has no freestanding constitutional entitlement to clemency. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that pardon and commutation are committed to executive discretion, not judicially enforceable right. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380–81 (1866); *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128, 147–48 (1872). Because clemency is an act of grace, not of right, it does not itself create a protected liberty interest. *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486–87 (1927); *Schick v. Reed*, 419 U.S. 256, 266 (1974). The Court has likewise explained that pardon and commutation decisions “have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). Although *Dumschat* postdates 1970, it reflects longstanding doctrine that clemency is an executive function. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81; *Biddle*, 274 U.S. at 486–87.

Second, the Constitution does not require trial-like due process protections in clemency proceedings. The Due Process Clause protects only interests recognized by law, not every hope or expectation. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 569–71 (1972); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 599–603 (1972). Before those cases, the Court already made clear that procedural protections arise only where the government has imposed legal constraints sufficient to create a right, not where it retains discretion. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886); *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473 v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895–99 (1961). Here, Ohio’s clemency scheme leaves the Governor free to grant or deny relief as he sees fit. That discretion defeats any claim of a constitutionally protected liberty interest in the process itself.

Third, the voluntary interview option does not violate the Fifth Amendment. The Self-Incrimination Clause prohibits compulsion, not every adverse consequence that may follow a person’s silence. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 67–68 (1906); *Mann v. People of State of Illinois*, 292 U.S. 602, 604–05 (1934). A prisoner may choose whether to speak, and the Constitution is not offended merely because silence may make clemency less likely. That is especially true where the interview is optional and the prisoner may present written materials instead. The Court has long held that a witness may be required to choose between silence and disadvantage without a constitutional violation unless the government actually compels testimony. *McCarthy v. Arndstein*, 266 U.S. 34, 40–41 (1924); *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943).

Nor does the possibility that a prisoner might voluntarily say something incriminating transform the process into unconstitutional compulsion. The privilege protects against being forced to testify against oneself; it does not bar the government from offering a discretionary opportunity to speak. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598–605 (1896); *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562–64 (1892). And the Court has made clear that adverse inferences, standing alone, do not amount to compulsion in contexts far more coercive than a clemency interview. *United States ex rel. Vajtauer v. Commissioner of Immigration*, 273 U.S. 103, 106–07 (1927); *Maggio v. Zeitz*, 333 U.S. 56, 63–64 (1948). If a prisoner elects to participate in a voluntary interview, he is simply making a strategic choice whether to speak in aid of mercy.

Finally, petitioner’s theory would improperly constitutionalize a traditionally executive act. The Court has warned against converting discretionary governmental procedures into judicially enforceable entitlements absent clear legal constraints. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers*, 367 U.S. at 895–99; *McAndrews v. State of North Carolina*, 154 U.S. 391, 399–400 (1894). Ohio’s clemency interview option is exactly the kind of discretionary, voluntary procedure that falls outside the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
