**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continuing majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may rebut that presumption with “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). When such doubt exists, the employer may withdraw recognition, petition for an RM election under 29 U.S.C. § 159(c)(1)(B), or conduct a poll. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1059-63 (1989).

The Board’s insistence that the evidentiary threshold for polling must equal the threshold for withdrawal of recognition renders polling “only marginally useful.” *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984). Three circuits have therefore held that an employer may poll if it possesses “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence would not justify withdrawal. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299. The Board’s contrary rule is arbitrary because it denies employers the very procedural safeguard the Act permits them to use to avoid unfair-labor-practice liability.

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked reasonable doubt is likewise unsupported by substantial evidence. Seven unit employees expressly repudiated the union. Three others—Ridgick, Marsh, and Bloch—made statements that, under settled Board precedent, constitute objective evidence of loss of support. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978); *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988). The Board’s refusal to credit these statements rested on nothing more than speculation about employee sincerity, contradicting its own cases. *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). The poll itself—conducted under the safeguards of *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), and supervised by a priest—produced a 19-13 vote against the union. That result confirms the objective doubt Allentown Mack possessed on January 25, 1991.

The Board’s order therefore rests on an impermissible legal standard and on a factual determination lacking substantial evidence in the record. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
