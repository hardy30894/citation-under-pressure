# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred by Instructing the Jury on "Shielding" Without Requiring Proof That Ye's Conduct Tended to Prevent Detection by Immigration Authorities.

This Court reviews de novo whether jury instructions fairly and accurately state the law, and reviews the district court's exercise of discretion in supplementing instructions for abuse of that discretion. *United States v. Woods*, 556 F.3d 616, 621 (7th Cir. 2009); *United States v. Curtis*, 324 F.3d 501, 503 (7th Cir. 2003). A supplemental instruction that permits a jury to convict on a standard broader than Congress intended cannot stand.

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) criminalizes concealing, harboring, or shielding an alien "from detection." Congress did not define "shielding," and the district court's bare-bones instruction—equating shielding with simply preventing discovery—collapsed the felony shielding offense into the misdemeanor hiring offense already charged in Count Two under 8 U.S.C. § 1324a(a)(1)(A). Every employer who fails to complete immigration paperwork "prevents discovery" of an illegal worker in some sense; if that alone satisfies "shielding," the felony provision adds nothing beyond the misdemeanor and criminalizes ordinary, unadorned employment.

Several circuits confronting this same statutory gap have declined to read "shielding" so broadly. The Second, Third, Ninth, and Eleventh Circuits have held that harboring-type conduct under § 1324 must tend to substantially facilitate an alien's remaining in the United States illegally *and* to prevent government authorities from detecting the alien's unlawful presence. *United States v. Ozcelik*, 527 F.3d 88, 100 (3d Cir. 2008); *United States v. Lopez*, 521 F.3d 1201, 1206 (9th Cir. 2008); *United States v. Zheng*, 306 F.3d 1080, 1085 (11th Cir. 2002); *United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567, 574 (2d Cir. 1999). This gloss traces to *United States v. Acosta de Evans*, 531 F.2d 428, 430 (9th Cir. 1976), which recognized that "harboring" language must be read to reach conduct that materially assists aliens in avoiding immigration enforcement, not conduct that merely coincides with an alien's illegal presence.

The district court's instruction ignored this consistent body of authority. It permitted the jury to convict on evidence showing only that Ye employed and housed workers he knew were undocumented—conduct indistinguishable from ordinary, if unlawful, employment—without requiring the jury to find that Ye's conduct was directed at preventing immigration authorities from detecting these workers. That omission is not harmless; it is the difference between the misdemeanor Congress created in § 1324a and the ten-year felony Congress created in § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii).

To the extent any ambiguity remains as to the scope of "shielding," that ambiguity must be resolved in Ye's favor. "The rule of lenity requires ambiguous criminal laws to be interpreted in favor of the defendants subjected to them." *United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008) (plurality op.). The district court's instruction resolved the statute's ambiguity against Ye, not in his favor, and the resulting conviction cannot stand.

## II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to Prevent the Government From Detecting His Illegal Alien Employees.

This Court asks "whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979); *United States v. Moses*, 513 F.3d 727, 733 (7th Cir. 2008). Even under that deferential standard, the record here does not permit a rational finding that Ye acted with the purpose of preventing government detection, as opposed to merely employing and housing workers in violation of the wage and hour and immigration-paperwork laws.

The evidence at trial showed that Ye readily answered agents' questions in April and August 2005, disclosed that he was leasing apartments for the Hispanic workers, and explained—truthfully—why he lacked Form I-9 documentation for them. He did not conceal workers during either government visit, did not instruct employees to hide, and did not lie about the number or location of his workforce. This is not the conduct of someone acting to shield employees from detection; it is the conduct of an employer who violated hiring and tax laws in plain view of the government he is accused of trying to evade.

Applying the standard adopted in *Ozcelik*, *Lopez*, *Zheng*, and *Kim*, this conduct falls short of shielding from detection because it did not tend to prevent immigration authorities from discovering the workers' presence—the government discovered them without difficulty, on two separate occasions, based on Ye's own candid disclosures. At most, the evidence establishes that Ye knowingly hired illegal aliens and failed to complete required paperwork, conduct fully captured by the misdemeanor conviction under § 1324a that Ye does not challenge here. Because the government failed to prove the distinct intent element required for the felony shielding offense, the conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed.
