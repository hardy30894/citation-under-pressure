The United States respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the district court's denial of its motion to dismiss Guam's CERCLA claim. The plain language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) dictates that Guam's 2004 consent decree with the EPA triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim, thereby precluding it from now pursuing a cost-recovery claim under § 107.

As this Court has recognized, CERCLA provides two avenues for entities to seek recoupment of cleanup costs from potentially responsible parties (PRPs): a § 107 "cost-recovery" action and a § 113(f) "contribution" action (Government of Guam v. United States, No. 19-5131, slip op. at 3 (D.C. Cir. Feb. 14, 2020)). The distinction between these two provisions is critical, as the statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, whereas the statute of limitations for a remedial cost-recovery action is six years (CERCLA § 113(g)(2)(B) and (g)(3)).

The key issue in this case is whether Guam's 2004 consent decree with the EPA "resolved [Guam's] liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action" within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B). If it did, then Guam's claim is time-barred as a contribution action, and the district court erred in allowing Guam to proceed under § 107.

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the 2004 consent decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B) (Government of Guam v. United States, No. 19-5131, slip op. at 16-20). The decree required Guam to take specific remedial actions, including closing the Ordot Dump and designing a cover system, which constitutes a "response action" under CERCLA (CERCLA § 101(24) and (25)). By agreeing to these terms, Guam effectively resolved its liability to the United States for the response action.

Guam's arguments to the contrary are unpersuasive. The decree's disclaimer of liability does not negate the fact that Guam agreed to undertake significant remedial actions, which is sufficient to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) (Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017)). Moreover, the fact that the EPA's complaint alleged Clean Water Act violations rather than CERCLA claims is irrelevant, as the consent decree's terms addressed CERCLA-defined "remedial actions" (CERCLA § 101(24)).

Furthermore, Guam's constitutional concerns regarding the alleged extinguishment of non-settling PRPs' contribution rights without due process are not properly before this Court, as Guam failed to raise them in the district court (Keepseagle v. Vilsack, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016)).

In conclusion, the D.C. Circuit correctly applied the plain language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) in holding that Guam's 2004 consent decree triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim. As a result, Guam's claim is time-barred, and the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss should be reversed.

Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), is not directly relevant to this case, but the principle that courts should apply the plain language of a statute is supported by Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958). The decisions in United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51 (1998), Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599 (2009), and Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157 (2004), provide guidance on the interpretation of CERCLA provisions. Additionally, the decisions in O'Neil v. Picillo, 883 F.2d 176 (1st Cir. 1989), Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996 (6th Cir. 2015), and Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108 (9th Cir. 2017), support the conclusion that a consent decree can trigger a PRP's right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B).
