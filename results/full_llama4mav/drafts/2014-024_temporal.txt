The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because the trial court's jury instruction on the standard for a "true threat" was erroneous. The instruction given to the jury stated that a statement is a true threat when a defendant intentionally makes a statement in a context or under circumstances that would lead a reasonable person to foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. However, this objective standard is inconsistent with the requirement that a defendant must have a subjective intent to threaten.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech, even if it is unpleasant or disturbing, unless it constitutes a true threat. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-310 (1940), the Court held that speech is not protected if it is "of such a character as to create a clear and present danger." However, the Court has also emphasized the importance of intent in determining whether speech is a true threat. In Frohwerk v. United States, 249 U.S. 204, 206 (1919), the Court noted that the defendant's intent was crucial in determining whether their speech was protected.

Furthermore, in Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616, 627-628 (1919) (dissenting opinion), Justice Holmes emphasized that the intent behind the speech is crucial in determining its constitutionality. Elonis' conviction was based on his Facebook posts, which were made in the context of his emotional distress following his separation from his wife. While the posts were certainly disturbing, they were not necessarily intended as threats.

The trial court's instruction to the jury, which relied on an objective standard, was therefore erroneous. The jury should have been instructed to consider whether Elonis subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. By failing to do so, the trial court effectively allowed the jury to convict Elonis based on a negligence standard, rather than a mens rea requirement.

In Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the Court held that the defendant's intent to cause harm was a necessary element of the crime. Similarly, in this case, the government should have been required to prove that Elonis subjectively intended to threaten others. The Third Circuit's decision affirming Elonis' conviction should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for a new trial with a jury instruction that requires a finding of subjective intent to threaten.
