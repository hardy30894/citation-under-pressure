**ARGUMENT**

**I. Exempt status is measured by the character of the asset when the bankruptcy petition is filed, not by what the asset once was in a predecessor's hands.**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds" held in certain tax-favored accounts. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That exemption is evaluated against the estate as it exists at the moment the petition is filed. This Court held long ago that a bankrupt's exemptions "are to be determined as of the date of the filing of the petition." *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924). The inquiry looks to the actual legal character of the property under governing law at that time—not to some earlier owner's use of it years before. *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903).

Measured at the proper moment, the account the Clarks seek to exempt bears none of the hallmarks of retirement savings. When Heidi Heffron-Clark filed for bankruptcy in 2010, the $300,000 she had inherited from her mother's IRA nine years earlier was already subject to a mandatory distribution schedule wholly unrelated to her own retirement. No new contributions could be made to it, and the balance could not be rolled over into any other account. Whatever role the funds played in Ruth Heffron's retirement planning during her life, they cannot retain that role once title passed to her daughter under rules that compel current withdrawal rather than continued deferral. The statute asks what the fund *is* for the debtor now, not what it *was* for someone else.

**II. Courts look to an asset's function, not its label, in deciding whether it serves the purpose an exemption protects.**

This Court has consistently refused to let a formal label substitute for substance. In *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465, 469 (1935), the Court asked "whether what was done, apart from the tax motive, was the thing which the statute intended," rather than accepting the parties' chosen characterization of a transaction. The word "IRA" in "inherited IRA" is exactly such a label: it identifies the *source* of the funds, not their present function. An account that must be drained on an accelerated schedule, with no possibility of deferral until the holder's own retirement, does not function as retirement savings simply because the Internal Revenue Code borrows the letters "IRA" to describe it.

This Court's bankruptcy jurisprudence confirms that substance controls over nomenclature. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966), the Court examined the practical operation of the disputed interest—rather than its formal characterization—in deciding whether it belonged to the estate. The same principle governed this Court's early insurance-exemption decisions, which tied exemption to whether the asset continued to serve its protective function for the debtor and his dependents, not merely to its name or origin. *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202, 213 (1905); *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459, 471 (1913). Here, the inherited IRA serves no ongoing retirement-protective function for the Clarks. From the moment Heidi inherited it, the account was a pool of money available for present use—precisely the sort of asset creditors are entitled to reach in bankruptcy.

**III. Treating an inherited IRA as exempt would defeat, not advance, the purpose of the bankruptcy exemption scheme.**

Exemptions exist to give the "honest but unfortunate debtor" a fresh start, not to immunize inherited windfalls from legitimate creditor claims. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (exemptions serve "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor who surrenders for distribution the property which he owns at the time of bankruptcy, a new opportunity in life"). That rationale supports protecting funds a debtor has personally set aside through years of contribution and forbearance toward his or her own retirement. It does not support shielding money the debtor never earned, never contributed to, and is legally required to spend down within a matter of years. Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would transform a narrow protection for self-funded retirement savings into a broad shelter for inheritances generally—a result neither the statutory text nor its purpose supports.

This Court has cautioned against literal readings that produce results the legislature could not have intended, directing courts instead to the "purpose of the act" as a whole. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). The purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is to protect retirement savings from creditors during a debtor's working life, not to convert any tax-deferred account, once inherited, into a permanently untouchable asset regardless of how it actually functions. Reading the exemption to cover the Clarks' inherited IRA would stretch the statute past its purpose and past its text, at the expense of the creditors the Bankruptcy Code is equally designed to protect.

**CONCLUSION**

The funds in Heidi Heffron-Clark's inherited IRA are not "retirement funds" within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). They are not held for the Clarks' retirement; they cannot be added to; and they must be distributed on a schedule tied to Ruth Heffron's death, not to Heidi's retirement. The judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
