**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Possesses No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings**

The clemency power is a pure act of grace, wholly discretionary and beyond judicial review. This Court has long recognized that the pardon power is “unlimited” except in cases of impeachment, *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120 (1925), and that a pardon “is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed.” *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. 150, 160 (1833). The clemency decision is “a determination of the executive that the public welfare will be better served by inflicting less than what the judgment fixed.” *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927). No inmate has a right or entitlement to that grace.

The Due Process Clause does not extend to such executive acts. Due process under the Fourteenth Amendment applies to judicial proceedings, not to the discretionary exercise of the pardoning power. *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 535 (1884) (due process “refers to that law which in the administration of justice . . . secures to the citizen protection against arbitrary action”). The clemency process is not a judicial proceeding; it is an executive function “exclusively” belonging to the sovereign. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. 333, 380 (1866). Because the state has not created any entitlement to clemency—and indeed the Ohio Constitution leaves the governor “almost unfettered discretion”—there is no life or liberty interest that triggers due process protection. *See Knote v. United States*, 95 U.S. 149, 153 (1877) (pardon does not restore rights lost by judgment; it is a matter of “grace”).

The Court of Appeals erred in importing due process requirements from *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), which involved a first appeal as of right—a judicial proceeding. Clemency is not an “integral part” of the adjudicatory system; it is a separate, extrajudicial act of mercy. The Constitution does not compel a state to provide any clemency procedure at all, and Ohio’s voluntary interview does not transform grace into a right.

**II. The Voluntary Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment**

The Fifth Amendment protects against “compelled” self-incrimination. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 114 (1908). The privilege is “not a right to refuse to answer any question, but only to refuse to answer those questions which would tend to incriminate.” *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 597 (1896). Compulsion requires some “threat of sanction” or penalty for silence. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892). Here, the inmate’s participation in the interview is entirely voluntary. The Ohio regulation explicitly states that the interview is “requested” by the prisoner; the inmate may decline without any adverse consequence. The clemency decision remains wholly discretionary, and the state may consider a prisoner’s silence or refusal to participate, but that is not compulsion—it is a permissible factor in a discretionary decision. *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609, 614 (1965), prohibits comment on silence at trial, but clemency is not a trial. *Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493, 497 (1967), held that statements coerced by threat of removal from office are involuntary, but here there is no threat—the inmate’s life is already forfeited by a valid judgment. The “risk” of self-incrimination is speculative. No actual questioning has occurred, and the regulation does not compel answers. The inmate remains free to say as much or as little as he wishes. The Fifth Amendment does not require the state to offer a “safe harbor” for voluntary statements in a non-custodial, non-coercive executive interview. *See Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964) (applying the privilege to the states, but only where compulsion exists).

The Court of Appeals’ remand for a “Hobson’s choice” analysis is premature. No interview has been conducted, and the district court cannot assess a hypothetical risk. *United States v. Burr*, 25 F. Cas. 30, 32 (C.C.D. Va. 1807) (Marshall, C.J.) (the privilege must be claimed in response to a specific question). Allowing litigation over a speculative Fifth Amendment claim would chill the state’s willingness to offer a voluntary interview, a procedure that benefits inmates by providing a personal hearing.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s dismissal of the complaint should be affirmed.
