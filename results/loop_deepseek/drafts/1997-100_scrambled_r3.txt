ARGUMENT

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE PRECLUDES RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION ONCE A COURT HAS FINALLY DETERMINED THAT THE EVIDENCE IS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT IT.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, protects a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy” for the same offense. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969). This protection is not limited to proceedings on the substantive charges; it extends to any stage at which the State seeks to prove facts that increase the punishment for a crime. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 134 (1904) (the Constitution protects against “a second trial for the same offense, whether the first trial resulted in a conviction or an acquittal”). A prior conviction allegation is such a fact: it exposes the defendant to a doubled sentence and an additional one-year enhancement. When the State has failed to prove that allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, and an appellate court has finally reversed the true finding for insufficiency of the evidence, that determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal. Retrial would place the defendant in jeopardy a second time for the same issue, in direct violation of the Clause.

The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that once a defendant is acquitted—whether by jury or by a court on review of the evidence—the Double Jeopardy Clause absolutely bars any further prosecution for the same offense. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896) (the accused, whether convicted or acquitted, is equally put in jeopardy at the first trial); *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. at 134; *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957) (the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits a second trial for the same offense, whether the first trial ended in an acquittal or a conviction). An appellate court’s reversal for insufficient evidence is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes; it is a final determination that the State failed to prove its case. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. Just as the State may not retry a defendant on a substantive charge after an appellate court has found the evidence insufficient, it may not retry a sentencing allegation after the same determination.

The Clause also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. at 187. A prior conviction allegation is not a new crime, but it is a “fact” that, when proved, authorizes a greater punishment. If the State could relitigate that fact after an insufficiency finding, the defendant would be forced to endure the risk of receiving a sentence that is, in effect, a second punishment for conduct that has already been determined not to support enhanced consequences. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not permit the State to “try again” to obtain a harsher sentence after it has once failed to prove the necessary facts. *United States v. Sanges*, 144 U.S. 310, 312 (1892) (holding that the government cannot relitigate a criminal judgment entered in favor of the defendant because of double jeopardy principles).

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that double jeopardy protections do not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause draws no such distinction. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. at 134 (applying double jeopardy to bar a second trial without regard to the severity of the punishment). In *Kepner*, the Court emphasized that the Clause’s protections are “not a matter of form, but of substance.” *Id.* The same principle applies here: the State may not seek an enhanced punishment after it has been finally determined that the enhancement is unsupported.

The California Legislature has chosen to provide a trial-like proceeding for prior conviction allegations, including a jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the right to confront witnesses. Those procedural protections transform the sentencing hearing into a “trial” on the enhancement issue. When the State invokes that mechanism, it must accept the consequences of an adverse verdict. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not permit the State to have a second chance to prove a fact simply because the proceeding is labeled “sentencing” rather than “guilt.” *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 134.

Petitioner Monge was subjected to a full trial on the prior conviction allegation. The trial court found the allegation true, but the Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon—a necessary element of the “serious felony” enhancement. That reversal was a final judgment of acquittal on the enhancement issue. The State may not retry that allegation. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars the door.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
