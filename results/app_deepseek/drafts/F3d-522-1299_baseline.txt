# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred by Conflating Claim Construction with the Written Description Inquiry

The district court committed reversible error when it relied on the 2000 CIP Application's new language to construe "customer interface" broadly and then used that same new language to deny priority under § 120. This analytical approach improperly permitted claim construction to drive the written description analysis. The proper inquiry is whether the Original Application "reasonably conveys to those skilled in the art that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter as of the filing date." *Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar*, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563-64 (Fed. Cir. 1991). This is a question of fact requiring consideration of all evidence, not merely the words used in the earlier application. *Tronzo v. Biomet, Inc.*, 156 F.3d 1154, 1158 (Fed. Cir. 1998).

Critically, the written description requirement is analyzed from the perspective of what the original application disclosed, not what the later-filed claims ultimately recited. *In re Lukach*, 442 F.2d 967, 969 (CCPA 1971). An earlier application need not use the same words as the later claims; it need only convey possession of the subject matter. *Union Oil Co. v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 208 F.3d 989, 997 (Fed. Cir. 2000).

## II. The Original Application Provides Written Description Support for the Claimed Invention

The asserted claims require a vending machine comprising various elements, including a "customer interface for indicating the status of said vending machine." The Original Application, which issued as the '643 patent, discloses a vending machine that communicates with a customer's laptop computer. The specification describes an apparatus wherein "the customer's computer" communicates with the vending machine, and the vending machine's status is conveyed through that communication. This disclosure reasonably conveys that the inventors possessed the concept of an interface enabling information to pass between the customer and the vending machine, wherever that interface may be physically located.

The Federal Circuit has consistently held that a prior application provides adequate written description for later claims that are "reasonably broadened" by an interpretation adopted in litigation. *See Martin v. Mayer*, 823 F.2d 500, 503 (Fed. Cir. 1987) (holding that "an inventor's original disclosure may provide support for later claims that are broader than the original claims" where the disclosure reasonably conveys possession). Here, the Original Application's disclosure of a laptop-communicating vending machine supports the construction that "customer interface" includes an interface located on the customer's laptop. The district court's conclusion that only the 2000 CIP Application's "substantial new matter" supported this construction improperly ignored the Original Application's teachings.

## III. The District Court's Adoption of T-Mobile's Narrow Construction to Invalidate the Claims Was Error

The district court's approach inverted the proper analytical framework. Rather than first determining the correct claim scope based on the full specification and then assessing whether the Original Application supports that scope, the district court effectively permitted T-Mobile to argue that the claims *must* be construed narrowly to lack priority support. This is legally erroneous. Claims should be given their "broadest reasonable interpretation consistent with the specification" when priority is at issue. *In re Hyatt*, 211 F.3d 1367, 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2000).

Moreover, "[a] party seeking to invalidate a patent must overcome the presumption of validity by clear and convincing evidence." *Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. P'ship*, 564 U.S. 91, 97 (2011). The district court's summary judgment was based on a disputed factual question—whether the Original Application conveys possession of a distributed customer interface. "[W]here there is a genuine issue of material fact regarding written description, summary judgment is inappropriate." *Wands v. Unocal Corp.*, 2002 WL 31246742, at *4 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The evidence, including expert testimony that a skilled artisan would understand the Original Application to disclose the claimed interface arrangement, created a genuine dispute that should have precluded summary judgment.

## IV. Conclusion

The district court improperly denied PowerOasis the benefit of its 1997 filing date based on a flawed written description analysis. Because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether the Original Application reasonably conveys possession of the claimed invention, and because the district court's legal framework was erroneous, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with the correct legal standard.
