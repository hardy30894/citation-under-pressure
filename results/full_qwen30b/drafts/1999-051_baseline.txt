**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that a state prisoner’s claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may constitute “cause” sufficient to excuse a procedural default of a substantive habeas claim—here, a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence—provided that the ineffective-assistance claim itself is not procedurally defaulted and is cognizable on federal habeas review. The Court has long recognized that “cause” for a procedural default must be “something external to the defense” that “prevents the defendant from raising the claim at the appropriate time.” Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). The failure of counsel to raise a claim on direct appeal, when that failure is constitutionally deficient, satisfies this standard.

Here, Carpenter’s claim that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective for failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge on direct appeal is not procedurally defaulted. It is a claim that may be raised in federal habeas corpus proceedings under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), and it is not barred by the state’s procedural rules because it is not a claim that was previously litigated or forfeited in state court. The claim is not “procedurally defaulted” in the sense that it is barred from federal review; rather, it is a claim that, if proven, can itself serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim. The Court has repeatedly held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute “cause” for a procedural default. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977) (“[T]he failure of counsel to raise a claim on appeal may constitute cause for a procedural default.”); see also Carrier, 477 U.S. at 488 (“[C]ounsel’s ineffectiveness may constitute cause for a procedural default.”).

The Sixth Circuit properly applied this principle. It found that Carpenter’s appellate counsel failed to raise a claim that was both “strong” and “obvious” in the record—namely, that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction for aggravated murder and aggravated robbery. The record showed that the State’s case rested on the testimony of a single eyewitness, whose identification was inconsistent and unreliable. Moreover, the State failed to produce any physical evidence linking Carpenter to the crimes. Under *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 318 (1979), a conviction must be supported by “evidence sufficient to support a rational finding of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” The failure to raise such a claim on direct appeal, when the claim is objectively meritorious, constitutes ineffective assistance under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), because counsel’s performance fell below an objective standard of reasonableness and prejudice is established when there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.

The Court has never held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse a procedural default simply because the claim itself is not “procedurally defaulted” in the state courts. To the contrary, the very purpose of the “cause” doctrine is to allow federal courts to reach the merits of claims that would otherwise be barred by state procedural rules. As the Court stated in *Carrier*, “the cause requirement is designed to ensure that a defendant is not denied federal habeas review simply because of a procedural default that was caused by the ineffectiveness of counsel.” 477 U.S. at 488. The Sixth Circuit did not err in recognizing that the ineffective-assistance claim was both timely and cognizable in federal habeas, and that it could therefore serve as cause to excuse the default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.

Nor does the fact that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was not raised in state post-conviction proceedings bar its consideration here. The state procedural rule requiring exhaustion of state remedies does not apply to claims that are not themselves procedurally defaulted. See *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 527 (1972) (“[T]he failure of counsel to raise a claim on appeal may constitute cause for a procedural default.”). The state courts did not bar the claim on the merits; they merely declined to consider it on the ground of timeliness. That is not a bar to federal habeas review when the claim is otherwise cognizable.

Finally, the Court has consistently held that the “cause” standard is not satisfied by mere speculation or conclusory allegations. But here, the record supports a finding that counsel’s failure to raise a clear sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was objectively unreasonable. The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that prejudice is established is likewise sound. Under *Strickland*, prejudice is shown when there is a “reasonable probability” that the outcome would have been different had the claim been raised. Given the weakness of the State’s case, that standard is met.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
