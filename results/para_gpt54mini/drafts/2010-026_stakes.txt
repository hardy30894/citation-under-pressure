**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision cannot be squared with FOIA’s text or this Court’s precedent. FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” and they must be “narrowly construed.” U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989); Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). Exemption 2 covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). That language does not reach technical safety documents about the effects of explosions that govern how the Navy stores ordnance and protect surrounding communities. The Ninth Circuit erred by stretching “internal personnel rules and practices” to include any document that is predominantly used inside the agency and that might, if disclosed, risk circumvention of agency rules.

1. **The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test is inconsistent with the statute.**

This Court in Rose explained that Exemption 2 was directed at “mundane” matters such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, sick leave,” not substantive operational materials. 425 U.S. at 363, 369. Nothing in the statutory text authorizes withholding because a document is chiefly used inside an agency. To the contrary, Congress used the limiting word “solely,” which cabins the exemption to personnel rules and practices that are internal in their object, not merely internal in their circulation. See id. at 361. The Ninth Circuit’s approach collapses that limitation into a free-ranging inquiry into whether disclosure would make agency operations less convenient.

That result conflicts with this Court’s insistence that FOIA exemptions are not to be expanded by judicial invention. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. at 151; Rose, 425 U.S. at 361. If Congress had wanted to exempt all internal technical materials that might aid circumvention, it could have said so. It did not.

2. **The documents here are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within any fair reading of Exemption 2.**

The requested records concern Explosive Safety Quantity Distance calculations, maps, and diagrams that measure the effects of explosions and guide ordnance storage and facility design. Those are operational and safety documents, not personnel rules. The Navy’s own description shows that the materials are used to “design and construct” storage facilities and to plan ammunition operations. Documents of that kind are far removed from the “internal personnel rules and practices” addressed in Rose. 425 U.S. at 363, 369.

This Court has not treated substantive operational guidance as exempt merely because it is used by government employees. In Church of Scientology of California v. U.S. Department of the Army, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979), the Ninth Circuit recognized that Exemption 2 turns on whether the material concerns internal practices, not whether disclosure may be inconvenient. Likewise, in Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085-87 (9th Cir. 1997), the court rejected the idea that information is exempt simply because it is internal and sensitive; it held the materials there did not “relate ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.’ ” Id. at 1085. The same is true here. ESQD maps are technical data about the physical effects of explosions, not personnel rules.

3. **“Risk of circumvention” cannot enlarge Exemption 2 beyond its text.**

The Ninth Circuit relied on the notion that disclosure might permit circumvention of agency regulation. But the circumvention concern comes from Rose, 425 U.S. at 369, and it cannot override the statute’s threshold requirement that the matter relate to “internal personnel rules and practices.” An agency cannot bootstrap any sensitive record into Exemption 2 by asserting that disclosure could make compliance harder. If that were enough, the exemption would swallow much of FOIA. This Court has warned against such expansive readings. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. at 151; Rose, 425 U.S. at 361.

Nor did the Court in Rose or any later decision hold that Exemption 2 covers safety or security materials simply because disclosure might help outsiders avoid agency restrictions. Rather, Exemption 2 remains tied to personnel rules and practices. Where an agency seeks to protect genuinely sensitive security information, FOIA supplies other exemptions. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), (7)(F). The Ninth Circuit’s construction of Exemption 2 thus distorts the statutory scheme by turning a narrow personnel exemption into a broad safety-and-security exemption.

4. **The decision below conflicts with FOIA’s structure and this Court’s cases.**

FOIA balances public access against limited, specific withholdings. John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). The Navy chose not to classify these documents, and the Ninth Circuit nevertheless allowed withholding under Exemption 2 on a rationale untethered to the text. That approach contravenes the principle that exemptions must be strictly limited to their statutory terms. Rose, 425 U.S. at 361; Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. at 151.

The judgment should be reversed.
