# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE HAS ALWAYS REQUIRED A PLAINTIFF TO ALLEGE DIFFERENTIAL TREATMENT OF SIMILARLY SITUATED PERSONS BASED ON MEMBERSHIP IN AN IDENTIFIABLE CLASS

The Equal Protection Clause commands that "[n]o State shall . . . deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court's foundational equal protection jurisprudence has consistently construed that guarantee as protecting *classes* of similarly situated individuals from invidious discrimination, not as creating a freestanding remedy for every instance of allegedly arbitrary government action visited upon a single individual.

In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), this Court's seminal equal protection decision, the Court found a constitutional violation where San Francisco's laundry ordinance was "applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances." *Id.* at 373-74 (emphasis added). The violation arose because Chinese laundry operators, as an identifiable class, were treated differently from similarly situated non-Chinese operators. The class-based nature of the claim was not incidental to the holding—it was the constitutional harm itself.

This class-based understanding pervades the Court's subsequent equal protection cases. In *Skinner v. Oklahoma ex rel. Williamson*, 316 U.S. 535 (1942), the Court struck down Oklahoma's Habitual Criminal Sterilization Act because it treated persons convicted of certain felonies differently from persons convicted of other felonies involving "intrinsically the same quality of violation," *id.* at 538, without rational justification. The claim succeeded because Skinner belonged to a disfavored *class*—habitual felons subject to sterilization—compared to a favored class exempted from the Act's reach.

Likewise, in *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955), this Court articulated the now-familiar rational basis standard: a classification will be upheld unless it is "without any reasonable basis." *Id.* at 489 (quoting *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911)). The very term "classification" presupposes a class—a group defined by some characteristic, treated differently from another group. Equal protection analysis, by its nature, is a *comparative* inquiry between groups, not an inquiry into whether a single individual received treatment that a court, in hindsight, considers unwise or even spiteful.

## II. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT'S "CLASS OF ONE" THEORY IS UNMOORED FROM THE TEXT AND PURPOSE OF THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of "equal protection of the laws" is inherently relational: it protects persons from being treated unequally *relative to others similarly situated*. As this Court explained in *Truax v. Corrigan*, 257 U.S. 312 (1921), the Equal Protection Clause "was intended to secure equality of protection not only for all but against all similarly situated." *Id.* at 332. Where, as here, a plaintiff alleges no membership in any class—suspect or otherwise—and instead alleges only that a single municipal decision as to a single property was different from decisions made as to other properties, the plaintiff has alleged nothing more than a garden-variety dispute over the reasonableness of governmental action.

This Court has long cautioned against constitutionalizing every instance of asserted governmental inconsistency. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court rejected an equal protection challenge brought by a single candidate who claimed the Illinois Board of Election Commissioners had erroneously certified election results as to him alone, explaining that "[t]he unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." *Id.* at 8. Critically, the *Snowden* Court required that such discrimination be "directed against an individual because of his race or [membership in some other class]." *Id.* This class-based limitation on the Equal Protection Clause was not dicta—it was the basis for rejecting Snowden's claim notwithstanding his allegation that state officials had treated him worse than similarly situated candidates.

Similarly, in *Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield*, 247 U.S. 350 (1918), the Court articulated the "intentional systematic undervaluation" standard for tax equal protection claims, again presupposing a comparative class of taxpayers subject to differential treatment as a matter of official policy or practice, not an isolated instance of an individualized dispute over a single assessment.

## III. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT'S RULE WOULD TRANSFORM EVERY LOCAL LAND-USE OR REGULATORY DISPUTE INTO A FEDERAL CONSTITUTIONAL CASE

The Seventh Circuit's newly minted "class of one" theory—permitting suit whenever government action is allegedly motivated by "a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective"—cannot be squared with this Court's repeated admonitions against converting the federal courts into general-purpose reviewing bodies for local administrative decisions. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court recognized the danger of construing constitutional provisions so broadly that they "make a federal crime of an ordinary [state law] violation," *id.* at 96, a concern equally applicable to expanding equal protection doctrine to cover every discretionary municipal decision, such as the width of an easement required for a water hookup.

Moreover, in *Barsky v. Board of Regents*, 347 U.S. 442 (1954), this Court emphasized substantial deference to state and local administrative determinations regarding the terms and conditions under which public benefits and services are extended, cautioning that federal courts should not "sit as a super board of review" over discretionary local decisions absent a genuine constitutional violation. *Id.* at 451.

## CONCLUSION

Respondent Olech never alleged membership in any class subjected to systematic or purposeful discrimination. She alleged, at most, a single disagreement with a single municipal decision regarding a single easement. Under this Court's longstanding equal protection precedents—*Yick Wo*, *Skinner*, *Snowden*, and their progeny—such an allegation fails to state a claim. The Seventh Circuit's judgment should be reversed.
