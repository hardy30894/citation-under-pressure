Ohio’s clemency process creates no protected life or liberty interest, and the optional interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

I. **No protected liberty interest exists in clemency.**  
A pardon or commutation has never been a matter of right. It is “the prerogative of the executive.” *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380–81 (1866). The Court likewise held long ago that a pardon “is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws.” *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). Because clemency is discretionary grace, not a legal entitlement, it does not itself create a protected liberty interest. *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486–87 (1927); *Schick v. Reed*, 419 U.S. 256, 266 (1974). Even where state law regulates the application process, the underlying decision remains committed to executive discretion. *In re Bonner*, 151 U.S. 242, 260–61 (1894).

That rule forecloses any due process claim grounded in the clemency proceeding itself. The Court has long distinguished between rights secured by law and mere expectations of favorable executive action. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369–70 (1886); *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473 v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895–99 (1961). Due process is implicated only where the government has deprived a person of a recognized legal interest. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 569–71 (1972); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 599–603 (1972). A clemency applicant has no such entitlement. The Sixth Circuit’s attempt to constitutionalize Ohio’s clemency process therefore conflicts with the basic principle that executive grace is not enforceable as a matter of right. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81; *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. at 160.

II. **The interview option does not compel self-incrimination.**  
The Fifth Amendment forbids compulsion, not choice. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 67–68 (1906). It protects a person from being “compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself,” U.S. Const. amend. V, but it does not prohibit the government from offering a voluntary opportunity to speak. *McCarthy v. Arndstein*, 266 U.S. 34, 40–41 (1924). Nor does the privilege bar adverse consequences that flow from a decision to remain silent when no legal compulsion exists. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598–605 (1896); *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943).

Ohio’s regulation does not require any inmate to submit to questioning, answer any question, or waive any privilege. It merely permits a prisoner to request a personal interview. A voluntary interview is not compulsion. The Constitution is not violated because a prisoner must decide whether a discretionary interview may help his clemency application. *Mann v. People of State of Illinois*, 292 U.S. 602, 604–05 (1934). The possibility that a prisoner may choose to speak, and that his statements may later be considered by the clemency authority, does not convert the option into unconstitutional coercion. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318–20 (1976). The privilege guards against forced testimony; it does not guarantee that a voluntary speaker can later insist his words have no consequence.

III. **The Sixth Circuit’s contrary theory has no support in precedent.**  
The court below effectively treated clemency as an extension of trial or direct appeal. That approach cannot be reconciled with the Court’s longstanding refusal to treat executive clemency as a judicial proceeding. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81; *Biddle*, 274 U.S. at 486–87; *In re Bonner*, 151 U.S. at 260–61. It also expands the Fifth Amendment beyond its text by equating voluntary participation with compulsion. But the Court has repeatedly required actual legal compulsion before finding a Fifth Amendment violation. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562–64 (1892); *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. at 598–605; *Hale*, 201 U.S. at 67–68.

The better rule is the one grounded in the historic character of clemency: it is an act of grace left to executive judgment, and a prisoner who chooses to seek mercy may choose whether to speak. That choice is not a deprivation of liberty, and it is not compulsion. The judgment below should be reversed.
