**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not require a plaintiff to plead membership in a suspect class or identifiable group in every case. The Clause commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has long recognized that the Clause is “essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr., Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). That principle is not limited to class-based discrimination. When the government intentionally singles out one person for different treatment from others who are similarly situated, the Constitution is implicated.

This Court’s decisions in *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), and *Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Wakefield Township*, 247 U.S. 350 (1918), establish that equal protection reaches arbitrary discrimination against an individual. In *Yick Wo*, the Court held that a facially neutral licensing regime violated equal protection when it was administered with “an evil eye and an unequal hand.” 118 U.S. at 373–74. *Sunday Lake Iron* likewise recognized that intentional and systematic underassessment of one taxpayer, even absent class-based discrimination, could state an equal protection violation. 247 U.S. at 352–53. These cases confirm that the Clause prohibits irrational, targeted governmental discrimination against a particular person.

That understanding was reaffirmed in *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000), where this Court held that a plaintiff who alleged she was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated, and that there was no rational basis for the difference in treatment, stated a viable equal protection claim. Id. at 564. The Court rejected the notion that a plaintiff must allege “some additional evidence of personal animus or ill will.” Id. at 565. Rather, the Court recognized a “class of one” claim where the challenged action is “irrational and wholly arbitrary.” Id. at 564; see also *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Comm’n of Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336, 345–46 (1989) (finding equal protection violation where county assessed similarly situated property owners in a grossly unequal manner); *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445–46 (1923) (holding that intentional assessment disparity against a single taxpayer violated equal protection).

The Court of Appeals erred in treating personal spite as the essence of the claim. Equal protection doctrine does not turn on whether officials were motivated by malice; it turns on whether the plaintiff was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated without a rational basis. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564. A requirement that the plaintiff allege membership in a broader class would contradict *Olech*’s holding and revive a limitation this Court rejected. Id. at 565. The constitutional injury is the arbitrary singling out itself.

That principle is especially apt here. Petitioner alleged that the Village conditioned a water connection on a 33-foot easement, while requiring only a 15-foot easement from other property owners. If true, the Village intentionally imposed a more burdensome condition on petitioner than on similarly situated applicants. Such disparate treatment is the precise kind of governmental favoritism the Equal Protection Clause forbids when unsupported by a rational basis. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564; *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439; *Allegheny Pittsburgh*, 488 U.S. at 345–46.

Respondent may argue that permitting “class of one” claims opens the door to ordinary state-law disputes. But this Court has already cabined the doctrine by requiring a showing of intentional differential treatment from similarly situated comparators and the absence of a rational basis. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564. Those requirements preserve the distinction between federal equal protection and everyday administrative disputes. The Constitution is not implicated by mere error or poor judgment; it is implicated when the government intentionally departs from equal treatment without justification. *Sunday Lake Iron*, 247 U.S. at 353; *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564.

Because petitioner alleged that the Village singled her out for more onerous treatment than others similarly situated, and did so without a rational basis, the complaint stated a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment below should be reversed.
