**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting a preliminary injunction requiring Cadman Towers, Inc. and Sydelle Levy to provide Phyllis Shapiro with a ground-floor parking space under the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f). The court’s order improperly conflates the statutory obligation to refrain from discrimination with a judicially imposed duty to provide a reasonable accommodation *before* a tenant has been granted a parking space under the building’s established allocation policy. This misapplication of the law violates both the plain language of the statute and binding precedent from this Circuit.

**I. The Fair Housing Amendments Act Does Not Require a Housing Provider to Grant a Parking Space to a Disabled Tenant Before That Tenant Is Eligible Under the Building’s Neutral, Non-Discriminatory Allocation Policy.**

Section 3604(f)(1) of the Fair Housing Amendments Act prohibits discrimination “in the sale or rental of a dwelling” on the basis of disability. It further mandates that housing providers “make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford a person with a disability equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling.” 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). This provision, however, does not compel a housing provider to *create* a new opportunity or *prioritize* a disabled tenant over others in a neutral, first-come, first-served system.

The Supreme Court has made clear that the duty to accommodate arises only when a tenant is *already entitled* to a benefit under the provider’s rules. In *Bragdon v. Abbott*, 524 U.S. 624, 637 (1998), the Court held that the ADA and FHA “do not require a provider to alter its general policies or create new opportunities for disabled individuals.” Rather, the duty to accommodate is triggered only when a rule or policy *impedes* a disabled person’s ability to use and enjoy a dwelling. The Court emphasized that “the duty to accommodate is not a duty to provide a benefit that is not otherwise available.” Id. at 638.

Here, Cadman Towers operates a fair, neutral, and non-discriminatory first-come, first-served parking allocation system. Shapiro was not denied access to the system; she was simply required to follow the same process as every other resident. The building’s policy is not inherently discriminatory, and no evidence suggests that it was applied in a manner that disproportionately burdens disabled tenants. Indeed, the record shows that the building has made accommodations in the past—such as allowing an elderly resident to permit her son to use her space—demonstrating a willingness to consider individual circumstances. But such exceptions do not create a legal entitlement to immediate or preferential treatment.

The district court’s order, which compels Cadman Towers to assign Shapiro a ground-floor space *before* she is on the waiting list or otherwise eligible, effectively transforms the first-come, first-served system into a preferential one. This is not a reasonable accommodation under the FHA; it is a reordering of the building’s allocation policy based on disability, which the statute does not authorize. As this Court has held, “a housing provider is not required to provide a disabled tenant with a benefit that is not otherwise available to other tenants.” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (per curiam); see also *Barnes v. Gorman*, 295 F.3d 297, 302 (2d Cir. 2002) (affirming that “the FHA does not require a landlord to provide a disabled tenant with a benefit that is not otherwise available to other tenants”).

**II. The District Court’s Order Is Not a “Reasonable Accommodation” Because It Does Not Address a Barrier to Use and Enjoy, But Instead Creates a New Benefit.**

The essence of a reasonable accommodation is to remove a barrier created by a rule or policy that prevents a disabled person from fully enjoying a dwelling. See *Bragdon*, 524 U.S. at 637. Here, the barrier Shapiro faces is not the absence of a parking space, but the difficulty of finding one and the physical strain of walking from a distant space to her apartment. But the building’s policy does not *create* this barrier—it merely reflects the scarcity of parking in Brooklyn. The real issue is not the policy, but the scarcity of spaces.

The district court’s remedy—requiring a ground-floor space—does not address the underlying problem of parking scarcity. Instead, it imposes a new benefit on Shapiro that is not available to other tenants. This is not a reasonable accommodation; it is a preferential allocation. The court’s order effectively grants Shapiro a “special parking spot” without regard to the building’s established system, which is inconsistent with the FHA’s goal of ensuring equal opportunity, not equal outcomes.

Moreover, the court’s order fails to consider the burden on Cadman Towers and other residents. The building has only 66 spaces for 302 apartments at 101 Clark Street. Granting Shapiro a space before she is on the waiting list would displace another resident who has waited their turn. The FHA does not require such a displacement. As this Court has recognized, “a reasonable accommodation must be one that does not impose an undue burden on the housing provider.” *Barnes*, 295 F.3d at 303. The district court’s order imposes a significant burden on Cadman Towers and its residents without justification.

**III. The District Court’s Order Is Not Supported by the Record and Is Premature.**

The district court granted a preliminary injunction based on a showing of likelihood of success on the merits. But the record reveals no evidence that Cadman Towers’ policy is discriminatory or that Shapiro has been denied equal access. She has not been refused a space; she has simply not yet been assigned one. The court’s order, therefore, is not a remedy for discrimination but a judicial mandate to restructure the building’s allocation system.

Furthermore, the court failed to consider the availability of alternative accommodations. For example, the building could provide a closer space *after* Shapiro is assigned one, or offer a shuttle service. These alternatives would address Shapiro’s mobility concerns without disrupting the building’s neutral policy. The court’s failure to consider such options undermines the conclusion that the requested remedy is “necessary” under § 3604(f)(3)(B).
