## ARGUMENT

### I. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73'S TERM LIMITS ON FEDERAL CONGRESSIONAL CANDIDATES VIOLATE THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution, found in Article I, Sections 2 and 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly determined that Amendment 73's attempt to limit the terms of federal congressional candidates by preventing their names from appearing on the ballot violates these constitutional provisions. The U.S. Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a comprehensive and exclusive list of the qualifications for congressional service. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that Congress may not add to or alter the qualifications set forth in the Constitution. Similarly, in U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, the U.S. Supreme Court later affirmed that states may not circumvent the Qualifications Clauses by achieving the same result through other means, such as ballot access restrictions (see the eventual U.S. Supreme Court decision in this case, U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995)).

The framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to establish uniform national qualifications for congressional service to ensure that members of Congress would be accountable to the people and not subject to the varying requirements of individual states. As the Court noted in Thornton, "the Constitution creates a uniform national body representing the interests of a single people" (514 U.S. at 783, citing The Federalist No. 52). Allowing states to impose additional qualifications would undermine this uniformity and create a patchwork of different requirements across the country.

The Qualifications Clauses have been consistently interpreted to preclude states from imposing additional qualifications for congressional service. In Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001), the Court struck down a state constitutional amendment that required a warning label on the ballot next to the name of any congressional candidate who failed to support term limits, holding that it was an attempt to impose an additional qualification on congressional candidates. Similarly, in Thornton, the Court held that Amendment 73's term limits on federal congressional candidates were unconstitutional because they effectively added a qualification not enumerated in the Constitution.

### II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY DETERMINED THAT SECTIONS 1 AND 2 OF AMENDMENT 73 ARE INEXTRICABLY LINKED TO SECTION 3 AND MUST FALL WITH IT.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 is insufficient to save Sections 1 and 2 from the constitutional infirmity of Section 3. In Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), this Court held that the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Here, it is impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the inclusion of term limits for federal congressional candidates. The sponsors of Amendment 73 presented the issue to the voters as an "all or nothing" package, lumping state and federal officials together and referring to them in the Preamble as "elected officials." This Court should not speculate about the voters' intentions but rather should hold that the entire Amendment is invalid due to the inseparability of its provisions.

The practice of coupling a legitimate objective with one of doubtful legality, as was done here, is not fair to voters and should be discouraged. As this Court noted in Hoban v. Hall, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958), such a practice can be misleading and potentially deceptive. The Court should make it clear to sponsors of constitutional amendments and initiated acts that they are on shaky ground when they rely on the redemptive power of a severability clause to bail out a questionable joinder.

In conclusion, the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73's term limits on federal congressional candidates violate the U.S. Constitution, and that Sections 1 and 2 of Amendment 73 are inextricably linked to Section 3 and must fall with it. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court.
