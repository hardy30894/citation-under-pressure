The question presented is whether, under the design-patent damages statute, an award of the infringer’s profits must be limited to those profits attributable to the patented component when the design patent covers only that component rather than the entire article of manufacture. The answer is yes.

The statute’s text and history require apportionment. When a design patent covers only a component, profits “must be apportioned between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884). This Court has long held that a patentee “must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” Id. The rule prevents recovery of profits “not attributable to the use of the invention.” Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1853).

Apportionment is required even when exact separation is difficult. The patentee bears the burden to “separate the profits made by the defendant” that are traceable to the patented invention from those traceable to other elements. Livingston v. Woodworth, 56 U.S. (15 How.) 546, 560 (1853). Where the patented design is applied only to a portion of the article sold, “the measure of damages is the difference between the value of the article with the patented feature and its value without it.” Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444 (1885). The same principle governs design patents. See Hamilton-Brown Shoe Co. v. Wolf Bros. & Co., 240 U.S. 251, 259 (1916) (profits recoverable only to the extent they result from the protected design).

This Court has repeatedly rejected awards based on the entire product when the patent covers less than the whole. In Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614 (1912), the Court held that profits “must be limited to those which are attributable to the patented invention.” Likewise, in Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646 (1915), recovery was confined to the profits “which the infringer derived from the use of the invention.” The rule applies with equal force when the patented design is embodied in only one part of a larger article. See Mishawaka Rubber & Woolen Mfg. Co. v. S.S. Kresge Co., 316 U.S. 203, 206 (1942) (recovery limited to profits “attributable to the infringement”).

The same apportionment requirement appears in analogous intellectual-property contexts. In Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 325, 336 (1940), the Court required separation of profits “attributable to the copyrighted material” from those attributable to other elements. No different rule obtains for design patents.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach—permitting recovery of total profits on the entire smartphone—contradicts these settled principles. Because the design patents here cover only individual components, the award must be limited to the profits fairly attributable to those components. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for apportionment.
