# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2's Text Limits Its Reach to "Internal Personnel Rules and Practices," Not to Sensitive Information Generally

The Ninth Circuit's adoption of the "High 2" exemption disregards the plain text of the statute Congress wrote. Exemption 2 shields matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The ESQD data at issue—maps depicting the blast radius of naval ordnance—has nothing to do with personnel rules. It does not govern hiring, discipline, leave policies, or any other employment-related practice. It is technical information about explosives.

This Court has repeatedly instructed that FOIA's nine exemptions "are explicitly exclusive," *U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989), and "must be narrowly construed" in light of FOIA's "general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976) (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" gloss, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), inverts this canon. Rather than narrowing the exemption to its statutory text, the court expanded "personnel rules and practices" to cover any agency document that is used internally and whose release might complicate agency operations—precisely the kind of judicial gloss this Court has rejected when it "distorts Congress's careful balance." *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989).

Indeed, this Court's own discussion of Exemption 2 in *Rose* focused on the ordinary meaning of "personnel rules and practices"—rules like those governing employee grading, attendance, and discipline. 425 U.S. at 363 (citing S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965)). Nothing in *Rose* suggests that Exemption 2 was designed to be a catch-all for any internally generated technical data that an agency prefers to keep confidential.

## II. The "High 2" Framework Adopted Below Has No Grounding in This Court's Precedent

The Ninth Circuit acknowledged that the phrase "law enforcement" — the touchstand of its prior Exemption 2 cases, see *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980) — "does not appear in the text of § 552(b)(2)." Its solution was not to return to the statute, but to import wholesale the D.C. Circuit's "predominantly internal" test from *Crooker*. That test asks only whether requested material is "predominantly internal" and whether disclosure would "risk circumvention of agency regulation." 670 F.2d at 1074. But this framework was invented by the D.C. Circuit through "an extensive analysis" of legislative history and policy, not derived from the statutory text Congress enacted. The panel below candidly admitted it was borrowing a standard "settled on" by another circuit to "balance the competing implications" of the statutory words—an admission that the standard is judicial construction, not congressional command.

This Court has never approved the *Crooker* framework. To the contrary, this Court's admonition that FOIA exemptions are to be read narrowly counsels against extending Exemption 2 beyond its core subject—internal personnel administration—into a broad national-security or operational-security exemption that Congress did not enact. If Congress intended to protect information whose disclosure might risk circumvention of agency safety regulations generally, it knew how to do so: Exemption 7(F) already protects law-enforcement information that "could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual," and Exemption 1 protects properly classified national-security information. 5 U.S.C. §§ 552(b)(1), (b)(7)(F). The Navy's failure to classify the ESQD maps under Exemption 1, despite Commander Whitbred's professed national-security concerns, underscores that the "High 2" exemption is being used to do work that the statute assigns to other, more demanding exemptions—exemptions with their own tailored standards that the Navy could not satisfy here.

## III. The Documents at Issue Are Not "Related Solely" to Internal Personnel Matters

Even if some internal-use documents might qualify as "High 2" material, the ESQD data here plainly does not. The data consists of technical calculations and arc maps showing the blast effects of explosions—information that concerns public safety, not personnel administration. As the Navy's own witness explained, the arcs are used "to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety." That is a description of a technical safety function, not a personnel rule.

This Court's precedent in *Rose* draws a line between mundane, low-public-interest administrative matters and information of "genuine and significant public interest." 425 U.S. at 369. Information bearing on the potential blast radius of munitions stored a few miles from Puget Sound communities is a paradigmatic matter of "genuine and significant public interest" to residents of Port Townsend and Port Hadlock who have a direct stake in understanding the risks in their own backyard. Petitioner does not seek this information out of idle curiosity; he seeks it as a member of a community organization concerned with public safety near a naval magazine. FOIA's core purpose—enabling citizens to know what their government is doing that affects them—is disserved by stretching Exemption 2 to cover core public-safety information simply because the agency finds it more convenient to withhold it than to justify withholding it under an exemption actually designed for the purpose, such as Exemption 7(F) or Exemption 1.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that Exemption 2 does not permit withholding of the ESQD arc data.
