The Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that federal courts possess exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim. The federal patent issue presented in this case is not substantial enough to trigger federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that not all cases involving federal patent law issues arise under federal jurisdiction. In Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that a suit to enforce a right that originates in federal law is not necessarily one arising under that law. Rather, a suit arises under federal law only if it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." Id. at 569. The Court has also cautioned against allowing state plaintiffs to open the "arising under" door by simply pleading a federal issue. Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936).

In this case, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim does not necessarily depend on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. While the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar is a federal patent law issue, it is not a necessary element of Minton's malpractice claim. The claim can be resolved without determining the validity of the patent or the applicability of the experimental use exception.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of considering federalism concerns when determining whether a state-based lawsuit with embedded federal issues arises under federal jurisdiction. In Gully, the Court noted that the "arising under" jurisdiction is not to be interpreted broadly, and that the federal courts should not be flooded with cases that do not truly involve federal questions. 299 U.S. at 117-18.

The decision of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to the state court for further proceedings. The state court has jurisdiction to hear Minton's malpractice claim, and the federal patent issue presented in the case is not substantial enough to trigger exclusive federal jurisdiction. 

The jurisdictional analysis should be guided by the principles set forth in cases such as Shulthis and Gully, which emphasize the need for a substantial federal question and caution against an overly broad interpretation of "arising under" jurisdiction. By applying these principles, it is clear that the federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim.
