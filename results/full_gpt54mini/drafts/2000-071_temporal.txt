Milford did not violate the Club’s First Amendment rights. When a public school opens its facilities after hours to district residents for civic, social, and recreational uses, it creates at most a limited forum, and it may reserve that forum for the purposes it has defined so long as it does not discriminate within those purposes on the basis of viewpoint. See Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496 (1939); Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (describing limited public forum principles). Milford’s policy did exactly that: it allowed community uses for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but excluded “religious purposes.” In this setting, the Club sought not merely to discuss morality in the abstract, but to conduct Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, prayer, and evangelical invitation. Those activities fall outside the school’s defined forum. A government speaker opening its property for limited uses may exclude uses that are incompatible with the forum’s designated ends. See Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators’ Association, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983).

Even if the Club’s proposed meetings could be characterized as speech, Milford was entitled to distinguish between secular moral instruction and religious worship or evangelism. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment does not require a state to use its own facilities to promote religious exercise. In Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947), the Court held that the Establishment Clause “requires the state to be neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers.” That neutrality permits exclusion of religious worship from a public-school facility. Milford’s concern was not hostility to religion, but the necessity of avoiding direct involvement with worship and proselytizing on school premises. That is a legitimate basis for excluding a use from a school’s after-hours community program. See Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1948) (condemning public-school use for religious instruction); Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 430-31 (1962) (forbidding state-composed prayer in public schools).

The Establishment Clause independently justified Milford’s decision. The Constitution forbids public schools from becoming vehicles for religious observance or indoctrination. See School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 223-24 (1963); Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952) (upholding release-time instruction only because “the public schools [did] not use their facilities to aid any or all religious faiths or sects”). Milford reasonably concluded that allowing the Good News Club to meet in school classrooms, with young children, under school sponsorship-like conditions, would blur the line between state and church. That concern was especially acute because the Club’s meetings included prayer, Bible stories, and calls to accept Jesus Christ. The school was not required to risk Establishment Clause entanglement in order to facilitate private religious exercise. See McCollum, 333 U.S. at 209-10; Schempp, 374 U.S. at 222-23.

Nor did Milford engage in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. The Court has distinguished between excluding religious worship and excluding a religious viewpoint from discussion of otherwise open secular subjects. But here the forum was not opened to all moral instruction; it was opened to uses “pertaining to the welfare of the community” and closed to religious purposes. Milford applied that restriction to the Club because the Club’s own proposal described worship-like activities, not because it objected to a viewpoint on an otherwise permitted topic. A school may protect children from religious exercises in schoolhouse settings without violating the Free Speech Clause. See Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510, 534-35 (1925) (recognizing parental, not state, control over religious upbringing); McCollum, 333 U.S. at 209-10.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed. Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was consistent with the limited forum it created, and, in any event, was justified by the School’s duty to avoid an Establishment Clause violation.
