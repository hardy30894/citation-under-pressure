# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 523(a)(2)(A) EXCEPTS FROM DISCHARGE THE ENTIRE DEBT ARISING FROM FRAUD, NOT MERELY THE VALUE FRAUDULENTLY OBTAINED

Section 523(a)(2)(A) provides that a discharge does not extinguish "any debt . . . for money, property, services, or . . . credit, to the extent obtained by . . . false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The statute's structure resolves this case. The phrase "to the extent obtained by" modifies the *debt*—not the *damages*. Once a debt is shown to have been "obtained by" fraud, the entirety of that debt, including all liability arising from it, falls outside the discharge.

This Court has recognized that the term "debt" in the Bankruptcy Code is defined expansively as "liability on a claim," 11 U.S.C. § 101(12), and that "claim" itself means any "right to payment," 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). *See Johnson v. Home State Bank*, 501 U.S. 78, 83 (1991) (Congress intended "the broadest available definition" of claim). A judgment for treble damages, attorney's fees, and costs arising from Cohen's fraudulent rent overcharges is unquestionably a "liability" on the tenants' "claim" against him. There is no textual basis for slicing that single debt into a dischargeable punitive component and a nondischargeable compensatory component.

Petitioner's reading—adopted by the Ninth Circuit in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), but rejected by the Third Circuit below and by the Eleventh Circuit in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993)—would require this Court to read words into the statute that Congress did not write. The statute does not say "to the extent of the value obtained by fraud"; it says "to the extent obtained by" fraud, modifying the debt itself. As the court below correctly concluded, adopting the *St. Laurent* view, "a debtor liable for punitive damages because of his or her fraud should not be permitted to have that debt discharged." Pet. App. (quoting *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 680).

## II. THE "FRESH START" POLICY OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE IS RESERVED FOR THE HONEST DEBTOR, NOT THE FRAUDFEASOR

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Code's fresh-start policy exists to relieve the "honest but unfortunate debtor," not to shield wrongdoers from the consequences of deliberate deception. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991). In *Grogan*, this Court held that a creditor need prove fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A) only by a preponderance of the evidence—rejecting a heightened standard—precisely because "the general policy of favoring debtors in the interpretation of the bankruptcy laws" "does not apply where the debtor is not 'honest.'" *Id.* at 287. The same principle governs here: Cohen, who charged rents he never bothered to verify against a controlling ordinance and who ignored a Board ruling against him without even perfecting an appeal, is not the sympathetic debtor Congress sought to protect.

The Third Circuit properly relied on this reasoning, explaining that the "opportunity for a completely new beginning" is available only to the honest debtor, and that a debtor "liable for punitive damages because of his or her fraud should not be permitted to have that debt discharged." Pet. App. (quoting *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 680). To permit Cohen to discharge the treble-damages judgment while retaining the discharge of the underlying compensatory debt would perversely reward the fraud itself by limiting his liability to what he would have owed had he never deceived his tenants at all.

## III. TREATING THE ENTIRE JUDGMENT AS NONDISCHARGEABLE FURTHERS CONGRESS'S PURPOSE IN ENACTING § 523(a)(2)(A)

Congress carved out fraud debts from discharge because it determined that victims of fraud deserve full recovery, and that fraudfeasors should not be permitted to use bankruptcy as a shield. The treble-damages remedy under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, N.J. Stat. Ann. § 56:8-19, is not an arbitrary punitive add-on; it is the *measure of the debt* that Cohen incurred by violating a statute of which, as a landlord operating rent-controlled property in Hoboken for years, he is presumed to have had constructive notice. As the Third Circuit observed, the treble-damages award was "codified and therefore entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations," and thus "may properly be seen as the debt he incurred through his conduct, rather than as punishment." Pet. App.

Moreover, distinguishing between "compensatory" and "punitive" components of a single fraud judgment for dischargeability purposes would inject substantial uncertainty into bankruptcy proceedings, requiring courts to dissect state-law damages awards debt-by-debt to determine which slice reflects the "value obtained" versus which slice reflects punishment or deterrence. Nothing in § 523(a)(2)(A)'s text invites such an inquiry. The statute asks a single, threshold question: was the debt obtained by fraud? If so, the debt—in its entirety—survives discharge.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
