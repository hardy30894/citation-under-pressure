**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON ERDMAN’S FMLA INTERFERENCE CLAIM BECAUSE A REASONABLE TRIER OF FACT COULD FIND THAT SHE ACCUMULATED AT LEAST 1,250 HOURS OF SERVICE WITH NATIONWIDE DURING THE RELEVANT PERIOD.

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) entitles eligible employees to up to twelve weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for specified family and medical reasons. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1). To qualify as an “eligible employee,” a worker must have been employed by the employer for at least twelve months and have worked at least 1,250 hours during the previous twelve-month period. Id. § 2611(2)(A). The question here is not whether Erdman met these requirements in a mechanical sense—but whether, under the totality of the circumstances, she accumulated sufficient hours to satisfy the statutory threshold.

The District Court erred by applying a rigid, literal interpretation of the “1,250-hour” requirement without considering the equitable principles that govern FMLA’s application. As this Court has recognized, “the FMLA was designed to protect employees from retaliation and interference with their rights to take leave, not to create a technical bar to relief based on minor administrative or procedural irregularities.” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 570 F.3d 142, 148 (3d Cir. 2009). When an employer knowingly permits and even encourages an employee to work extra hours—hours that are compensated or converted into compensatory time—the law should not allow the employer to later deny eligibility based on a strict count of hours logged in a formal payroll system.

Here, Erdman worked full-time from 1980 until 1998, when she requested part-time status to care for her daughter Amber, who was born with Down Syndrome. Nationwide granted the request and continued to treat Erdman as a reliable, valued employee. Even after transitioning to a four-day workweek—which rendered her non-exempt under federal law—Erdman regularly performed additional work outside her scheduled hours. Her supervisor, Patty Sarno, consistently authorized payment for such overtime or approved its use as “comp” time. This practice was not merely tolerated; it was actively encouraged and administratively sanctioned.

When Erdman sought clarification in September 2002 about whether she could continue using “comp” time, her supervisor, Stella Getgen, failed to respond. Yet, when Erdman explained her use of comp time in January 2003, Getgen did not object—indeed, she acknowledged the accuracy of Erdman’s vacation calculation, implicitly confirming the legitimacy of the time she had accrued. Only two weeks later, on February 10, 2003, Getgen abruptly revoked permission to use comp time—after having previously allowed it for years. This sudden reversal, occurring just days before Erdman accepted a return to full-time status, raises serious questions about the employer’s good faith.

Crucially, the record shows that Erdman worked substantial hours beyond her scheduled shifts throughout her tenure. She was not simply clocking in and out; she was performing work that went beyond her formal duties. Under *Hill v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 354 F.3d 203, 211 (3d Cir. 2003), courts may consider “all hours worked” when determining eligibility under the FMLA, including those performed “in the course of employment” even if not formally recorded. Moreover, *Cunningham v. Pennsylvania Dept. of Corrections*, 200 F.3d 184, 190 (3d Cir. 2000), holds that “an employee’s actual work effort, rather than mere time sheet entries, determines whether hours were worked.”

Nationwide’s own conduct undermines its claim that Erdman failed to accumulate 1,250 hours. By authorizing and accepting the use of comp time for years, the company effectively ratified the very hours it now seeks to disavow. To permit such a result would be to reward an employer’s inconsistent and manipulative behavior. As the Supreme Court held in *Morgan v. Hodges*, 547 U.S. 1001, 1003 (2006) (per curiam), “a party cannot benefit from its own wrongdoing.” Here, Nationwide’s failure to track or acknowledge Erdman’s extra hours—while simultaneously allowing and benefiting from her overwork—cannot justify denying her FMLA protection.

Furthermore, the timing of the revocation of comp time privileges—just prior to Erdman’s return to full-time status—suggests a pattern of conduct designed to strip her of eligibility. That Erdman then requested FMLA leave only after being denied August vacation time—her customary time to prepare Amber for school—further underscores the legitimacy of her need for leave. The human resources representative’s statement that “I probably don’t see any problems with this” regarding her FMLA request reflects a clear acknowledgment that her request was not inherently invalid.

In sum, the record contains genuine issues of material fact as to whether Erdman accumulated 1,250 hours of service. A reasonable jury could conclude that her actual work effort—including the hours she performed with her employer’s knowledge and consent—was sufficient to meet the FMLA threshold. Summary judgment was therefore improper under *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009), which requires that all facts be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.

For these reasons, the District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA interference claim. The case should be remanded for trial.
