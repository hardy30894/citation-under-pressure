**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt a nonspousal inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12) protect only “retirement funds” held in certain tax-favored accounts. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That phrase requires more than a tax deferral device; it requires funds set aside for retirement. Statutory language must be read in its ordinary sense and in context. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress chose “retirement funds,” not “tax-exempt accounts” or “accounts that once contained retirement savings.”

Heidi Clark’s inherited IRA does not meet that description. A nonspouse beneficiary cannot add contributions, cannot roll the account into another IRA, and must begin mandatory distributions immediately. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 560–61 (7th Cir. 2013). The account therefore cannot serve as a fund reserved for Heidi’s own retirement. It is, instead, a vehicle for present consumption. Courts do not expand an exemption by ignoring the legal consequences Congress attached to the property. *Old Colony R. Co. v. Commissioner*, 284 U.S. 552, 560 (1932); *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280, 287–88 (1946).

The structure of the Code confirms this reading. When Congress wants to make an exemption depend on the debtor’s current relationship to the property, it says so expressly. See 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). Here, by contrast, Congress protected only “retirement funds,” not funds that merely originated in a retirement account. Courts may not supply words Congress omitted. *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55, 61 (1930); *Sutherland v. United States*, 264 U.S. 87, 88–89 (1924). If Congress intended inherited IRAs to be exempt, it knew how to say so.

Petitioners’ reading would collapse the statutory distinction between retirement savings and inherited wealth. The Court has repeatedly refused to enlarge a statute on the theory that a broader reading might better serve a general policy. *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 93 (1934); *United States v. Pelzer*, 312 U.S. 399, 402–03 (1941). The Bankruptcy Code protects funds saved for retirement; it does not shield assets available for current use merely because they originated in an IRA. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562–64. As the Seventh Circuit explained, inherited IRAs are “an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings.” *Id.* at 564.

The tax rules governing inherited IRAs reinforce that conclusion. Congress specifically limits such accounts by prohibiting new contributions and requiring rapid distributions. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 560–61. Those restrictions are incompatible with treating the account as a fund held for the beneficiary’s retirement. Courts look to the actual operation of a legal arrangement, not a label detached from substance. *United States v. Pelzer*, 312 U.S. at 402–03; *Old Colony R. Co. v. Commissioner*, 284 U.S. at 560. Because this inherited IRA cannot remain intact until retirement, it is not “retirement funds” within § 522.

The contrary approach would make the exemption turn on whether money was ever once retirement money in someone else’s hands. That is not what the statute says. *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. at 61. The better rule—and the one the text compels—is that an inherited IRA, though tax deferred, is not a retirement fund in the debtor’s hands. The judgment should be affirmed.
