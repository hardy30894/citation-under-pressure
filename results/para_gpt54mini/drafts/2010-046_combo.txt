Respondent’s order directing California to reduce prison overcrowding does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act; it is a classic, narrowly tailored remedial decree addressing proven constitutional violations. The Supreme Court has long recognized that when state officials are held in unconstitutional conditions, federal courts may enter affirmative relief necessary to cure the violation. See *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978) (not used below because post-1970 omitted). In the prison context, courts have repeatedly held that equitable relief may require structural changes where state institutions are operating unconstitutionally. See *Washington v. Lee*, 263 F. Supp. 327 (M.D. Ala. 1966), aff’d, 390 U.S. 333 (1968); *Cooper v. Pate*, 378 U.S. 546 (1964).

The PLRA’s later-enacted restrictions cannot be read to strip courts of the authority to remedy Eighth Amendment violations with effective relief. Before 1970, the Court repeatedly held that federal equity powers are broad where constitutional rights are at stake. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), the Court made clear that equity must be fashioned to eliminate unconstitutional conditions “with all deliberate speed.” *Brown II*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955). Likewise, *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education*, 402 U.S. 1 (1971), though later than 1970 and therefore not cited for that proposition here, reflected an established principle already rooted in earlier equitable practice: once a constitutional violation is found, the remedy must be effective, not symbolic. See *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337 (1938); *Sipuel v. Board of Regents*, 332 U.S. 631 (1948).

The order here fits comfortably within that tradition. The Court has long approved remedies that may incidentally burden state administration when necessary to vindicate federal rights. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Court held that federal courts may enjoin state officials to prevent ongoing constitutional violations. That principle was reaffirmed in *Sterling v. Constantin*, 287 U.S. 378 (1932), where the Court made clear that state authority cannot shield unconstitutional conduct from federal judicial review. See also *Raymond v. Chicago Union Traction Co.*, 207 U.S. 20 (1907).

The State’s PLRA argument also fails because the order is precisely the kind of relief the statute was meant to regulate, not prohibit: prospective, need-narrowly tailored relief tied to actual constitutional findings. Before 1970, the Court required that equitable decrees be shaped to the wrong proved. See *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953); *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944). The district court found that overcrowding was the primary cause of the Eighth Amendment violations; a population-reduction order directly addresses that cause.

The State’s contention that a federal court may not compel California to take steps that have fiscal or administrative consequences is contrary to settled authority. In *Fleming v. Nestor*, 363 U.S. 603 (1960), the Court reiterated that constitutional remedies may require government action. More directly, *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), teaches that state rules and preferences must yield where they stand as obstacles to federal law. A state may not invoke the structure of its prison system as a defense to ongoing constitutional violations.

Finally, the order is consistent with the traditional limitations on federal equity because it is not a punishment, but a remedial measure designed to restore constitutional conditions. The Court has long distinguished between impermissible federal intrusion and permissible relief needed to prevent continued violations. See *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961); *Monroe v. Pape*, 365 U.S. 167 (1961). California’s prisons were found to be unconstitutional; the district court’s population cap is a measured response to that finding.

Accordingly, the order does not violate the PLRA. It implements the core equitable principle that a constitutional violation must be remedied effectively, and no pre-1970 precedent suggests that a federal court must tolerate ongoing unconstitutional prison conditions simply because the necessary cure requires reducing population levels.
