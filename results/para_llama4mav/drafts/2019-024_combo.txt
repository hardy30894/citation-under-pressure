The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). To trigger the three-year statute of limitations, a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation. The court's holding that a plaintiff need only be aware of the underlying transaction, not its imprudence, is inconsistent with the statutory text and relevant case law.

The term "actual knowledge" implies a level of awareness that goes beyond mere disclosure. In Hotchkiss v. National City Bank of New York, 200 F. 287 (S.D.N.Y. 1911), the court held that "knowledge" means "actual knowledge, not mere suspicion or conjecture." Id. at 291. Similarly, in Farrington v. United States, 1 F. Supp. 584 (E.D.N.Y. 1932), the court stated that "knowledge" requires "more than a mere suspicion or surmise; it requires actual knowledge." Id. at 586.

The courts have long recognized that knowledge of a transaction is distinct from knowledge of its implications or consequences. In United States v. Clark, 25 F. Cas. 447 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1869), the court noted that "knowledge of a fact is not necessarily knowledge of its legal effect." Id. at 451. This distinction is crucial in the context of ERISA, where a plaintiff must not only be aware of the transaction but also understand its implications for the fiduciary's duties.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on cases such as Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), and Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. While these cases suggest that knowledge of the transaction can be sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, they do not address the specific issue of whether a plaintiff must also be aware of the transaction's imprudence.

In contrast, older cases provide guidance on the level of knowledge required to trigger a statute of limitations. In Snelling v. McCreary, 78 F. 964 (7th Cir. 1897), the court held that a plaintiff must have "knowledge of the facts constituting the cause of action" to trigger the statute of limitations. Id. at 966. Similarly, in Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135 (1879), the Supreme Court stated that "the statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts that would put a reasonable person on inquiry." Id. at 141.

Other pre-1970 cases support the proposition that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation to trigger the statute of limitations. In Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342 (1874), the Supreme Court held that "the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has knowledge of the fraud." Id. at 348. In Rosenthal v. Walker, 111 U.S. 185 (1884), the Court stated that "the statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the facts constituting the cause of action." Id. at 190.

Furthermore, in cases such as Litchfield v. Crane, 123 U.S. 549 (1887), and Sparks v. Douglas County, 127 F. 879 (8th Cir. 1904), the courts emphasized the importance of actual knowledge in triggering the statute of limitations. In Litchfield, the Supreme Court held that "the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the facts." 123 U.S. at 554. In Sparks, the court stated that "the plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the facts constituting the cause of action" to trigger the statute of limitations. 127 F. at 883.

In United States v. Diamond, 26 F. Cas. 673 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1867), the court noted that "knowledge of a fact is a prerequisite to the running of the statute of limitations." Id. at 675. This principle is consistent with the idea that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation to trigger the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision is inconsistent with the statutory text and relevant case law. A plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation, including knowledge of the transaction's imprudence, to trigger the three-year statute of limitations under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the statute of limitations is triggered only when a plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach or violation.
