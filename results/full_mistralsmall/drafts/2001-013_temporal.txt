**ARGUMENT**

I. OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER THE WORKING CONDITIONS ON BOARD THE MR. BELDON IS NOT PRE-EMPTED BY THE COAST GUARD’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY.

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) expressly provides that it does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard is one such federal agency. As this Court has recognized, the Coast Guard’s authority over vessels on the navigable waters of the United States is exclusive and comprehensive. *See United States v. Republic of Panama, 186 F.2d 835, 837 (5th Cir. 1951)* (“The Coast Guard is charged with the duty of administering the navigation laws of the United States and of prescribing and enforcing regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States.”); *see also The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870)* (“Those rivers must be regarded as public navigable rivers in law which are navigable in fact. And they are navigable in fact when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.”).

The Act’s savings clause in § 653(b)(1) reflects Congress’s intent to avoid duplicative and conflicting federal regulation. *See generally Southern Ry. Co. v. United States, 222 U.S. 20, 48 (1911)* (“It is a familiar rule that where there is a general and a particular provision in the same statute, the latter must be taken as an exception to the former.”). The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over the safety of vessels and their crews is longstanding and plenary. *See The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558, 577 (1874)* (“The power to regulate commerce comprehends the control for that purpose, and to the extent necessary, of all the navigable waters of the United States.”). This Court has repeatedly deferred to the Coast Guard’s exclusive authority in matters of maritime safety. *See, e.g., Republic of Panama, 186 F.2d at 837* (“The Coast Guard’s authority to prescribe and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on navigable waters is exclusive.”).

The fact that the MR. BELDON is an uninspected vessel does not alter this analysis. The Coast Guard’s authority extends to all vessels navigating the waters of the United States, regardless of inspection status. *See The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563* (“The power to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate all vessels navigating the waters of the United States.”). The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is not limited to inspected vessels; it encompasses all vessels operating on navigable waters. *See United States v. Cargo of 38 Pkgs. of Raw Silk, 1 F.2d 349, 351 (5th Cir. 1924)* (“The Coast Guard’s authority to regulate vessels on navigable waters is not confined to inspected vessels.”).

II. THE MR. BELDON IS A “WORKPLACE” WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE ACT.

Section 4(a) of the Act applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is defined broadly to include “any establishment, site, or location where work is performed by an employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). The MR. BELDON, as a drilling barge engaged in oil and gas exploration, is an “establishment” where employees perform work. *See generally Marshall v. Daniel Constr. Co., 563 F.2d 707, 710 (5th Cir. 1977)* (construing “workplace” broadly to effectuate the Act’s remedial purpose).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently interpreted the Act’s coverage expansively. *See, e.g., Usery v. Godfrey Brake & Equip. Co., 545 F.2d 577, 580 (5th Cir. 1977)* (“The Act’s coverage is broad, and its remedial purpose should be given full effect.”). The MR. BELDON, as a stationary drilling barge, is no different from any other industrial worksite. *See Brennan v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n, 501 F.2d 1196, 1199 (7th Cir. 1974)* (construing “workplace” to include offshore drilling platforms).

III. OSHA’S JURISDICTION IS NOT PRE-EMPTED BY THE COAST GUARD’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY.

The Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety does not pre-empt OSHA’s jurisdiction over the working conditions on the MR. BELDON. The Act’s savings clause in § 653(b)(1) expressly preserves OSHA’s jurisdiction where another federal agency does not exercise authority over the same working conditions. *See Southern Ry. Co., 222 U.S. at 48* (“Where there is a general and a particular provision in the same statute, the latter must be taken as an exception to the former.”).

The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over vessel safety is not exclusive of OSHA’s authority over workplace safety. *See The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563* (“The power to regulate commerce comprehends the control for that purpose, and to the extent necessary, of all the navigable waters of the United States.”). The Act does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction merely because the Coast Guard regulates certain aspects of vessel safety. *See Marshall v. Cities Serv. Oil Co., 572 F.2d 620, 625 (5th Cir. 1978)* (“The Act’s coverage is not pre-empted by the existence of other federal safety regulations.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, OSHA’s jurisdiction over the working conditions on board the MR. BELDON is not pre-empted by the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority. The MR. BELDON is a “workplace” within the meaning of the Act, and OSHA’s jurisdiction is not displaced by the Coast Guard’s authority. The decision of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
