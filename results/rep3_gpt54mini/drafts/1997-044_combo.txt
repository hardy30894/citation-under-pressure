**Argument**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) does not permit a debtor who procured money or property by fraud to wipe out only the compensatory portion of the resulting liability while retaining immunity from the rest. Under the Bankruptcy Code’s text and settled pre-Code law, the nondischargeable “debt” is the full liability flowing from the fraud, including statutory damages, fees, and costs awarded because of it.

The Code excepts from discharge “any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). That language reaches the debt “for” the fraud, not merely the portion equal to the victim’s out-of-pocket loss. In ordinary legal usage, “debt” denotes a liability to pay money. See Black’s Law Dictionary 532 (4th ed. 1951); see also 17 Oxford English Dictionary 41 (1933) (defining debt as something “due from one person to another”). The Bankruptcy Code nowhere narrows that liability to actual damages alone. Had Congress meant to limit nondischargeability to compensatory loss, it could have said so.

Pre-Code authorities confirm that liabilities arising from fraud were not discharged. In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704 (1878), this Court construed the predecessor discharge exception for debts created by “fraud or embezzlement” and held that the exception reaches actual, not constructive, fraud. Id. at 709. The Court emphasized that the Bankruptcy Act should not be used to protect a debtor guilty of real fraud. Id. at 709-10. Likewise, *Strang v. Bradner*, 114 U.S. 555 (1885), held that a partner’s fraudulent conduct rendered the resulting debt nondischargeable as to his innocent copartners, explaining that debts obtained by fraud are within the exception because the statute denies discharge to liabilities springing from fraudulent procurement. Id. at 561. *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), likewise treated fraud liabilities as outside the discharge, rejecting a reading that would let a debtor avoid the consequences of fraudulent conduct through bankruptcy. Id. at 185-86.

This Court has long recognized that bankruptcy does not provide refuge for wrongdoers who incur liabilities by fraudulent means. In *Morris v. United States*, 174 U.S. 196 (1899), the Court explained that discharge statutes are to be construed in light of Congress’s policy of protecting honest debtors, not insulating fraudfeasors. Id. at 200. Earlier, in *Gifford v. Helms*, 98 U.S. 248 (1878), the Court treated fraud-based liabilities as surviving discharge, again reflecting that bankruptcy does not cleanse debts rooted in dishonest conduct. Id. at 250. And *Tiffany v. Lucas*, 15 Wall. 410 (1873), underscored that a discharge does not operate to release debts excepted by statute, including those arising from fraudulent behavior. Id. at 418-19.

The same principle applies here to the entire judgment, not merely the rent overcharge. Once Cohen obtained money through actual fraud, every component of the resulting liability awarded because of that fraud became part of the nondischargeable debt. Statutory treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not some independent post hoc punishment detached from the fraud; they are part of the legal consequence the State attached to the fraudulent taking. To allow discharge of those amounts would permit the debtor to do exactly what the fraud exception forbids: retain the benefits of dishonest conduct while shifting the costs of enforcement to the victims. That result is incompatible with *Neal*, *Strang*, and *Forsyth*.

Nor does the Bankruptcy Code’s fresh-start policy help Cohen. This Court has repeatedly held that bankruptcy relief is for the honest debtor. See *Gifford*, 98 U.S. at 250; *Morris*, 174 U.S. at 200. The policy cannot override the statute’s specific exception for debts obtained by actual fraud. Specific statutory text controls over general policy. *Tiffany*, 15 Wall. at 418-19.

The judgment below should therefore be affirmed. Cohen’s fraudulent conduct produced a single debt, and § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts that debt from discharge in full. Attempting to sever compensatory from statutory damages finds no support in the text, no support in the pre-Code authorities, and no support in the historic rule that bankruptcy does not extinguish liabilities procured by fraud.
