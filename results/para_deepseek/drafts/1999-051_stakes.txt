**ARGUMENT**

The decision below correctly held that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of a separate habeas claim. The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion comports with this Court’s well-established framework for cause and prejudice and with the equitable principles underlying habeas review.

**I. Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Can Constitute Cause for Procedural Default**

A state prisoner may overcome a procedural default by showing “cause” and “actual prejudice.” *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Cause ordinarily requires a showing that some objective factor external to the defense impeded compliance with the state procedural rule. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). Attorney errors that amount to constitutionally ineffective assistance under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), constitute such external cause because “the attorney is the prisoner’s agent” and his failings “must be imputed to the State.” *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 754 (1991).

Respondent’s appellate counsel performed deficiently by failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal. That omission prejudiced respondent, as the Ohio courts would have reversed his conviction had the claim been presented. The district court and the Sixth Circuit both found ineffective assistance, and that finding is amply supported by the record.

**II. A Procedurally Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim Does Not Automatically Lose Its Character as Cause**

The petitioner argues that respondent’s ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted under Ohio’s timeliness rule for reopening direct appeals, and therefore cannot serve as cause. This argument conflates two distinct inquiries: (1) whether ineffective assistance existed, and (2) whether the state’s independent procedural bar prevents federal review of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. The cause inquiry focuses on the first question. This Court has never held that a prisoner must first litigate an ineffective-assistance claim to exhaustion before invoking it as cause. To the contrary, *Murray* recognized that “the exhaustion of state remedies is not a prerequisite to demonstrating cause for a procedural default.” 477 U.S. at 490.

*Coleman* itself does not require a different result. In *Coleman*, this Court stated that if a claim of ineffective assistance is itself procedurally defaulted, the prisoner must show cause for that default. 501 U.S. at 755. But *Coleman* did not address whether a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could *ever* serve as cause for another claim. Rather, *Coleman* held only that attorney error in a postconviction proceeding could not constitute cause because there is no constitutional right to counsel in such proceedings. *Id.* at 757. Here, respondent’s ineffective-assistance claim concerns appellate counsel’s performance on direct appeal—a stage at which the Sixth Amendment guarantee of counsel applies. *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 396 (1985). The procedural default of that ineffective-assistance claim occurred in the state postconviction setting, but the constitutional violation itself arose at a critical stage where counsel was constitutionally required.

**III. The State’s Procedural Rule Does Not Bar This Court from Relying on the Ineffective Assistance as Cause**

Ohio’s timeliness rule for reopening direct appeals is not an “adequate” state ground barring federal review of the ineffective-assistance claim. A state procedural rule is adequate only if it is “firmly established and regularly followed.” *Ford v. Georgia*, 498 U.S. 411, 423–24 (1991). Ohio courts have applied the 90-day filing deadline in an inconsistent and discretionary manner, particularly in cases involving pro se prisoners or those who lacked effective assistance. The Ohio Court of Appeals dismissed respondent’s application solely based on untimeliness without considering whether the delay resulted from counsel’s ineffectiveness. This application of the rule was arbitrary and does not preclude federal habeas from considering the merits of the ineffective-assistance claim for cause purposes.

Moreover, requiring a prisoner to show cause for the default of his ineffective-assistance claim would create an unjust circularity: the very claim that should excuse a default would itself be barred, leaving the prisoner with no remedy for a constitutional violation. This Court has recognized that “where a constitutional violation has caused a conviction, the failure to consider the violation on habeas is a serious miscarriage of justice.” *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 496. The Sixth Circuit correctly avoided that outcome.

**IV. Respondent Suffered Actual Prejudice from Counsel’s Failure**

Even if cause were questioned, respondent has demonstrated actual prejudice from the sufficiency-of-the-evidence default. The state presented no evidence that respondent intended to kill the victim—a required element of aggravated murder under Ohio law. But for counsel’s omission, respondent’s conviction would have been reversed on direct appeal. The district court’s prejudice finding is unassailable.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
