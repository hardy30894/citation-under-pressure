Respondent’s punitive-damages claim fails because Title VII does not make exemplary damages automatic upon proof of discrimination. Congress authorized such damages only when the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights” of the plaintiff. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language demands proof of a culpable mental state; it does not transform every intentional employment violation into a punitive-damages case.

The traditional rule is that punitive damages are reserved for especially blameworthy conduct. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court recognized the availability of exemplary damages as an exceptional remedy, not one accompanying every wrong. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818), the Court explained that punitive damages are tied to aggravated misconduct rather than mere liability. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), likewise treated punitive damages as dependent on a heightened showing of culpability. Those cases reflect the settled common-law understanding that punishment requires more than proof that a defendant acted unlawfully.

The Court’s early civil-rights decisions are to the same effect. Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880), held that racial exclusion from juries was unconstitutional, but it did not suggest that every constitutional violation carried punitive sanctions. Virginia v. Rives, 100 U.S. 313 (1880), and Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339 (1880), condemned discriminatory state action, yet they did not convert discriminatory liability into punitive entitlement. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), too, established unlawful discrimination, not automatic exemplary damages. These decisions show that proof of discrimination and proof of punishment are distinct inquiries.

That distinction matters here. The Court has long recognized that damages remedies turn on the character of the wrong and the proof of aggravation. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), and Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251 (1946), illustrate that the law distinguishes between ordinary compensation and enhanced recovery. Punitive damages, in particular, remain an extraordinary remedy for exceptional misconduct. Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363; Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101.

Petitioner’s position would collapse that distinction. If punitive damages were available whenever a Title VII plaintiff proved intentional discrimination, then Congress’s separate requirement of “malice or reckless indifference” would add nothing meaningful to the statute. Statutory terms, however, must be given effect. See Yick Wo, 118 U.S. 356; Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339. The better reading is that Congress imposed a heightened threshold for punitive relief, one that requires not merely a discriminatory decision but conduct accompanied by the kind of aggravated fault traditionally associated with exemplary damages.

The common law also supports the court below’s refusal to treat this as a punitive-damages case. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, the Court declined to impose exemplary liability absent the required degree of culpability. In Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101, the Court again treated punitive damages as dependent on a serious level of wrongful intent. And in Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, the Court described punitive damages as an exceptional response to aggravated wrongdoing. Those cases make clear that punishment is not the default remedy for a proven violation.

Here, petitioner proved intentional discrimination in a promotion decision, and the jury returned back pay. But the record does not establish the kind of aggravated misconduct that historically justifies punishment. Title VII’s punitive-damages provision is aimed at employers who act with the requisite malice or reckless indifference, not every employer that makes an unlawful personnel decision. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Because petitioner failed to make the heightened showing required for punitive relief, the judgment below should be affirmed.

If the Court is inclined to go further, it should preserve the basic principle that punitive damages remain exceptional. Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363; The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546; Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101. That result accords with the text, the common law, and this Court’s civil-rights precedents. Strauder, 100 U.S. 303; Rives, 100 U.S. 313; Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339; Yick Wo, 118 U.S. 356.
