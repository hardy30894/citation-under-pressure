## ARGUMENT

The question presented is whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as amended by the Civil Rights Act of 1991. The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where the plaintiff demonstrates that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This standard, rooted in the common law of punitive damages as it existed before 1970, requires conduct that is not merely intentional but also egregious or outrageous. The district court correctly denied punitive damages because the evidence did not meet this threshold.

### I. The Common Law Requires Egregious or Outrageous Conduct for Punitive Damages

Punitive damages have long been reserved for conduct that is “gross and wanton outrage.” *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 558 (1818). This Court has consistently held that punitive damages are not available for mere intentional wrongdoing; they require an element of “aggravation” or “outrage.” *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851) (punitive damages appropriate for “gross fraud, malice, or oppression”). In *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858), the Court explained that punitive damages may be awarded only where the defendant’s conduct is “wanton, reckless, or grossly negligent.” Similarly, *Missouri Pacific Ry. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885), stated that punitive damages require “gross negligence, malice, or wantonness.” And in *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), the Court emphasized that punitive damages are proper only when the defendant’s conduct contains “some element of wantonness or malice.”

These decisions establish a uniform principle: punitive damages demand conduct that is “outrageous” or “egregious” in character, not merely intentional. The same principle applies under federal civil rights statutes. In *Basista v. Weir*, 340 F.2d 74, 87 (3d Cir. 1965), the court held that punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 require “actual malice” or “wantonness” — a standard that “goes beyond mere intentional discrimination.” The court reasoned that punitive damages are “not compensatory” but are “imposed for the purpose of punishing the defendant for his outrageous conduct.” *Id.* at 84. This reasoning reflects the common law tradition that punitive damages are an extraordinary remedy reserved for the most egregious misconduct.

### II. Section 1981a(b)(1) Incorporates the Common Law Standard

Congress enacted § 1981a(b)(1) against this common law backdrop. The statute’s language — “malice or reckless indifference” — mirrors the traditional formulations. “Malice” at common law meant not merely intentional wrongdoing but “evil motive” or “wanton disregard.” *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107. “Reckless indifference” similarly requires a conscious disregard of a known risk that is “so wanton as to be the equivalent of malice.” *Quigley*, 62 U.S. at 214. The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to adopt the common law standard. The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act stated that punitive damages are available only where the defendant’s conduct is “egregious” and that the plaintiff must meet “an even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 29 (1991). Although the Report is not binding, it reflects the settled understanding that punitive damages require more than a bare finding of discrimination.

The respondent’s argument that “egregious” conduct is not required misreads the statute. The phrase “malice or reckless indifference” describes a state of mind, but that state of mind must be manifested in conduct that is “outrageous” or “egregious.” As this Court held in *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371, punitive damages are not warranted for “every act of negligence or even of fraud”; they require “gross fraud, malice, or oppression.” The same is true under § 1981a(b)(1). A plaintiff must show that the employer acted with a culpable state of mind *and* that the resulting discriminatory practice was sufficiently egregious to warrant punishment.

### III. The Evidence Here Falls Short of the Egregiousness Standard

The district court properly found that the evidence did not support punitive damages. The jury found that the American Dental Association intentionally discriminated against Carole Kolstad by promoting a man instead of her. But intentional discrimination alone is not enough. There was no evidence that the ADA’s conduct was “wanton,” “outrageous,” or “grossly negligent.” The preselection of a male candidate, while relevant to liability, does not rise to the level of egregious misconduct required for punitive damages. *Cf. Basista*, 340 F.2d at 87 (requiring “actual malice” beyond mere discrimination). The district court therefore correctly denied Kolstad’s request for punitive damages.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s denial of punitive damages should be affirmed.
