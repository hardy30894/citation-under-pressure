# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1)'s Text and the Governing Canons of Construction Confirm That OSHA May Not Regulate Where the Coast Guard Already Possesses Authority to Do So.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act withdraws OSHA's jurisdiction over "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The statute asks only whether another agency possesses such authority—not whether that agency has exercised it to the same comprehensive degree as OSHA would. Congress vested the Coast Guard, through its predecessor agencies, with plenary authority over the safety of vessels and their crews, including uninspected vessels such as Rig 52. That authority is comprehensive by design, even though Congress chose to regulate uninspected vessels with a lighter regulatory hand than inspected ones.

This Court has long recognized that a specific statutory scheme controls over a general one addressed to the same subject. In Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204 (1932), the Court explained that "[s]pecific terms prevail over the general in the same or another statute which otherwise might be controlling." Id. at 208. The maritime safety statutes committed to the Coast Guard are specific to vessels; the Act is a general workplace statute. Under the ordinary canon, the specific vessel-safety scheme controls.

That conclusion is reinforced by the presumption against implied displacement of an existing regulatory regime. In Posadas v. National City Bank, 296 U.S. 497 (1936), this Court held that even outright repeals by implication require that "the intention of the legislature to repeal must be clear and manifest," id. at 503, and that where two statutory schemes are "capable of co-existence," courts must give effect to both absent such clear intent. Nothing in the Act's text or history clearly manifests an intent to displace, on uninspected vessels, the historic maritime safety regime simply because Congress chose not to subject those vessels to the full inspection regime applicable to larger commercial vessels. Congress's decision to regulate uninspected vessels more lightly than inspected ones is a considered legislative choice about the *degree* of Coast Guard regulation—not an invitation for a second agency to fill the perceived gap.

## II. The Coast Guard's Exclusive Authority Over the Working Conditions of Seamen Reflects the Longstanding Maritime Principle That a Vessel's Internal Affairs Are Governed by a Single, Uniform Body of Federal Law.

Rig 52 was a vessel, and the men aboard it performed the work of seamen in furtherance of the vessel's mission. That places their working conditions within the traditional core of federal maritime law, a body of law whose defining characteristic, this Court has repeatedly held, is its insistence on uniformity.

In Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917), the Court held that a state compensation law could not govern an injury suffered by a stevedore aboard a vessel in navigable waters because doing so would "contravene[] the essential purpose expressed by an act of Congress" or "interfere[] with the proper harmony and uniformity" of maritime law "in its international and interstate relations." Id. at 216. That same concern for uniformity animates Congress's grant of exclusive maritime safety authority to the Coast Guard. Just as Jensen forbade a patchwork of state regulation of vessel conditions, the structure of the Act—read together with the Coast Guard's statutory mandate—forbids a patchwork in which a vessel's safety obligations shift depending on whether OSHA or the Coast Guard happens to reach it first.

This Court's decision in Panama Railroad Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924), upholding the Jones Act, likewise recognized that the need for maritime law to operate uniformly "throughout the country" was among the principal reasons Congress and the courts have treated vessel-based employment as a discrete category subject to a single federal law rather than to the ordinary land-based regulatory apparatus that governs shoreside industry. Id. at 386. The workers aboard Rig 52 were performing traditional seaman's duties in the course of the vessel's drilling operations, the very type of work that this Court's early maritime decisions treated as falling within the vessel owner's nondelegable duties under general maritime law. See The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903) (recognizing the vessel owner's duty to provide a safe vessel and safe conditions for those serving aboard it, as an incident of the vessel's status under maritime law). Extending the label "seaman" flexibly to reach all those engaged in a vessel's characteristic work, this Court held in International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50 (1926), that Congress could not have intended maritime protections to "vary" based on technical distinctions among vessel workers performing the same essential maritime function. Id. at 52.

## III. Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit correctly held that Congress committed the safety of vessels like Rig 52—inspected or not—to the Coast Guard alone. Permitting OSHA to supplement that scheme on uninspected vessels would introduce precisely the overlapping, non-uniform regulation that Section 4(b)(1)'s text, the canons of construction, and this Court's maritime uniformity jurisprudence all counsel against. The judgment below should be affirmed.
