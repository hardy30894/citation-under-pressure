# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 355(f) Authorizes Judicial Review Only of Final Decisions on the Merits of a Benefits Claim, Not of Discretionary Refusals to Reopen

The Railroad Retirement Act permits circuit courts to review "any final decision" of the Board under the same procedures that govern judicial review under the Social Security Act. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). Because Congress borrowed this review mechanism from the Social Security Act, this Court's construction of the parallel provision in that statute controls here. In Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), this Court held that a decision by the Secretary refusing to reopen a previously adjudicated claim is not a "final decision" reviewable under 42 U.S.C. § 405(g), even though the underlying merits decision was reviewable after exhaustion. The Court explained that "the opportunity to reopen final decisions and any hearing convened to determine the propriety of such action are afforded by the Secretary's regulations and not by the Social Security Act." Sanders, 430 U.S. at 108. Accordingly, the Court concluded that "§ 205(g) cannot be read to authorize judicial review of alleged abuses of agency discretion in refusing to reopen claims." Id. at 107-08.

That reasoning applies with equal force to § 355(f). A decision declining to reopen a stale claim is not a decision "on the merits" of the claim; it is an exercise of discretion under regulations the Board itself promulgated, here 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, governing reopening based on new evidence or administrative error. As the Fifth Circuit correctly held in Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), "the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals is limited to the review of Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted." Id. at 140. A refusal to reopen does not readjudicate the merits; it merely declines to disturb a decision that already became final years earlier. Salinas's 2006 denial became final on February 9, 2007, when he failed to pursue further administrative appeal. The Board's 2015 refusal to reopen that final decision is a distinct, discretionary act outside the scope of § 355(f).

## II. The Overwhelming Weight of Circuit Authority Confirms This Reading

Every circuit to have addressed the question until the D.C. Circuit's outlier decision agreed that § 355(f) does not extend to reopening denials. In Roberts, the Fifth Circuit "joined several of our sister circuits in determining that we have no jurisdiction [under section 355(f)] to review the Board's decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits." 346 F.3d at 140. The court expressly found "the reasoning of the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh and Tenth circuits persuasive" in light of Sanders. Id. at 141; see Harris v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999); Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995); Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 708 F.2d 250, 254-55 (7th Cir. 1983). Four circuits, applying Sanders's reasoning to the materially identical statutory scheme, reached the same conclusion independently. That consensus reflects the correct understanding that reopening decisions are creatures of agency grace, not statutory entitlement, and therefore fall outside "final decision" review.

This Court's later decision in Your Home Visiting Nurse Services, Inc. v. Shalala, 525 U.S. 449 (1999), reinforces that conclusion in the Medicare context, confirming that discretionary reopening determinations under analogous benefits statutes are not subject to judicial review absent express statutory authorization. The consistency of this rule across benefit programs modeled on the Social Security Act's review provisions demonstrates that Congress's silence regarding reopening decisions was deliberate, not oversight.

## III. Petitioner's Reliance on the Minority View Is Unavailing

Salinas relies on Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Bd., 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which broke from the majority rule to hold that § 355(f) permits review of reopening denials. But Stovic is an outlier that cannot overcome the persuasive force of Sanders and the four-circuit consensus it produced. Nor does Bowen v. City of New York, 476 U.S. 467 (1986), rescue Salinas's position. Bowen recognized only a narrow exception to Sanders for claims alleging systemic constitutional violations infecting the administrative process itself—a circumstance entirely absent here, where Salinas simply seeks belated reconsideration of a routine, individualized denial based on failure to show good cause for an untimely filing.

## IV. Sound Policy Supports Finality

Permitting judicial review of reopening denials would undermine the finality Congress sought to protect. As Sanders explained, unlimited review of reopening decisions would "frustrate the congressional purpose ... of imposing a 63-day limitation upon judicial review." 430 U.S. at 108. The same concern for administrative finality animates the Railroad Retirement Act. Salinas's 2006 claim became final in 2007; permitting review of the Board's 2015 refusal to revisit that stale claim, seven years later, would eviscerate the statutory limitations period Congress established.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
