**Argument**

I.  The Statute Requires the Injury to Occur on the Outer Continental Shelf

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) unambiguously requires the injury to occur on the outer continental shelf. The statute provides coverage for the "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." The injury must occur on the shelf to be "as the result of" operations on the shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test is a novel reading that ignores the plain meaning of "occurring."

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the geographic limitation of OCSLA is a defining feature of the statute. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, the Court noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." 477 U.S. at 219. This principle dictates that the location of the injury is the primary determinant of coverage. The injury in this case occurred onshore, far from the operations on the shelf, and therefore falls outside the geographic scope of the statute.

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied this principle in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*. The court adopted a "situs-of-injury" requirement, holding that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf. 877 F.2d at 362. *Mills* is the most on-point precedent for this Court, as it directly addresses the interpretation of § 1333(b) and rejects the "substantial nexus" theory advanced by the petitioner. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that the phrase "injury occurring as the result of" requires the injury to take place on the shelf itself.

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, which the Ninth Circuit relied upon, is distinguishable. *Curtis* rejected a strict "situs" test in favor of a "but for" test, finding that an employee injured in a car accident on his way to a platform was covered. 849 F.2d at 809-11. However, *Curtis* involved an injury that was arguably a direct result of the employment relationship, whereas the injury here was a result of a forklift accident at a land-based processing facility. The "but for" test in *Curtis* does not override the explicit geographic limitation of the statute, which the Fifth Circuit correctly identified.

For these reasons, the Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage to injuries occurring onshore. The injury must occur on the outer continental shelf to trigger coverage under § 1333(b).
