## ARGUMENT

### I. The Board’s Polling Standard Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act

The National Labor Relations Act does not expressly govern employer polling of incumbent unions. The Board’s rule—that an employer may poll only when it already possesses “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status”—renders polling a dead letter. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). As the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have recognized, this standard is irrational because it requires the employer to have enough evidence to withdraw recognition before it can use a poll to determine whether withdrawal is warranted. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). Those courts correctly held that an employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal.” *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867.

The Board’s own reasoning is internally inconsistent. It claims that Board-conducted RM elections are the “preferred method” for testing union support, *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061, yet it applies the identical evidentiary standard to polling. If RM elections are preferable, the standard for polling should be *more* stringent, not the same. Conversely, if the same standard applies to polling and RM elections, then the standard for withdrawing recognition—which lacks any procedural safeguards—should be *more* stringent. The Board’s failure to reconcile these contradictions renders its rule arbitrary. *See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (Board must give reasoned explanation for its policies).

The Board’s justification—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061—does not salvage its rule. The Act’s primary purpose is to protect employee free choice. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 610 (1969). A poll conducted with the procedural safeguards of *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)—secret ballot, no coercion, no interrogation—is far less disruptive than an employer’s unilateral withdrawal of recognition. The Board’s rule forces employers to choose between risking an unfair labor practice by withdrawing recognition without a poll, or forgoing any test of union support. That is not a rational accommodation of the competing interests. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987) (Board’s judgment entitled to deference only if rational and consistent with the Act).

### II. Even Under the Board’s Standard, the Record Compels a Finding of Reasonable Doubt

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The record contains overwhelming, unrefuted evidence that a majority of employees no longer supported the union.

Seven employees made direct statements repudiating the union. The Board improperly discounted the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick said “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” The Board dismissed this as “equivocal” because it was made during a job interview after the employer stated it would be non-union. But the Board’s own precedent, *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), does not require automatic disregard of such statements; it merely cautions that they may be “unlikely to be sincere.” Here, Ridgick had previously asked about decertifying the union—a fact the Board ignored. Marsh said he was “not getting his money’s worth” from union dues. The Board treated this as mere dissatisfaction, not opposition. But dissatisfaction with representation is a classic indicator of loss of support. *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995) (distinguishing dissatisfaction from rejection, but recognizing that repeated complaints can support reasonable doubt).

The Board also rejected the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr, who reported that entire groups of employees—the night shift and the service department—did not want the union. The Board faulted Bloch for not testifying and Mohr for an “off-the-cuff” remark. But the employer is entitled to rely on such reports when they are consistent with other evidence. *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board where employer had reports from multiple employees). Here, the reports were corroborated by the poll itself, which showed 19-13 against the union—a result the Board never challenged as procedurally flawed.

The Board’s counting methodology was also erroneous. It excluded three employees hired after January 25, 1991, but the employer’s doubt crystallized before that date. The Board’s rigid cutoff ignores the reality that an employer’s reasonable doubt can develop over time. *See Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 41 n.8 (employer may act on doubt “at any time” after the first year). Moreover, the Board excluded employees who had left the unit, but their prior statements remain probative of the unit’s overall sentiment. The Board’s approach artificially narrowed the evidence.

In sum, the Board’s factual determination is arbitrary and capricious. The record compels the conclusion that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt, and its poll was lawful. The Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that the Board’s standard is irrational, or alternatively, that the Board’s application of that standard to this record is unsupported by substantial evidence.
