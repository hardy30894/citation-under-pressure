**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of MSPB decisions to the Federal Circuit, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), except for “cases of discrimination” that “shall be filed in district court,” § 7703(b)(2). A case falls within the exception when an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination” prohibited by enumerated statutes. § 7702(a)(1). Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L. Ed. 2d 433 (2012), held that this language places review in district court whenever the Board dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds without reaching the merits of the discrimination claim. Id. at 604.

The same rule governs jurisdictional dismissals. The statutory text turns on whether the employee “alleges” discrimination in connection with an action that “may” be appealed to the Board, not on whether the Board ultimately exercises jurisdiction. Kloeckner, 133 S. Ct. at 604. Once that allegation is made, the case is a mixed case whose judicial review lies in district court. Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which reached the opposite conclusion for jurisdictional dismissals, is incompatible with Kloeckner’s construction of the identical statutory language.

The distinction between procedural and jurisdictional grounds finds no support in the statute. Both categories involve an employee who presents a facially appealable action together with a discrimination claim; the Board simply declines to proceed further. Kloeckner rejected any such line-drawing, emphasizing that Congress “meant to impose the same procedural requirements” on all mixed cases. 133 S. Ct. at 604. Allowing the Board’s characterization of its dismissal to dictate the forum would reintroduce the very uncertainty Kloeckner eliminated.

The Board’s voluntariness determination here is not severable from the discrimination allegations. Perry asserted that the settlement agreement was the product of coercion rooted in race, age, and disability discrimination. That claim directly implicates whether the retirement and suspension were “actions which [he] may appeal to the” Board under § 7702(a)(1)(A). The statutory scheme therefore requires district-court review.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding rests on a distinction the Supreme Court has already rejected. Kloeckner controls, and review belongs in district court.
