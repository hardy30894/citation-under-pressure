# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Imposes a Causal Requirement, Not a Geographic One.

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That language asks only whether the injury was causally connected to shelf operations; it says nothing about where the employee's body happened to be at the moment of injury. This Court has long held that "if [the statute's language] is plain . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Congress chose causal phrasing—"resulting from," "as the result of"—not locational phrasing. And Congress knew how to write a locational limitation when it wanted one: it defined the Shelf itself by reference to "submerged lands lying seaward and outside" state jurisdiction, 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a), yet used no comparable geographic language in § 1333(b). This Court should not read into the compensation provision a situs-of-injury requirement that Congress omitted.

## II. OCSLA and the Longshore Act Are Remedial Statutes That Must Be Construed to Serve Their Coverage Purposes.

Congress enacted OCSLA to ensure that offshore extraction of the Nation's resources would not proceed in a legal vacuum, extending to fixed structures on the Shelf a body of law—including compensation for injured workers—adequate to that enterprise, and treating those structures, for many purposes, as if they were themselves islands within a State. Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969). That remedial design counsels against stranding from coverage a worker whose fatal injury was causally bound up with shelf operations merely because the fatal blow landed onshore.

The same principle pervades this Court's Longshore Act jurisprudence. In Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114 (1962), the Court construed the Act to extend coverage as far as Congress's power over maritime injuries reasonably allowed, refusing a cramped reading that would leave workers without a remedy. In South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260 (1940), the Court resolved coverage questions at the margins of the Act's reach with due regard for its basic remedial purpose, deferring to administrative judgments that further rather than frustrate coverage. And this Court has repeatedly instructed that doubts about the scope of workers' compensation statutes should be resolved in favor of coverage for the injured worker and his family. Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953).

Cardillo v. Liberty Mutual Insurance Co., 330 U.S. 469 (1947), confirms that the Act's causal language—"arising out of and in the course of employment"—receives a broad, practical construction, sustaining coverage on reasonable inferences drawn from the employment relationship rather than rigid formalism. Id. at 478-80. Section 1333(b)'s parallel causal phrase, "as the result of," warrants the same practical approach.

This Court's decision in O'Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc., 340 U.S. 504 (1951), is directly on point. There the Court rejected a narrow, situs-bound conception of coverage, holding that "the test of recovery is not a causal relation between the nature of employment and the accident" and that "all that is required is that the obligations or conditions of employment create the zone of special danger out of which the injury arose." Id. at 507. Juan Valladolid's employment as a roustabout on the Hogan placed him within precisely such a zone. His duty of periodically consolidating scrap metal generated by shelf operations—performed at the very facility that existed to receive and process the Shelf's extracted resources—was an integral incident of his offshore employment. The substantial nexus between his death and shelf operations satisfies § 1333(b) exactly as O'Leary's functional test contemplates, without regard to which side of the three-mile line his body happened to occupy.

## III. Isolated, Unnecessary Statements in Prior Opinions Do Not Control.

To the extent Pacific Operations relies on stray language from an opinion addressing an altogether different OCSLA provision, that reliance is misplaced. This Court has long recognized that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and that "if they go beyond the case, they may be respected, but ought not to control the judgment in a subsequent suit when the very point is presented for decision." Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821); accord Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). A passing observation offered without briefing or analysis of the precise question here—whether § 1333(b) contains a situs-of-injury requirement—cannot override the statute's causal text or the remedial construction this Court has consistently applied to compensation legislation of this kind.

## CONCLUSION

Because § 1333(b) asks only whether an injury resulted from shelf operations, and because this Court's precedents require causal, remedial language to be construed in favor of coverage, the judgment below should be affirmed.
