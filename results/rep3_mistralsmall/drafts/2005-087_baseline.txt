**ARGUMENT**

I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE DOES NOT VIOLATE DUE PROCESS

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause does not guarantee a criminal defendant an unfettered right to present expert testimony about his mental state to negate mens rea, independent of an insanity defense. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) (holding that due process does not require a state to adopt a particular rule of criminal responsibility); *see also Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535-36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that states have substantial latitude in defining criminal responsibility and the defenses thereto). Arizona’s rule is a permissible exercise of that latitude.

A. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a State to Permit the Use of Expert Mental Health Evidence to Negate Mens Rea

This Court has repeatedly affirmed that states retain broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the defenses available to criminal defendants. *See, e.g., Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977) (upholding a state’s allocation of the burden of proof on an element of a crime); *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 235 (1987) (holding that a state may require a defendant to prove an affirmative defense). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence to the insanity defense is consistent with this authority.

In *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006), this Court upheld Arizona’s narrow definition of insanity and its allocation of the burden of proof on that defense. *Id.* at 752-53. The Court recognized that Arizona’s approach was a rational legislative choice designed to address concerns about the reliability and administrability of broader insanity defenses. *Id.* at 753. The Court did not, however, address whether Arizona could also limit the use of expert mental health evidence to the insanity defense when offered to negate mens rea. *Id.* at 754-55. That question is now before the Court.

The Due Process Clause does not require states to permit defendants to use expert mental health evidence to negate mens rea. *See Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798. In *Leland*, the Court rejected a due process challenge to Oregon’s requirement that a defendant pleading insanity bear the burden of proof. *Id.* The Court explained that due process does not mandate a particular rule of criminal responsibility, and states are free to define the defenses available to criminal defendants. *Id.* Similarly, Arizona’s rule limiting the use of expert mental health evidence to the insanity defense is a permissible legislative choice.

B. Arizona’s Rule Is Rationally Related to the State’s Interest in Avoiding Unreliable Expert Testimony

Arizona’s rule is also consistent with this Court’s recognition that expert mental health testimony can be unreliable and confusing to juries. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 777 (Kennedy, J., concurring) (noting that expert testimony about mental illness can be “difficult for jurors to assess” and may “obscure rather than clarify the issues”); *see also Barefoot v. Estelle*, 463 U.S. 880, 899-900 (1983) (recognizing the risk of “junk science” in expert testimony). By limiting the use of such testimony to the insanity defense, Arizona seeks to avoid the risk that jurors will be misled by unreliable expert evidence when assessing the defendant’s mens rea.

This Court has repeatedly deferred to state legislative judgments designed to address the risks posed by expert testimony. *See, e.g., Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 61-62 (1987) (upholding a state’s per se rule excluding hypnotically refreshed testimony); *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 311-12 (1998) (upholding a military rule excluding polygraph evidence). Arizona’s rule is a similar legislative judgment designed to ensure the reliability of expert testimony in criminal trials. *See Clark*, 548 U.S. at 777 (Kennedy, J., concurring).

II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT VIOLATE CLARK’S RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert mental health evidence does not violate Clark’s right to present a defense. *See Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 23 (1967) (holding that the Compulsory Process Clause guarantees a defendant the right to present witnesses in his favor). The right to present a defense is not absolute, and states may impose reasonable restrictions on the admission of evidence. *See, e.g., Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43 (1996) (upholding a state’s rule eliminating voluntary intoxication as a defense); *see also Clark*, 548 U.S. at 754-55 (recognizing that states may define the defenses available to criminal defendants).

Arizona’s rule is a reasonable restriction designed to ensure the reliability of expert testimony and to avoid confusion among jurors. *See id.* at 777 (Kennedy, J., concurring). The rule does not prevent Clark from presenting evidence of his mental state; it merely channels that evidence into the insanity defense, where it is subject to the heightened standards and safeguards that Arizona has chosen to impose. *See id.* at 752-53. This is a permissible legislative choice, and it does not violate Clark’s right to present a defense.
