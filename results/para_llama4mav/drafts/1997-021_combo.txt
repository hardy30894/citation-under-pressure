The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, but the NLRB has established a standard for allowing employers to conduct polls. The NLRB's standard requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll (Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1989 WL 224426 (1989)). However, this standard is too strict and renders employer polls useless (Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984)).

The courts have recognized that polling can be a "useful and legitimate tool" for employers with "sincere doubts" about a union's support (A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981)). In Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982), the Sixth Circuit held that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal" of recognition. This standard is more reasonable and consistent with the NLRA.

The NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the principles established in NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575 (1969), and NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., is not applicable here as it was decided after 1970, but the reasoning in Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, is relevant. In Fall River, the Court held that a successor employer inherits the bargaining obligation of its predecessor (482 U.S. 27, 36-41 (1987)). However, the factual underpinnings for the presumption of continuing status are weaker when a unit contains less than all of the previously certified employee group, as in this case.

The NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) and the NLRB failed to consider the statements of several employees, including Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, which were not probative of their union sympathies (Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978)). The NLRB also failed to credit the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr, which were reliable evidence of the views of other employees in the unit.

In Joy Silk Mills, Inc. v. NLRB, 185 F.2d 732 (D.C. Cir. 1950), this Court held that an employer's good-faith doubt about a union's majority status is a relevant factor in determining whether the employer has committed an unfair labor practice. Similarly, in NLRB v. San Diego Gas & Electric Co., 325 F.2d 237 (9th Cir. 1963), the Ninth Circuit held that an employer's poll of employees was lawful when conducted in good faith and with proper safeguards.

The NLRB's decision in this case is arbitrary and capricious, as it failed to consider the overwhelming objective evidence of the loss of majority support for the union. The Court should reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is not rational and consistent with the NLRA, and the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The Court should defer to the more reasonable standard established by the courts, which allows employers to poll employees with "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support."
