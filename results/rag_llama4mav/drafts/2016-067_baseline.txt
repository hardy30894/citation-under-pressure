The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The key issue in this case is whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander), a company that purchased defaulted car loans from CitiFinancial Auto, is a "debt collector" under the FDCPA. The petitioners argue that Santander is a debt collector because it acquired the debts after they had defaulted. However, this argument is flawed.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another. The statute distinguishes between a debt collector and a creditor based on whether the person acts in an agency relationship with the person to whom the borrower is indebted. See Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). A creditor, on the other hand, is a person to whom the debt is owed, and when a creditor collects its debt for its own account, it is not generally acting as a debt collector.

The petitioners' argument relies on a misinterpretation of the statutory definitions. They claim that because Santander acquired the debts after they had defaulted, it must be a debt collector. However, the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition set forth in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). See Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 136 (4th Cir. 2016). The determination is based on whether a person collects debt on behalf of others or for its own account.

The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the plaintiffs' loans on December 1, 2011, and thereafter attempted to collect on the loans for its own account. This allegation is consistent with public SEC filings, which reveal that Santander purchased $3.55 billion in loan receivables from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011. As Santander was collecting debts for its own account, it was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector.

The petitioners' interpretation would create a loophole in the FDCPA, allowing an entity acting as a debt collector while servicing defaulted debts to become a creditor simply by purchasing the defaulted debt. However, this interpretation is not supported by the statutory text. See Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, L.P.A., 559 U.S. 573 (2010). The FDCPA regulates debt collectors, not creditors, and the statute's definitions are designed to capture the conduct of those who regularly collect debts on behalf of others.

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting debts for its own account, not on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.
