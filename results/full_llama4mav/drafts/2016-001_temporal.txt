The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, rather than limiting damages to the profits attributable to the infringing design elements.

The governing statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that a design patent infringer shall be liable to the owner "to the extent of his total profit" from the infringing article. However, this provision must be interpreted in light of the principle that damages should be apportioned to reflect the relative value of the infringing design. As the Supreme Court held in Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), when a patented feature is not the sole basis for the infringer's profits, the patentee is not entitled to the entirety of those profits.

In Westinghouse, the Court considered a case involving a patent for an improvement to an electric motor. The infringer argued that the damages award should be limited to the profits attributable to the patented improvement, rather than the entire profits from the sale of the motor. The Court agreed, holding that "the complainant is entitled to recover such gains and profits as have resulted from the unlawful use of [the patented] invention" (id. at 623, quoting Coupe v. Royer, 155 U.S. 565, 582 (1895)). This principle is directly applicable to the present case, where the infringing design elements are only a component of the Samsung smartphones.

Furthermore, in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), the Supreme Court held that when a patent covers only an improvement to a larger device, the patentee is entitled only to the profits attributable to that improvement. The Court stated that "the patentee must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features" (id. at 121). This principle is relevant here, as the design patents at issue cover only specific design elements of the Samsung smartphones, not the entire device.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits. The correct approach is to apportion damages to reflect the relative value of the infringing design elements, rather than awarding the entirety of Samsung's profits from the sale of its smartphones. We urge the Court to reverse the Federal Circuit's decision and remand for a recalculation of damages in accordance with this principle.
