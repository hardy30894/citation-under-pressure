**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for the United States Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—is governed by settled constitutional principles established long before the adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing additional qualifications on candidates for federal office, violates the exclusive and exclusive nature of the qualifications set forth in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3 of the United States Constitution. This Court has consistently held that the Constitution’s framers intended the qualifications for federal office to be uniform and exclusive, and that states may not add to or alter them. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court must be affirmed.

**I. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Non-Delegable**

The Constitution explicitly prescribes the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the sole and exclusive standards by which federal officeholders must be judged. As this Court held in *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, which conflict with it, are void.” The qualifications for Congress are part of the Constitution’s framework, and no state may impose additional or different requirements.

In *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1, 45 (1849), this Court emphasized that the Constitution “is a fundamental law, and the only one which can be enforced by the courts.” The qualifications for Congress are not subject to state modification because they are “expressed in the Constitution itself,” and “no state can alter or add to them.” The framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to vest the power to regulate qualifications for federal office in the national government alone. As Justice Story observed in *The Amistad*, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 518, 600 (1841), “The Constitution is a compact between the people of the several States, and the powers granted are not to be enlarged by the States, but are to be strictly construed.”

**II. The States’ Power to Regulate State Elections Does Not Extend to Federal Officeholders**

The argument that states may regulate the qualifications of federal officeholders under their police powers or their authority over elections is foreclosed by precedent. In *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 345 (1879), this Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, which conflict with it, are void.” The Constitution’s qualifications for Congress are not subject to state regulation, even in the context of elections. The power to regulate elections for federal office is not plenary in the states; it is limited by the Constitution’s express provisions.

In *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1, 24 (1892), this Court affirmed that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “the qualifications for electors of presidential electors are prescribed by the Constitution.” The Court further stated that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, which conflict with it, are void.” The same principle applies to the qualifications for members of Congress. The Constitution’s framers intended that the qualifications for federal office be uniform and exclusive, and that no state could impose additional requirements.

**III. The Arkansas Amendment Violates the Supremacy Clause**

The Supremacy Clause of Article VI of the Constitution provides that “this Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof… shall be the supreme Law of the Land.” The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment directly conflicts with the Constitution’s express qualifications for Congress. By barring individuals who have served three terms in the House or two in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, the amendment creates a qualification not found in the Constitution. This is a direct violation of the Supremacy Clause.

In *Ware v. Hylton*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 199, 237 (1796), this Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “all laws, state or federal, which conflict with it, are void.” The Arkansas amendment is a law of a state that conflicts with the Constitution, and therefore it is void. The Court in *Fletcher v. Peck*, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87, 136 (1810), emphasized that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law can be valid if it contradicts the Constitution.” The Arkansas amendment contradicts the Constitution’s qualifications for Congress, and is therefore invalid.

**IV. The Amendment’s Severability Clause Cannot Save It**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the severability clause in the amendment cannot save the unconstitutional provisions. In *Henderson v. United States*, 98 U.S. 245, 250 (1878), this Court stated that “a law which is in conflict with the Constitution is void, and cannot be saved by a severability clause.” The Court in *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1875), held that “a law which is in conflict with the Constitution is void, and cannot be saved by a severability clause.” The Arkansas amendment is in conflict with the Constitution, and therefore it is void.

Moreover, the amendment was presented to the voters as an “all or nothing” package. In *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185, 190 (1958), the Arkansas Supreme Court held that “a constitutional amendment which is presented to the voters as a single package cannot be severed if the voters did not have the opportunity to vote on the parts separately.” The Arkansas amendment was presented as a single package, and the voters did not have the opportunity to vote on the state and federal provisions separately. Therefore, the severability clause cannot save the amendment.
