# ARGUMENT

Respondent Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim satisfies the "cause" requirement of this Court's procedural-default doctrine, notwithstanding that the Ohio courts rejected his application to reopen his direct appeal as untimely. The court of appeals correctly held that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may excuse the default of a separate substantive claim, because the comity and federalism concerns that animate the cause-and-prejudice standard were fully satisfied when Carpenter presented his Sixth Amendment claim to the Ohio courts as an independent claim, even though that presentation ultimately failed on procedural grounds.

## I. The Cause Requirement Is Satisfied When the Ineffective-Assistance Claim Has Been Fairly Presented to the State Courts, Regardless of the Outcome of That Presentation.

This Court's procedural-default jurisprudence rests on principles of comity: a federal habeas court will not upset a state judgment on a ground the state courts had no opportunity to address. See *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U. S. 478 (1986). In *Carrier*, the respondent's procedural default of a discovery claim was potentially excused by an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim that he raised for the first time in his federal habeas petition, after the State asserted that "respondent had not exhausted his state remedies because he could bring an ineffective assistance of counsel claim in the state courts to establish that his procedural default should be excused." *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U. S. 478 (1986). The concern animating that exhaustion principle is not that the excusing claim must succeed in state court, but that the state court must be given the opportunity to adjudicate it in the first instance.

That opportunity was afforded here. Carpenter did not, as the petitioner in *Carrier* originally did, raise his ineffective-assistance claim for the first time in federal court. He instead petitioned the Ohio Court of Appeals to reopen his direct appeal specifically on the ground that appellate counsel had been constitutionally ineffective in failing to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence. The Ohio Court of Appeals considered and rejected that application, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. The state courts thus had a full and fair opportunity to pass on the ineffective-assistance claim as an independent matter. Nothing in this Court's cause-and-prejudice jurisprudence requires more.

This is consistent with the principle recognized in *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U. S. 255 (1989), that the exhaustion inquiry "necessarily applies only when a state court has been presented with the federal claim," and that "a federal habeas court need not require that a federal claim be presented to a state court if it is clear that the state court would hold the claim procedurally barred." *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U. S. 255 (1989). If exhaustion may be dispensed with entirely where presentation to the state courts would be futile, it follows a fortiori that exhaustion is satisfied where, as here, the claim was actually presented to the state courts and adjudicated, even unsuccessfully. To hold otherwise would impose a more demanding requirement on a defaulted claim that was raised and rejected than on one that was never raised at all — an anomalous result the exhaustion doctrine cannot sensibly be read to require.

## II. Imposing a Freestanding Cause-and-Prejudice Requirement on the Excusing Claim Itself Would Produce an Unworkable Rule Inconsistent With the Equitable Character of Habeas Review.

Petitioner's position — that an ineffective-assistance claim used as "cause" must itself satisfy an independent showing of cause and prejudice before it may excuse the default of another claim — would generate an infinite regress that this Court's precedents do not contemplate. Habeas corpus has traditionally been governed by equitable principles that guard against rigid, mechanical application of procedural bars where the underlying values of finality and comity are not meaningfully served. See *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U. S. 722 (1991) (adopting, in the adequate-and-independent-state-ground context, a presumption designed to avoid federal courts inferring procedural bars the state courts did not "clearly and expressly" invoke). The same equitable concern for practical, non-mechanical application of default rules counsels against layering successive cause requirements onto an ineffective-assistance claim that has already been presented to, and rejected by, the state courts.

This Court's decision in *Terrell v. Morris*, 493 U. S. 1 (1989), illustrates the danger of rigid retroactive application of procedural bars. There, the Ohio courts applied a timeliness rule that postdated the petitioner's direct appeal, even though "Terrell thus could not have known that he would default his ineffective-assistance claim." *Terrell v. Morris*, 493 U. S. 1 (1989). Carpenter's situation is analogous: he was represented by new counsel in state post-conviction proceedings and promptly sought to reopen his direct appeal once he learned of appellate counsel's failure to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence. To deny him the benefit of that claim as cause — merely because the Ohio courts deemed the reopening application untimely — would penalize him for the very default his ineffective-assistance claim seeks to explain, without regard to whether the state courts actually had a fair opportunity to consider the claim, which they did.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
