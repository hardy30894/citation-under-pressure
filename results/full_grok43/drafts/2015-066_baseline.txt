**ARGUMENT**

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penaliz[es] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 747 (1994). It therefore does not place the predicate tribal convictions “in issue” at the guilt phase. *Id.* at 748. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel “does not obtain” in tribal proceedings that result in no imprisonment, *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 372 (1979), and the Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes,” *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Because Bryant’s tribal convictions “do[] not violate the Sixth Amendment,” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47, they may serve as predicates under § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding rests on the premise that an uncounseled conviction valid when rendered becomes “constitutionally infirm” when used to prove an element. That premise is irreconcilable with *Nichols*. There the Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor may be used to enhance punishment so long as the conviction itself complied with *Scott*. The same rule governs here: the statute “accommodates” reliability concerns by requiring proof of the predicates beyond a reasonable doubt while leaving the underlying tribal judgments untouched. *See Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747.

Tribal sovereignty reinforces the point. Congress’s “plenary power” over Indian affairs, *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004), permits it to treat tribal convictions as predicates without imposing the full Sixth Amendment on tribes. *See United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978). The Indian Civil Rights Act supplies the governing procedural floor; federal courts may not “exercise unnecessary judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance.” *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir. 1997).

*Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), confirms the distinction between conviction and sentence. A sentence of imprisonment imposed on an uncounseled misdemeanor may be invalid, but “the underlying misdemeanor conviction surely is not.” *Id.* at 661-62. Every other circuit to address the question has therefore permitted the use of valid tribal convictions as § 117(a) predicates. The Ninth Circuit’s rule, by contrast, “intrude[s] needlessly on tribal self-government.” *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
