**Argument**

Johnson announced a substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review. The Supreme Court has long held that new substantive rules—those that “alter[] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes”—apply retroactively because they necessarily carry a “significant risk that a defendant faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” See Bouie v. City of Columbia, 378 U.S. 347 (1964); Giaccio v. Pennsylvania, 382 U.S. 399 (1965). By contrast, retroactivity is denied only to new procedural rules, which regulate the manner of determining guilt but do not “necessarily carry a significant risk” of an unlawful sentence. See Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969); Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963).

Johnson is substantive because it invalidated one of ACCA’s three paths to the “violent felony” designation. The residual clause—covering offenses that “otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—was not merely a difficult standard to apply; it was void for vagueness. As this Court explained in Bouie, due process requires “fair warning of the conduct which [a criminal statute] prohibits.” 378 U.S. at 350. And in Giaccio, the Court invalidated a law that failed to provide “definite standards” and thus invited “arbitrary and discriminatory” enforcement. 382 U.S. at 403-04. Johnson stands in the same line: it held that the residual clause failed to give ordinary people fair notice and invited arbitrary judicial guessing. That is a substantive due process ruling, not a procedural one.

The government’s contrary position cannot be reconciled with the Court’s retroactivity cases. In Williams v. Kaiser, 323 U.S. 471 (1945), Reynolds v. Cochran, 365 U.S. 525 (1961), and Gideon, the Court recognized that convictions obtained without constitutional authority cannot stand, even on collateral review. So too here. Once Johnson removed the residual clause, some defendants—like Welch—no longer qualify as armed career criminals unless their prior convictions satisfy ACCA’s remaining clauses. A rule that narrows the class of persons eligible for a mandatory fifteen-year sentence is substantive by definition.

Nor is this a case about a mere change in evidentiary or procedural rules. Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196 (1948), makes clear that due process requires a conviction and sentence to rest on the offense actually charged and proven. Welch’s sentence rested, in part, on a clause the Supreme Court has now said could not constitutionally support punishment. Because Johnson removed that invalid basis, Welch is serving a sentence the law no longer authorizes. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. See Bouie, 378 U.S. 347; Giaccio, 382 U.S. 399.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach also conflicts with the Court’s insistence that unconstitutional criminal laws cannot be enforced retroactively merely because the state obtained a final judgment. In Lynumn v. Illinois, 372 U.S. 528 (1963), the Court rejected a state’s effort to salvage a conviction obtained by unconstitutional means. In Gilbert v. California, 384 U.S. 985 (1966), the Court similarly recognized that constitutional violations in the investigatory and adjudicatory process can invalidate the resulting conviction. Johnson’s invalidation of ACCA’s residual clause is even more fundamental: it withdrew statutory authority for a class of enhanced sentences.

Finally, the retroactive application of Johnson follows from the nature of vagueness doctrine itself. A vague criminal law is invalid because it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice,” Bouie, 378 U.S. at 351, and because it permits punishment without “definite standards,” Giaccio, 382 U.S. at 403. When the Court declares such a provision void, it does not announce a new procedural preference; it declares that the law was incapable of supporting punishment in the first place. That is why Johnson applies retroactively to Welch’s collateral challenge.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that Johnson announced a substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review and reverse the judgment below.
