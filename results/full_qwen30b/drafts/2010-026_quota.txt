**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of a broad “predominantly internal” standard, divorced from the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent, improperly expands Exemption 2 beyond its narrow scope. The ESQD maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), and their disclosure does not present a cognizable risk of circumvention. This Court should reverse.

**I. Exemption 2 Applies Only to Rules and Practices Governing Agency Personnel, Not Technical Safety Data**

Exemption 2 shields from disclosure only information that is “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that this exemption is narrowly construed and must be interpreted in light of its statutory text and legislative history. *Rose v. Dep’t of Air Force*, 425 U.S. 352, 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592, 48 L.Ed.2d 11 (1976) (“Exemptions are to be interpreted narrowly.”); *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841, 106 L.Ed.2d 112 (1989) (“The nine exemptions are explicitly exclusive.”). The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from the D.C. Circuit’s *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc)—is inconsistent with this principle. *Crooker* itself acknowledged that the “predominantly internal” test was not a statutory mandate but a judicial gloss, and it was never adopted by this Court. The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Crooker* to expand Exemption 2 beyond its textual bounds is misplaced.

The ESQD arc maps at issue here are not “rules and practices” of any kind. They are technical safety calculations used to determine safe storage distances for explosives. As the Navy’s own commander stated, these maps are “used to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not internal personnel practice; it is engineering and operational planning. *Maricopa Audubon Soc. v. United States Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997), held that goshawk nesting site data—like the ESQD maps—did not “relate ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.’” Id. (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2)). The same logic applies here: ESQD maps are not internal rules; they are technical data used to ensure public and operational safety.

**II. The “Risk of Circumvention” Standard Requires a Direct Link to Law Enforcement or Regulatory Enforcement**

Even if the documents were internal, Exemption 2 requires a showing that disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Schwaner v. Dep’t of Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793, 794 (D.C. Cir. 1990). This risk must be real and substantial, not speculative. *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), held that Exemption 2 applies to “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” But the ESQD maps are not law enforcement materials. They were not compiled for enforcement or adjudicative purposes. *Church of Scientology v. IRS*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979) (holding that materials not compiled for law enforcement purposes are not exempt under Exemption 7(F), and by extension, not under Exemption 2). The Navy’s own affidavit confirms this: the maps are used for “design, array, and construction” of storage facilities, not for enforcement.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that disclosure risks circumvention rests on speculation. Commander Whitbred’s affidavit claims that an adversary could “reverse engineer” the maps to discover “particular ammunition, explosive[s and] weapons systems.” But this is not a risk of circumventing regulation—it is a risk of revealing technical capabilities. That risk is properly addressed under Exemption 1, not Exemption 2. *U.S. Dep’t of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173, 112 S.Ct. 541, 116 L.Ed.2d 526 (1991) (agency bears burden of proving exemption applies). The Navy has not classified these documents, nor has it demonstrated that their release would jeopardize national defense. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841 (Exemption 1 applies only to classified information). The failure to classify is fatal to any claim of national security risk.
