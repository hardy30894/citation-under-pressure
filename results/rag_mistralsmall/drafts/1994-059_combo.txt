**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT IMPOSES ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESS IN VIOLATION OF THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.**

The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution are exclusive and preempt state attempts to add to them. *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58, 65 (1900) (“The office [of Representative] . . . is created by [the federal Constitution] and by that alone. It also declares how it shall be filled, namely, by election.”). The Constitution sets the sole qualifications for service in Congress: age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2 (House); id. art. I, § 3, cl. 3 (Senate). Any state-imposed requirement that goes beyond these—including a state’s attempt to disqualify candidates based on prior service—is void. *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 615 (1929) (Senate’s power to judge elections, returns, and qualifications is exclusive and not subject to state interference).

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does exactly that. It bars from the ballot any candidate who has served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate. Ark. Const. amend. 73, § 3. This is not a procedural regulation of elections; it is a substantive qualification. *Wiley*, 179 U.S. at 65. The Court has long held that the Qualifications Clauses are not subject to state augmentation. *Newberry v. United States*, 256 U.S. 232, 256 (1921) (state cannot impose qualifications for federal office beyond those in the Constitution). The fact that Arkansas couches its disqualification in ballot-access terms does not change its nature. *Brockington v. Rhodes*, 396 U.S. 41, 42 (1969) (ballot-access rules must not “effectively eliminate” a candidate’s opportunity to run).

The Court has repeatedly invalidated state-imposed qualifications that conflict with the federal Constitution. *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221, 227 (1920) (state referendum cannot nullify a congressionally proposed constitutional amendment). Just as a state may not add to the ratification process, it may not add to the qualifications for office. *Fortson v. Morris*, 385 U.S. 231, 232 (1966) (state may not alter the manner of choosing electors for federal office). The uniformity of congressional qualifications is essential to the federal structure. *Wiley*, 179 U.S. at 65.

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY DEEMED SECTION 3 UNCONSTITUTIONAL AND SEVERABLE.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that Section 3 violated the federal Constitution and was severable from Sections 1 and 2, which imposed term limits on state officers. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 312 Ark. 186, 207 (1993). This severability determination is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 752 (1964) (severability clause is enforced unless the unconstitutional portion is “so interwoven” with the rest that the legislature would not have enacted the remainder alone). The Arkansas court found no such interdependence. *Thornton*, 312 Ark. at 207.

This Court has long respected severability clauses in state constitutional amendments. *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 424 (1958) (applying severability clause to uphold part of an amendment while striking another). The presence of a severability clause is a strong indication that the voters would have approved the valid portions even without the invalid ones. *Combs*, 237 Ark. at 752. The Arkansas court’s severance of Section 3 from Sections 1 and 2 was proper.

**III. THE ABSENCE OF AN ENACTING CLAUSE DOES NOT INVALIDATE THE AMENDMENT.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court also invalidated Amendment 73 for lack of an Enacting Clause. Ark. Const. amend. 7. However, this defect is procedural and does not implicate federal constitutional concerns. *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. at 227 (state procedural rules do not bind federal constitutional amendments). The Enacting Clause requirement is a matter of state law, not federal. *Wirtz v. Local Union No. 125*, 389 U.S. 477, 482 (1968) (federal courts do not enforce state procedural rules in federal constitutional matters).

Moreover, the Arkansas Constitution permits amendments to be proposed by initiative, and the full text of the amendment was published before the election. Ark. Const. amend. 7. The absence of an Enacting Clause does not deprive the amendment of legal effect where the text is otherwise valid. *Hughes v. WMCA, Inc.*, 379 U.S. 694, 700 (1965) (procedural irregularities in state law do not invalidate a duly enacted constitutional provision).

**CONCLUSION**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment violates the federal Constitution by imposing additional qualifications for Congress. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly severed the unconstitutional portion and upheld the rest. The absence of an Enacting Clause is a state-law defect that does not affect the amendment’s validity. The judgment below should be affirmed.
