**ARGUMENT**

## I. The severance agreements were not a valid WARN Act remedy because DHL did not comply with the Act’s mandatory notice scheme.

The WARN Act requires an employer to give affected employees and local government officials at least 60 days’ written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). Congress enacted that notice requirement to give workers “some transition time to adjust to the prospective loss of employment” and to seek alternative jobs or retraining. *See* *Bader v. Northern Line Layers, Inc.*, 503 F.3d 813, 817 (9th Cir. 2007); *United Paperworkers Int’l Union, Local 340 v. Specialty Paperboard, Inc.*, 999 F.2d 51, 53 (2d Cir. 1993). WARN’s protections are not satisfied by a post-notice severance bargain that depends on employees’ surrender of statutory claims. *See* *Bader*, 503 F.3d at 817-18; *United Mine Workers of Am. v. Martinka Coal Co.*, 44 F.3d 478, 483-84 (7th Cir. 1995).

Here, DHL announced on November 10, 2008, that it would end U.S. domestic shipping on January 30, 2009. That announcement triggered WARN obligations for the Chicagoland facilities whose work was overwhelmingly domestic-parcel handling. Yet rather than comply with the Act’s notice and backpay remedy, DHL and Local 705 negotiated severance packages that required employees to sign away WARN rights. The releases expressly included “the Federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act.” That is precisely the sort of end-run around statutory protections WARN forbids. A statutory right designed to protect workers as a class cannot be nullified by a waiver procured as part of a severance transaction induced by the employer’s own failure to provide notice. *See* *Lloyd v. Local 881, United Food & Commercial Workers Union*, 37 F.3d 1218, 1222-23 (7th Cir. 1994) (statutory employment rights are not lightly waived and waiver must be knowing and voluntary); *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1, 4-7 (1966) (waiver of rights may not be presumed from ambiguous conduct).

## II. The district court erred in treating the severance releases as an effective waiver of WARN claims.

A waiver of federal statutory employment rights must be knowing and voluntary. *Lloyd*, 37 F.3d at 1222-23; *Wright v. Universal Maritime Service Corp.*, 525 U.S. 70, 80-81 (1998). WARN claims are especially unsuitable for casual waiver because the statute confers rights on affected employees and serves a public policy of advance notice to the workforce and community. *See* *Martinka Coal*, 44 F.3d at 483-84; *Bader*, 503 F.3d at 817.

The releases here were not the product of an informed, voluntary relinquishment of a matured claim. Employees had only seven days to consider a document drafted by DHL, were instructed to sign promptly in order to receive severance, and were told they were giving up “any and all” claims, including WARN claims. That pressure is inconsistent with a valid waiver of federal rights. *See* *Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co.*, 415 U.S. 36, 52 n.15 (1974) (“an employee’s rights under Title VII are not susceptible of prospective waiver”); *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80-81.

Nor does the fact that the releases recited that the severance was “beyond anything to which I am already or otherwise entitled” solve the problem. A release induced by payment of consideration cannot waive a statutory entitlement where the consideration is itself offered in exchange for surrender of the right. *See* *Dice v. Akron, Canton & Youngstown R.R. Co.*, 342 U.S. 359, 361-62 (1952) (waivers of federal employment rights are subject to special scrutiny); *Hogue v. Southern Ry. Co.*, 390 U.S. 516, 517-18 (1968).

## III. The agreements were not negotiated on behalf of the affected class in a way that extinguished the employees’ WARN claims.

Even if a union may bargain over plant-closing effects, WARN rights do not vanish merely because a union negotiates severance after an announced shutdown. The Act contemplates that employees will receive timely advance notice, and nothing in the statute authorizes an employer to condition severance on the surrender of WARN claims after the employer has already decided to close. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a); *Bader*, 503 F.3d at 817-18. The union’s participation therefore does not transform DHL’s post-announcement bargaining into statutory compliance.

The district court’s contrary approach also conflicts with the policy behind WARN’s remedial scheme. Congress created a backpay remedy for violations. 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1). Allowing an employer to buy a release after the violation is apparent would convert the statutory remedy into a bargaining chip and defeat the deterrent function of the Act. *See* *Martinka Coal*, 44 F.3d at 483-84; *Bader*, 503 F.3d at 817.

## IV. The class claims remain viable because the releases cannot bar WARN liability for all affected employees.

The named appellants challenge DHL’s conduct on behalf of employees who were presented with the severance releases under the same coercive circumstances. The releases were standardized, not individualized, and each expressly named WARN. That uniformity underscores the legal flaw: DHL used the same form to extinguish statutory claims across the workforce. A common release cannot validate a common violation. *See* *Lloyd*, 37 F.3d at 1222-23; *Wright*, 525 U.S. at 80-81.

Because DHL failed to satisfy WARN’s notice requirement and because the severance agreements did not constitute valid waivers of federal statutory rights, the judgment should be reversed.
