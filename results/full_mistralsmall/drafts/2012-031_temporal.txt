**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it rests on an unsupported assumption that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This predominance requirement cannot be satisfied where the plaintiffs offer no admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) (“We find nothing in either the language or history of Rule 23 that gives a court any authority to conduct a preliminary inquiry into the merits of a suit in order to determine whether it may be maintained as a class action.”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* does not alter this fundamental requirement. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008) (citing *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177).

Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class based on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which the court acknowledged was “unprecedented” and “highly speculative.” Behrend v. Comcast Corp., 264 F.R.D. 150, 190 (E.D. Pa. 2010). The court’s reliance on this model violates the longstanding principle that class certification cannot rest on “speculative” or “unreliable” methodologies. *Dailey v. Societe Generale*, 108 F.R.D. 201, 205 (S.D.N.Y. 1985) (citing *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177). As this Court has recognized, “[t]he predominance requirement is not satisfied where damages must be calculated on an individual basis.” *In re Warfarin Sodium Antitrust Litig.*, 391 F. Supp. 1013, 1020 (D. Minn. 1975) (citing *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177).

The District Court’s certification order also conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931), which held that damages in antitrust cases must be proven with reasonable certainty. The plaintiffs’ damages model fails this standard because it relies on assumptions about hypothetical competition that cannot be verified. *Id.* at 563 (“Where the tort itself is of such a nature as to preclude the ascertainment of the amount of damages with certainty, it would be a perversion of fundamental principles of justice to deny all relief to the injured person, and thereby relieve the wrongdoer from making any compensation for his acts.”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* does not excuse this failure. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (requiring rigorous analysis but not abandoning Rule 23’s predominance requirement).

II. The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it fails to ensure that common questions predominate over individual ones.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that common questions predominate over individual ones. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The District Court’s certification order does not satisfy this requirement because it allows individual damages calculations to overwhelm the common issues. *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177 (“Rule 23 does not permit a district court to ‘conduct a preliminary inquiry into the merits of a suit in order to determine whether it may be maintained as a class action.’”).

The plaintiffs’ damages model requires individualized inquiries into each class member’s cable service history, subscription choices, and hypothetical alternatives. This individualized inquiry defeats predominance. *In re Rhone-Poulenc Rorer Inc.*, 51 F.3d 1293, 1298 (7th Cir. 1995) (citing *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177) (“The need for individual hearings on damages precludes a finding of predominance under Rule 23(b)(3).”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* does not alter this principle. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (requiring rigorous analysis but not abandoning Rule 23’s predominance requirement).

The District Court’s certification order also conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32 (1940), which held that class actions must ensure that individual rights are not sacrificed for the sake of convenience. The plaintiffs’ damages model sacrifices individual rights by forcing class members to rely on a speculative methodology. *Id.* at 42 (“The judgment in the prior case is not, therefore, conclusive as to the rights of the plaintiffs in the present suit, and cannot be used as a collateral estoppel against them.”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* does not excuse this failure. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (requiring rigorous analysis but not abandoning Rule 23’s predominance requirement).

III. The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it fails to ensure that the class action is superior to other methods of adjudication.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that a class action be superior to other methods of adjudication. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The District Court’s certification order does not satisfy this requirement because it forces class members to rely on a speculative damages model that cannot be verified. *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177 (“Rule 23 does not permit a district court to ‘conduct a preliminary inquiry into the merits of a suit in order to determine whether it may be maintained as a class action.’”).

The plaintiffs’ damages model is so speculative that it cannot be verified through individual trials. This defeats the superiority requirement. *In re Rhone-Poulenc Rorer Inc.*, 51 F.3d at 1298 (citing *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177) (“The need for individual hearings on damages precludes a finding of superiority under Rule 23(b)(3).”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* does not alter this principle. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (requiring rigorous analysis but not abandoning Rule 23’s superiority requirement).

The District Court’s certification order also conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Cooper v. Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond*, 467 U.S. 867 (1984), which held that class actions must ensure that class members’ rights are not sacrificed for the sake of convenience. The plaintiffs’ damages model sacrifices class members’ rights by forcing them to rely on a speculative methodology. *Id.* at 876 (“The class action device was designed as an exception to the usual rule that litigation is conducted by and on behalf of the individual named parties only.”). The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* does not excuse this failure. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318 (requiring rigorous analysis but not abandoning Rule 23’s superiority requirement).
