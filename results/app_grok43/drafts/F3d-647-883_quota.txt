I. The Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the Certiorari Clock.

A judgment of conviction becomes final for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1) when the time for seeking certiorari review expires or the Supreme Court denies review. Clay v. United States, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003). The 90-day period prescribed by Supreme Court Rule 13(1) runs from the date of the denial of rehearing “if the lower court appropriately entertains” a petition for rehearing. Sup. Ct. R. 13(3). This Court’s June 12, 2007 order, which construed and denied Buckles’s timely Rule 4-1(e) motion and recalled the mandate, constituted the operative final action from which the certiorari period began anew. See Gonzalez v. Thaler, 565 U.S. 134, 150 (2012) (finality turns on the last “order resolving the case”). Because Buckles filed his petition within 90 days of that order, the petition was timely and his § 2255 motion was therefore timely as well.

II. Equitable Tolling Applies.

Even if the certiorari petition was untimely, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling. A habeas petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling if he shows “(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way.” Holland v. Florida, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (quoting Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005)). Misinformation from a court clerk constitutes an extraordinary circumstance when the petitioner reasonably relies on it. See Lawrence v. Florida, 549 U.S. 327, 336 (2007); see also Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990). Buckles’s sister was told by the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” Buckles reasonably relied on that representation in calculating his filing deadline.

Attorney abandonment likewise supports tolling. Maples v. Thomas, 565 U.S. 266, 283 (2012). Retained counsel failed to assist Buckles despite the duty imposed by Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) and despite Buckles’s explicit request, leaving him without effective representation during the critical period. See also United States v. Kwai Fun Wong, 575 U.S. 402, 408 (2015) (equitable tolling available where claimant was “induced or tricked” by government conduct). Buckles acted with diligence by repeatedly attempting to contact counsel, filing his own motion, and seeking clarification from the Supreme Court. Because the district court failed to consider these facts, the dismissal must be reversed.
