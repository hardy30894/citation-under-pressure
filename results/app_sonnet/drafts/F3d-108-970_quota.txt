**ARGUMENT**

**I. Sieben Articulated Legitimate, Nondiscriminatory Reasons for Its Actions, and Feltmann Failed to Prove Those Reasons Were a Pretext for Sex Discrimination.**

Title VII claims proceed under the familiar burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). The plaintiff bears the "initial burden . . . of establishing a prima facie case," *id.* at 802, after which the burden shifts to the employer to articulate "some legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason" for its conduct. *Id.* That burden, the Supreme Court has explained, "is not onerous." *Texas Dep't of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). Once the employer satisfies it, the plaintiff must prove that the stated reason was pretextual and that discrimination was the real reason. *St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993). Critically, "[i]t is not enough . . . for the [factfinder] to disbelieve the employer; the [factfinder] must believe the plaintiff's explanation of intentional discrimination." *Id.*

Sieben met its burden at every turn. Rich placed Feltmann on probation because her gross-profit-per-sale figures were the lowest of any consultant for five consecutive months—a performance metric, not a gender classification. Rich restricted demo-car use because declining sales after the incentive program ended made the dealership cost-conscious, and he sent Feltmann a personal reminder only because she continued driving the car 300 miles outside the permitted area, months after the restriction issued. Neither the Chicago sales-trip decision nor the demo-car memorandum was tied to Feltmann's sex; Rich did not extend the Chicago invitation to several male consultants either.

Feltmann offered nothing beyond her own suspicion to rebut these explanations. That is insufficient. Moreover, several of the acts she complains of—an unwelcome memo about her sales technique, exclusion from a discretionary sales trip, a demo-car reminder—do not even rise to the level of an actionable adverse employment action. As this Court has held, "[n]ot everything that makes an employee unhappy is actionable adverse action." *Krenik v. County of Le Sueur*, 47 F.3d 953, 960 (8th Cir. 1995). An adverse employment action requires a tangible change in duties, compensation, or benefits, and minor workplace irritants do not suffice. *Harlston v. McDonnell Douglas Corp.*, 37 F.3d 379, 382 (8th Cir. 1994). Feltmann's pay was not cut, her title was not changed, and she was not terminated as a result of any of these events. The district court erred in permitting the jury to find pretext on this record.

**II. Feltmann Failed to Establish the Causal Connection Necessary to Sustain Her Retaliation Claim.**

A retaliation plaintiff must show (1) she engaged in protected activity, (2) an adverse employment action followed, and (3) a causal connection between the two. *Jackson v. Delta Special Sch. Dist. No. 2*, 86 F.3d 1489, 1494 (8th Cir. 1996). Feltmann's theory rests almost entirely on timing: she complained about Anzalone's comment in November 1992, and Rich's critical memo followed in January 1993, with probation following in March. But this Court has repeatedly cautioned that temporal proximity alone, without more, cannot establish the causal link Title VII requires. *Rath v. Selection Research, Inc.*, 978 F.2d 1087, 1090 (8th Cir. 1992). Here there is far more than mere proximity to explain the intervening events: Rich's own testimony, corroborated by sales records, shows Feltmann's average gross profit was the lowest among all consultants during precisely the period at issue. Sieben's owners, moreover, promptly investigated Feltmann's complaint, reprimanded Anzalone, and warned him that a repeat incident would result in termination—hardly the conduct of an employer bent on retaliating against the complainant. No further vulgar comments were ever made. Feltmann's own speculation that a co-worker's stray remark about a "personnel file" reflected retaliatory animus by management, rather than mere office gossip, cannot substitute for evidence of causation.

**III. The MHRA Claim Fails for the Same Reasons, and the Missouri Common-Law Claim Fails Because No Recognized Public-Policy Exception Applies.**

This Court has recognized that the elements of a discrimination or retaliation claim "are analyzed in the same manner" under the Missouri Human Rights Act as under Title VII. *Tart v. Hill Behan Lumber Co.*, 31 F.3d 668, 671 (8th Cir. 1994). Because Feltmann's federal claims fail as a matter of law, her MHRA claims fail for the identical reasons.

Feltmann's common-law claim likewise cannot stand. Missouri recognizes only a narrow public-policy exception to at-will employment, applicable where an employee is discharged for refusing to violate the law or for exercising a legally protected right in a manner clearly mandated by a constitutional provision, statute, or regulation. *Boyle v. Vista Eyewear, Inc.*, 700 S.W.2d 859, 871 (Mo. Ct. App. 1985). Feltmann was not discharged; she resigned. Even accepting her characterization of the workplace conditions, she has identified no clear mandate of Missouri public policy implicated by internal sales-performance criticism, exclusion from a discretionary trip, or enforcement of a demo-car policy. The common-law claim should have been dismissed as a matter of law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and judgment entered for Sieben, or, alternatively, a new trial should be ordered.
