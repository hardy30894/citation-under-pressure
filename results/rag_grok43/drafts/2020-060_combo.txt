The United States captured Guam in 1898 and exercised sovereignty through the Navy until 1950. Pearcy v. Stranahan, 205 U.S. 257, 260 (1907). Upon cession, “the municipal laws of the country, that is, laws which are intended for the protection of private rights, continue in force until abrogated or changed by the new government or sovereign.” Vilas v. City of Manila, 220 U.S. 345, 357 (1911). The Navy constructed and operated the Ordot Dump for military waste, including munitions and chemicals, and retained responsibility for resulting contamination. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845) (“Nothing remained to the United States, according to the terms of the agreement, but the public lands.”).

The Guam Organic Act transferred only civil administration; it did not extinguish federal liability for prior military pollution. Gonzales v. Williams, 192 U.S. 1, 13 (1904). “Whenever political jurisdiction and legislative power over any territory are transferred from one nation or sovereign to another, the municipal laws of the country * * * continue in force until abrogated or changed by the new government or sovereign.” Vilas, 220 U.S. at 357. The Navy’s continued use of the site through subsequent wars confirms that the United States never relinquished its obligations. Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad, 118 U.S. 394, 396 (1886).

Military tribunals and installations remain subject to federal oversight even after local governance begins. Duncan v. Kahanamoku, 327 U.S. 304, 313 (1946); Burns v. Wilson, 346 U.S. 137, 139 (1953). “The federal civil courts have jurisdiction over such applications.” Burns, 346 U.S. at 139. The same principle governs environmental liabilities arising from naval operations on ceded territory. Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock, 347 U.S. 110, 116 (1954) (procurement powers “shall be construed in the broadest manner possible”).

The 2004 consent decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations and did not resolve CERCLA liability. National Labor Relations Board v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 331 U.S. 416, 422 (1947) (“It is significant that the Board * * * has arrived at a result which coincides with that reached by the War Department.”). Guam therefore retains its cost-recovery action. Mora v. McNamara, 389 U.S. 934, 935 (1967) (questions of military activity and constitutional limits remain open for judicial resolution).

The Navy, not Guam, bears financial responsibility for the Ordot Dump hazards.
