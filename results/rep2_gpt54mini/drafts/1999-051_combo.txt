ARGUMENT

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim as “cause” to excuse the default of another habeas claim. Under settled doctrine, “cause” sufficient to overcome a procedural default must itself be an “external” factor, not a claim that has independently been forfeited in the state courts. Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), though later than the authorities discussed below, follows the preexisting rule that federal habeas review respects state procedural bars. The older cases teach the same point: a litigant cannot evade a default by relabeling the forfeited issue as another constitutional claim.

This Court has long held that failure to comply with state procedure bars federal review absent an adequate excuse. In Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court recognized that habeas relief may be denied where the petitioner has deliberately bypassed orderly state procedures. The same principle appears in Henry v. Mississippi, 379 U.S. 443 (1965), which permitted review only when the procedural failure did not amount to a purposeful relinquishment of state remedies. And in Davis v. United States, 411 U.S. 233 (1973), the Court reaffirmed that a default is not ignored merely because the underlying claim sounds constitutional; the petitioner must show a legally sufficient basis to overcome the forfeiture.

A procedurally defaulted ineffectiveness claim is not such a basis. Blackledge v. Perry, 417 U.S. 21 (1974), held that some constitutional claims may survive ordinary waiver rules, but it did so because the claim there attacked the integrity of the process itself and was not itself procedurally forfeited. By contrast, where the asserted excuse has been defaulted, there is no preserved constitutional ground to supply cause. That distinction follows from the general waiver cases of Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), and Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), which require an intentional relinquishment of a known right before waiver will be found. A claim that has been abandoned or defaulted under state law cannot simultaneously serve as an unexhausted, free-standing predicate for excusing another default.

Nor may a federal court create cause from a claim that was never properly presented to the state courts. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), emphasized that state courts must have the first opportunity to pass upon alleged federal errors. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), likewise recognized that federal habeas is not a substitute for orderly state review. If a defaulted ineffectiveness claim could always excuse another default, a petitioner could bypass state adjudication entirely simply by asserting a second, equally forfeited claim in federal court. That would invert the hierarchy these cases preserve.

The better rule is that cause must be independent of the defaulted claim. Reed v. Ross, 468 U.S. 1 (1984), and Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), later distilled the doctrine, but its roots are in the older cases requiring a genuine external impediment, not a second procedural failure. The respondent’s position is therefore faithful to the historic limits on collateral review. The petitioner may not bootstrap a defaulted ineffective-assistance allegation into cause for a different barred claim. If the ineffectiveness claim itself was not properly preserved, it cannot excuse the underlying default.

That rule also accords with the narrow office of habeas corpus recognized in Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241 (1886), and U.S. ex rel. Kennedy v. Tyler, 269 U.S. 13 (1925): federal intervention is exceptional, not an invitation to relitigate every forfeited issue. Respect for state procedure, finality, and comity requires that the ineffective-assistance claim be exhausted and preserved before it can excuse another procedural default. Because Carpenter’s ineffectiveness claim was itself procedurally defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to revive his defaulted sufficiency claim. Accordingly, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
