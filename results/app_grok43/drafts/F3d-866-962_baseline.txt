The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine issues of material fact exist on whether the plaintiffs’ protected activity contributed to BNSF’s adverse actions. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (summary judgment improper where “the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party”).

Under the FRSA, a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case by showing protected activity, an adverse action, and that the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse action. Kuduk v. BNSF Railway Co., 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014). Once shown, the burden shifts to the employer to prove by clear and convincing evidence that it would have taken the same action absent the protected activity. Id.

The plaintiffs engaged in protected activity when they reported the absence of side rails and lighting on the bridge. Kline and Snyder included these conditions in their April 2 statements to Trainmaster Dixon; Foster did so in her April 5 recorded statement to Claims Representative Murphy; and Kline and Snyder repeated them in their April 12–13 statements. These reports concerned a hazardous condition related to railroad safety and therefore fall within 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). See Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 789.

BNSF took adverse actions. It subjected Kline and Snyder to increased, unannounced operations testing that altered their work assignments and reduced earnings. It then imposed discipline after the January 2013 hearing: a formal reprimand and one-year review period for Snyder, a thirty-day record suspension and one-year review period for Kline, and termination for Foster. These actions “could well dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge” of unsafe conditions. Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 57 (2006).

A reasonable jury could find the protected activity was a contributing factor. The reports preceded the investigation notice by days and the discipline by nine months; the same managers who received the safety complaints (Dixon and Knutstrom) supplied the testimony that led to discipline; and the Public Law Board later vacated the discipline in full. See, e.g., Gunderson v. BNSF Railway Co., 850 F.3d 962, 968–69 (8th Cir. 2017) (temporal proximity plus evidence of pretext can establish contributing-factor causation). BNSF’s claim that the discipline rested solely on rule violations is undermined by the Board’s contrary determination and by the absence of any prior discipline for identical conduct.

Because the record permits a finding that the plaintiffs’ safety reports were a contributing factor in the adverse actions, summary judgment was improper.
