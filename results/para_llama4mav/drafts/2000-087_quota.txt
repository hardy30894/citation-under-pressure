The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court has already resolved this issue in Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), holding that the statute contains an implicit "reasonable time" limitation.

The government's argument that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention is unpersuasive. The statute's language, which allows detention "beyond the removal period," does not explicitly authorize indefinite detention. In the absence of clear congressional intent to permit such detention, the Court should construe the statute narrowly to avoid serious constitutional concerns, as required by the canon of constitutional avoidance (see, e.g., Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988)).

The Supreme Court's decision in Zadvydas v. Davis is controlling here. The Court held that "indefinite detention of aliens" ordered removed "would raise serious constitutional concerns" and therefore construed § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation (533 U.S. at 682). The Court's reasoning was grounded in the Due Process Clause, which prohibits arbitrary detention (see, e.g., Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992); United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 746 (1987)).

The government's reliance on Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. Mezei involved an alien who had not effected entry into the United States, whereas Zadvydas is a resident alien who has been ordered removed (see Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 693). The distinction between excludable and deportable aliens is significant, as the Court has recognized (see, e.g., Landon v. Plasencia, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982)).

Furthermore, the government's argument that the Attorney General has broad discretion to detain aliens pending removal is not supported by the statute. Section 1231(a)(6) does not grant the Attorney General unfettered authority to detain aliens indefinitely. Rather, the statute must be read in light of the Constitution's demands, which require a "reasonable time" limitation on detention (see Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 689).

The Court's decision in Clark v. Martinez, 543 U.S. 371 (2005), reinforces this interpretation. In Clark, the Court held that the same "reasonable time" limitation applies to aliens who are inadmissible under § 1182 (see Clark, 543 U.S. at 378). The Court's reasoning in Clark is consistent with its earlier decision in Zadvydas, which emphasized the importance of construing § 1231(a)(6) narrowly to avoid constitutional concerns.

Other cases, such as Demore v. Kim, 538 U.S. 510 (2003), and Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), are distinguishable. Demore involved detention during the removal proceedings, whereas Zadvydas concerns post-removal-period detention. Wong Wing, which held that aliens subject to deportation may be detained pending removal, does not address the issue of indefinite detention.

In conclusion, the post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court's decision in Zadvydas v. Davis is controlling, and the government's argument is unpersuasive in light of the Court's reasoning and relevant case law, including Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council, Foucha v. Louisiana, United States v. Salerno, Landon v. Plasencia, and Clark v. Martinez.
