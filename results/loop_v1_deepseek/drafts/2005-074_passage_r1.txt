## ARGUMENT

### I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date

The Immigration and Nationality Act's reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. This Court has long held that "words in a statute ought not to have a retrospective operation, unless they are so clear, strong, and imperative, that no other meaning can be annexed to them." *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806). Nothing in Section 1231(a)(5)'s text manifests such a clear intent to reach prior reentries. The statute provides that "if the Attorney General finds that an alien has reentered" illegally, the prior order shall be reinstated. This language speaks in the present tense concerning reentries occurring after enactment. This ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the alien, for "deportation, while it may be burdensome and severe," is a serious measure. *Mahler v. Eby*, 264 U.S. 32, 39 (1924). This Court has further instructed that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction" of deportation statutes. *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). Congress easily could have written "who reentered at any time" had it intended retroactive application. It did not. The statute therefore applies only to reentries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute Retroactively Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, such application would attach new legal consequences to past conduct, violating the bedrock principle against retroactive legislation. *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). This Court has declared that "a statute shall not be given retroactive effect, unless such construction is required by explicit language." *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). Here, no explicit language exists.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the law permitted him to later apply for adjustment of status if he married a United States citizen. See *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 639 (1954) (recognizing the availability of administrative hearings on adjustment eligibility). The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring any form of relief. This new bar alters the legal consequences of a reentry that was completed long before the statute's enactment. Such a change is impermissibly retroactive because it "impairs rights a party possessed when he acted." *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199.

This Court has consistently held that deportation statutes must not be applied retroactively to attach new penalties to past conduct. In *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947), the Court refused to apply a deportation ground to an alien who had returned to the United States before the ground's enactment, stating that "the law must be construed to be prospective only unless a contrary intent is clearly expressed." The same principle governs here. The reinstatement statute imposes a new, harsh consequence—foreclosing all relief—on petitioner's pre-1997 reentry.

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner lacked a "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status. An alien who reenters illegally does not forfeit all future claims to relief. *See Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10 (requiring "resolution of doubts in favor of that construction" favorable to the alien). The expectation of applying for adjustment under then-existing law was a legitimate right that the government could not retroactively extinguish without clear congressional command. *See Heth*, 3 Cranch at 413. No such command appears in Section 1231(a)(5).

Retroactive application also offends due process by upsetting settled reliance interests. When petitioner returned in 1982, the legal landscape permitted him to later seek adjustment if he married a citizen. The 1997 statute retroactively destroyed that avenue, making his earlier conduct the basis for permanent exclusion from relief. This is precisely the kind of retrospective change the presumption against retroactivity is designed to prevent. *St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. at 3.

For these reasons, Section 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. Even if it did, applying it retroactively would be impermissible. The petition for review should be granted.
