# ARGUMENT

## The Procedural Default of an Ineffective-Assistance-of-Counsel Claim Does Not Bar That Claim From Serving as Cause to Excuse the Default of Another Claim

### A. Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Constitutes "Cause" Because It Is an Objective Factor External to the Petitioner

This Court has long recognized that a procedural default may be excused upon a showing of "cause and prejudice." *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977). In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Court held that "cause" requires "some objective factor external to the defense" that impeded compliance with the State's procedural rule. *Id.* at 488. Constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel satisfies that standard precisely because "the exhaustion doctrine ... generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause," *id.* at 489, but the ineffectiveness itself remains an external impediment attributable to the State, not to the petitioner. As the Court explained, "[s]o long as a defendant is represented by counsel whose performance is not constitutionally ineffective under the standard established in *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), we discern no inequity in requiring him to bear the risk of attorney error." *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488. The necessary corollary is that where counsel's performance *does* fall below the *Strickland* standard, the petitioner should not bear that risk—regardless of whether a separate procedural default later attaches to the vehicle by which the ineffectiveness claim is raised.

*Strickland* itself makes clear that constitutionally deficient performance is a violation of the Sixth Amendment, not a matter of petitioner's own default. 466 U.S. at 687. Once counsel's failure to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is shown to be constitutionally deficient and prejudicial, the "cause" for the underlying default is established by an independent constitutional wrong—one that exists whether or not it was itself timely presented to the state courts.

### B. Requiring Independent Exhaustion of the Cause Claim Conflates the "Cause" Inquiry With Adjudication on the Merits

*Carrier*'s exhaustion language addressed only the orderly administration of habeas review, not a jurisdictional prerequisite to the cause-and-prejudice analysis. The exhaustion requirement of 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b) serves comity by "giv[ing] state courts the first opportunity to correct any constitutional violation." *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 518 (1982). But when a habeas petitioner such as Carpenter files an application in state court seeking to reopen his appeal on ineffective-assistance grounds—as he did here—that comity interest has already been honored. The state courts had a full opportunity to correct the constitutional violation and declined to do so on timeliness grounds, not on the merits. Nothing in *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), forecloses this result. *Coleman* held that "[a]ttorney error that does not violate the Constitution ... does not qualify as 'cause,'" *id.* at 754, distinguishing ordinary attorney negligence from constitutionally deficient performance. It follows that where the attorney error *does* violate the Constitution, as it did here, the *Coleman* bar simply does not apply, whatever procedural obstacle later attaches to presenting that claim.

Requiring a freestanding, independently exhausted and non-defaulted ineffective-assistance claim before it may serve as "cause" effectively demands that the petitioner litigate the cause claim to full merits adjudication before it can unlock review of the underlying claim—collapsing the threshold cause inquiry into a second round of procedural-default litigation. That result multiplies, rather than streamlines, the very layers of litigation this Court's cause-and-prejudice framework was designed to avoid. *See McCleskey v. Zant*, 499 U.S. 467, 493 (1991) (cause-and-prejudice standard designed to "reduce the potential costs" of habeas litigation while still permitting meritorious constitutional claims to be heard).

### C. The Cause-and-Prejudice Standard Already Protects the State's Legitimate Interests

The comity and finality interests underlying procedural-default doctrine, *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 126-129 (1982), are fully vindicated by requiring the petitioner to demonstrate both cause and actual prejudice, or a fundamental miscarriage of justice. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 496. Once a habeas petitioner has shown that counsel's constitutionally deficient performance caused the default and that prejudice resulted, the State's default rule has served its evidentiary and finality purposes; no additional procedural gauntlet is needed to protect the State's interest in the integrity of its own appellate processes. Imposing a further layer—requiring the ineffective-assistance claim itself to have avoided any independent procedural pitfall—serves no interest the cause-and-prejudice standard does not already serve, and instead risks foreclosing review of serious constitutional violations such as the one found by both the District Court and the Court of Appeals in this case.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, holding that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, should be affirmed.
