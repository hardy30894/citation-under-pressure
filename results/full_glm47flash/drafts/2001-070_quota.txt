**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CATEGORICALLY PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF THE MENTALLY RETARDED.**

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied the law established in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989). In *Penry*, the Supreme Court held that "the Constitution does not forbid the execution of the mentally retarded." This precedent remains binding. The State contends that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically bar the death penalty for this class of offenders. While the Constitution prohibits the execution of a person who is mentally incompetent to understand that he is to be put to death and why, *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), Atkins is competent to understand his punishment. Therefore, his mental retardation alone does not render his execution unconstitutional.

**II. THE "EVOLVING STANDARDS OF DECENCY" TEST REQUIRES A NATIONAL CONSENSUS, WHICH IS LACKING.**

Under the "evolving standards of decency" test, a punishment is cruel and unusual only if it is "universally rejected." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86 (1958). The Court looks to the laws enacted by the people's elected representatives to determine this consensus. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976). Currently, a majority of states have not enacted statutes prohibiting the execution of the mentally retarded. Therefore, no national consensus exists to bar Atkins' execution. The execution of the mentally retarded is not "cruel and unusual punishment" under the Eighth Amendment.

**III. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PRECLUDE A SENTENCER FROM CONSIDERING MITIGATING EVIDENCE.**

The Constitution does not require a categorical exclusion of the mentally retarded from capital punishment. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 319. Furthermore, the Eighth Amendment does not preclude a sentencer from considering any mitigating evidence. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586 (1978). The jury in this case was permitted to consider Atkins' mental retardation as a mitigating factor. The death penalty is not cruel and unusual punishment simply because a defendant has a disability. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238 (1972). The sentence of death is not disproportionate to the crime of capital murder.
