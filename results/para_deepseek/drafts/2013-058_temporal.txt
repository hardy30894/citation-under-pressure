**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts “retirement funds” from a debtor’s estate, but an inherited IRA does not qualify. The plain meaning of the term “retirement funds,” the structure of the exemption provisions, and settled principles of statutory construction all compel the conclusion that the exemption is limited to funds held for the debtor’s own retirement.

**I. The Text of §522 Requires That Funds Be Held for the Debtor’s Retirement**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds” that are in a tax-exempt account. The term “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but its ordinary meaning is clear: funds set aside for the owner’s retirement. This Court has long held that where Congress uses words of common usage, they must be given their ordinary meaning. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). A fund that is required to be distributed before the owner reaches retirement age is not “retirement funds” in any ordinary sense.

The inherited IRA at issue here is not a retirement vehicle for Heidi Heffron-Clark. Under the Internal Revenue Code, she cannot contribute to it, cannot roll it over, and must begin withdrawing from it within one year of her mother’s death. 26 U.S.C. §§408(d)(3)(C), 401(a)(9)(B). The account is a time-limited tax-deferral device, not a repository for savings to be used after she stops working. Money that must be taken out during the debtor’s working years is not “retirement funds” under any reasonable construction of the statute.

**II. The Context of the Exemption Confirms That It Protects Only the Debtor’s Own Retirement Savings**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption scheme makes clear that the character of an asset is determined by the debtor’s use, not the use of a predecessor. For example, the homestead exemption under §522(b)(3)(A) requires that the debtor have occupied the property as a domicile for at least 730 days before filing. If a debtor inherited a house from her mother and rented it out, she could not claim the exemption simply because the house was once her mother’s home. The exemption depends on the debtor’s own use. Similarly, the exemption for “retirement funds” depends on whether the funds are held for the debtor’s retirement.

This Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be strictly construed, because they are in derogation of the rights of creditors. *Sturgis v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. 122, 200 (1819). The purpose of exemptions is to protect the debtor and his family from destitution, not to shield inherited wealth from creditors. *See, e.g., In re H. M. & W. L. Co.*, 2 F. Cas. 756, 757 (D. Mass. 1876) (exemptions “are not to be extended by construction”). Here, the Clarks could freely use the inherited IRA for current consumption, and the funds are thus indistinguishable from any other asset in the bankruptcy estate.

**III. The Funds in an Inherited IRA Are No Longer Retirement Funds in Anyone’s Hands**

When Ruth Heffron died, the funds in her IRA ceased to be “retirement funds” in the sense that the Code protects. The money was no longer Ruth’s, and it was not Heidi’s retirement money. The funds became a source of current income, subject to mandatory distribution. This is analogous to a situation where a debtor withdraws money from her own IRA and then deposits it in a checking account. The money would no longer be “retirement funds,” even if it had been so in the original account. *See, e.g., Nelson v. Welch*, 137 U.S. 555, 558 (1891) (exemption depends on “the character of the property as it exists in the hands of the debtor”).

The Fifth Circuit’s view that the funds retain their character because they were once someone’s retirement funds ignores this fundamental principle. Property does not carry its exemption status through inheritance. *Browne v. Commissioner*, 74 F.2d 1, 2 (1st Cir. 1934) (inherited property takes its character from the beneficiary’s use, not the decedent’s). The exemption for retirement funds is no different.

**IV. The Statute’s Purpose Supports the Conclusion That Inherited IRAs Are Not Exempt**

Congress enacted the retirement-fund exemption to encourage debtors to save for their own retirement, not to create a wealth-transfer mechanism insulated from creditors. To hold otherwise would allow debtors to shelter assets that are freely available for current consumption, undermining the fundamental bankruptcy policy of equitable distribution to creditors. *See, e.g., Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 60 (1906) (statutes must be interpreted to effectuate their purpose, not to produce absurd results). An inherited IRA is a “pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption,” not a fund of retirement savings. The exemption does not apply.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
