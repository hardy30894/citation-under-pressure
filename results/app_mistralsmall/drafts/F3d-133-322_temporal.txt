**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE INDICTMENT FAILED TO STATE AN OFFENSE BECAUSE § 201(b)(2)(C) REQUIRES A PUBLIC OFFICIAL TO POSSESS AUTHORITY TO PERFORM THE ALLEGEDLY CORRUPT ACT.**

Mrs. Parker’s conviction cannot stand because the indictment did not allege that she possessed the authority to approve or deny SSI benefits—an essential element of § 201(b)(2)(C). Section 201(b)(2)(C) criminalizes the bribery of a public official for an “official act,” which is defined in § 201(a)(3) as “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” pending before or within the official’s jurisdiction. However, this Court has long held that a public official must possess the authority to perform the act in question for it to qualify as an “official act” under the statute.

In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), the Supreme Court construed the predecessor to § 201(b)(2)(C) and held that a public official’s duties must be “defined by law or by settled practice” for an act to constitute an “official act.” *Id.* at 231. The Court emphasized that an official’s authority is not unlimited; rather, it is circumscribed by the scope of their legal or customary duties. *Id.* Thus, an act performed outside an official’s authority cannot be deemed an “official act” for purposes of the bribery statute.

Similarly, in *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503 (1943), the Supreme Court reiterated that the bribery statute requires proof that the official acted within the scope of their authority. The Court explained that the statute targets the corruption of an official’s *legitimate* functions, not actions taken entirely outside their purview. *Id.* at 512-13. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Sacher*, 182 F.2d 416 (2d Cir. 1950), where the court held that an official’s unauthorized acts—even if performed in their official capacity—do not constitute “official acts” under the statute.

Here, the record establishes that Mrs. Parker lacked the authority to approve or deny SSI benefits. Her duties were clerical: assisting the ALJ, recording hearings, and typing decisions. She did not have the power to sign ALJ Aronson’s name or grant benefits. Her fraudulent conduct—altering letters and forging signatures—was entirely outside her authority. Thus, the acts for which she was convicted cannot qualify as “official acts” under § 201(b)(2)(C).

**II. THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PROVE THAT MRS. PARKER’S ACTS WERE WITHIN THE SCOPE OF HER OFFICIAL DUTY.**

Even if this Court were to assume that Mrs. Parker’s duties included some discretionary functions, the Government did not prove that her corrupt acts were performed in her official capacity. The bribery statute requires proof that the official acted *as an official*, not merely in a manner that appeared official. *Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 231.

In *United States v. George*, 174 F.2d 262 (7th Cir. 1949), the court held that a postmaster’s unauthorized sale of stamps to a private party did not constitute an “official act” because the postmaster acted outside his lawful authority. The court reasoned that the bribery statute targets the corruption of an official’s *legitimate* functions, not private misconduct disguised as official action. *Id.* at 264.

Here, Mrs. Parker’s scheme was a private venture, not an exercise of her official duties. She did not approve benefits as part of her job; she did so for personal gain, using her access to office materials to perpetrate a fraud. The Government’s theory—that her clerical work provided the opportunity for corruption—does not transform her private misconduct into an “official act.” *See Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 231.

**III. MR. PARKER’S CONVICTION CANNOT STAND BECAUSE THERE WAS INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE THAT HE PARTICIPATED IN THE SCHEME.**

Mr. Parker’s conviction rests on Mrs. Parker’s uncorroborated accusation that he assisted her in the bribery scheme. However, the Government presented no independent evidence linking Mr. Parker to the offense. In *Cogen v. United States*, 278 U.S. 221 (1929), the Supreme Court held that a conspiracy conviction cannot rest on the uncorroborated testimony of a co-conspirator. The Court explained that such testimony is inherently suspect and must be supported by independent evidence to avoid the risk of false convictions. *Id.* at 224.

Here, the only evidence implicating Mr. Parker is Mrs. Parker’s confession, which she later recanted. There is no documentary evidence, no eyewitness testimony, and no other corroborating proof. Thus, Mr. Parker’s conviction violates the rule of *Cogen* and must be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the convictions of both Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker must be reversed. The indictment failed to state an offense as to Mrs. Parker, and the Government did not prove that her acts were within the scope of her official duty. Mr. Parker’s conviction cannot stand due to insufficient evidence. The judgment below should be vacated in its entirety.
