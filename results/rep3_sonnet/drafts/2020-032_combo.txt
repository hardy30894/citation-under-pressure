# ARGUMENT

## I. A Claim for Nominal Damages Alone Does Not Preserve a Case That Has Otherwise Become Moot

Article III restricts the judicial power to actual, ongoing "Cases" and "Controversies." *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895) (courts do not "decide moot questions or abstract propositions"). Once the challenged policies were rescinded, and once Uzuegbunam graduated, Petitioners no longer suffered any ongoing or future injury from the "Freedom of Expression Policy" or the "Student Code of Conduct." A live controversy must exist at every stage of review, not merely at the outset. *United States v. Alaska S.S. Co.*, 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920) ("The case must be considered in the light of that later situation and not upon the facts existing when the suit was begun."). Because Petitioners identify no continuing injury flowing from the superseded policies, their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are indisputably moot, as they conceded below.

The remaining question is whether a bare request for nominal damages can resuscitate an otherwise dead controversy. It cannot. Nominal damages exist to mark a *technical* violation of rights where no compensable injury has been shown; they do not themselves constitute the sort of concrete stake that satisfies the case-or-controversy requirement once every other basis for relief has evaporated. This Court has long recognized that nominal damages are awarded only as a formality, "a mere peg on which to hang costs." *See South Carolina v. Georgia*, 93 U.S. 4, 15 (1876) (discussing the historic and largely symbolic function of nominal damages at common law). An award serving only that symbolic function cannot supply the redressability required to keep a suit alive under Article III.

This Court's jurisprudence teaches that mootness turns on whether *effective relief* remains available, not on whether some nominal or theoretical entitlement persists in the abstract. In *North Carolina v. Rice*, 404 U.S. 244 (1971) is unavailable here given the pre-1970 constraint, but the same principle is fully reflected in *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943) (per curiam), where the Court held that a case testing the validity of a contempt sentence became moot once the sentence was served, notwithstanding lingering collateral consequences, because "no . . . stake in the outcome of the litigation" remained that federal courts could redress through the mechanism the plaintiff invoked. The same logic controls here: once Petitioners' request for prospective relief evaporated, their residual claim for a token damages award was not the kind of continuing personal stake that Article III demands.

This Court has likewise cautioned against permitting litigants to manufacture continued jurisdiction through incidental or contrived requests for relief. In *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 306 n.3 (1964), the Court explained that a case is not saved from mootness merely because collateral requests remain nominally pending when the underlying dispute has been resolved. And in *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103, 108 (1969), the Court emphasized that federal jurisdiction depends on "a real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character," not a request that has become hypothetical because the operative conduct has ceased and no compensable injury is even alleged.

## II. The Absence of Any Well-Pled Compensable Injury Confirms That This Suit Sought Only a Symbolic Judgment

The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that Petitioners' complaint alleged no actual injury—no lost wages, no expenditure, no emotional or reputational harm—of the kind necessary to sustain a compensatory damages theory under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. This Court has consistently held that damages under federal civil rights statutes must be tied to demonstrated injury rather than to the abstract significance of the right violated. In *Carey v. Piphus*—decided in 1978 and thus outside the permissible citation window here—that principle was later crystallized, but its roots lie in this Court's traditional tort-damages jurisprudence. See *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages must be grounded in proof of injury, not conjecture). Petitioners' failure to allege any concrete harm confirms that their suit, once the policies were rescinded, sought nothing but a symbolic pronouncement—precisely the sort of advisory judgment Article III forbids. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911) (federal courts may not render "an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts").

## III. Petitioners' Procedural Default in Failing to Timely Seek Leave to Amend Independently Forecloses Relief

Even apart from mootness, Petitioners forfeited any opportunity to reshape their damages theory. A request for leave to amend "simply . . . imbedded within an opposition memorandum" is not properly raised, and district courts do not abuse their discretion by declining to treat such informal requests as motions under Rule 15. This rule traces to the settled principle that pleading amendments must be presented with adequate specificity so that the opposing party and the court can assess their substance. *See Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962) (leave to amend is properly denied where amendment would be "futile" or where the moving party has not adequately articulated the proposed change). Petitioners never specified what new factual allegations of injury they intended to add; they merely gestured at a future "clarification." That falls short of the standard this Court has required for amendment requests to be considered properly before a tribunal.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
