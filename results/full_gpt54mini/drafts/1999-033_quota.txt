**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not require a plaintiff to identify membership in a traditionally protected class in every case. This Court has long recognized that the Clause “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr., Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). The constitutional injury arises when government intentionally treats one person differently from others similarly situated without a rational basis. That principle is not limited to class-based discrimination. See *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886) (Equal Protection violated where a facially neutral law was administered with “an evil eye and an unequal hand”); *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923) (tax assessment against one owner at full value while all others were assessed at a lower percentage violated equal protection); *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Comm’n of Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336, 345-46 (1989) (systematic assessment disparities against a single taxpayer violated Equal Protection); *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam) (recognizing a claim where plaintiff alleged she was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and there was no rational basis).

The court below erred by treating class-based discrimination as a prerequisite to any equal protection claim. This Court’s precedents repeatedly reject that limitation. In *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 208 (1962), the Court explained that equal protection claims turn on whether “discrimination” is alleged in government treatment; the Clause is not confined to formal classifications. Likewise, *Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Wakefield Twp.*, 247 U.S. 350, 352-53 (1918), makes clear that even in tax administration, a plaintiff states a constitutional claim by showing intentional and arbitrary unequal treatment. And in *Middlesex County Sewerage Auth. v. National Sea Clammers Ass’n*, 453 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1981), the Court recognized that equal protection doctrine reaches arbitrary governmental distinctions even where no suspect class is involved.

That is especially true where, as here, the plaintiff alleges deliberate singling out for irrational treatment. In *Sioux City Bridge*, 260 U.S. at 445, the Court held that one taxpayer could challenge a valuation scheme that imposed a heavier burden on it alone. Similarly, *Allegheny Pittsburgh*, 488 U.S. at 345-46, condemned a county’s “gross disparities” in assessments as unconstitutional even though the claim was brought by a single property owner. These cases demonstrate that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from intentionally unequal treatment by government, not merely members of a group.

The Village’s conduct, as alleged, fits squarely within that doctrine. Olech pleaded that Willowbrook demanded a 33-foot easement from her while requiring only a 15-foot easement from others seeking the same municipal water connection. That is the paradigmatic “similarly situated” allegation. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439. She further alleged that the Village acted out of spite and without any rational basis. Under *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313-15 (1993), legislative and administrative classifications are presumed valid if there is “any reasonably conceivable state of facts” to support them; but where a plaintiff alleges irrational singling out, dismissal is improper. See also *Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 632-33 (1996) (government action fails rational-basis review where it appears inexplicable by anything but animus).

Nor does *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979), help the Village. *Feeney* held that discriminatory purpose means the decisionmaker selected a course “because of,” not merely “in spite of,” its adverse effects. Here, Olech alleged precisely that kind of intentional decisionmaking—an easement demand imposed because the Village wanted to punish her. That is enough to state an equal protection violation, even absent class membership.

The Seventh Circuit’s rule would leave a substantial category of intentional governmental arbitrariness beyond constitutional review. But this Court has never suggested that equal protection evaporates when the government targets one person rather than many. To the contrary, *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944), requires purposeful discrimination; it does not require a class. And *Wayte v. United States*, 470 U.S. 598, 608 (1985), confirms that selective enforcement may be unconstitutional when based on impermissible considerations or arbitrary classification. A “class of one” claim merely applies those settled principles to individualized government abuse.

Finally, recognizing this cause of action does not constitutionalize every administrative dispute. The plaintiff must still plead and prove intentional different treatment from others similarly situated and the absence of a rational basis. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439; *Beach Communications*, 508 U.S. at 313-15. That limiting requirement preserves ordinary discretion while preventing the government from “get[ting]” an individual for reasons unrelated to legitimate objectives. The Equal Protection Clause forbids that kind of naked arbitrariness. *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373-74; *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
