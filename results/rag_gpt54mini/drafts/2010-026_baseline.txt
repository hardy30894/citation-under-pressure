**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in stretching FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its text and this Court’s decisions. FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). The exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)), and “are to be interpreted narrowly.” *Lahr v. NTSB*, 569 F.3d 964, 973 (9th Cir. 2009). The court below disregarded those commands by exempting ESQD documents merely because they are “predominantly” for internal use and might help someone avoid agency regulation. That reading cannot be squared with the statutory text.

Exemption 2 covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has made clear that FOIA exemptions cannot be expanded by judicial gloss. In *Rose*, the Court recognized a limited “High 2” category for internal matters whose disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” 425 U.S. at 369. But *Rose* did not authorize exemption of any internal agency material bearing on safety or planning. To the contrary, *Rose* and later cases insist that the inquiry remain tethered to the statute’s text. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test is not the statute’s test. In *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, this Court reiterated that exemptions are exclusive and may not be enlarged by policy concerns. 492 U.S. at 151. And in *Dep’t of Defense v. FLRA*, the Court explained that FOIA analysis turns on the public’s interest in disclosure as it sheds light on agency performance, not on generalized concerns about efficient administration. 510 U.S. 487, 495-96 (1994). Exemption 2 asks whether the material relates “solely” to internal personnel rules and practices. That word “solely” cannot be rewritten out of the statute in favor of a broad “predominantly” standard.

The Ninth Circuit itself had recognized the narrowness of Exemption 2. In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), the court held that certain law-enforcement materials may be exempt if disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” But *Hardy* did not transform Exemption 2 into a general safety or security exception. Nor did *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085-87 (9th Cir. 1997), authorize withholding information simply because it is operationally useful or could be misused. *Maricopa* actually rejected Exemption 2 where the data did not relate “solely,” or even predominantly, to internal personnel rules and practices. Id. at 1085.

Here, the Navy’s ESQD materials are not “personnel rules and practices” at all. They are technical safety and siting documents describing the effects of explosions and the blast distances needed to protect people and facilities. A document does not become exempt under Exemption 2 merely because it is useful to the agency and might, if disclosed, facilitate evasion of agency policy. That is exactly the kind of functional expansion this Court has rejected. See *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 292-94 (1979) (FOIA does not authorize withholding beyond statutory exemptions); *Weinberger v. Catholic Action of Hawaii/Peace Education Project*, 454 U.S. 139, 144-45 (1981) (Congress struck the disclosure-secrecy balance through FOIA’s exemptions, and courts should not engraft “unique concepts” onto the statute).

Nor does the Navy’s invocation of security concerns justify Exemption 2. FOIA contains other exemptions designed for that purpose. *Weinberger*, 454 U.S. at 144-45. Exemption 1 protects properly classified national-security information. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). Exemption 7 protects qualifying law-enforcement records. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7); *National Labor Relations Board v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co.*, 437 U.S. 214, 224-25 (1978). Where Congress has supplied specific tools for withholding sensitive information, courts may not repurpose Exemption 2 as a catchall. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

That is especially important here because the Navy’s own position shows why Exemption 2 is the wrong fit. The agency does not contend these records are mundane personnel rules. It contends they are operational safety calculations that could be used to infer blast effects. Whatever merit that concern may have under some other exemption, it does not satisfy Exemption 2’s requirement that the records relate “solely” to internal personnel rules and practices. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding dissolves the statutory limit and converts Exemption 2 into a free-ranging secrecy provision.

The judgment should be reversed.
