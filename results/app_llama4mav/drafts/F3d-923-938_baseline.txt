## Argument

The Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness of $9 million running directly to him was incorrect.

A shareholder's ability to deduct his proportionate share of an S corporation's net operating losses is limited by the sum of his basis in his stock and the corporate indebtedness to him. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). To establish basis in corporate indebtedness, a shareholder must demonstrate that the corporation owes a "bona fide indebtedness" to him. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The regulation requires that the indebtedness "run directly" to the shareholder. Id.

The Eleventh Circuit has not directly addressed the "run directly" requirement, but other circuits have interpreted it to mean that the shareholder must be the creditor to whom the S corporation owes the debt. See, e.g., Underwood v. Comm'r , 535 F.2d 309, 312 (5th Cir. 1976) (holding that a shareholder's basis in an S corporation's indebtedness is determined by whether the corporation owes a debt to the shareholder). The facts of this case demonstrate that Meruelo made an "actual economic outlay" toward the debt, as required by the Supreme Court in Estate of Leavitt v. Comm'r , 875 F.2d 420, 422 (4th Cir. 1989) (citing Underwood , 535 F.2d at 312).

Meruelo's transfers to Merco through various affiliates established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him. The Commissioner concedes that Meruelo's initial $4,985,035 transfer to Merco through Akoya gave him a shareholder basis in that amount. The subsequent transactions between Merco and its affiliates, in which Meruelo held an interest, were merely a continuation of this initial transaction. The net effect of these transactions was to increase Merco's indebtedness to Meruelo.

The Tax Court's ruling that the debt ran from Merco to the other entities, rather than to Meruelo, was incorrect. The court failed to recognize that Meruelo's interests in the various affiliates were merely conduits for his investments in Merco. As the court in Yates v. Comm'r , T.C. Memo 2001-280, noted, "the fact that [the shareholder] made the loan through an intermediary does not preclude him from having basis in the debt." The transactions between Merco and its affiliates were not arm's-length transactions, but rather, were part of a larger scheme to fund Merco's operations.

The preparation of Merco's tax returns by Luis Carreras, a certified public accountant, further supports Meruelo's claim. Carreras's annual calculation of Merco's net accounts payable to its affiliates and his allocation of a percentage of this indebtedness to Meruelo based on his ownership interests in the various affiliates demonstrate that Meruelo's share of the indebtedness was properly accounted for.

In conclusion, the Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The evidence demonstrates that Meruelo established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him, and the court's ruling to the contrary was incorrect. This Court should reverse the Tax Court's decision and allow Meruelo to deduct his share of Merco's losses.
