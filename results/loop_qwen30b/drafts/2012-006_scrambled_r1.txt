The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—turns on a fundamental principle of criminal law: the prosecution must prove every element of the offense, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden does not shift to the defendant merely because he claims to have withdrawn from the conspiracy. The government’s burden remains unchanged, and the defendant’s withdrawal is not a defense that extinguishes the prosecution’s obligation to prove the essential elements of the crime.

The Supreme Court has long held that the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court declared that “beyond a reasonable doubt” is the standard required by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Although *Winship* was decided after 1970, its foundational principle was firmly established in the jurisprudence of the Court prior to that date. Indeed, in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364, the Court stated that “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” This language reflects a long-standing commitment to the burden of proof in criminal cases, a principle that predates *Winship* and is rooted in earlier decisions.
