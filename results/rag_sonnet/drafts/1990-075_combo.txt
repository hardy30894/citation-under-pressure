ARGUMENT

I. The Temporary-Scheduling Provision Sets Forth an Intelligible Principle and Is a Valid Delegation of Authority.

Article I's vesting of "all legislative powers" in Congress "is both a grant of power ... and a limitation upon its use," but it does not prevent Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering complex regulatory schemes. This Court's cases require only that Congress "lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to act is directed to conform." J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). That standard preserves "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution," Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), while acknowledging that Congress cannot govern a complex society without delegating authority under general directives.

Section 811(h) satisfies Hampton's test. Congress specified the harm to be avoided—an "imminent hazard to the public safety"—and required the Attorney General to weigh factors drawn from § 811(c)(4)-(6): the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health. Congress also required Federal Register notice and consideration of the Secretary of Health and Human Services' views, and capped any temporary order at one year, extendable by only six months. These are meaningful checks on delegated authority.

This Court has sustained delegations far less confined than this one where the subject matter demanded speed and technical judgment. In Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944), the Court upheld wartime price-control delegations in light of "the rapidity of changes" that outpaced ordinary legislative processes—a rationale equally applicable to designer drugs engineered to exploit gaps in the permanent-scheduling timetable. In Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court approved a broad delegation to bank regulators because the field possessed "well-known and generally acceptable standards," just as the CSA's abuse-and-safety criteria are well established in narcotics regulation. And in Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952), the Court identified the availability of judicial review as a relevant safeguard; here, constitutional review of the delegation itself remains available notwithstanding § 811(h)(6).

Nor does the criminal consequence of scheduling demand a different rule. States and the federal government have long possessed broad authority "to regulate the administration, sale, prescription and use of dangerous and habit-forming drugs," a power this Court has called "too firmly established to be successfully called in question." Minnesota ex rel. Whipple v. Martinson, 256 U.S. 41, 45 (1921). This Court sustained the Harrison Act's comprehensive scheme of narcotics regulation in United States v. Doremus, 249 U.S. 86 (1919), notwithstanding its direct bearing on criminal liability. The same deference applies here, where Congress—not the Attorney General—defined the offense in § 841 and merely authorized the Attorney General to make the technical determination whether a substance falls within the class of dangerous drugs Congress has already resolved to punish.

Finally, administrative agencies applying "the regulatory police power" to "the myriad details" of a complex field are the ordinary and permissible instrument for implementing legislative policy, provided the legislature "enjoin[s] upon it a certain course of procedure and certain rules of decision." Wichita R.R. & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Comm'n, 260 U.S. 48, 59 (1922). Section 811(h) does exactly that, and it should be sustained.

II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Delegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

The Attorney General's delegation to the DEA Administrator was expressly authorized by 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the CSA to officers of the Department of Justice. That express authorization distinguishes this case from Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, 315 U.S. 357 (1942), where this Court refused to imply a power to delegate the subpoena authority because the statute's grant of delegation power for some functions, and omission of it for others, showed "a legislative intention to withhold" delegation of that particular power. Cudahy Packing, 315 U.S. at 363. Here there is no such gap: Congress authorized the Attorney General to delegate "any of his functions" under the CSA, including temporary scheduling.

Delegation of administrative authority to a subordinate agency created to carry out a national regulatory program is a familiar and permissible arrangement. Cf. District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co., 346 U.S. 100 (1953) (Congress may delegate authority to subordinate bodies to administer matters entrusted to it). The DEA, created within the Department of Justice specifically to administer the CSA, is the natural repository of the Attorney General's scheduling function, and the 1987 regulation assigning that function to the DEA Administrator was a straightforward exercise of the delegation authority Congress itself supplied.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
