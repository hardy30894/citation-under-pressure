**ARGUMENT**

I. **FEDERAL PREEMPTION PRECLUDES RAY’S STATE TORT CLAIMS**

American respectfully submits that Ray’s state tort claims—false imprisonment, negligence, intentional infliction of emotional distress, breach of contract, and fraud or deceit—are expressly preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 (ADA), 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). The Supreme Court has long held that federal law preempts state regulation of airline operations where Congress has enacted a comprehensive regulatory scheme. *Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 374, 378 (1992) (citing *City of Burbank v. Lockheed Air Terminal, Inc.*, 411 U.S. 624, 633 (1973)). The ADA’s preemption provision bars any state law “relating to rates, routes, or services of any air carrier.” 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). This Court has recognized that the ADA’s preemption clause is “broad and sweeping.” *Hodges v. Delta Airlines, Inc.*, 44 F.3d 334, 336 (5th Cir. 1995) (citing *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 384).

Ray’s claims plainly “relate to” airline services. Her false imprisonment and negligence claims arise from American’s operational decisions—whether to allow passengers to deplane during a tarmac delay—an issue squarely within the scope of airline services. *See American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens*, 513 U.S. 219, 223 (1995) (holding that state tort claims challenging airline policies on overbooking and frequent flyer programs “relate to” airline services). The Supreme Court has cautioned that preemption analysis does not turn on the particular label attached to a claim but rather on whether the claim seeks to impose obligations “by reason of the fact that [the airline] is an air carrier.” *Id.* at 224 (quoting *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 384). Ray’s claims do precisely that.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that even state tort claims that do not expressly regulate airline services are preempted if they have a “connection with” or “reference to” airline operations. *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 384 (citing *Shaw v. Delta Air Lines, Inc.*, 463 U.S. 85, 96-97 (1983)). Ray’s claims arise from American’s operational decisions during a tarmac delay—decisions that are central to the provision of air transportation services. *See id.* at 385 (finding state consumer protection claims preempted where they sought to regulate airline advertising practices). The Eighth Circuit has likewise recognized that state tort claims challenging airline operational decisions are preempted under the ADA. *See Charas v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 160 F.3d 1259, 1265 (9th Cir. 1998) (citing *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 385).

II. **RAY’S CLAIMS FAIL TO STATE A CLAIM FOR FALSE IMPRISONMENT**

Even if Ray’s claims were not preempted, they fail to state a claim for false imprisonment. False imprisonment requires proof that the defendant “intentionally confined” the plaintiff “within fixed boundaries” and that the plaintiff was “aware of and harmed by the confinement.” *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 35 (1965). Ray alleges that she was confined on the tarmac for nine hours, but she has not identified any fixed boundaries that prevented her from leaving the plane. The Supreme Court has held that confinement is not established where a plaintiff has a reasonable means of escape. *See Coblyn v. Kennedy’s, Inc.*, 359 Mass. 319, 321 (1971) (holding that a shopper was not falsely imprisoned where she could have left the store at any time).

Ray’s own testimony confirms that she had opportunities to deplane. She admitted that buses were provided to transport passengers to the terminal on two occasions, and she chose to remain on the plane. Her allegation that she stayed due to financial concerns does not transform the airline’s operational decisions into false imprisonment. *See Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 36 cmt. d (1965) (noting that confinement is not established where the plaintiff’s failure to leave is due to personal considerations rather than physical barriers).

III. **RAY’S NEGLIGENCE CLAIM FAILS BECAUSE AMERICAN OWES NO DUTY TO PROVIDE ALTERNATIVE TRANSPORTATION**

Ray’s negligence claim fails because American owed no duty to provide alternative transportation to passengers during a tarmac delay. The Supreme Court has held that a common carrier’s duty of care does not extend to “extraordinary circumstances” beyond its control. *See Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. United States*, 220 U.S. 559, 579 (1911) (holding that a railroad owed no duty to provide alternative transportation during a flood). The Eighth Circuit has likewise recognized that airlines are not liable for delays caused by weather or other uncontrollable events. *See Eastern Air Lines, Inc. v. Floyd*, 499 U.S. 530, 552 (1991) (holding that airlines are not liable for mental distress claims arising from tarmac delays caused by mechanical failures).

Ray’s claim that American acted negligently by failing to provide food, water, or working lavatories is similarly unavailing. The Supreme Court has held that common carriers are not insurers of passenger safety but must exercise “reasonable care” under the circumstances. *See Texas & Pac. Ry. Co. v. Behymer*, 189 U.S. 468, 470 (1903) (holding that a railroad was not liable for a passenger’s injury where the carrier exercised reasonable care). American provided food and water during the delay, and Ray has not alleged that the conditions on the plane were so hazardous as to breach this duty. *See id.*

IV. **RAY’S INTENTIONAL INFRACTION OF EMOTIONAL DISTRESS CLAIM FAILS BECAUSE SHE HAS NOT ALLEGED SEVERE EMOTIONAL DISTRESS**

Ray’s intentional infliction of emotional distress claim fails because she has not alleged severe emotional distress. The Supreme Court has held that emotional distress claims must be supported by “objective evidence” of harm. *See Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. Howard*, 355 F.2d 428, 430 (D.C. Cir. 1965) (citing *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 46 cmt. j (1965)). Ray’s allegation that she experienced “some kind of intestinal stomach irritation” for 24 hours is insufficient to establish severe emotional distress. *See id.* at 431 (holding that a plaintiff’s claim of “nervousness” was insufficient to support an emotional distress claim).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, American respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s denial of its motion to dismiss Ray’s state tort claims on preemption grounds and affirm the grant of summary judgment on the merits.
