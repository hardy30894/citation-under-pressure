# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park held "immemorially . . . for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to "social, civic and recreational" activities benefiting the community. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. As this Court has explained, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). A government need not "convert its property" into a forum for every use citizens might wish to make of it merely because it has opened the property for some limited purposes. *Id.* This authority to condition access to public property according to its dedicated purpose was recognized as early as *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897), where the Court sustained a municipal ordinance restricting public speaking on the Boston Common, holding that the legislature's power to control the use of public grounds is the same power an owner has over his own land. Milford may likewise exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. This Court has long recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding licensing scheme as a permissible "regulation of the times, the place, and the manner" of using public ways, "without unwarranted discrimination"). Milford's Community Use Policy operates the same way: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). Nor may the state sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting; while the Court has acknowledged that "the State may not establish a 'religion of secularism' in the sense of affirmatively opposing or showing hostility to religion," it has equally made clear that the state must maintain neutrality rather than favor devotional practice in its schools. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963).

The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, and directed at the same young children who attend that school during the day. This Court has long insisted on a rigorous separation between government-controlled educational property and religious exercise, holding in *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947), that the Establishment Clause "erected a wall between church and state" that "must be kept high and impregnable."

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands that "government must be neutral" among religions and between religion and nonreligion. *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *Everson* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
