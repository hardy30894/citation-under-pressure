# ARGUMENT

## THE STATUTORY LANGUAGE OF SECTION 1981a REQUIRES ONLY A SHOWING OF THE EMPLOYER'S STATE OF MIND, NOT INDEPENDENTLY EGREGIOUS CONDUCT

Petitioner asks this Court to graft onto 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) a requirement that the underlying discriminatory conduct be "egregious" or "outrageous" apart from the employer's state of mind. That request cannot be reconciled with the statute's text, this Court's precedents construing analogous punitive-damages standards, or the structure Congress enacted in the Civil Rights Act of 1991.

### A. The Text of Section 1981a Conditions Punitive Damages on State of Mind Alone

Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages where the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant "engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." The statute's focus is unmistakably on mental state—"malice" and "reckless indifference"—not on the severity of the underlying conduct. Congress did not write a statute requiring "egregious discrimination undertaken with malice," nor did it require that the discriminatory practice itself be "outrageous." Had Congress intended to impose such a dual requirement, it knew how to do so with clear statutory language, as it demonstrated throughout Title VII's carefully calibrated remedial scheme.

This Court's decision in *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), is instructive by analogy. There, in construing the punitive-damages standard applicable to actions under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, this Court held that "reckless or callous indifference" to federally protected rights suffices to trigger consideration of punitive damages, without requiring an independent showing of outrageous conduct. *Id.* at 30. The *Smith* Court expressly rejected the argument that punitive damages require a heightened threshold of misconduct beyond the liability standard itself, explaining that while "punitive damages are never awarded as of right, no matter how egregious the defendant's conduct," the availability of such damages turns on the defendant's state of mind rather than a separate egregiousness inquiry. *Id.*

Congress modeled Section 1981a's malice/reckless-indifference standard on this common-law and § 1983 framework. Representative Edwards's contemporaneous statement that "[p]unitive damages are available under [§ 1981a] to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981" confirms that Congress intended the traditional state-of-mind inquiry, not a heightened egregiousness test layered atop it. 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991).

### B. Requiring Independent Egregiousness Would Improperly Conflate the Liability and Damages Inquiries

Petitioner's proposed rule would require courts to ask not only whether the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference, but also whether the discriminatory conduct itself was sufficiently outrageous—effectively demanding proof of an aggravated tort within the tort of discrimination. This Court has recognized that Title VII violations are themselves serious injuries to federally protected rights. In *United States v. Burke*, 504 U.S. 229 (1992), this Court observed that Title VII protects against "tort-like personal injuries," explaining that "[d]iscrimination in the workplace being no less injurious than discrimination elsewhere, the rights asserted by persons who sue under Title VII are just as tort-like as the rights asserted by plaintiffs" in other civil rights contexts. *Id.* at 229. Requiring plaintiffs to prove that their employer's discrimination was *additionally* egregious—on top of proving malice or reckless indifference—would undermine Congress's judgment that intentional discrimination committed with a culpable state of mind is itself sufficiently serious to warrant deterrence through punitive damages.

Moreover, this Court's teaching in *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), confirms that the 1991 Act's damages provisions, including Section 1981a, represented a considered congressional judgment to expand remedies for intentional discrimination. This Court described the compensatory and punitive damages provisions as producing "a sea change in employer liability for Title VII violations." *Id.* at 244. That sea change reflected Congress's recognition, stated in its findings, "that additional remedies under Federal law are needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a note. An egregiousness requirement independent of state of mind would substantially narrow this expansion, contrary to Congress's stated purpose.

### C. The Statutory Caps Already Address Congress's Concern for Proportionality

Petitioner's egregiousness requirement is also unnecessary because Congress addressed proportionality concerns through the damages caps in Section 1981a(b)(3), which limit punitive damages, together with compensatory damages, to amounts between $50,000 and $300,000 depending on employer size. These caps—not an atextual egregiousness requirement—represent Congress's chosen mechanism for ensuring that punitive damages remain proportionate to the wrong committed. Reading an additional, undefined egregiousness threshold into the statute would duplicate work Congress already accomplished through the caps, while introducing the very uncertainty and inconsistency among lower courts that Congress sought to avoid through clear statutory standards.

### CONCLUSION

The malice/reckless-indifference standard Congress enacted in Section 1981a(b)(1) requires proof of the employer's culpable state of mind. It does not require, in addition, an independent showing that the underlying discriminatory conduct was egregious or outrageous. This Court should so hold.
