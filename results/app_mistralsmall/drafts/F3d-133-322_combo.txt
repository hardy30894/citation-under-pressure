**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Indictment Failed to State an Offense Because Mrs. Parker Lacked Authority to Approve SSI Benefits and Did Not Perform an “Official Act” Within the Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3).**

Mrs. Parker’s conviction cannot stand because the indictment did not allege that she performed an “official act” as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). That section defines an “official act” as “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity, or in such official’s place of trust or profit.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). The Government did not—and could not—allege that Mrs. Parker had authority to approve or deny SSI benefits or to sign decisions on behalf of ALJ Aronson. Her duties were clerical: assisting the ALJ, recording hearings, and typing and mailing decisions. She did not decide any “question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” within the meaning of the statute.

This Court has long held that an “official act” must be one that the defendant was authorized to perform. In *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), the Supreme Court explained that the bribery statute reaches only acts that the official “was authorized to do.” *Id.* at 231. The Court rejected the argument that an official act could include conduct outside an official’s authority, holding that the statute “does not include acts which the officer was not authorized to perform.” *Id.* at 231-32. The Court further explained that duties not defined by written rules may be established by “settled practice,” but only where the official was authorized to perform the act in question. *Id.* at 231. Here, Mrs. Parker was not authorized to approve benefits or sign decisions. Her clerical acts—typing and mailing decisions—were ministerial and did not constitute an “official act” under § 201(a)(3).

The Government’s reliance on *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968 (6th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. *Gjieli* did not expand the definition of “official act” beyond acts authorized by law; it merely held that an official’s corrupt agreement to perform an authorized act in exchange for a bribe suffices, even if the act was never actually performed. *Id.* at 972. Here, Mrs. Parker lacked authority to approve benefits or sign decisions. Her acts were not “official acts” under § 201(a)(3), and the indictment failed to state an offense.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Mrs. Parker Performed an “Official Act” Because She Did Not Have Authority to Approve or Deny Benefits.**

Even if the indictment were sufficient, the evidence was insufficient to prove that Mrs. Parker performed an “official act” as defined in § 201(a)(3). The Government’s theory—that Mrs. Parker’s forgery of ALJ Aronson’s signature constituted an “official act”—fails because forgery is not an act authorized by law. In *United States v. Cohn*, 120 F.2d 60 (2d Cir. 1941), Judge Learned Hand explained that an “official act” must be one that the official “was empowered to do.” *Id.* at 62. Forgery is not an act an official is empowered to perform. *See also United States v. Sacher*, 182 F.2d 416 (2d Cir. 1950) (L. Hand, J.) (holding that an official act must be one authorized by law).

The Government’s reliance on *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), is again misplaced. *Birdsall* involved an official who was authorized to perform the act in question (issuing permits) but did so corruptly. *Id.* at 231. Here, Mrs. Parker was not authorized to approve benefits or sign decisions. Her forgery was not an “official act” under § 201(a)(3).

**III. The Jury Instructions Misstated the Law by Failing to Require Proof That Mrs. Parker Performed an “Official Act.”**

The jury instructions compounded the error by failing to require the jury to find that Mrs. Parker performed an “official act” as defined in § 201(a)(3). In *United States v. Nardello*, 348 U.S. 214 (1955), the Supreme Court held that in a bribery prosecution under § 201, the Government must prove that the defendant performed an “official act.” *Id.* at 217. The Court explained that the statute “requires proof of an official act.” *Id.* Here, the jury instructions did not require the jury to find that Mrs. Parker performed an “official act.” This failure was plain error under *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), and requires reversal.

**IV. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Mr. Parker Conspired to Commit or Committed Public Bribery Because He Did Not Perform an “Official Act.”**

Mr. Parker’s conviction likewise cannot stand because the evidence was insufficient to prove that he performed an “official act” as defined in § 201(a)(3). Mrs. Parker’s testimony implicated Mr. Parker in the scheme, but her testimony was not sufficient to prove that Mr. Parker performed an “official act.” In *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503 (1943), the Supreme Court held that a conspiracy conviction requires proof that the defendant agreed to commit an offense against the United States. *Id.* at 515. Here, the evidence did not prove that Mr. Parker agreed to perform an “official act” because he lacked authority to do so. *See also United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205 (1940) (holding that a conspiracy conviction requires proof of an agreement to commit an offense).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the convictions of Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker must be reversed. The indictment failed to state an offense, the evidence was insufficient to prove that Mrs. Parker performed an “official act,” the jury instructions misstated the law, and the evidence was insufficient to prove that Mr. Parker conspired to commit or committed public bribery.
