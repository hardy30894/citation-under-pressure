**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in holding that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him. The court’s formalistic approach ignores the economic substance of the transactions and the well-established principle that a shareholder may acquire basis in an S corporation by paying its obligations or advancing funds on its behalf.

**I. The taxpayer’s advances through his controlled entities were actual economic outlays creating indebtedness to him.**

A shareholder who pays corporate obligations or advances funds to his corporation obtains an indebtedness running to him. *Mallon v. Commissioner*, 108 F.2d 174, 176 (7th Cir. 1939); *Perry v. Commissioner*, 152 F.2d 183, 186 (8th Cir. 1945). In *W. B. R. Oil Co. v. Commissioner*, 127 F.2d 566, 568 (4th Cir. 1942), the court held that a shareholder’s payment of corporate debts with his own funds created an indebtedness from the corporation to the shareholder. Here, Meruelo’s controlled entities paid more than $15 million of Merco’s expenses. These payments were recorded as accounts payable on Merco’s books. The taxpayer, as the common owner, bore the economic burden of these payments. The Tax Court’s insistence that the debt run “directly” from Merco to Meruelo elevates form over substance. *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465, 470 (1935).

**II. The promissory note and the annual reclassification of accounts payable as shareholder loans establish a bona fide indebtedness.**

Meruelo executed a $10 million promissory note making a line of credit available to Merco. Each year, his accountant charged Merco’s line of credit for the net accounts payable owed to the affiliates. This recharacterization reflects the substance of the transactions: the affiliates were conduits for Meruelo’s capital. The Supreme Court has long held that the substance of a transaction controls over its form. *Higgins v. Smith*, 308 U.S. 473, 477 (1940); *Commissioner v. Culbertson*, 337 U.S. 733, 741 (1949). The Tax Court disregarded the note because no funds flowed directly from Meruelo to Merco. But the funds flowed through the affiliates, which were acting on Meruelo’s behalf. The debt therefore runs to him.

**III. The Tax Court’s “actual economic outlay” requirement is satisfied.**

Meruelo personally borrowed funds and transferred them to Akoya, which then transferred them to Merco. The Commissioner conceded basis for that amount. The remaining transfers were made by affiliates in which Meruelo held substantial interests. These transfers reduced the value of Meruelo’s ownership interests in the affiliates, constituting an economic outlay by him. The Supreme Court has recognized that a taxpayer may make an economic outlay by using property or funds under his control. *Helvering v. Clifford*, 309 U.S. 331, 335 (1940). The affiliates’ payments to Merco were not loans from the affiliates to Merco; they were advances made on behalf of the common owner, Meruelo. The debt therefore runs to him. The Tax Court’s contrary view would allow a taxpayer to avoid basis by routing advances through a controlled entity, a result inconsistent with the purpose of Subchapter S. *See* *John Kelley Co. v. Commissioner*, 326 U.S. 521, 530 (1946) (substance of the transaction controls in determining debt versus equity).
