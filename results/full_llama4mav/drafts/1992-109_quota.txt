The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is more stringent than that for standing trial, as it demands a defendant's capacity for "reasoned choice" among available alternatives (Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); see also Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986) ("Evans II"); Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981)).

In contrast, a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and can assist their counsel (Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975); Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam)). The trial court's failure to apply this heightened standard when accepting Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty pleas was a due process violation.

The state court's post-conviction ruling, which relied on psychiatric evaluations assessing Moran's competency to stand trial, did not cure this error (see Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263, 272-73 (9th Cir. 1992)). Those evaluations did not address Moran's capacity for reasoned choice, as required by the heightened standard (see Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973)). The trial court's failure to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive constitutional rights, despite substantial evidence of incompetence, was a due process violation (Harding, 834 F.2d at 856; Chavez, 656 F.2d at 518).

The Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with Supreme Court precedent, which emphasizes the importance of ensuring a defendant's competency before accepting a waiver of constitutional rights (Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385-86 (1966); see also Godinez v. Moran, to be decided). The heightened standard for competency to waive constitutional rights is necessary to protect a defendant's due process rights (see Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam)).

Furthermore, a state court's determination of competency is entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d) only if it applies the correct legal standard (see Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 313-14 (1963)). The state court's failure to apply the heightened standard in Moran's case renders its determination of competency unreliable.

In conclusion, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when determining a defendant's competency to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed, as it correctly applied this standard to Moran's case.
