**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits only punishments that are “cruel and unusual” in the sense of being barbaric or grossly disproportionate to the crime. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135–36 (1879) (death by shooting not cruel); *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (electrocution not cruel). Execution for murder is not inherently barbaric. *Badders v. United States*, 240 U.S. 391, 394 (1916) (death penalty for murder not cruel). Even a second attempt at electrocution does not violate the Amendment. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947). The death penalty is thus a permissible punishment for the most serious crimes.

Mental retardation does not automatically render a defendant less culpable or the punishment disproportionate. This Court has never held that mental deficiency alone bars capital punishment. To the contrary, the sentencer may consider mental condition as a mitigating factor, but the Eighth Amendment does not require that such evidence override the jury’s determination. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 244–45 (1949) (sentencing judge may consider “the most detailed information” about defendant’s mental condition). The only categorical bar recognized by this Court is for those who are insane at the time of execution, because they cannot understand the punishment. *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950) (states may determine procedure for sanity before execution). Mental retardation is not equivalent to insanity; many mentally retarded individuals are fully competent to understand the nature of their punishment.

The evolving standards of decency that inform the Eighth Amendment are measured by objective evidence, primarily state legislation. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion) (the Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society”). At the time of this case, only a small minority of states had banned execution of the mentally retarded, demonstrating no national consensus. This Court has repeatedly deferred to legislative judgments on capital punishment. *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535–36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (rejecting Eighth Amendment challenge to punishment for public drunkenness, emphasizing deference to state legislatures). Where legislatures have not acted, courts should not impose a categorical ban.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit punishment for conduct even when the defendant suffers from a condition that may contribute to that conduct. In *Powell*, the Court upheld a conviction for public drunkenness despite the defendant’s chronic alcoholism, holding that the Amendment does not bar punishment for “the act of being drunk in public.” *Id.* at 532. Similarly, mental retardation does not excuse the crime of murder, and the death penalty is not disproportionate to that crime.

The jury in this case heard expert testimony about Atkins’s mental retardation and still imposed the death penalty. The Eighth Amendment does not require that such evidence automatically preclude capital punishment. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251 (sentencer may weigh all relevant information). The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied this principle.

Finally, the death penalty serves legitimate penological goals of retribution and deterrence. *Badders*, 240 U.S. at 394. Mental retardation does not undermine those goals to the extent that execution becomes “cruel and unusual.” The Amendment’s prohibition is reserved for punishments that are “so disproportionate to the offense as to shock the conscience.” *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910) (striking down a non-capital punishment as disproportionate). Execution for murder is not such a punishment.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
