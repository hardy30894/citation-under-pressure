**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that "the test must be whether [the defendant] has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." 362 U.S. at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reaffirmed that same standard, quoting *Dusky*'s formulation that the test must be "whether ... [the defendant] has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding - and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.'" 383 U.S. at 385. The Court in *Pate* explained that "emphasis is on capacity to consult with counsel and to comprehend the proceedings." *Id.* at 385.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and instructed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* at 464. That test asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the constitutional focus is on the adequacy of the record demonstrating an informed choice, not on any separate showing of heightened rationality; the Court there declined to draw an inference of incapacity from the mere circumstance that counsel was appointed only at sentencing. *See id.* at 174–75. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because he may competently elect to conduct his own defense—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent, observing that "[s]everal federal constitutional rights are involved in a waiver that takes place" when a plea of guilty is entered, *id.* at 243 & n.12, but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that the defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Establish a Substantive Competency Standard Higher Than *Dusky*.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard cannot be traced to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam). There, the Court vacated and remanded, explaining that "whether this 'protecting duty' was fulfilled should be re-examined in light of our decision this Term in Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 86 S.Ct. 836," and that "the judgment of the Supreme Court of Arizona is vacated and the case is remanded to that court." *Westbrook*, 384 U.S. at 150. The Court in *Westbrook* did not announce any standard different from the one already governing competency generally; it simply directed reconsideration under *Pate*. *Westbrook* thus stands only for the unremarkable proposition that a court must adequately determine, on an adequate record, whether the *Pate*/*Dusky* standard was satisfied—not that a new, heightened mental-capacity threshold applies to waiver of counsel or entry of a guilty plea.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
