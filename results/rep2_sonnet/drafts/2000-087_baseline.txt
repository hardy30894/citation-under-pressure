# ARGUMENT

## I. THE INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE RESIDENT ALIEN UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) VIOLATES DUE PROCESS

The Fifth Circuit's holding that Zadvydas may be detained indefinitely so long as the Government makes "good faith efforts" to deport him and he cannot prove deportation "impossible" cannot stand. That standard inverts the constitutional order: it requires an alien to disprove the Government's power to detain him forever, rather than requiring the Government to justify detention through a proceeding "adequate to determine" a legitimate basis for confinement. See *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 751 (1987).

**A. Freedom from bodily restraint lies at the heart of the liberty the Due Process Clause protects.**

The Fifth Amendment's protection against deprivation of liberty "applies to all 'persons' within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent." *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67, 77 (1976). "Freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that Clause protects." This Court has recognized that once an alien is admitted to the country, his constitutional status differs from that of an alien seeking initial entry. *See Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982) ("once an alien gains admission to our country and begins to develop the ties that go with permanent residence, his constitutional status changes accordingly").

Zadvydas was lawfully admitted in 1956 and resided in the United States for decades. He therefore falls squarely within the class of aliens whose liberty interest triggers the most searching due process scrutiny. *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is inapposite because Mezei was an excluded alien who had never effected entry; the Fifth Circuit erred in relying on *Mezei* to sanction indefinite detention of a resident alien who has already gained entry into the national community. *See* 185 F.3d at 290, 294–97.

**B. Civil detention requires a special justification that outweighs the individual's constitutionally protected interest in avoiding physical restraint, and § 1231(a)(6) as construed below provides none.**

In *Salerno*, this Court sustained pretrial detention of dangerous defendants only because the statute was "carefully limited" to a specific category of individuals, subject to prompt judicial review, and constrained by a "stringent time limitation." 481 U.S. at 747. The detention here bears none of those hallmarks. Section 1231(a)(6), as the Fifth Circuit construed it, applies not to "a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals," but broadly to any alien whom the Attorney General deems either "inadmissible," a flight risk, or a danger—sweeping in individuals ordered removed for reasons as varied as visa violations. There is no fixed durational limit; detention continues so long as removal is not affirmatively shown to be "impossible."

This Court has likewise required a specific justification—dangerousness combined with proof of mental illness—to sustain indefinite civil commitment, and has insisted upon periodic review to ensure that the factual predicate for confinement continues to exist. *See Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 79–83 (1992) (rejecting continued confinement of an individual no longer mentally ill, even though he might be dangerous, because "freedom from bodily restraint has always been at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause"); *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972) (holding that "due process requires that the nature and duration of commitment bear some reasonable relation to the purpose for which the individual is committed").

The purpose of post-removal detention under § 1231(a)(6) is to secure the alien's presence for removal. Once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable, that purpose can no longer be served, and continued detention becomes—in the words of *Jackson*—untethered from any "reasonable relation" to its statutory purpose. 406 U.S. at 738. The Fifth Circuit's "good faith efforts" test permits detention to continue in perpetuity even where, as here, the record establishes that no country will accept the alien and years of diplomatic effort have proven fruitless. Such detention is no longer regulatory but has become "punishment," *Foucha*, 504 U.S. at 80, imposed without any of the procedural protections that attend criminal punishment.

**C. The constitutional infirmity requires construing § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit limitation on the duration of detention.**

Because the Fifth Circuit's construction of § 1231(a)(6) raises grave constitutional doubts, this Court should invoke the canon that statutes must be construed, where "fairly possible," to avoid constitutional infirmity. *See Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288, 348 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring); *United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909) ("where an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress").

Nothing in the text of § 1231(a)(6) compels the Government's reading. The statute states only that a removable alien "may be detained beyond the removal period." It does not say "detained indefinitely" or "detained until deportation is proved impossible." Read against the backdrop of the serious constitutional concerns identified above, the statute is most reasonably construed to authorize detention only for the period reasonably necessary to accomplish the alien's removal. This construction harmonizes with the statutory scheme's own recognition, in § 1231(a)(3), that supervised release—not perpetual detention—is the ordinary means of managing aliens who cannot immediately be removed.

**D. The Fifth Circuit's "good faith efforts"/"not impossible" standard is unworkable and effectively forecloses judicial review.**

Requiring an alien to prove that deportation is "impossible" —rather than merely improbable, indefinite, or not reasonably foreseeable—sets a standard virtually no petitioner could satisfy, since the Government can always posit some hypothetical future circumstance permitting removal. This standard renders habeas review illusory, contrary to the "fundamental" principle that "the Great Writ" must remain a meaningful check on Executive detention. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 301 (2001) (citing the "historic use of habeas corpus" as a check against Executive detention). A habeas court cannot meaningfully review detention where the burden imposed on the petitioner is impossible to carry.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and § 1231(a)(6) should be construed to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien's removal, with release required once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.
