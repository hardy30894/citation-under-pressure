**Argument**

The Constitution sets the qualifications for Members of Congress, and those qualifications are exclusive. Article I specifies that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, have been seven years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the State when elected; a Senator must be thirty, have been nine years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. In **Powell v. McCormack**, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that Congress may not add to the constitutionally prescribed qualifications for office, explaining that the Constitution’s text and history establish the listed qualifications as the sole conditions for service. See also **Davis v. Hildebrant**, 241 U.S. 565 (1916); **Smiley v. Holm**, 285 U.S. 355 (1932). If Congress itself cannot supplement those qualifications, a fortiori a State may not do so.

Arkansas Amendment 73 does exactly what the Constitution forbids: it makes prior service in Congress a disqualification for reelection. That is not a mere ballot-access regulation. It is an additional qualification. The amendment says that a person who has been elected to three terms in the House, or two terms in the Senate, “shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible” to appear on the ballot. A State may regulate the manner of elections, but it may not alter who may serve in Congress. **Ex parte Siebold**, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), recognized that States may enact election regulations consistent with federal law; it did not permit States to impose substantive qualifications for federal office. Likewise, **United States v. Classic**, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), and **Smiley v. Holm**, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), address the conduct of elections, not eligibility criteria for office.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated constitutional qualifications as fixed by the Constitution itself. In **Luther v. Borden**, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849), the Court distinguished questions committed to the political branches from those governed by the Constitution’s own allocations of authority. Here, the Constitution allocates qualifications expressly. In **In re McConaughy**, 119 U.S. 1 (1886), the Court likewise recognized the judiciary’s duty to enforce constitutional limits when a state measure transgresses them. Amendment 73 cannot be saved by calling it an initiative, because state sovereignty does not include power to revise the federal charter. **McPherson v. Blacker**, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), held that States possess broad authority over the mechanics of choosing electors, but it emphasized that such power exists only within the bounds of the federal Constitution.

The historical evidence confirms this result. In **The Federalist No. 52**, Madison explained that the qualifications for the House were designed in the constitutional text itself, and **The Federalist No. 60** rejects the notion that States may manipulate qualifications to control federal representation. The Founders’ choice to enumerate qualifications was deliberate and exclusive. Early constitutional practice likewise reflects that understanding. **Opinion of the Justices**, 303 Mass. 631, 21 N.E.2d 123 (1939), recognized that state action may not conflict with constitutional assignments of authority. And in **State ex rel. Smith v. Hester**, 227 Ala. 277, 149 So. 683 (1933), the court explained that state election procedures must yield to superior constitutional commands.

Amendment 73 also cannot be defended as a permissible “ballot access” rule. A ballot-access regulation is valid only if it governs the election process without changing the constitutional prerequisites for office. **Anderson v. Millikin**, 82 F.2d 700 (2d Cir. 1936), and **Sailors v. Board of Education**, 387 U.S. 105 (1967), recognize broad election administration authority, but neither permits a State to transform ineligibility into procedure. A rule that says an otherwise qualified candidate “shall not be eligible” after serving a specified number of terms is substantive disqualification, not administration.

Finally, the severability argument fails unless the invalid congressional term limits can be severed without defeating the voters’ overall design. **Allen v. Louisiana**, 103 U.S. 80 (1880), and **Dorchy v. Kansas**, 264 U.S. 286 (1924), make clear that severability turns on whether the valid provisions can stand independently and whether the enacting body would have adopted them alone. Here, Amendment 73 bundled state and federal term limits into a single political package. But even if the state provisions could survive as a matter of state law, the federal provisions cannot. Under **Powell v. McCormack**, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), and the Constitution’s text, Arkansas had no power to make prior service in Congress a disqualification for congressional office.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
