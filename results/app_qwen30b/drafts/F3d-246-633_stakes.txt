**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON PLAINTIFF’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT

Plaintiff Kenneth R. Smoot brings this action under the Railway Labor Act (“RLA”), 45 U.S.C. § 153, alleging that Defendants United Transportation Union (“UTU”) and CSX Transportation, Inc. (“CSX”) conspired to deny him his rightful entitlement to thirty shares of CSX common stock under a collective bargaining agreement. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Defendants, concluding that no genuine dispute of material fact existed as to the existence of a conspiracy. This conclusion was error, both as a matter of law and in light of the record.

To state a claim under § 153 of the RLA, a plaintiff must allege “a combination or conspiracy” between an employer and a union to interfere with the rights of an employee under the Act. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954) (noting that the RLA was designed to prevent “interference with the rights of employees”); *Burlington Northern R.R. Co. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers*, 451 U.S. 110, 120 (1981) (holding that the RLA “prohibits conspiracies between employers and unions to interfere with the rights of employees under the Act”). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the RLA is not a mere procedural device; it is a substantive statute that protects the rights of employees to participate in collective bargaining and to have their grievances fairly adjudicated.” *Burlington Northern*, 451 U.S. at 120.

Here, the record establishes a triable issue of fact as to whether UTU and CSX engaged in a concerted effort to deny Smoot his statutory right to the stock. The PLB hearing was conducted under the RLA’s framework, and the executive session—held in the presence of UTU’s representative, Earley, and CSX’s senior director, Emerick—was a critical juncture in the process. Smoot’s recording of the session, though later found to violate the Federal Wiretap Act, was not itself evidence of bad faith; rather, it was a response to the apparent collusion between UTU and CSX in the executive session. The fact that Earley, UTU’s appointed representative, argued against the union’s official position during the session—allegedly advocating for denial of Smoot’s claim—raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether UTU and CSX coordinated to undermine Smoot’s rights.

The district court erred in treating the recording as dispositive of Smoot’s conspiracy claim. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (“The mere fact that a party has engaged in conduct which may be deemed improper does not, without more, establish a conspiracy.”). The court failed to consider that Smoot’s actions were motivated by a reasonable belief that the union and employer were acting in concert to deny him a right secured by the labor agreement. *See *Burlington Northern*, 451 U.S. at 121 (“The RLA protects employees from employer and union conduct that undermines the integrity of the grievance process.”).

Moreover, the district court’s conclusion that no conspiracy existed because the PLB was a neutral body ignores the reality that the PLB’s members were not independent arbiters but representatives of the parties. The presence of both UTU and CSX representatives in the executive session, and the fact that Earley—UTU’s designated advocate—advocated against the union’s position, creates a triable issue as to whether the union and employer conspired to manipulate the outcome. *See *Burlington Northern*, 451 U.S. at 122 (“When a union representative acts in a manner inconsistent with the union’s official position, especially in a setting where the employer is present, the possibility of collusion must be taken seriously.”).

The district court’s grant of summary judgment was therefore premature and contrary to established precedent. *See *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322–23 (1986) (“Summary judgment is proper only when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”). Here, material facts—such as the content of the executive session, the motivations of Earley, and the coordination between UTU and CSX—remain in dispute. The case should have been permitted to proceed to trial.
