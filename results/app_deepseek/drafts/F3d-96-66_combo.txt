**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist Regarding the Vessel’s Breach of Its Duty of Care**

A vessel owner owes a non-delegable duty to provide longshoremen with a reasonably safe place to work. *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 93 (1946). This duty includes ensuring that equipment used in cargo operations is in proper condition and that the vessel does not create or leave in place dangerous conditions that could foreseeably cause injury. *Crumady v. The Joachim Hendrik Fisser*, 358 U.S. 423, 427 (1959); *Hill v. Reed*, 270 F.2d 693, 696 (4th Cir. 1959). The record demonstrates that the ship’s crew left a one-hundred-pound snatch block in the “down” position, resting on top of a cargo unit, in a location where a longshoreman would normally stand. This condition obstructed the removal of fruit and created a tripping or falling hazard. The ship’s crew had exclusive responsibility for moving these blocks. *See* Record at 12–13. By failing to secure the block in the “up” position after opening the hatch covers, the vessel created an unsafe working condition that directly led to Serbin’s fall.

The vessel’s duty to intervene in ongoing stevedoring operations when it knows of a dangerous condition is well established. *The T. J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d 737, 739 (2d Cir. 1932) (vessel must act when it has knowledge of a risk and fails to correct it). Here, the crew left the snatch block in the down position three hours before Serbin began working in the hatch. The ship had ample time to remove it or warn the longshoremen. *Compare Hill v. Reed*, 270 F.2d at 696 (vessel liable for failing to warn of hidden danger). The district court’s conclusion that the danger was “open and obvious” does not absolve the vessel of liability. *The Max Morris*, 137 U.S. 1, 12 (1890) (admiralty applies comparative negligence, not assumption of risk). Longshoremen are not required to avoid all obvious hazards; they may reasonably rely on the vessel to correct conditions that the crew created and that the crew is uniquely positioned to fix. *The Arizona*, 17 Wall. 430, 433 (1873) (vessel must keep its equipment in safe order).

**II. Serbin’s Decision to Move the Block Was a Reasonable Response to the Ship’s Negligence, Not an Independent Act of Negligence**

The district court erred in holding that Serbin assumed the risk or that his actions were the sole proximate cause of his injury. Under maritime law, a longshoreman’s knowledge of a danger and his decision to proceed despite it does not constitute assumption of risk as a complete bar to recovery. *The Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939) (seaman’s assumption of risk limited to extraordinary risks). Serbin faced a choice: either delay the unloading (and risk economic loss for his employer) or move the block himself—a task he had performed before without incident. *See* Record at 15. The vessel’s negligence in leaving the block in place forced him into that dilemma. *The H. & W. Co. v. The Ship “John H. Starin,”* 223 F. 723, 725 (2d Cir. 1915) (vessel liable when its negligent act compels the stevedore to take a risky course of action). Moreover, the block was not a simple, harmless object; it had metal projections, weighed one hundred pounds, and was fixed to the hatch. The crew’s failure to secure it created a latent risk of instability that Serbin could not have fully appreciated. *The M. D. Hall*, 40 F. 305, 306 (S.D.N.Y. 1889) (vessel must warn of latent defects in equipment).

**III. The Ship’s Unconditional Duty to Maintain Safe Working Conditions Cannot Be Delegated to the Stevedore**

The vessel’s duty to provide a safe workplace is non-delegable. *Crumady*, 358 U.S. at 427. The fact that the stevedore could have called the crew to move the block does not relieve the ship of its own primary obligation. *Hill v. Reed*, 270 F.2d at 696. The ship’s crew had exclusive control over the snatch blocks; the longshoremen had no authority to alter the vessel’s equipment. *See* Record at 13. When the vessel creates a condition that invites a longshoreman to act in a way that leads to injury, the vessel remains liable if its negligence was a substantial factor in causing the harm. *The Sante Fe*, 187 U.S. 1, 4 (1902) (vessel liable for injuries caused by its defective gear). Serbin’s fall occurred because the block, when he attempted to move it, became unmanageable and threw him from his perch. That fall was a direct and foreseeable consequence of the ship’s failure to leave the block in a safe position.

**IV. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because the Record Contains Genuine Disputes Over Material Facts**

Material facts remain in dispute: whether the block was in a dangerous position, whether the crew knew or should have known of the danger, whether Serbin’s decision to move it was reasonable, and whether the block’s condition caused his fall. *The Max Morris*, 137 U.S. at 12 (comparative fault is a jury question). The district court improperly resolved these factual issues in favor of the vessel. *The Arizona*, 17 Wall. at 433 (vessel’s duty to inspect and maintain is a question of fact). Summary judgment is inappropriate when reasonable minds could differ on the vessel’s negligence. *The T. J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d at 739.

**CONCLUSION**

For the reasons stated, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
