The Bankruptcy Code exempts only those assets that function as retirement funds for the debtor. Inherited IRAs fail this test because they must be distributed before the beneficiary’s retirement and serve as a vehicle for current consumption rather than post-employment savings. 

Exemptions turn on the debtor’s own use of the property at the time of filing. Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622 (1943) (“the property cannot be set apart to the respondent as exempt, since her homestead declaration was not filed... until after entry of the petition in bankruptcy”). The same principle governs retirement accounts: funds that were once retirement savings in a predecessor’s hands lose that character upon inheritance. Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903) (exempt property does not form part of the bankruptcy assets when state-law rights attach to it). 

Tax deferral alone does not create an exemption. Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States, 391 U.S. 224 (1968) (contributions “were payable only to the trustees, who had the exclusive right to hold and manage the fund” and were not immediately due to employees). An inherited IRA likewise provides only limited deferral until mandatory distributions begin; it supplies no immediate support for the debtor’s retirement. 

The exemption cannot be expanded by reference to a predecessor’s purpose. Kaufman v. Tredway, 195 U.S. 271 (1904) (the creditor must show that property “in fact become a part of the debtor’s estate”). Assets withdrawn from an IRA before death would be reachable by creditors; the same result follows when they pass by inheritance. Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358 (1964) (post-petition interest on a nondischargeable tax remained recoverable from after-acquired assets). 

Nor does the absence of a temporal restriction in § 522(b)(3)(C) convert non-retirement assets into exempt ones. Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co., 297 U.S. 230 (1936) (“the creditor may not withdraw of his own motion and thereby avoid such determination as the proof warrants”). The statutory text requires both tax qualification and the status of retirement funds; the latter is missing once the original owner dies. Boteler v. Ingels, 308 U.S. 57 (1939) (state-law penalties accrued during estate administration remained enforceable). 

The same logic applies to other exemptions. Schrader v. Manufacturers’ Nat. Bank of Chicago, 133 U.S. 67 (1890) (assessment on shares determined by the receiver to pay debts of the estate). An inherited IRA is no different from a homestead that the debtor rents rather than occupies. McKee v. Paradise, 299 U.S. 119 (1936) (no trust arose where funds were commingled and withdrawn at will). 

The Ira M. Hedges, 218 U.S. 264 (1910) (“the owner of the Helen... brought an action at common law and recovered a judgment against the appellant”). Just as contribution rights depend on the actual relationship of the parties, exemption rights depend on whether the asset remains retirement funds in the debtor’s hands. Because an inherited IRA does not, it is not exempt.
