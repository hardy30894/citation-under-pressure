# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 107 Remains Available Unless a Settlement Resolves CERCLA Liability, Not Liability Under a Different Statute

CERCLA provides two "avenues" of recovery: a cost-recovery action under section 107 and a contribution action under section 113. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 163 & n.3, 167 (2004). These are not interchangeable. As this Court has explained, section 107 permits "potentially responsible parties" to recover costs they have "incurred" in cleaning up a site, while section 113(f) allows a party to seek "contribution" only after it "has resolved its liability" for a CERCLA "response action" through a "settlement." *Atlantic Research Corp. v. United States*, 551 U.S. 128, 138–39 (2007) (quoting 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B)). The two remedies protect different interests, and a party's right to proceed under section 107 evaporates only when the specific triggering condition of section 113(f)(3)(B)—resolution of *CERCLA* liability—has been satisfied. *Id.* at 139 n.6.

That triggering condition was not satisfied here. The 2004 Consent Decree resolved Guam's liability under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. See 33 U.S.C. §§ 1251 *et seq.* Congress enacted CERCLA "in response to the serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution" through a specific statutory scheme with its own liability provisions, defenses, and cost-allocation mechanisms. *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998). It did not thereby transform every environmental consent decree—whatever statute it enforces—into a CERCLA settlement. Because the underlying enforcement action here was brought "under the Clean Water Act," Compl. ¶ 26, J.A. 85, and sought relief for "discharging pollutants . . . into waters of the United States without obtaining a permit," *id.*, the resulting Decree resolved *Clean Water Act* liability. Section 113(f)(3)(B), by its terms, applies only to a party that "has resolved its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action" under CERCLA. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Nothing in the Decree purports to resolve Guam's CERCLA liability, and the court of appeals erred in reading CERCLA's contribution trigger into an agreement that never invoked CERCLA at all.

## II. The Consent Decree's Express Disclaimer of Liability Confirms That Guam's CERCLA Liability Was Never Resolved

The Sixth Circuit has recognized that a settlement's disclaimer of liability is powerful evidence that the parties did not intend to resolve liability for section 113(f)(3)(B) purposes. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding no resolution of liability where "the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability"). Here, the Decree states unambiguously that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. This is not idle boilerplate; it reflects the parties' considered choice to resolve a live Clean Water Act enforcement dispute without adjudicating—or admitting—responsibility for the environmental contamination the Navy caused over five decades of military use. See *Trinity Industries, Inc. v. Chicago Bridge & Iron Co.*, 735 F.3d 131, 136 (3d Cir. 2013) (looking to whether an agreement "clearly and unambiguously" resolves the party's liability before treating it as a section 113(f)(3)(B) settlement). Guam should not be penalized for having negotiated favorable disclaimer language that reflects the actual, limited scope of what it resolved.

## III. Petitioner's Reading Avoids the Anomalous Result the Court of Appeals' Decision Produces

The court below effectively held that any settlement touching on "removal" or "remedial" measures—regardless of the statute under which it arises—starts CERCLA's three-year contribution clock. That approach produces exactly the kind of trap for the unwary that this Court's contribution jurisprudence forbids. *Cooper Industries* recognized that section 113(f) contribution actions exist to protect PRPs who have "resolved their liability" from double recovery, not to ambush parties who settled under an entirely different regulatory regime. 543 U.S. at 167–68. Treating Guam's Clean Water Act settlement as a CERCLA-triggering event means that, by the time the United States was finally identified in CERCLA's own administrative processes as a potentially responsible party, Guam's contribution window had already closed—years before this suit was filed. Congress could not have intended a regime under which parties lose CERCLA rights based on settlements that never mentioned CERCLA. As the D.C. Circuit itself has acknowledged, CERCLA's remedial design "enable[s] the prompt cleanup of hazardous waste sites and . . . ensure[s] that responsible parties foot the bill." *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). Barring Guam's section 107 claim undermines that very purpose by shielding a longtime polluter—the Navy—from liability for its own contamination.

Because the Consent Decree resolved only Clean Water Act liability, expressly disclaimed any admission of liability, and cannot fairly be read to satisfy section 113(f)(3)(B)'s CERCLA-specific trigger, Guam's section 107 cost-recovery action remains timely under CERCLA's six-year limitations period, 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). This Court should reverse.
