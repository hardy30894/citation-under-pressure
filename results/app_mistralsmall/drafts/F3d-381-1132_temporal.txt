**ARGUMENT**

I. The Government’s Unauthorized Expansion of the Easement Constitutes a Physical Taking Under the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment provides that private property shall not “be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical occupation of property, even if pursuant to an easement, constitutes a per se taking requiring compensation. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 266 (1946) (“If the government physically takes possession of an interest in property, for whatever use, it must compensate the former owner.”). The Supreme Court has long held that “[t]he power to take private property for public use is limited by the Fifth Amendment. When the taking is physical in nature, the Court has held that the Fifth Amendment exacts compensation regardless of the public interests that motivated the taking.” *Id.* at 266-67 (citing *United States v. Pewee Coal Co.*, 341 U.S. 114, 115 (1951)).

Here, the government’s conversion of the BPA’s signal lines to fiber optic cables, and its subsequent leasing of excess capacity to third-party telecommunications companies, constitutes a physical occupation of Tuthill’s property beyond the scope of the original easement. The easement granted the BPA only the right to maintain “signal lines ... necessary and in connection with” the power transmission lines. The government’s installation of fiber optic cables far exceeding the BPA’s current operational needs, and its leasing of unused capacity to unrelated third parties, exceeds the scope of the easement and effects a physical taking. *See United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748 (1947) (“Where the government by its agents enters upon the land of another and appropriates it for its own purposes, it is a taking within the scope of the Fifth Amendment.”).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. 162.20 Acres of Land*, 129 F. Supp. 597 (D. Idaho 1955), is misplaced. In that case, the court found no taking where the government merely maintained an existing easement for its intended purpose. *Id.* at 600. Here, by contrast, the government has expanded the easement’s scope by installing excess capacity and permitting third-party use, thereby effecting a physical occupation. *See Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266-67.

II. The Government’s Misuse of the Easement Constitutes a Physical Taking Regardless of Whether the Fiber Optic Cables Themselves Occupy the Land.

The Supreme Court has recognized that a physical taking may occur even where the government’s actions do not involve the literal occupation of land. In *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316 (1917), the Court held that the government’s flooding of land pursuant to a flowage easement constituted a taking, even though the flooding was temporary and the land was not continuously occupied. *Id.* at 327-28. Similarly, in *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745 (1947), the Court found a taking where the government’s agents entered upon land to conduct surveys and tests, even though the occupation was temporary. *Id.* at 748.

Here, the government’s installation of fiber optic cables and leasing of excess capacity to third parties constitutes a physical occupation of Tuthill’s property, regardless of whether the cables themselves occupy the land continuously. The government’s actions exceed the scope of the easement and effect a permanent physical taking. *See Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266-67; *Cress*, 243 U.S. at 327-28.

III. The Government’s Actions Are Not Shielded from Liability by the Original Easement Grant.

The government argues that Tuthill’s claim fails because the original easement grant authorized the BPA’s actions. However, the Supreme Court has long held that an easement’s scope is limited to the purposes for which it was granted. *See, e.g., United States v. 1,584.00 Acres of Land*, 146 F. Supp. 606, 610 (D. Idaho 1956) (“An easement is limited to the purposes for which it was granted, and any use beyond that scope constitutes a trespass.”). Here, the government’s conversion of the signal lines to fiber optic cables and leasing of excess capacity to third parties exceeds the scope of the easement and constitutes a physical taking. *See Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266-67.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. 162.20 Acres of Land*, 129 F. Supp. 597 (D. Idaho 1955), is again misplaced. In that case, the court found no taking where the government merely maintained an existing easement for its intended purpose. *Id.* at 600. Here, the government has expanded the easement’s scope by installing excess capacity and permitting third-party use, thereby effecting a physical taking. *See Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266-67.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tuthill respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
