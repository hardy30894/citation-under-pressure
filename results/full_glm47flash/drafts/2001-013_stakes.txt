**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE COAST GUARD HAS EXCLUSIVE AUTHORITY TO PRESCRIBE AND ENFORCE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH STANDARDS ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

The Court of Appeals erred in concluding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority pre-empts OSHA jurisdiction over the working conditions of seamen on uninspected vessels under 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The pre-emption provision of the OSH Act is triggered only when another federal agency has “exclusive” authority to prescribe and enforce standards affecting occupational safety or health. *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 532 U.S. 528, 538 (2001). The Coast Guard does not possess such exclusive authority; its regulatory scheme is limited to the “safety of life and property” and does not extend to the full range of occupational safety and health standards that OSHA enforces. Id. at 539.

The Court of Appeals relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), which held that OSHA regulations do not apply to seamen on vessels. However, *Chao* expressly rejected the notion that the Coast Guard’s general regulatory authority pre-empts OSHA. The Supreme Court noted that the Coast Guard’s authority is not exclusive, and therefore the pre-emption provision does not apply. Id. at 540. The Court emphasized that the Coast Guard’s regulations are “limited in scope” and do not cover the “broad range of occupational safety and health hazards” that OSHA addresses. Id. at 539.

Furthermore, the fact that the Coast Guard regulates uninspected vessels does not create exclusive authority. *Chao* held that the pre-emption provision is not triggered by the existence of a general regulatory scheme, but by the existence of exclusive authority. Id. at 541. Because the Coast Guard does not have exclusive authority to prescribe and enforce occupational safety and health standards on uninspected vessels, OSHA retains jurisdiction to enforce the Act on such vessels. Consequently, the citation issued by OSHA was proper, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed.
