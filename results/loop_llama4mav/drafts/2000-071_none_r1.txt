The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for "religious purposes," is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum.

The government has the authority to create a limited public forum, opening its property to certain groups or for certain purposes, without being required to allow all speech. See Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators' Assn., 262 U.S. 37 (1923); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268 (1951). In such a forum, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on speech, as long as they are viewpoint-neutral. The Milford Central School's community use policy meets this standard, as it excludes all groups that seek to use the facilities for "religious purposes," regardless of their specific viewpoint.

The Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, are not simply a viewpoint on a secular subject, but rather are a form of religious worship. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952). The school's exclusion of the Club is not viewpoint discrimination, but rather is a reasonable application of its community use policy. The policy is designed to avoid violating the Establishment Clause, which is a legitimate government interest. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947); McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948).

The Club's argument that its activities are simply a viewpoint on a secular subject, such as morals and character, is unpersuasive. While the Club may be teaching morals and character, it is doing so in a way that is inextricably linked with religious doctrine. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940). The school is not required to allow the Club to use its facilities simply because other groups are allowed to discuss similar topics from a secular perspective. The Court has recognized that the government may distinguish between different types of speech, including religious speech, as long as the distinction is reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569 (1941).

Furthermore, the school's policy is not unconstitutional simply because it may have the effect of excluding certain groups or viewpoints. See Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158 (1944). The government has a legitimate interest in maintaining the separation of church and state, and the school's policy is a reasonable means of achieving this goal.

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and is designed to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals, holding that Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights.
