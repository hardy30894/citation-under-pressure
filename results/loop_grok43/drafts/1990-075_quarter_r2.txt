ARGUMENT

Congress may delegate its legislative power to another branch when it provides an intelligible principle to guide the exercise of that authority. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). The Controlled Substances Act supplies such a principle guiding the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority. The Act meets this standard by requiring the Attorney General to consider the history, pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse together with the risk to public health before finding an imminent hazard. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988).

This guidance is comparable to that sustained in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), where Congress directed the Price Administrator to fix prices that “effectuate the purposes” of preventing inflation. It likewise parallels the delegation upheld in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), which authorized rules for associations “in accordance with the standards” set forth in the statute.

The two decisions invalidating delegations are distinguishable. In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the statute “did not declare any policy” or establish any “criterion to govern” the President’s course. Here the enumerated factors and the imminent-hazard limitation supply the missing criteria.

Congress may also authorize the Attorney General to subdelegate. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court approved the transfer of functions vested in the President to subordinate officers. Earlier, Wayman v. Southard, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825), recognized that Congress may “commit the details” to those charged with execution. Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), and United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911), confirm that administrative officers may promulgate rules carrying criminal consequences when acting within statutory bounds.

The temporary scheduling provisions therefore rest on a valid delegation of authority that the Attorney General lawfully transmitted to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.
