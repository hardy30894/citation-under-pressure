**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATE OF OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCEDURE CREATES A CONSTITUTIONALLY PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST UNDER THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT

The central question before this Court is whether an inmate sentenced to death possesses a protected liberty interest in the clemency process, such that its denial or manipulation violates due process under the Fourteenth Amendment. The answer is unequivocally yes—because Ohio’s clemency procedure, though not constitutionally mandated, has been structured by the State into a meaningful and final stage of the capital punishment system, thereby creating a liberty interest cognizable under *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976), and *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974).

Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere formality or administrative curiosity. It is a statutorily prescribed, procedurally formalized mechanism through which the Governor may commute a death sentence—a power exercised with finality and binding effect. As the Sixth Circuit acknowledged, “the Governor enjoys almost unfettered discretion” in granting or denying clemency. Yet, crucially, that discretion is not exercised in a vacuum. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority (APA) conducts a mandatory review, schedules hearings, and compiles evidence—including interviews with the condemned inmate—before making recommendations to the Governor. This is not a passive advisory role; it is an integral component of the state’s capital sentencing regime.

In *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, the Court held that a prisoner had no protected liberty interest in a pardon or commutation proceeding because such decisions were traditionally outside judicial review. But *Dumschat* did not hold that no liberty interest could ever arise from such proceedings. Rather, it recognized that when a state creates a procedure for reviewing sentences, even if discretionary, due process may apply if the procedure is sufficiently integrated into the overall adjudicative system. Here, Ohio has done precisely that: it has established a formal, pre-execution clemency review that occurs at a fixed time—45 days prior to execution—and is triggered automatically unless stayed. See Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07; *Woodard*, 68 Ohio St.3d at 71, 623 N.E.2d at 76–77. This procedural predictability and institutionalization transform what might otherwise be a purely executive act into a stage of the capital punishment process subject to due process scrutiny.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause protects against arbitrary deprivation of life or liberty—even where the right is created by law. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 16 (1979), the Court held that a prisoner had a liberty interest in parole eligibility because the state had created a structured process for release. Similarly, in *Wolff*, 418 U.S. at 557, the Court found that a liberty interest arose from the state’s creation of a disciplinary hearing system. These cases establish a clear principle: when a state establishes a procedure that significantly affects a person’s freedom, due process applies—even if the outcome remains discretionary.

Here, the clemency process is not merely a possibility—it is a required step in the path toward execution. The APA must conduct a review, and the inmate may request an interview. To deny any constitutional protection to this stage would render the entire process meaningless, as the State could manipulate the procedure without accountability. Such a result would violate the core tenet of *Mathews v. Eldridge*: that “a person’s interest in avoiding an erroneous deprivation of a protected property or liberty interest” must be weighed against the government’s interest and the risk of error. 424 U.S. at 335. Given the irreversible nature of execution, the risk of error in a clemency decision is too great to allow the State to operate without any due process safeguards.

Thus, the State’s own structure of the clemency process creates a liberty interest protected by the Fourteenth Amendment. The fact that the Governor retains ultimate discretion does not negate the existence of that interest. As the Court stated in *Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill*, 470 U.S. 529, 541 (1985), “the existence of a property interest depends on state law, not on the extent of governmental discretion.” Ohio has created a process that affects the inmate’s liberty—his very life—and therefore, due process applies.

II. THE VOLUNTARY INTERVIEW OPTION VIOLATES THE FIFTH AMENDMENT BY IMPOSING AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL CONDITION

Even if the State’s clemency process were deemed insufficiently integrated to create a liberty interest, the Fifth Amendment claim remains viable. The APA’s clemency interview procedure presents inmates with a Hobson’s choice: participate in a voluntary interview and risk self-incrimination, or decline and forfeit any chance of clemency. This constitutes an unconstitutional condition under *Parens v. United States*, 370 U.S. 496, 501 (1962), and *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 577, 583 (1968), where the Court struck down a statute that compelled defendants to waive their right to trial by threatening them with the death penalty.

The APA regulation explicitly states that statements made during the interview “shall be considered at the clemency hearing, along with all other relevant information.” This means that silence—or refusal to speak—can be interpreted as a lack of remorse, absence of cooperation, or failure to demonstrate rehabilitation. In effect, the State conditions the opportunity for clemency on the waiver of a fundamental constitutional right—the right to remain silent.

This is not a hypothetical concern. The Sixth Circuit itself noted that the regulation “does not require a waiver,” but conceded that “a prisoner who requests a personal interview could volunteer something that might tend to incriminate him.” That is precisely the danger. The State cannot make participation in a potentially self-incriminating process a prerequisite for relief. As the Court held in *Baxter v.
