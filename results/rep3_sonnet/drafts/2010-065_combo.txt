ARGUMENT

I. The Tenth Amendment Protects the Prerogatives of the States, and a Private Litigant May Not Invoke It Absent the State or Its Officers.

The Tenth Amendment provides that powers not delegated to the United States "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." By its terms and by settled construction, the Amendment safeguards the sovereign interests of the States as governmental entities, not the personal liberty interests of individual citizens. This Court so held in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), where private utility companies argued that TVA's sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court rejected the challenge, holding that the companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." Id. at 144. That holding controls here. Bond, like the utilities in Tennessee Electric, is a private party seeking to litigate an interest that belongs, if it belongs to anyone, to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania has never suggested that § 229 offends its sovereignty, and Bond cannot manufacture standing on the State's behalf.

The same principle animates Frothingham v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), where this Court held that a party invoking the judicial power to invalidate an Act of Congress "must be able to show not only that the statute is invalid but that he has sustained or is immediately in danger of sustaining some direct injury as the result of its enforcement, and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally." Id. at 488. An alleged intrusion on the reserved powers of the States is precisely such an indefinite, structural grievance—one shared, if at all, by every resident of the Commonwealth, and vindicable through the State's own political and legal processes, not through a criminal defendant's personal appeal. See also Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126, 129-30 (1922) (a citizen's undifferentiated interest in the validity of legislation affecting government structure does not supply the "direct injury" required for judicial relief).

II. A Criminal Defendant May Not Rest a Constitutional Defense on Rights That Belong to a Third Sovereign.

This Court has repeatedly refused to permit litigants to vindicate rights that belong to someone else. In Tileston v. Ullman, 318 U.S. 44 (1943), a physician sought to challenge a Connecticut statute by invoking the constitutional rights of his patients; the Court dismissed for want of standing because the physician's "own rights are [not] in anywise jeopardized." Id. at 46. The logic of Tileston applies with full force where, as here, the "third party" whose rights are asserted is not a private citizen but an entire State. If a physician cannot borrow his patient's constitutional rights to escape a state statute, a defendant convicted under a federal criminal statute cannot borrow the Commonwealth's Tenth Amendment interest to escape a federal one.

This Court's decision in United States v. Raines, 362 U.S. 17 (1960), reinforces the point from another angle. There the Court held that "one to whom application of a statute is constitutional will not be heard to attack the statute on the ground that it might be applied unconstitutionally to others in situations not before the Court." Id. at 21. Bond's federalism argument is, at bottom, an argument that § 229 might unconstitutionally invade state authority in some hypothetical prosecution of "local" conduct that Congress never intended to reach. But Bond's own conduct—repeatedly deploying toxic chemicals in a sustained campaign against a private citizen—is precisely the sort of conduct that falls within the plain terms of the statute Congress enacted to implement a validly ratified treaty. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920). Holland teaches that "there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could." Id. at 433. Whatever force Bond's abstract federalism argument might have in a different case, she is not the party to raise it, any more than the litigant in Raines could raise the rights of absent third parties.

III. Prudential Principles of Constitutional Avoidance Counsel Against Reaching the Merits Where Standing Is Absent.

This Court has long observed that it will not pass upon a constitutional question "if there is also present some other ground upon which the case may be disposed of." Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288, 341 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring). The Tenth Amendment's core function, as this Court has explained, is to state "but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered," United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941)—it does not create a personal constitutional right enforceable by a defendant independent of the State whose sovereignty is allegedly at stake. Nor does the ordinary balance of federal and state criminal jurisdiction, in which "the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States," Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945), transform every federal prosecution into an occasion for an individual defendant to litigate the States' reserved powers.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
