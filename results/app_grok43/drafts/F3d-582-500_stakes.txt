I. Erdman Accumulated the Requisite 1,250 Hours of Service.

The FMLA limits eligibility to employees who have worked at least 1,250 hours during the preceding 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). Hours of service are measured according to FLSA principles. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). Those principles count all time an employee is suffered or permitted to work, regardless of whether the employer later grants compensatory time off instead of immediate pay. See 29 C.F.R. § 785.11; see also Christensen v. Harris County, 529 U.S. 576, 578 (2000) (recognizing compensatory time as an alternative form of compensation for hours already worked).

Nationwide’s own records and the testimony of Erdman’s supervisors establish that Erdman regularly performed extra hours outside the office and that those hours were either paid or banked as comp time. Because the hours were actually worked with the employer’s knowledge and acquiescence, they count toward the statutory threshold. The District Court’s exclusion of those hours therefore rests on an incorrect legal premise.

II. Nationwide Cannot Defeat Eligibility by Its Own Failure to Maintain Accurate Records.

An employer that fails to keep accurate records of hours worked bears the burden of proving that the employee did not meet the 1,250-hour requirement. See Reich v. Gateway Press, Inc., 13 F.3d 685, 701 (3d Cir. 1994). Nationwide authorized and tracked Erdman’s extra hours for years; it cannot now disclaim those hours to render her ineligible. Once the properly counted hours are included, Erdman satisfied the statutory minimum and was an eligible employee entitled to FMLA protection.

III. The District Court’s Eligibility Ruling Requires Reversal of the Summary Judgment on Both FMLA Claims.

Because Erdman met the hours threshold, the District Court’s alternative grounds for dismissing the interference and retaliation claims must be reexamined on remand in light of the full record. See McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009).
