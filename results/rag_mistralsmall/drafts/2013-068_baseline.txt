**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL, NARROWLY TAILORED TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION THAT LEAVES AMPLE ALTERNATIVE CHANNELS FOR SPEECH.**

The Massachusetts statute at issue here, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½, is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. As this Court has repeatedly held, such regulations are permissible if they are (1) content-neutral, (2) narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984). The First Circuit correctly upheld the statute on these grounds, and this Court should affirm.

**A. The Massachusetts Buffer Zone Is Content-Neutral.**

The statute is content-neutral because it does not discriminate based on the viewpoint or subject matter of speech. *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 386 (1992). It applies equally to all speakers—whether pro-life advocates, pro-choice supporters, or individuals with no viewpoint at all—who seek to engage in expressive activity within the 35-foot buffer zone. The statute’s exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and others further demonstrate its neutrality, as they apply without regard to the content of any speech. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 177 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d*, 130 S. Ct. 1881 (2010).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. In *Sorrell*, the Court struck down a statute that selectively regulated speech based on its content—specifically, the sale and use of prescription data for marketing purposes. *Id.* at 566-67. The Massachusetts statute, by contrast, applies neutrally to all speakers within the buffer zone, regardless of their message. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar content-neutral buffer zone around healthcare facilities).

**B. The Buffer Zone Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve Significant Governmental Interests.**

The Massachusetts statute advances two compelling interests: (1) ensuring public safety and access to healthcare facilities, and (2) protecting the privacy and psychological well-being of patients and clinic staff. *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994); *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-16.

This Court has repeatedly recognized the legitimacy of these interests. In *Madsen*, the Court upheld a 36-foot buffer zone around an abortion clinic, finding that it served the significant governmental interest in protecting patients and staff from harassment and obstruction. *Id.* at 767-68. Similarly, in *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado statute prohibiting certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facilities, concluding that it advanced the State’s interests in public safety, patient access, and privacy. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-16.

Petitioners’ argument that the Massachusetts statute is not narrowly tailored because it is broader than necessary is unavailing. The First Circuit correctly held that the 35-foot buffer zone is narrowly tailored because it is the “minimum intrusion” necessary to achieve the State’s interests. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 179. The statute does not prohibit speech outside the buffer zone, and it leaves ample alternative channels for communication, including sidewalks and public areas beyond the 35-foot radius. *Id.* at 180.

**C. The Buffer Zone Leaves Ample Alternative Channels for Communication.**

Petitioners claim that the buffer zone prevents them from engaging in effective speech, but this argument ignores the ample alternatives still available to them. As this Court has held, a regulation does not violate the First Amendment merely because it imposes some burden on speech, so long as ample alternative channels remain. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791; *Clark*, 468 U.S. at 293.

The Massachusetts statute leaves open numerous alternative channels for petitioners’ speech, including:
- **Verbal communication**: Petitioners may speak to individuals outside the buffer zone, including on sidewalks and public areas beyond the 35-foot radius. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 180.
- **Literature distribution**: Petitioners may distribute literature to individuals outside the buffer zone. *Id.*
- **Signage and symbols**: Petitioners may display signs and symbols outside the buffer zone. *Id.*
- **Prayer and other expressive conduct**: Petitioners may engage in prayer, singing, and other expressive conduct outside the buffer zone. *Id.*

The First Circuit correctly concluded that these alternatives are ample, noting that petitioners “have achieved success in their counseling efforts” despite the buffer zone. *McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen III)*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213 (D. Mass. 2012). The fact that petitioners must move slightly farther from clinic entrances does not render the statute unconstitutional. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720 (upholding a buffer zone despite the burden it placed on speakers).

**II. HILL V. COLORADO REMAINS GOOD LAW AND SHOULD NOT BE LIMITED OR OVERRULED.**

Petitioners argue that *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), should be limited or overruled, but this argument is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. *Hill* is indistinguishable from the Massachusetts statute, and this Court has repeatedly reaffirmed its reasoning.

In *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado statute prohibiting certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facilities, including abortion clinics. *Id.* at 715-16. The Court held that the statute was a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation that left open ample alternative channels for speech. *Id.* at 720. The Court also rejected the argument that the statute was not narrowly tailored, noting that the 100-foot buffer zone was the “minimum intrusion” necessary to achieve the State’s interests in public safety, patient access, and privacy. *Id.*

Petitioners’ reliance on *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357 (1997), is misplaced. In *Schenck*, the Court struck down a “floating buffer zone” that moved with clinic escorts, but it did not question the validity of fixed buffer zones like the one at issue here. *Id.* at 377. The Court distinguished *Madsen* and *Hill* on the ground that the floating buffer zone was not narrowly tailored, as it imposed a greater burden on speech than necessary. *Id.* The Massachusetts statute, by contrast, is a fixed buffer zone that is narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed. The Massachusetts buffer zone is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that leaves ample alternative channels for speech. *Hill v. Colorado* remains good law and should not be limited or overruled.
