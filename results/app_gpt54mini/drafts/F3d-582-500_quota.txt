**Argument**

The District Court erred in entering summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims. This Court reviews that ruling de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in Erdman’s favor. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). Summary judgment is improper where the record permits a rational jury to find in favor of the nonmovant. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248–50 (1986).

I. **The record contains sufficient evidence for a jury to find that Erdman met the FMLA’s 1,250-hour requirement.**

The FMLA protects “eligible employee[s]” who have been employed “for at least 12 months” and have worked “at least 1,250 hours of service” during the preceding 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The statute’s implementing regulation directs employers to count as hours worked those hours actually worked under the FLSA framework. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). Where the record is disputed, eligibility is ordinarily a question for the factfinder. *Wysong v. Dow Chem. Co.*, 503 F.3d 441, 447–48 (6th Cir. 2007); *Brennan v. Metro. Opera Ass’n, Inc.*, 192 F.3d 310, 317–18 (2d Cir. 1999).

Here, the evidence showed that Erdman had long been a reliable employee who regularly worked extra hours beyond her part-time schedule, and her supervisor, Sarno, regularly authorized payment or “comp” time for those hours. A reasonable jury could find that these extra hours were “hours of service” under the Act. The District Court instead resolved the factual dispute against Erdman, contrary to the summary judgment standard. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255; *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150–51 (2000).

Nationwide may argue that Erdman’s part-time and non-exempt status precluded eligibility. But the FMLA does not exclude part-time or non-exempt workers; it asks only whether the employee worked the required hours. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A); *Peters v. Gilead Sciences, Inc.*, 533 F.3d 594, 601–02 (7th Cir. 2008). Nor may an employer defeat eligibility by retroactively disavowing extra hours that supervisors knew of and permitted. An employer’s records and conduct matter, especially where the employer controlled the timekeeping process. *Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946).

II. **Nationwide’s conduct supports both FMLA interference and retaliation claims.**

The FMLA makes it unlawful to “interfere with, restrain, or deny” the exercise of protected rights and separately prohibits retaliation. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1), (2). This Court has recognized distinct theories for interference and retaliation. *Lichtenstein v. Univ. of Pittsburgh Med. Ctr.*, 691 F.3d 294, 301–02 (3d Cir. 2012); *Erdman v. Nationwide Ins. Co.*, 582 F.3d 500, 509–10 (3d Cir. 2009). An employer interferes when its actions would discourage an employee from exercising FMLA rights, and retaliation may be shown by adverse action motivated by the protected request. *Lichtenstein*, 691 F.3d at 301–03; *Millea v. Metro-North R.R. Co.*, 658 F.3d 154, 164–65 (2d Cir. 2011).

A jury could find interference from Nationwide’s treatment of Erdman’s leave inquiry. Erdman requested clarification about August vacation, then announced she would seek FMLA leave if vacation was unavailable. Shortly thereafter, Nationwide eliminated her part-time position, required her to return full-time, and then terminated her after she submitted FMLA paperwork. Timing, standing alone, can be probative of retaliation when combined with other evidence. *Jalil v. Avdel Corp.*, 873 F.2d 701, 708 (3d Cir. 1989); *Krouse v. Am. Sterilizer Co.*, 126 F.3d 494, 503–04 (3d Cir. 1997).

The record also contains evidence from which a jury could infer pretext. Getgen’s shifting explanations about Erdman’s work, comp time, and alleged misconduct, together with the close temporal proximity between Erdman’s leave request and her termination, permit a finding that Nationwide’s asserted reasons were not its true reasons. Under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973), and *Fuentes v. Perskie*, 32 F.3d 759, 764–65 (3d Cir. 1994), Erdman need only point to evidence from which a factfinder could reasonably disbelieve Nationwide’s explanation or believe retaliation was more likely than not a determinative factor. She did so.

III. **Nationwide’s reliance on Erdman’s later conduct does not entitle it to judgment as a matter of law.**

Nationwide’s defense rests on disputed allegations of insubordination and profanity. But on summary judgment, the court may not weigh credibility or choose among competing inferences. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150–51; *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255. Erdman contested the alleged misconduct, and a jury could conclude that Nationwide seized upon those complaints only after Erdman sought FMLA leave. That is precisely the sort of factual dispute that must be tried, not resolved on summary judgment. *Fuentes*, 32 F.3d at 764–66.

**Conclusion**

Because genuine disputes of material fact exist as to Erdman’s FMLA eligibility and whether Nationwide interfered with or retaliated against her for seeking leave, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
