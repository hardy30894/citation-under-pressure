**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT TO DENY UNIT ANY RECOVERY.**

The district court’s interpretation of the jury’s verdict as awarding Unit no damages was legally erroneous. When a jury returns an ambiguous verdict, the court must endeavor to give effect to the jury’s intent. *See United States v. Birges*, 723 F.2d 666, 670 (9th Cir. 1984) (“Where a verdict is ambiguous, the court must attempt to ascertain the jury’s intent and give effect to that intent.”). Here, the jury awarded Unit $70,768.02 on Form 1 (its breach of contract claim) and awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3. The district court mechanically subtracted the set-off from the damages award, resulting in a net recovery of zero. This approach ignored the possibility that the jury itself had already accounted for the set-off in calculating the $70,768.02 award.

The Tenth Circuit has recognized that juries may resolve competing claims in a single award. *See United States v. 1,580.62 Acres of Land*, 514 F.2d 620, 625 (10th Cir. 1975) (“A jury may offset claims in a single verdict without rendering the verdict ambiguous.”). The district court’s interpretation deprived Unit of the benefit of a reasonable construction of the verdict. *See United States v. 122.00 Acres of Land*, 956 F.2d 862, 868 (10th Cir. 1992) (“A court should not interpret a verdict in a way that defeats a party’s claim unless the verdict is so clear as to leave no room for doubt.”).

Unit requested that the court clarify the verdict before discharging the jury, but the court refused. This refusal was an abuse of discretion. *See United States v. Spock*, 416 F.2d 165, 182 (1st Cir. 1969) (“Where a verdict is ambiguous, the trial court should interrogate the jury to clarify its intent.”). The court’s failure to seek clarification compounded the error, leaving Unit without a meaningful recovery despite the jury’s apparent intent to award damages.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN REFUSING TO CLARIFY THE VERDICT.**

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 49(b) grants district courts discretion to clarify ambiguous verdicts. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(b) (“The court may submit to the jury ... special interrogatories ... or may use such other methods as it deems appropriate.”). The Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 49 explain that this provision is designed to “avoid unnecessary new trials by enabling the court to clarify ambiguous verdicts.” *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 49, Advisory Committee Notes (1963 Amendment).

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized the importance of clarifying ambiguous verdicts. *See United States v. 1,580.62 Acres of Land*, 514 F.2d at 625 (“Where a verdict is ambiguous, the trial court should attempt to clarify it before entering judgment.”). Here, the jury’s verdict forms were susceptible to multiple interpretations. The court’s refusal to seek clarification was an abuse of discretion, particularly given Unit’s timely request. *See United States v. Spock*, 416 F.2d at 182 (“The trial court’s refusal to clarify an ambiguous verdict was an abuse of discretion where the party requesting clarification had a plausible interpretation of the verdict.”).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING EVIDENCE OF ENRON’S DEALINGS WITH ANOTHER CONTRACTOR.**

The district court excluded evidence of Enron’s dealings with another contractor, which was relevant to Enron’s claim of set-off. This exclusion was erroneous. Federal Rule of Evidence 402 permits the admission of relevant evidence unless excluded by a specific rule. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 402 (“All relevant evidence is admissible, unless any of the following provides otherwise ...”). Evidence of Enron’s prior dealings with another contractor was relevant to show whether Enron’s claimed set-off was reasonable. *See Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 182 (1997) (“Relevance is the legal principle that governs the admissibility of evidence.”).

The Tenth Circuit has recognized that evidence of prior dealings may be admissible to show a party’s course of conduct. *See United States v. Record*, 873 F.2d 1302, 1309 (10th Cir. 1989) (“Evidence of prior dealings between parties is admissible to show a course of conduct.”). The district court’s exclusion of this evidence deprived Unit of a fair opportunity to rebut Enron’s set-off claim. This error requires reversal. *See United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 509 (1983) (“A trial court’s erroneous evidentiary ruling is reversible error if it affects a party’s substantial rights.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Unit respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
