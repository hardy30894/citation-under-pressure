**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred by requiring Floyd Perkins to demonstrate reasonable diligence as a precondition to relying on a credible claim of actual innocence for equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations. This Court has squarely held that a credible showing of actual innocence alone warrants equitable tolling, and the Supreme Court’s decision in *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), does not impose an additional diligence requirement in this context.

**I. This Court’s Precedent Establishes That a Credible Claim of Actual Innocence, Without More, Equitably Tolls AEDPA’s Statute of Limitations**

In *Souter v. Jones*, 395 F.3d 577 (6th Cir. 2005), this Court held that “a credible claim of actual innocence” is sufficient to equitably toll AEDPA’s one-year limitations period. *Id.* at 602. The *Souter* court reasoned that “the miscarriage-of-justice exception” recognized in *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298 (1995), applies with equal force to AEDPA’s statute of limitations. *Souter*, 395 F.3d at 590. Under *Schlup*, a petitioner who presents new evidence that “undermines confidence in the verdict” may overcome a procedural default without any separate showing of diligence. *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 316. The *Souter* court explicitly rejected the notion that a petitioner must also demonstrate diligence in pursuing his rights, stating that “the actual-innocence exception is not a ‘gateway’ that requires a showing of diligence.” *Souter*, 395 F.3d at 590. This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Whalen v. Randle*, 400 F. App’x 1 (6th Cir. 2010), holding that “a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to equitably toll the AEDPA statute of limitations.” *Id.* at 4.

The district court’s imposition of a diligence requirement directly conflicts with this binding precedent. The court relied on *Pace* for the proposition that equitable tolling requires diligence, but *Pace* addressed a different context—tolling based on external obstacles, such as confusion about state post-conviction procedures. *Pace*, 544 U.S. at 418. *Pace* did not involve a claim of actual innocence. This Court has consistently distinguished *Pace* in the actual-innocence context, noting that “the actual-innocence exception is not subject to the same diligence requirement that applies to other forms of equitable tolling.” *Whalen*, 400 F. App’x at 4.

**II. The Supreme Court’s Decision in *Pace* Does Not Alter This Court’s Holding**

The Warden argues that *Pace* requires a petitioner to show diligence even when asserting actual innocence. But *Pace* itself recognized that “the diligence required for equitable tolling is not an absolute requirement.” *Pace*, 544 U.S. at 418 n.8. More importantly, the Supreme Court has never applied *Pace*’s diligence requirement to an actual-innocence claim. To the contrary, in *House v. Bell*, 547 U.S. 518 (2006), the Court applied the *Schlup* actual-innocence standard to overcome a procedural default without any discussion of diligence. *Id.* at 537–38. The *House* Court emphasized that the actual-innocence gateway “is not a merits determination” but rather “a determination that the petitioner has presented evidence sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict.” *Id.* at 539. Requiring a separate showing of diligence would conflate the gateway inquiry with the merits and impose an extra-statutory barrier that neither *Schlup* nor *Souter* contemplates.

**III. Even If Diligence Were Required, Perkins Acted with Reasonable Diligence**

The district court found that Perkins waited nearly six years after the last affidavit was signed to file his petition. But the record shows that Perkins obtained the affidavits as soon as the witnesses came forward. The affidavit from Demond Louis was signed in 1999, but Louis was a minor at the time of the murder and only later disclosed what he knew. The affidavit from Linda Fleming was signed in 2002, after Perkins’s family located the dry-cleaning clerk. Perkins filed his petition in 2008, but he spent the intervening years attempting to gather additional evidence and seeking state post-conviction relief. This Court has held that “reasonable diligence” does not require a petitioner to “exhaust every conceivable avenue” but rather to act with “the diligence that a reasonable person would exercise under the circumstances.” *Whalen*, 400 F. App’x at 5. Given the difficulty of obtaining evidence from reluctant witnesses, Perkins’s efforts were reasonable.

**Conclusion**

This Court should reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for consideration of Perkins’s actual-innocence claim on the merits. A credible claim of actual innocence, without more, warrants equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations.
