**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a critical conflict in the interpretation of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), the statute governing judicial review of mixed cases. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, which holds that such jurisdictional dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit, is contrary to the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012), and must be reversed.

### I. The D.C. Circuit’s Distinction Between “Procedural” and “Jurisdictional” Dismissals Is Contrary to *Kloeckner* and the Text of the Statute

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that *Kloeckner* does not govern cases like this one rests on a distinction between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” dismissals that the Supreme Court never endorsed. In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a *procedural* ground—such as untimeliness—judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court emphasized that the statute’s mixed-case exception applies “when an employee has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” Id. (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A)). The Court made clear that the key inquiry is whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB”—a question that turns not on the *reason* for dismissal, but on the *nature of the underlying action*.

Here, the MSPB dismissed Perry’s appeal because it concluded that his retirement and suspension were voluntary—i.e., that the action was not one “which [he] may appeal to the MSPB” under 5 U.S.C. § 7512. This is not a procedural defect; it is a jurisdictional bar. Yet the D.C. Circuit, despite acknowledging that the Board’s dismissal was based on a determination of *voluntariness*, insists that such a dismissal falls outside the scope of *Kloeckner* because it is “jurisdictional.” That distinction is not only unsupported by *Kloeckner*, but directly contradicts its reasoning.

The Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* did not draw a line between “procedural” and “jurisdictional” dismissals. It did not say that only *procedural* dismissals trigger district court review. Instead, it held that *any* dismissal of a mixed case—regardless of the ground—must be reviewed in district court *if the employee was affected by an action that could have been appealed to the MSPB*. 133 S. Ct. at 604. The Court’s focus was on the *action itself*, not the *reason* the Board declined to hear it. As the Court explained: “The question is not whether the Board’s decision was correct, but whether the employee was affected by an action which she may appeal to the MSPB.” Id. at 604. That inquiry is dispositive.

In this case, Perry was affected by a suspension and retirement—actions that are clearly appealable to the MSPB under 5 U.S.C. § 7512. He alleged that these actions were the result of discrimination and retaliation. The MSPB’s dismissal was not because the action was not appealable, but because it concluded that the action was voluntary. That is a *factual* determination about the nature of the action, not a procedural defect. The Board did not say Perry filed late or failed to comply with a deadline. It said he *voluntarily* accepted the action. That is precisely the kind of determination that *Kloeckner* held must be reviewed in district court.

The D.C. Circuit’s insistence that *Kloeckner* applies only to “procedural” dismissals is a misreading of the statute and the Supreme Court’s opinion. The Court in *Kloeckner* did not create a new category of “procedural” dismissals. It applied the plain language of § 7702(a)(1)(A) to a case where the Board dismissed a mixed case on a procedural ground. The Court did not say that the same rule would not apply if the Board dismissed on a different ground. Indeed, the Court’s repeated reference to “an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB” makes clear that the inquiry is about the *action*, not the *reason* for dismissal.

### II. The D.C. Circuit’s Reliance on *Powell* Is Incompatible with *Kloeckner*

The D.C. Circuit attempts to preserve its precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), by arguing that *Kloeckner* did not “eviscerate” *Powell* because the two cases involve different grounds for dismissal. But this argument fails. *Powell* held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction—because the action was voluntary—review lies in the Federal Circuit. That holding is directly at odds with *Kloeckner*, which holds that when an employee is affected by an action that may be appealed to the MSPB and alleges discrimination, review lies in district court.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* by claiming it involved only “procedural” grounds is not only unsupported by the text of the opinion, but also undermines the very purpose of the mixed-case exception. The exception exists to ensure that employees who face discrimination can seek review in a court that can consider both the merits of the discrimination claim and the underlying adverse action. If the Board dismisses a mixed case on the ground that the action was voluntary, the employee is still entitled to have that factual determination reviewed in a forum that can consider the full scope of the claim—including whether the action was coerced due to discrimination.
