ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation. In Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that when a sentencing proceeding “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” an acquittal on facts necessary to impose an enhanced sentence precludes retrial. Id. at 439. The proceeding here possesses those same hallmarks.

California law grants the defendant a statutory right to a jury trial on the prior-conviction allegation, requires the prosecution to prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, and applies the rules of evidence. See Cal. Penal Code § 1025; People v. Tenner, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The trial court here conducted precisely such a proceeding: it received documentary evidence, entertained argument on whether the prior assault qualified as a serious felony, and rendered a formal finding that the allegation was true. When the Court of Appeal later determined that the evidence was insufficient, that determination operated as an acquittal on the enhancement. Bullington, 451 U.S. at 445; see also Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203, 211 (1984) (holding that a “judgment, based on findings sufficient to establish legal entitlement to the life sentence, amounts to an acquittal”).

The State’s attempt to distinguish Bullington on the ground that the case involved capital sentencing is unavailing. The Court’s reasoning turned not on the penalty’s severity but on whether the proceeding “resemble[d] a trial.” Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209. Nothing in the Double Jeopardy Clause’s text or the Court’s subsequent decisions confines that inquiry to capital cases. See, e.g., Lockhart v. Nelson, 488 U.S. 33, 40 n.7 (1988) (noting the Clause’s application whenever the State seeks to relitigate facts already resolved in the defendant’s favor). Because the California proceeding was functionally indistinguishable from the one in Bullington, the Clause bars retrial.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
