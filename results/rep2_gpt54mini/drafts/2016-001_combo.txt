Samsung’s damages award cannot stand because the design-patent statute does not authorize recovery of an infringer’s total profits unless the patented design, as applied, is the article of manufacture whose profits are being sought. The governing text says that one who infringes “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit, but not less than $250.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. That language presupposes an identifiable “article of manufacture” on which the design is applied; it does not authorize disgorgement of profits attributable to unpatented components or to the product as a whole when the patent covers only a component. See Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10, 17–18 (1886); Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 234 F. 79, 81–82 (2d Cir. 1916).

The Supreme Court has long held that design-patent remedies must track the actual contribution of the patented design, not the value of unpatented features bundled with it. In Dobson, the Court reversed an award measured by the defendant’s entire profits because the patent covered only a design and the record did not show that the profits were attributable to the design itself. 118 U.S. at 17–18. The Court explained that damages cannot rest on speculation about profits “due to the design.” Id. at 18. That principle remains controlling here: where the patented design is only one component of a multi-feature smartphone, the patentee must prove the profits attributable to that component, not seize the full profits of the finished device. See Elizabeth v. Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126, 137–38 (1878) (damages must reflect what the invention actually contributed); Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884) (requiring proof of “the value which the use of the invention added to the article”).

Garretson is especially instructive. There, the Court held that a patent owner seeking recovery for an improvement must “give evidence tending to separate or apportion” the defendant’s profits between the patented and unpatented features. 111 U.S. at 121. The Court rejected recovery based on the value of the entire article where only part was patented, because the patentee must prove the portion “properly and legally attributable” to the invention. Id. That rule is directly applicable to design patents covering only a component of a smartphone. Apple’s theory collapses the statutory distinction between the design and the finished product, allowing recovery based on the whole handset even though the asserted design claim covers only a part.

The Court’s broader patent-remedies cases confirm the same limitation. In Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614–15 (1912), the Court held that where a patent covers only part of a machine, recovery is limited to the profits that the patented feature produced, unless the patentee can show that the whole profit is attributable to the invention. The Court recognized that apportionment is required when a patent contributes only a part of the value of the accused product. Id. at 615. Likewise, in Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 647–48 (1915), the Court required apportionment of profits where the patent covered only an improvement, because profits from the entire machine could not be assumed to flow from the patented feature.

The same logic governs design patents. The design statute does not create a punitive forfeiture of all profits from any product that happens to contain an infringing component. See S. Rep. No. 49-206, at 1 (1886) (design-patent bill addressing recovery for “ornamental design”); Dobson, 118 U.S. at 17–18. Where a smartphone contains dozens of unpatented features—processor, screen size, software, network capabilities, brand, and other components—profits from those features cannot be swept into a design-patent award absent proof of causal attribution. See Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490–91 (1854) (rejecting recovery beyond the invention’s actual contribution); Suffolk Co. v. Hayden, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 315, 320–21 (1866) (damages must be tied to the patented feature, not the whole machine).

Apple may argue that § 289’s “total profit” language permits recovery of the infringer’s total profits from the finished smartphone. But statutory words must be read in context. See McClain v. Ortmayer, 141 U.S. 419, 425–26 (1891). The “total profit” recoverable under § 289 is the total profit on the relevant “article of manufacture” to which the design is applied; it is not the total profit on an entire commercial product containing that article as one component. See Dobson, 118 U.S. at 17–18; Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121. Otherwise, a design patent on a small ornamental feature could convert a limited design right into a windfall over unrelated technological and brand-driven value.

This Court should reverse and hold that, where a design patent covers only a component of a product, damages under § 289 must be limited to the profits attributable to that component. That rule follows directly from Dobson, Garretson, Westinghouse, and Dowagiac, and it preserves the historic patent-law distinction between a patented contribution and the rest of the accused product.
