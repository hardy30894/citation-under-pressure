## Argument

The Ninth Circuit’s decision cannot be reconciled with FOIA’s text, structure, or this Court’s precedents. FOIA requires disclosure unless a document falls within one of nine “explicitly exclusive” exemptions, and those exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976); *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). Exemption 2 applies only to matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit transformed that narrow text into a broad shield for operational safety documents because they are “predominantly” internal and might allow someone to “circumvent” agency policy. That rewrite should be rejected.

### I. Exemption 2 does not cover documents like the ESQD materials at issue here.

This Court has never endorsed the Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test. In *Rose*, the Court explained that Exemption 2 protects relatively trivial “internal” matters such as parking, lunch hours, and sick leave, while reserving the possibility that some internal materials may be withheld if disclosure risks circumvention of law. 425 U.S. at 363, 369. But *Rose* did not say that any operational document helpful to agency administration may be withheld. To the contrary, it emphasized that FOIA’s exemptions are limited and that Congress chose the words “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” *Id.* at 360-61.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with *Klamath Water Users Protective Ass’n v. Department of the Interior*, 532 U.S. 1 (2001), where this Court confirmed that Exemption 2 is confined to internal agency matters and does not extend to documents whose disclosure serves public understanding of governmental action. *Id.* at 8-9. Here, Milner sought maps and documents showing the effects of explosions near Puget Sound—information directly bearing on public safety and the Navy’s interactions with surrounding communities. That is not the sort of internal personnel matter Congress had in mind.

Nor does *Department of the Air Force v. Rose* support withholding. There the Court allowed disclosure of sanitized honor and ethics proceedings because FOIA’s exemptions do not create a broad confidentiality privilege for agency records simply because disclosure may be inconvenient or sensitive. 425 U.S. at 369-70. The ESQD materials are no more exempt merely because they inform the Navy’s safety planning.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “circumvention” rationale expands Exemption 2 beyond the statute.

The Ninth Circuit relied on the possibility that disclosure might help a bad actor evade safety rules. But that reasoning untethers Exemption 2 from its text. This Court has repeatedly held that agencies may not enlarge FOIA exemptions by appeal to policy concerns alone. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

The Court’s decision in *Department of Justice v. Julian*, 486 U.S. 1 (1988), is instructive. There the Court rejected a broad confidentiality claim under FOIA where Congress had not clearly exempted the requested material, explaining that FOIA’s scheme favors disclosure absent a specific exemption. *Id.* at 8-9. Likewise, *United States Department of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164 (1991), held that courts must require a concrete statutory basis for withholding, not a generalized fear that disclosure might be undesirable. *Id.* at 173. The Navy’s concerns may be serious, but they are not a license to recast Exemption 2 into a safety-secrecy provision.

If the Navy believed the ESQD materials truly implicated national defense or physical safety, FOIA already provides other exemptions. *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369; *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). Exemption 1 covers properly classified material, and Exemption 7(F) may reach law-enforcement records that could endanger life or safety. But the Ninth Circuit’s decision used Exemption 2 as a catchall, contrary to FOIA’s “explicitly exclusive” scheme. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on cases involving law-enforcement manuals is misplaced.

The Ninth Circuit leaned on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), and the D.C. Circuit’s now-discredited “predominantly internal” line of cases. But *Hardy* involved a raid manual for law-enforcement personnel, not technical blast-radius maps used for long-term safety planning. *Id.* at 656. Likewise, *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), and *Schwaner v. Department of the Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793 (D.C. Cir. 1990), addressed law-enforcement or closely analogous operational materials; they do not authorize withholding any document that agencies label “internal.”

And even in the circuits that once embraced broader Exemption 2 reasoning, the doctrine was not limitless. The Ninth Circuit itself in *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997), refused to treat wildlife location data as exempt simply because it was operationally sensitive. *Id.* at 1085-87. The ESQD documents here are even further removed from “personnel rules and practices.” They are technical safety records, not rules governing agency employees.

### IV. FOIA’s presumption of disclosure requires reversal.

This Court has repeatedly said that FOIA is a “general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The Navy bears the burden to justify withholding. *Ray*, 502 U.S. at 173; 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). It cannot meet that burden by labeling blast-effects documents “predominantly internal” or by speculating about hypothetical misuse. The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a new safety exemption untethered to Congress’s text and this Court’s cases.

The judgment should be reversed.
