**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights by pleading guilty or discharging counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Nevada trial court erred in failing to conduct a competency hearing when substantial evidence suggested Moran lacked the capacity for reasoned choice. This Court should affirm.

**I. Due Process Demands a Heightened Competency Standard for Waivers of Fundamental Rights**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee of due process prohibits the criminal prosecution of an incompetent defendant. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) ("[T]he test must be whether [the defendant] has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding"). While this standard suffices for competency to stand trial, a defendant seeking to waive constitutional rights must possess a higher degree of mental functioning. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399 (1993) ("[C]ompetency to waive the right to counsel or to plead guilty is ‘akin to the mental state required for a knowing and voluntary waiver of the right to counsel at trial’") (quoting *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 836 (1975)).

This Court has long recognized that waivers of fundamental rights—such as the right to counsel and the privilege against self-incrimination—require a defendant to possess the capacity for "reasoned choice." *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987) (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)). The standard is not satisfied by a mere rational and factual understanding of proceedings, but instead demands the ability to weigh alternatives and make a deliberate, informed decision. *Id.* (citing *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981)).

**II. The Trial Court Erred in Failing to Conduct a Competency Hearing**

Due process requires a trial court to initiate a competency hearing sua sponte whenever it has a "good faith doubt" about a defendant’s competency to waive rights. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *Seiling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973)). Substantial evidence triggering such doubt includes psychiatric evaluations, medication effects, and erratic or irrational behavior. *Id.*

Here, the record contained ample evidence to warrant a competency hearing. Moran had recently attempted suicide, was heavily medicated with four different drugs, and responded to the court in monosyllables. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 267 (9th Cir. 1992). The trial court’s perfunctory inquiry—merely asking if Moran was on medication without probing its effects—was constitutionally insufficient. *Id.* at 268.

**III. Post-Conviction Proceedings Cannot Cure the Error**

The Nevada Supreme Court erroneously applied the lower "competency to stand trial" standard in post-conviction proceedings, rather than the heightened "reasoned choice" standard required for waivers. *Id.* at 271-72. This misapplication deprived Moran of due process. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963) (federal courts must ensure state courts applied the correct legal standard).

Moreover, a retrospective competency determination cannot retroactively validate an unknowing and involuntary waiver. *Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637, 645 (1976) ("[A] plea of guilty to a charge of second-degree murder cannot be voluntary in the sense that it constituted an intelligent admission of guilt unless the defendant received ‘real notice of the true nature of the charge against him’").

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court’s failure to apply the heightened "reasoned choice" standard violated due process. This Court should affirm its judgment.
