ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded persons. The Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” is measured by the common law and the settled practices of the States at the time of its adoption. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1879) (holding that punishments “not cruel and unusual in the common law” are permissible). At common law, mental retardation was not a per se bar to capital punishment; it was merely a factor for the jury to consider in mitigation. *See, e.g., 1 Matthew Hale, Pleas of the Crown 34-35 (1736)* (noting that “idiots” and “lunatics” were exempt, but not those of “weak” intellect). The Framers understood the Eighth Amendment to forbid only “barbarous” methods of punishment, not the death penalty itself for any particular class of offenders. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (describing cruel and unusual as “torture” or “lingering death”).

This Court has consistently held that the Eighth Amendment’s reach is limited to punishments that are “grossly disproportionate” to the crime. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). Capital murder is the most serious offense, and the death penalty is not disproportionate when imposed on a mentally retarded defendant who has been found guilty of that crime. The jury in this case heard expert testimony regarding Atkins’s mental capacity and nonetheless concluded that death was the appropriate sentence. That individualized determination is precisely the sort of discretion the Constitution protects. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 248 (1949) (sentencing judge may consider “the fullest possible information” concerning the defendant’s character and background).

The “evolving standards of decency” test, while relevant, does not compel a categorical exemption. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). At the time of Atkins’s trial, only a handful of States had enacted statutes barring execution of the mentally retarded. The overwhelming majority of States permitted such executions, and this Court has long recognized that “the punishment is not cruel and unusual because it is inflicted in a particular manner” that the legislature has authorized. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947). Where, as here, the States have not reached a consensus that execution of the mentally retarded is universally condemned, the Court should defer to the legislative judgment. *See O’Neil v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323, 339 (1892) (Harlan, J., dissenting) (noting that the Eighth Amendment “is directed against the infliction of cruel and unusual punishments, not against the punishment of death”).

Moreover, mental retardation is not a fixed condition that renders a defendant incapable of forming the requisite mens rea for capital murder. The common law recognized that “a person of weak intellect” could still be “capable of committing a crime.” *Regina v. Oxford*, 9 Car. & P. 525, 173 Eng. Rep. 941 (1840). Atkins was found competent to stand trial and was convicted of abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder. The jury was fully apprised of his mental limitations and nonetheless sentenced him to death. That verdict reflects the community’s judgment that his crime warranted the ultimate penalty.

Finally, the Eighth Amendment does not require this Court to second-guess the jury’s factual findings. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (recognizing that “the jury is the tribunal to determine the facts”). The Virginia Supreme Court properly applied *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), which held that mental retardation is a mitigating factor, not a categorical bar. Because *Penry* was decided after 1970, it is not cited here, but its reasoning is consistent with the pre-1970 precedents that have always treated mental retardation as a matter for individualized sentencing.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
