**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Does Not Require a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Standard Distinct From the Ordinary Competency Inquiry.**

This Court's due process precedents have never treated the capacity to waive counsel or plead guilty as requiring a quantum of rationality greater than the capacity to stand trial. In *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that due process requires a state court to conduct an adequate inquiry whenever the evidence before it raises a genuine question about a defendant's mental competence, and that a defendant may not be tried or convicted while legally incompetent. *Id.* at 385–86. Nothing in *Pate* suggests that this obligation shifts, or that a different and more demanding measure of mental capacity applies, merely because the defendant elects to forgo trial altogether by pleading guilty or representing himself. The competency inquiry protects the same interest—an accused's basic mental capacity to understand and participate in the proceedings against him—regardless of the procedural posture in which the question arises.

*Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), confirms as much. There the Court remanded because the record failed to establish "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel," *id.* at 150, but it articulated no standard different from ordinary competency doctrine. *Westbrook* required only that the trial court determine capacity to waive; it did not superimpose a separate, heightened threshold of rationality onto that determination. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" test finds no footing in *Westbrook* or in any decision of this Court.

**II. This Court's Waiver-of-Counsel and Guilty-Plea Decisions Require Voluntariness and Understanding, Not a Separate Elevated Capacity Standard.**

The decisions governing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas ask whether the defendant's choice was voluntary and knowing—an inquiry distinct from, and not additive to, the question of basic mental capacity. In *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), the Court examined whether a guilty plea was voluntarily and understandingly made and treated that question as the full measure of the constitutional inquiry, without reference to any separate "reasoned choice" capacity. *Id.* at 174–75. Similarly, in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court recognized that a defendant may elect to conduct his own defense and held that such an election is valid so long as it is knowingly and intelligently made; the Court did not require proof that the defendant possessed some heightened mental capacity beyond that required to stand trial. *Id.* at 275–79.

*Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise focused the inquiry on the adequacy of the trial court's investigation into the circumstances of the waiver, not on a separately calibrated competency threshold. The plurality explained that a judge can be satisfied that a waiver of counsel is valid "only from a penetrating and comprehensive examination of all the circumstances under which such a plea is tendered." *Id.* at 724. That standard governs the thoroughness of the trial court's inquiry into voluntariness and understanding; it does not describe, and this Court has never described, a heightened mental-capacity standard applicable only to waiver decisions.

*Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), confirms that courts must guard against involuntary or unwitting relinquishment of constitutional rights by indulging every reasonable presumption against waiver, explaining that courts "do not presume acquiescence in the loss of fundamental rights." *Id.* at 70. That presumption operates to ensure the record affirmatively shows a knowing and voluntary choice; it does not convert the competency determination itself into a different, more searching mental-capacity test. And *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), reflects this Court's traditional understanding that due process in the right-to-counsel context turns on the fundamental fairness of the proceeding as a whole, not on rigid, freestanding capacity thresholds untethered from the ordinary competency standard. *Id.* at 462.

Finally, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), requires that the record affirmatively disclose that a guilty plea was voluntarily and understandingly entered, because such a plea effects the waiver of several constitutional rights, including the privilege against self-incrimination, the right to trial by jury, and the right to confront witnesses. *Id.* at 242–43. *Boykin*'s concern was with the adequacy of the record demonstrating voluntariness and understanding—precisely the inquiry *Dusky*-type competency doctrine and this Court's waiver cases already supply. *Boykin* does not require, and this Court has never required, that a defendant clear some additional, higher bar of "reasoned choice" before his plea or waiver may be accepted.
