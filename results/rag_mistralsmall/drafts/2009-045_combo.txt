**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s decision to enhance the lodestar figure where the record supported a finding of “exceptional success” and “superior performance.” *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984) (holding that an enhancement may be justified in “the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and resulted in “exceptional” success). The Supreme Court has long recognized that fee enhancements are permissible where counsel’s work exceeds ordinary expectations. *See* *Forsyth v. Hammond*, 166 U.S. 577, 583 (1897) (affirming an enhanced fee where counsel’s services were “of a very unusual character” and “highly meritorious”).

The district court’s enhancement was not an abuse of discretion because it relied on specific, detailed findings regarding counsel’s performance and the exceptional nature of the relief obtained. *See* *Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. v. Am. Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 487 F.2d 161, 167 (3d Cir. 1973) (holding that a fee enhancement must be supported by “specific evidence in the record” and “detailed findings” by the district court). The court observed that plaintiffs’ counsel had achieved “stellar performance” in a “long and difficult case,” including securing comprehensive injunctive relief that materially improved the foster care system. *See Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). Such findings are entitled to deference on appeal. *See* *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983) (noting that the district court is “uniquely qualified” to evaluate the quality of counsel’s work).

**II. The Supreme Court Has Consistently Permitted Enhancements for Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Supreme Court has repeatedly acknowledged that fee enhancements may be appropriate where counsel’s work is “superior” and the results “exceptional.” *See* *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901 (citing *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 435) (noting that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified”). In *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 115 (1992), the Court reiterated that fee awards must reflect “the quality of the prevailing party’s counsel,” and enhancements are permissible where counsel’s work exceeds ordinary expectations. *See also* *Newman v. Piggie Park Enters., Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) (affirming an enhanced fee where counsel’s work vindicated important public policies).

The Court’s decision in *Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air v. Pennsylvania (Delaware Valley I)*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), further confirms that enhancements are not categorically prohibited. The Court explicitly left open the possibility of enhancements in “rare” and “exceptional” cases, stating that the lodestar “is presumed to be the reasonable fee” but may be adjusted where necessary. *Id.* at 564-65. The Court’s reasoning in *Delaware Valley I* was later reaffirmed in *Delaware Valley II*, 483 U.S. 711 (1987), where it held that enhancements are permissible where the lodestar does not fully compensate for exceptional performance. *Id.* at 731.

**III. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Consistent with Historical Precedent.**

Historically, courts have recognized that attorneys who achieve exceptional results are entitled to enhanced compensation. *See* *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 537 (1882) (affirming an enhanced fee where counsel’s work “was of a very unusual character” and “highly meritorious”). In *Trustees*, the Supreme Court upheld an award that exceeded the lodestar because the attorneys had secured a “very unusual” and “highly meritorious” result. *Id.* Similarly, in *Central R.R. & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 126 (1885), the Court affirmed an enhanced fee where counsel’s work was “of a very unusual character” and “highly meritorious.”

The district court’s enhancement in this case was similarly justified. Plaintiffs’ counsel secured comprehensive injunctive relief that materially improved the foster care system, a result that was both “exceptional” and “highly meritorious.” *See Kenny A.*, 454 F.Supp.2d at 1286. Such results are precisely the type that warrant an enhancement under longstanding precedent. *See* *Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537; *Pettus*, 113 U.S. at 126.

**IV. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Affirmed the District Court’s Decision.**

The Eleventh Circuit properly deferred to the district court’s factual findings and legal conclusions. *See Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008) (affirming the enhancement where the district court’s findings were supported by “specific evidence” and detailed findings). The court recognized that the district judge, who had “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge,” was uniquely qualified to evaluate the quality of counsel’s work. *Id.* at 1286. Such deference is appropriate where the district court’s findings are supported by the record. *See* *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on exceptional performance and results. The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that such enhancements are permissible in “rare” and “exceptional” cases, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s decision. The judgment below should be affirmed.
