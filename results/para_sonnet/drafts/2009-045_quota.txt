# ARGUMENT

## THE REASONABLE ATTORNEYS' FEE UNDER A FEDERAL FEE-SHIFTING STATUTE MAY BE ENHANCED IN A RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASE SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE OF SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS

### A. This Court's Precedents Establish That Lodestar Enhancements Are Permissible When Supported by Specific Evidence of Superior Performance and Exceptional Results

The lodestar—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—provides the starting point for a reasonable fee, not an inflexible ceiling. In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), this Court explained that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee," and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435. That principle survived, and was refined rather than repudiated, in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984). Although this Court in *Blum* rejected the enhancement before it because counsel had "offer[ed] no evidence" of superior performance, *id.* at 899, it did not hold that quality-based enhancements are categorically unavailable. To the contrary, the Court recognized that an upward adjustment may be justified in the "rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged" and produced an "exceptional" result. *Id.* Critically, the *Blum* Court expressly "reject[ed] petitioner's argument that an upward adjustment to an attorney's fee is never appropriate under § 1988." *Id.* at 901.

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), confirms this reading. Far from overruling *Blum*, the Court there "further refined [its] views" on the lodestar and reaffirmed that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 564–65 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). The Court's caution that "the overall quality of performance *ordinarily* should not be used to adjust the lodestar," *id.* at 566 (emphasis added), is itself proof that the Court contemplated a non-ordinary case in which such an adjustment would be proper. This Court does not select its words carelessly; the word "ordinarily" necessarily preserves an exception for the extraordinary case.

### B. Every Court of Appeals to Consider the Question Has Recognized That Quality-Based Enhancements Remain Available in Rare and Exceptional Circumstances

The unbroken consensus among the circuits confirms that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* permit, rather than forbid, enhancement in the rare case supported by specific evidence. The Sixth Circuit has held that *Delaware Valley I* "permits enhancements based on quality of representation and results obtained in rare and exceptional cases." *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004). The Ninth Circuit likewise requires only that the fee applicant present "specific evidence that the quality of service was superior in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional." *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000). The Fifth, Eighth, Fourth, Tenth, and Second Circuits have reached the same conclusion. *See Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993) (upward adjustments proper where "supported by specific evidence on record and detailed findings by lower courts"); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999) (applicant "must establish that the quality of service rendered and the results obtained were superior to what one reasonably should expect"); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999) (affirming enhancement "on account of the exceptional results obtained"); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997) (lodestar "may be adjusted . . . especially where the degree of success achieved is exceptional"); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999) (lodestar "may be adjusted based on several factors, including in particular the results obtained"). No circuit has held otherwise. This uniform body of authority, spanning nearly every regional circuit, reflects the correct understanding of this Court's precedents and should not be disturbed.

### C. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Applied This Standard, and Its Own Precedents Are Consistent with This Court's Rule

The Eleventh Circuit's decisions in *Norman v. Housing Authority of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), and *NAACP v. City of Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), likewise recognized that "superior results coupled with superior performance can be the basis for an enhancement of the lodestar amount." *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1238 (11th Cir. 2008). Those cases, and the panel decision below, faithfully applied the *Blum*/*Delaware Valley I* framework by requiring specific record evidence and detailed findings before permitting any enhancement—precisely the showing this Court has always demanded.

### D. The District Court's Findings Satisfy the Rare-and-Exceptional Standard

The district court did not rely on generalized praise. It made detailed, record-based findings that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee," drawing on the court's own "58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge." *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). Such findings are exactly what *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require: specific, case-particular evidence justifying an enhancement in a case that is, by any measure, rare and exceptional.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
