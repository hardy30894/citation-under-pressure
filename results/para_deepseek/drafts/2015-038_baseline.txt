**ARGUMENT**

**I. ANILCA § 103(c) Does Not Strip the National Park Service of Authority to Enforce Generally Applicable Regulations on Non-Federal Lands Within Park Units.**

The plain text of § 103(c) provides that the Secretary “shall not impose any new or additional restrictions on the use of non-public lands” within a conservation system unit unless those restrictions “are necessary to implement the provisions of this Act or the provisions of law pertaining to the administration of such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). By its terms, the prohibition targets restrictions that are *new or additional*—that is, restrictions that apply *only to non-public lands* and impose burdens beyond those that apply to federal lands within the same unit. The hovercraft ban at issue here is not such a restriction. It is a uniform, park-wide regulation prohibiting hovercraft operation on *all* lands and waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, regardless of ownership. 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e).

A regulation that applies equally to federal and non-federal lands cannot be characterized as a “restriction on the use of non-public lands” within the meaning of § 103(c). The statute draws a distinction between restrictions that target non-public lands and those that apply to an entire unit. Because the hovercraft ban makes no distinction based on ownership, it falls outside the scope of § 103(c)’s limitation. *See Robinson v. Shell Oil Co.*, 519 U.S. 337, 341 (1997) (“The plainness or ambiguity of statutory language is determined by reference to the language itself, the specific context in which that language is used, and the broader context of the statute as a whole.”).

**II. Even if § 103(c) Applies, the Hovercraft Ban Is “Necessary to Implement” the Laws Pertaining to Administration of the Park System.**

The exception in § 103(c) permits restrictions on non-public lands that are “necessary to implement” the provisions of ANILCA or other laws governing park administration. 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The National Park Service Organic Act directs the Secretary to “promote and regulate the use of the Federal areas known as national parks … by such means and measures as conform to the fundamental purpose of the said parks … which purpose is to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations.” 16 U.S.C. § 1.

The hovercraft ban is necessary to fulfill that mandate. Hovercrafts can cause erosion of riverbanks, disturbance of wildlife, and noise pollution that impairs visitor enjoyment. The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that the ban is a reasonable exercise of NPS’s authority to protect park resources. *See Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Cmtys. for a Great Oregon*, 515 U.S. 687, 698 (1995) (deferring to agency’s reasonable interpretation of statutory authority). Moreover, ANILCA itself preserves pre-existing regulatory authority: “Nothing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as … superseding, modifying, or repealing … existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The Organic Act is such a law, and the hovercraft ban is a proper exercise of that authority.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Comports with ANILCA’s Structure and Purpose.**

ANILCA was designed to balance conservation with the protection of Alaska Native and state interests. Section 103(c) was intended to prevent the Secretary from singling out non-federal lands for *new* regulatory burdens that did not apply to federal lands within the same unit. *See H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 96-1578, at 17 (1980)* (expressing concern that the Secretary not “impose restrictions on non-federal lands that are more stringent than those imposed on federal lands”). The hovercraft ban imposes the same restriction on federal and non-federal lands alike, and thus does not offend the statutory compromise.

Other provisions of ANILCA confirm that Congress intended NPS to have comprehensive authority over activities within park units. For example, § 1313 authorizes the Secretary to regulate boating and other uses on waters within conservation system units. 16 U.S.C. § 3203. If § 103(c) were read to bar all regulation of non-federal lands, it would render § 1313 a nullity, as many navigable waters within park units are state-owned. Courts must avoid interpretations that create such internal conflicts. *See TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001).

**IV. The NPS’s Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference.**

To the extent ANILCA is ambiguous, this Court has long held that an agency’s reasonable interpretation of a statute it administers is entitled to deference. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984). The NPS has consistently interpreted § 103(c) as permitting generally applicable regulations to apply to non-federal lands. This interpretation is reasonable and deserves deference. *See United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 229 (2001) (agency interpretations in formal rulemaking carry Chevron weight).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
