# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING HABEAS RELIEF BECAUSE PROSECUTOR HILL'S DUAL ROLE AS ADVERSE WITNESS AND CHIEF PROSECUTOR DEPRIVED RIDDLE OF A FUNDAMENTALLY FAIR TRIAL.

The Due Process Clause guarantees a criminal defendant a prosecution untainted by personal interest in the outcome. *Marshall v. Jerrico, Inc.*, 446 U.S. 238, 242 (1980) (the Due Process Clause "entitles a person to an impartial and disinterested tribunal in both civil and criminal cases"). A prosecutor is not an ordinary advocate; his obligation is one "whose interest . . . is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done." *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). That obligation is fundamentally compromised when the prosecutor himself becomes a witness against the accused on a contested credibility issue and then resumes his role as advocate, urging the jury to credit his own testimony over the defendant's. The Supreme Court has recognized that structural conflicts of this kind threaten the reliability and fairness of criminal proceedings even without a showing of actual prejudice, because the prosecutor's dual loyalties—to the State's case and to his own credibility as a witness—cannot be reconciled. *See Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton et Fils S.A.*, 481 U.S. 787, 807 (1987) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that a prosecutor laboring under a conflict of interest calls into question "the integrity of, and public confidence in," the criminal justice process).

Here, District Attorney Hill was not a peripheral witness. Riddle testified that Hill personally threatened him with the death penalty, testimony offered to explain Riddle's escape attempts and, by implication, his state of mind. Hill took the stand to rebut that testimony and then continued to try the case as chief prosecutor. In closing argument, the assistant prosecutor expressly invited the jury to conclude that Riddle was "incredulous" and a liar because Hill—the very prosecutor seeking Riddle's conviction and death sentence—had contradicted him. This transformed Hill's unsworn advocacy into corroboration of his own sworn testimony, blurring the line between witness and prosecutor that due process requires be kept distinct. The prejudice from such a conflation is not merely that Hill was biased; it is that the jury was invited to weigh the prosecutor's institutional authority as evidence of truth-telling, a risk the Court in *Berger* and *Marshall* sought to guard against.

Prosecutorial conduct of this kind is properly assessed under the settled due process standard: whether the challenged conduct "so infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process." *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974). Allowing the chief prosecutor to testify on a central credibility dispute and then argue his own credibility to the jury as chief advocate satisfies that standard. The Texas courts' rejection of this claim was contrary to, and an unreasonable application of, this clearly established federal law. *See Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 362, 405 (2000) (a state court decision is an "unreasonable application" of federal law when it "unreasonably applies [the] principle to the facts of the prisoner's case"). Riddle is entitled to habeas relief on this ground.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN DENYING RIDDLE AN EVIDENTIARY HEARING.

A federal habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing where the material facts were not adequately developed in state court and he was diligent in attempting to develop the record. *Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 420, 437 (2000) (a petitioner who was diligent in pursuing his claims in state court is not barred from securing an evidentiary hearing under § 2254(e)(2)). Riddle's diligence is evident from the record: he timely notified the district court of his intent to exhaust state remedies, promptly filed his state habeas application once counsel was secured, and pursued federal relief without unreasonable delay upon the state court's denial.

The conflict-of-interest claim rests on facts that were never fully explored below—specifically, the content and timing of Hill's alleged threat, the extent of his participation in strategic decisions after testifying, and the degree to which the assistant prosecutor's closing argument relied on Hill's testimony to bolster the State's case. Because these factual questions bear directly on whether Riddle's trial was fundamentally fair, and because the state courts summarily rejected the claim without an evidentiary hearing of their own, the district court was obligated to hold a hearing rather than resolve disputed facts on a paper record. *Cf. Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (federal habeas hearing required where the state trier of fact did not resolve the material factual dispute). The grant of summary judgment against Riddle, without affording him the opportunity to develop this record, was error.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Riddle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment below, grant an evidentiary hearing, and grant the writ of habeas corpus.
