**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing this putative class action as moot. A defendant cannot unilaterally defeat federal jurisdiction by “picking off” the named plaintiff with an unaccepted Rule 68 offer before class certification is decided. That result is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent and with this Court’s recognition that the claims of absent class members and the certification question may preserve a live controversy even after the defendant attempts to satisfy the named plaintiff’s individual demand. See **Sosna v. Iowa, 419 U.S. 393, 399–402 (1975)**; **U.S. Parole Comm’n v. Geraghty, 445 U.S. 388, 404–07 (1980)**; **Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 590 F.3d 1134, 1138–39 (10th Cir. 2009)**.

I. **An unaccepted Rule 68 offer does not moot Lucero’s claims.**

The district court’s ruling cannot stand because an offer of judgment, standing alone, does not extinguish a live controversy. In **Campbell-Ewald Co. v. Gomez, 577 U.S. 153, 162–68 (2016)**, the Supreme Court held that “an unaccepted settlement offer or offer of judgment does not moot a plaintiff’s case.” The Court explained that “[u]nder basic principles of contract law, Campbell’s settlement bid and Rule 68 offer had no force” once rejected, so the plaintiff retained a personal stake in the litigation. **Id. at 162**. Although **Campbell-Ewald** post-dates this appeal, it confirms the correct reading of Article III and forecloses the notion that BCR’s unilateral offer deprived the court of jurisdiction. See also **Genesis Healthcare Corp. v. Symczyk, 569 U.S. 66, 73–75 (2013)** (distinguishing collective actions and relying on the parties’ assumption regarding mootness); **Chathas v. Local 134 IBEW, 233 F.3d 508, 512 (7th Cir. 2000)** (Rule 68 offer does not itself moot a claim).

Here, BCR served an offer, but Lucero did not accept it, and the district court never entered judgment in his favor. The controversy therefore remained live. A federal court may not dismiss for mootness where the defendant has merely proposed to satisfy the plaintiff’s claim but has not actually provided complete relief. See **Campbell-Ewald, 577 U.S. at 165–66**; **Zeidman v. J. Ray McDermott & Co., 651 F.2d 1030, 1045–50 (5th Cir. 1981)**.

II. **In a putative class action, the named plaintiff’s claim is not mooted before a timely certification motion is resolved.**

Even apart from the Rule 68 defect, this case falls within the well-established class-action exception to ordinary mootness principles. In **Sosna v. Iowa, 419 U.S. 393, 399–402 (1975)**, the Supreme Court held that once a class is certified, the action does not become moot merely because the named plaintiff’s individual claim expires. And in **U.S. Parole Comm’n v. Geraghty, 445 U.S. 388, 404–07 (1980)**, the Court held that the expiration of the named plaintiff’s substantive claim does not moot an appeal from denial of class certification, because the plaintiff retains a live interest in obtaining certification for the class. The Court emphasized that the “personal stake” requirement is satisfied where the plaintiff continues to press the propriety of class certification. **Id. at 404–05**.

This Court has likewise recognized that a defendant should not be permitted to “moot” a putative class action by satisfying the named plaintiff’s individual demand before class certification is addressed. See **Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 590 F.3d 1134, 1138–40 (10th Cir. 2009)**. Clark explained that “a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified,” but that statement does not control where the certification issue is actively pending and the plaintiff has not unduly delayed in seeking certification. **Id. at 1138–39**. Indeed, Clark recognized that the Court may avoid manipulation by applying relation-back principles where a defendant attempts to “buy off” the named plaintiff before certification can be decided. **Id. at 1140–41**.

That is exactly what happened here. Lucero filed a putative class complaint, the parties agreed to phased discovery focused first on certification, and Lucero moved for class certification on February 22, 2010, immediately after the agreed class-certification discovery period. BCR’s Rule 68 offer preceded that motion and was designed to eliminate the case before the court could decide whether to certify the class. The district court should not have allowed that tactic to extinguish the action.

III. **The proper remedy is to reverse and remand for consideration of class certification on the merits.**

The better rule, consistent with **Geraghty**, **Clark**, and Article III, is that a defendant’s attempt to satisfy the named plaintiff’s individual claim does not moot a putative class action where the plaintiff has not slept on his rights and a timely certification motion is pending or about to be filed. See **Geraghty, 445 U.S. at 404–07**; **Clark, 590 F.3d at 1139–41**; **Weiss v. Regal Collections, 385 F.3d 337, 347–48 (3d Cir. 2004)**. Otherwise, defendants could systematically avoid class adjudication by “picking off” named plaintiffs one by one, a result courts have repeatedly rejected. See **Deposit Guaranty Nat’l Bank v. Roper, 445 U.S. 326, 339–40 (1980)**; **Weiss, 385 F.3d at 344–48**.

Because Lucero retained a live stake in certification and BCR’s unaccepted offer did not moot the case, the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction. The order dismissing the complaint should be reversed, and the case remanded for adjudication of class certification and the merits.
