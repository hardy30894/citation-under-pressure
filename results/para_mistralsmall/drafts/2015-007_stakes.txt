**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment as interpreted by this Court in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the existence of any fact that increases the maximum punishment for a crime. *Id.* at 609. The Court reasoned that "[c]apital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants, [are] entitled to a jury determination of any fact...that exposes them to a greater punishment than that authorized by the jury’s verdict alone." *Id.* at 602.

Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement. Under Florida law, a jury must unanimously find at least one statutory aggravating factor before a defendant may be eligible for the death penalty. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2015); *Hurst v. State*, 18 So. 3d 975, 1008 (Fla. 2009). The jury’s finding of an aggravating factor is a prerequisite to the trial court’s consideration of the death penalty. *See* § 921.141(2)-(3). Thus, the jury—not the judge—determines the existence of the fact that exposes the defendant to the greater punishment of death. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 602.

Petitioner’s reliance on *Ring* is misplaced. In *Ring*, Arizona’s scheme allowed a judge to find aggravating factors and impose the death penalty based solely on those findings. *Id.* at 590. Florida’s scheme, by contrast, requires a jury to find at least one aggravating factor unanimously before the trial court may even consider imposing the death penalty. *See* § 921.141(2)-(3). The Court in *Ring* expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme, noting that it "permits the jury to render an advisory verdict on the question of life imprisonment or death, and the judge alone imposes the sentence." *Id.* at 590 n.1 (citing *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984)). Thus, Florida’s scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme also comports with the Eighth Amendment. The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual," but it does not require a particular method of sentencing. *See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976) (plurality opinion). This Court has repeatedly recognized that states have broad discretion in designing their capital sentencing schemes, so long as they provide sufficient safeguards to ensure reliability. *See id.* at 195-96.

Florida’s scheme provides such safeguards. The jury must unanimously find at least one statutory aggravating factor before the trial court may consider the death penalty. *See* § 921.141(2)-(3). The trial court must then weigh the aggravating factors against the mitigating factors and determine whether the death penalty is warranted. *Id.* § 921.141(3). This process ensures that the death penalty is imposed only in the most aggravated and least mitigated cases, thereby satisfying the Eighth Amendment’s requirement of reliability. *See, e.g., Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 653 (1990) (upholding Arizona’s scheme that required the trial court to find aggravating factors by a preponderance of the evidence).

Petitioner’s argument that Florida’s scheme violates the Eighth Amendment because it does not require a unanimous jury recommendation of death is unavailing. This Court has never held that the Eighth Amendment requires a unanimous jury recommendation of death. *See, e.g., Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465 (upholding Florida’s scheme that allowed the trial court to override a jury’s recommendation of life imprisonment). Moreover, the Court has recognized that states have broad discretion in designing their capital sentencing schemes, and it has never held that a unanimous jury recommendation is constitutionally required. *See, e.g., Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 195-96.

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME FOR DETERMINING MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

Florida’s scheme for determining mental retardation also comports with the Eighth Amendment. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded defendants. The Court, however, left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction would be enforced. *Id.* at 317.

Florida has complied with *Atkins* by enacting a statutory scheme that sets forth a three-pronged test for determining mental retardation. *See* § 921.137(1). Under this scheme, a defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that he has significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning, concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior, and that the condition manifested before age eighteen. *Id.* This scheme ensures that mentally retarded defendants are not executed, thereby satisfying the Eighth Amendment. *See, e.g., Ex parte Briseno*, 135 S.W.3d 1, 8 (Tex. Crim. App. 2004) (upholding Texas’s scheme for determining mental retardation under *Atkins*).

Petitioner’s argument that Florida’s scheme violates the Eighth Amendment because it does not require the jury to determine mental retardation is unavailing. The Eighth Amendment does not require a jury to determine mental retardation; it merely prohibits the execution of mentally retarded defendants. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. Florida’s scheme ensures that mentally retarded defendants are not executed by requiring the trial court to determine mental retardation based on the statutory factors. *See* § 921.137(4). Thus, Florida’s scheme comports with the Eighth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
