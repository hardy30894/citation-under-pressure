ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act supplies an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority. In J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), the Court held that Congress may delegate authority when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to fix such rates is directed to conform. The Act meets this standard by requiring the Attorney General to consider the history, pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse together with the risk to public health before finding an imminent hazard. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988).

This guidance is comparable to that sustained in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), where Congress directed the Price Administrator to fix prices that effectuate the purposes of preventing inflation. It likewise parallels the delegation upheld in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), which authorized rules for associations in accordance with the standards set forth in the statute.

Congress may also authorize the Attorney General to subdelegate. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court approved the transfer of functions vested in the President to subordinate officers. Earlier, Wayman v. Southard, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825), recognized that Congress may commit the details to those charged with execution. Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), and Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), confirm that administrative officers may promulgate rules carrying criminal consequences when acting within statutory bounds.

The two decisions invalidating delegations are distinguishable. In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the statute did not declare any policy as to the transportation of the excess production, did not qualify the President’s authority, and did not establish any criterion to govern the President’s course. In A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 541-42 (1935), Congress supplied no standards whatever. Here the enumerated factors and the imminent-hazard limitation supply the missing criteria.

The temporary scheduling provisions therefore rest on a valid delegation of authority that the Attorney General lawfully transmitted to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.
