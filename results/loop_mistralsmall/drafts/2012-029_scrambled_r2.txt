**ARGUMENT**

I. **FEDERAL JURISDICTION OVER STATE MALPRACTICE CLAIMS ARISES WHEN A PATENT ISSUE IS NECESSARY, DISPUTED, AND SUBSTANTIAL**

The question presented is whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state legal malpractice claims that require resolution of a substantial and disputed issue of federal patent law. The answer is yes. When a state-law claim necessarily raises a contested federal patent issue that is substantial in the context of the litigation, federal jurisdiction exists under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988). This Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction extends to cases in which a plaintiff’s right to relief depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) (holding that a suit arises under federal law only if it involves a controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of federal law, upon which the result depends).

Here, Minton’s legal malpractice claim turns entirely on whether his attorneys’ failure to raise the experimental use exception proximately caused the loss of his patent infringement suit. *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The experimental use exception is a creature of federal patent law. *Minton v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373, 1379–81 (Fed. Cir. 2003). Without resolving whether Minton’s TEXCEN lease qualified for the exception, the malpractice claim cannot succeed. Thus, the patent issue is not merely tangential—it is *necessary* to the malpractice claim. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936) (federal issue must be “substantial” and “not merely colorable”).

II. **THE PATENT ISSUE IN THIS CASE IS SUBSTANTIAL AND DISPUTED**

The experimental use exception is not a frivolous or insubstantial defense. It is a well-established doctrine in patent law that has been applied by federal courts for decades. *See, e.g., Smith v. Magic City Kennel Club, Inc.*, 188 F.2d 407, 409 (D.C. Cir. 1949) (recognizing experimental use as a defense to patent invalidity). The Federal Circuit has repeatedly enforced the exception, and its application often turns on fact-intensive inquiries that are uniquely suited to federal adjudication. *Minton*, 336 F.3d at 1379–81. Because the exception is a core component of patent law, its resolution in this case is substantial and requires uniform federal interpretation. *See Moore v. Chesapeake & O. Ry. Co.*, 291 U.S. 205, 212 (1934) (federal jurisdiction exists where uniformity in federal law is essential).

Moreover, the patent issue is *disputed*. Gunn argues that the experimental use exception was not viable under the facts known at the time of the underlying litigation. This dispute cannot be resolved without interpreting federal patent law. *See United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (federal jurisdiction exists where the dispute requires construction of federal law).

III. **FEDERAL JURISDICTION DOES NOT DISTURB THE BALANCE OF STATE AND FEDERAL JUDICIAL RESPONSIBILITIES**

The Supreme Court has made clear that federal jurisdiction exists only where the federal issue is substantial and does not disrupt the balance between state and federal courts. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). Here, the patent issue is not merely incidental—it is the *linchpin* of Minton’s malpractice claim. Without resolving the experimental use exception, the malpractice claim fails. *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569. This is precisely the type of case in which federal jurisdiction is appropriate to ensure uniformity in patent law. *Moore*, 291 U.S. at 212.

The Texas Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion misapplies the *Grable* test by treating the patent issue as merely collateral. But the experimental use exception is not collateral—it is *necessary* to the malpractice claim. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly held that legal malpractice claims arising from patent litigation fall within federal jurisdiction when they require resolution of a substantial patent issue. *Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007); *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007).

IV. **CONCLUSION**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in dismissing this case for lack of jurisdiction. The experimental use exception is a substantial and disputed issue of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of Minton’s malpractice claim. Federal jurisdiction exists under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), and this Court should reverse the judgment below.
