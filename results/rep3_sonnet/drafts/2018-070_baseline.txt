# ARGUMENT

## I. OCSLA's Text and Structure Show That State Law Is Adopted as Surrogate Federal Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) declares that "the Constitution and laws … of the United States" apply to fixed structures on the Shelf "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Congress thus treated the Shelf as a federal enclave, not as an extension of state territory. Only *after* declaring federal law supreme did Congress add a narrow borrowing provision: state law applies "[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That structure—federal law first, state law only as a supplement—mirrors the classic federal-enclave model in which state law is assimilated solely to occupy the space that federal law leaves vacant. This Court recognized exactly that analogy in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), explaining that a fixed drilling platform must be treated as "an upland federal enclave," id. at 366, and that state wrongful-death law could be borrowed only because the federal remedy under the Death on the High Seas Act did not "appl[y] to these accidents actually occurring on the islands," ibid. It was the *absence* of a federal remedy that "remove[d]" any "obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]." Id. at 366. Where, as here, Congress has already legislated comprehensively through the Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, there is no such vacancy for state law to fill.

## II. This Court's Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Borrows State Law Only as a Gap-Filler.

*Rodrigue* did not stand alone. In *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), this Court again confronted OCSLA's choice-of-law clause and reaffirmed that state law is adopted "as federal law" only where federal law does not otherwise supply the governing rule. And in *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Court described OCSLA's scheme as one in which the Shelf is subject to a body of law "read as a whole," with state law entering only where federal sources leave the field open. This gap-filling design tracks the model long applied to other federal enclaves, including the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13(a), which borrows state criminal law only for conduct "not made punishable by any enactment of Congress." The whole point of assimilation statutes of this kind is to avoid a regulatory void on federal land—not to layer state regulation atop an existing, comprehensive federal regime. See *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958) (upholding prospective assimilation of state criminal law on federal enclaves precisely because it filled gaps left open by Congress).

The Fifth Circuit—the court with the longest and most extensive experience applying OCSLA to Shelf platforms—has likewise held that "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" is the touchstone for borrowing state law under § 1333(a)(2)(A). *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). That test respects Congress's evident purpose in enacting OCSLA: to create "a federal enclave" with a single, coherent body of law, not a patchwork in which fifty different states' labor codes might govern identical platforms depending on which State happens to lie closest to a given rig. See *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep't of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988) (OCSLA was designed "to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures" on the Shelf) (quoting *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355).

## III. The FLSA Leaves No Gap for California Law to Fill.

The FLSA is not silent on the subjects Newton raises. It regulates minimum wages and overtime pay comprehensively, and it does so through a statute Congress designed to operate uniformly across the Nation. Congress anticipated that some States might wish to impose more protective standards, and it addressed that possibility explicitly through the FLSA's own savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), which preserves stricter state standards *for employment occurring within state boundaries*—not for employment on the Shelf, where OCSLA's enclave framework governs instead. The existence of federal wage-and-hour law addressing exactly the subject matter of Newton's claims—minimum wages, overtime, and payment of wages—forecloses the conclusion that OCSLA's narrow gap-filling mechanism authorizes the wholesale importation of California's separate and more onerous wage-and-hour code, including its distinctive meal-and-rest-break requirements, penalty provisions, and private attorney general enforcement mechanism.

The Ninth Circuit's rule—that state law applies whenever it is not "inconsistent" with federal law, without regard to whether federal law already occupies the field—cannot be reconciled with *Rodrigue*'s enclave analogy or with the gap-filling principle *Continental Oil* correctly derived from OCSLA's text and purpose. This Court should hold that state law may be borrowed as surrogate federal law under 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) only when federal law does not already address the relevant subject matter, and should reverse the judgment below.
