**ARGUMENT**

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. *U.S. Const. amend. IV*. A police officer may conduct a protective pat-down for weapons—commonly known as a “Terry frisk”—only when the officer has a reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). Here, the district court erred in concluding that the officers had reasonable suspicion to justify the pat-frisk of Michael Soares. The totality of the circumstances, as viewed through the lens of *Terry* and its progeny, fails to support the intrusion on Soares’s Fourth Amendment rights. The officers’ actions were not justified by objective facts indicating a threat to their safety, and the frisk was instead driven by subjective animus and speculative concern. The evidence obtained must be suppressed.

First, the initial stop was lawful. The officers observed the Lexus driving without headlights in a high-crime area at 1:15 a.m.—a circumstance that justified a traffic stop under *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 229 (1985), which permits stops based on visible violations of traffic laws. However, the legality of the stop does not automatically justify the subsequent frisk. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. The frisk must be supported by reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 21–22.

Second, the officers’ observations of “furtive movements” in the car were insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. The officers observed the driver motion toward his door and the front-seat passenger toward the console, but no movement was seen in the back seat. *See United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d 57, 62 (1st Cir. 2007) (holding that “mere furtive movements” are not sufficient to justify a frisk absent additional indicia of criminality or danger). The First Circuit has emphasized that “a fleeting, ambiguous gesture does not, by itself, justify a frisk.” *United States v. Maldonado*, 477 F.3d 1, 6 (1st Cir. 2007). Here, the movements were brief, unexplained, and not clearly indicative of weapon concealment. The officers’ subjective concern, however, cannot substitute for objective reasonable suspicion. *United States v. Johnson*, 455 F.3d 1, 5 (1st Cir. 2006).

Third, Soares’s subsequent conduct—rocking back and forth, using profanity, and moving his hands—was not, standing alone, sufficient to justify a frisk. While *Terry* permits officers to respond to behavior that suggests a threat, the conduct must be “of a nature that would warrant a reasonable officer in believing that his safety or that of others is in danger.” *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. The First Circuit has held that “verbal abuse and nervousness” do not, by themselves, justify a frisk. *United States v. Maldonado*, 477 F.3d at 6. In *United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d at 62, the court rejected a frisk based on “agitated behavior” and “verbal defiance” that were not accompanied by any physical threat or indication of weapon possession. Similarly, in *United States v. Johnson*, 455 F.3d at 5, the court found that “nervousness and verbal resistance” did not justify a frisk. Soares’s conduct, while disruptive, did not rise to the level of a threat to officer safety.

Fourth, the decision to remove Soares from the car and conduct a frisk was not supported by reasonable suspicion. The officers had not observed Soares reach for anything, make any threatening gestures, or display a weapon. The fact that Soares was “rocking back and forth” and “flailing” his hands does not, in itself, constitute a threat. *See United States v. Williams*, 455 F.3d 105, 110 (1st Cir. 2006) (holding that “non-threatening physical movements” do not justify a frisk). The officers’ decision to remove Soares from the vehicle and conduct a frisk was based on subjective unease, not objective facts. *See United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d at 62 (emphasizing that “subjective fear” cannot justify a frisk). The First Circuit has repeatedly held that “a police officer’s personal discomfort or anxiety cannot substitute for reasonable suspicion.” *United States v. Johnson*, 455 F.3d at 5.

Fifth, the presence of additional officers at the scene does not justify the frisk. The arrival of backup officers does not transform a lack of reasonable suspicion into a lawful frisk. *See United States v. Maldonado*, 477 F.3d at 6 (holding that “the presence of additional officers does not justify a frisk absent reasonable suspicion”). The officers’ decision to frisk Soares was not based on a collective assessment of danger but on Reid’s individual unease. *See United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d at 62.

Finally, the district court’s conclusion that the frisk was justified under *Terry* is contrary to binding precedent. The First Circuit has consistently held that “a frisk must be based on specific and articulable facts, not on hunches or generalized suspicion.” *United States v. Johnson*, 455 F.3d at 5. Here, the officers had no specific facts indicating that Soares was armed or dangerous. The frisk was not a reasonable response to a threat, but a reaction to Soares’s demeanor and the officers’ subjective discomfort.

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress. The gun was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. *See *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. The exclusionary rule applies. *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897, 906 (1984). The evidence must be suppressed.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed,
