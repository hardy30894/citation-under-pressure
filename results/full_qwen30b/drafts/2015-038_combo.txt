**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service (“NPS”) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—must be answered in the negative. The plain text of ANILCA, read in context and interpreted in light of settled principles of statutory construction, unambiguously permits the NPS to regulate activities on non-federal lands within National Park System units in Alaska, including the operation of hovercrafts on navigable waters. The lower court correctly concluded that Sturgeon’s interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute’s language and that Alaska lacks standing to challenge the regulations. This Court should affirm.

**I. ANILCA’s Plain Text Permits the NPS to Regulate Activities on Non-Federal Lands Within National Park Units in Alaska**

The starting point of statutory interpretation is the language of the statute itself. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). When the language is clear and unambiguous, “there is no need for resort to legislative history or other aids to construction.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543. Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides:

> “The Secretary of the Interior shall have authority to administer and manage the lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System units in Alaska, including the authority to regulate the use of such lands and waters, subject to the provisions of this Act.”

16 U.S.C. § 3203(c). This provision grants the Secretary of the Interior broad authority to administer and manage lands and waters within the National Park System units in Alaska, including the authority to regulate their use. The statute does not limit this authority to federally owned lands. Indeed, it explicitly contemplates regulation of “lands and waters” within the boundaries of the System, regardless of ownership. The use of the term “lands and waters” in the plural, without qualification, indicates Congress intended to include both federal and non-federal property within the NPS’s regulatory reach.

Moreover, the statute’s structure reinforces this interpretation. Section 103(c) is part of a broader framework that vests the Secretary with comprehensive management authority over the entire National Park System in Alaska. See *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (statutes should be read as a whole, with each part interpreted in light of the others). Section 103(a) provides that the Secretary “shall have authority to administer and manage” the lands and waters within the System, and § 103(c) adds the specific power to regulate their use. The absence of any limiting language—such as “federal lands only” or “lands owned by the United States”—demonstrates Congress’s intent to grant broad regulatory authority.

Sturgeon’s argument that § 103(c) is limited to federal lands is contradicted by the statute’s own terms. The provision does not condition regulatory authority on ownership; it conditions it only on location within the boundaries of a National Park System unit. This interpretation is consistent with the long-standing principle that “the power to regulate is not limited by the ownership of the land.” *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 518 (1911). The federal government’s authority to regulate activities on lands within its jurisdiction—whether federal or non-federal—arises from its sovereign power to manage public resources and protect the environment. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 260 (1946) (“The power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters.”).

Sturgeon’s reliance on *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459 (1915), is misplaced. That case held that the federal government could not regulate the use of non-federal lands within a national forest unless Congress had expressly authorized such regulation. But ANILCA does expressly authorize regulation. The statute’s language is not silent; it is affirmative and comprehensive. The fact that Congress chose to include non-federal lands within the scope of the National Park System in Alaska—by designating them as part of the System—necessarily implies that the NPS may regulate activities on those lands, even if they are not federally owned.

**II. The NPS’s Hovercraft Ban Is a Valid Exercise of Regulatory Authority Under ANILCA**

The NPS regulation prohibiting the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—is a reasonable and necessary measure to protect the natural and cultural resources of the preserve. The regulation applies to all lands and waters within the preserve’s boundaries, regardless of ownership. This is consistent with the NPS’s long-standing authority to regulate activities that threaten the integrity of protected areas. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946); *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506 (1911).

Sturgeon argues that the regulation is invalid because it applies to state-owned land. But the Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal government may regulate activities on non-federal lands within federal enclaves or protected areas. *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459 (1915); *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946). The fact that the submerged lands beneath the Nation River are owned by the State of Alaska does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on those waters when they fall within a National Park System unit. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506 (1911). The federal government’s authority to regulate navigable waters is derived from the Commerce Clause and the navigational servitude, which “is a power of the United States to regulate the use of navigable waters for the public good.” *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. at 260.
