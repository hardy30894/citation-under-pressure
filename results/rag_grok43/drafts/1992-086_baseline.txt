The District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s applications to exhibit films addressing family values and child-rearing from a Christian perspective violated the First Amendment. The facilities constitute a limited public forum. Once a school district opens its property to after-hours expressive activity by community groups, “the first amendment protections provided to traditional public forums only apply to entities of a character similar to those the government admits to the forum.” The District permitted numerous nonreligious groups to use the premises for lectures, performances, and films on comparable secular subjects. It therefore could not exclude speech on the basis of its religious viewpoint.

This exclusion constitutes impermissible content discrimination. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that a university regulation prohibiting “the use of University buildings or grounds ‘for purposes of religious worship or religious teaching’” effected “a content-based discrimination against religious speech, for which it could find no compelling justification.” *Id.* at 265, 1315. The same principle governs here. The District’s Local Rule No. 7 and its application of New York Education Law § 414 barred Lamb’s Chapel solely because the proposed films carried “religious connotations” and were “church related.” No narrower justification supports the restriction.

*Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), confirms the point. There the Court recognized that once a school creates a limited open forum by permitting noncurriculum-related student groups, it may not “discriminat[e], based on the content of the students’ speech.” *Id.* at 247. Although *Mergens* arose under the Equal Access Act, its First Amendment analysis is identical: selective exclusion of religious speech from an otherwise open forum is unconstitutional. The District’s repeated allowance of secular programs on family life, psychology, and cultural topics while barring the identical subject matter when presented from a religious perspective cannot be reconciled with these precedents.

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies no compelling basis for the discrimination. *Widmar* rejected an identical justification, holding that equal access to a forum already open to secular groups does not advance religion. 454 U.S. at 273. Because the District opened its facilities to a wide range of community expression, the denial of access to Lamb’s Chapel on the ground that its speech was religious abridged the First Amendment.
