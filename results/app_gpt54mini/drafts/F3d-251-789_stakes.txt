**Argument**

The Hague Convention and the International Child Abduction Remedies Act require return of a child only when the petitioner proves that the removal or retention was “wrongful” under the Convention. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(1)(A); *Mendez Lynch v. Mendez Lynch*, 220 F. Supp. 2d 1347, 1358 (M.D. Fla. 2002). A removal is not wrongful if the left-behind parent consented to it. *Baxter v. Baxter*, 423 F.3d 363, 371 (3d Cir. 2005); *Duarte v. Bardales*, 526 F.3d 563, 567 (9th Cir. 2008). But consent must be established from the totality of the circumstances and cannot be expanded beyond the scope actually agreed to by the petitioning parent. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *Duarte*, 526 F.3d at 567-68.

The district court’s finding of consent is clearly erroneous because the undisputed evidence shows, at most, a limited and conditional permission to take Danelsy temporarily while Gonzalez-Caballero arranged to follow. The Convention does not permit a taking parent to convert a temporary visit into permanent retention by unilaterally changing the terms of the parties’ understanding. See *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 372 (consent to a trip abroad did not establish consent to retention); *Duarte*, 526 F.3d at 567-68 (same); *Rydder v. Rydder*, 49 F.3d 369, 372 (8th Cir. 1995) (finding no consent where evidence showed only permission for a temporary visit).

Here, the record shows that Gonzalez-Caballero lived with Danelsy in Panama from birth until October 1999, that Mena left Panama before the child was born, and that Mena traveled to Panama only after learning of Danelsy’s existence. Mena did not bring Danelsy to the United States over Gonzalez-Caballero’s objection; rather, the parties discussed plans for the family to travel together. Gonzalez-Caballero’s testimony consistently reflected an understanding that Mena would take Danelsy for a short period while she completed immigration arrangements. That is not consent to permanent removal or indefinite retention. See *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 372; *Duarte*, 526 F.3d at 567-68.

The district court appears to have relied heavily on the fact that Gonzalez-Caballero handed Mena Danelsy’s documents and accompanied them to the airport. But those facts are equally consistent with a temporary, cooperative travel arrangement. As the Third Circuit has explained, “consent” under the Hague Convention is not established merely because the left-behind parent facilitated travel or initially cooperated in the child’s departure. *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72. The decisive question is whether the petitioning parent agreed to the specific removal and retention that actually occurred. *Id.* The evidence here shows she did not.

Nor does Mena’s promise to help Gonzalez-Caballero immigrate support a finding of consent to permanent retention. At most, it confirms that the understanding was contingent: Danelsy would travel while Gonzalez-Caballero pursued lawful entry to the United States. Once Mena failed to honor that understanding and failed to return Danelsy, any earlier permission could not rationally be stretched into permission for indefinite retention. See *Duarte*, 526 F.3d at 567-68; *Rydder*, 49 F.3d at 372.

This Court reviews factual findings for clear error, but a finding is clearly erroneous when, although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). That standard is met here. The record does not show a clear, unconditional waiver of custody rights. Instead, it shows a mother who allowed the father to take the child while immigration arrangements were being made and who promptly sought the child’s return when the promised assistance never materialized. On these facts, the district court’s consent finding cannot stand. See *Baxter*, 423 F.3d at 371-72; *Duarte*, 526 F.3d at 567-68.

Accordingly, the Court should reverse and order Danelsy’s return to Panama.
