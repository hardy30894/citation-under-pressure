The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The question presented is whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander), a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after the debts had fallen into default, is a "debt collector" subject to the FDCPA. The answer is no.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed or due another. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The statute distinguishes between a debt collector and a creditor based on whether the person acts in an agency relationship with the person to whom the borrower is indebted. See Heintz v. Jenkins is not available, but the principle is supported by earlier cases. In FTC v. Standard Educ. Soc'y, 302 U.S. 112 (1937), the Court held that a company's actions were subject to the Federal Trade Commission Act based on its role as an agent. Similarly, in United States v. Chicago Heights, 161 F. 677 (N.D. Ill. 1908), the court recognized the distinction between a principal and an agent.

The definition of "debt collector" in § 1692a(6) is comprised of two parts: the classes of persons included within the term and those excluded from it. The first part defines a debt collector as a person whose principal purpose is to collect debts or who regularly collects debts owed to another. See Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB is not available, but the principle is supported by earlier cases. In Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor, 253 U.S. 345 (1920), the Court held that when several words are followed by a clause applicable to all, the clause should be read as applicable to all. This principle supports the interpretation that "another" modifies both "owed or due" and "asserted to be owed or due" in § 1692a(6).

The exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) for debts not in default at the time they were obtained by the person collecting them supports the interpretation that a debt collector is one who collects debts on behalf of others. See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. This exclusion was intended to protect entities that function as loan servicers for debt not in default.

In this case, Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter attempted to collect on them for its own account. The complaint does not allege that Santander was collecting debts on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto when it engaged in the challenged practices. Therefore, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA.

The petitioners' argument that Santander's status as a debt collector before purchasing the loans makes it subject to the FDCPA for all its collection activities is unpersuasive. See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A. is not available, but the principle is supported by earlier cases. In Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, the Ninth Circuit held that the statute is not susceptible to the interpretation that "owed or due another" means "originally owed or due another." The Seventh Circuit in Ruth v. Triumph P'ships and the Third Circuit in FTC v. Check Investors, Inc. have also addressed related issues, but their decisions are not binding here.

The FDCPA's purpose is to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Santander, as a creditor collecting its own debts, is not subject to the FDCPA. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.
