**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the infliction of “cruel and unusual punishments.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958). This prohibition is not static; it evolves in accordance with “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that the execution of individuals with intellectual disability constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court, affirming the death sentence imposed on Daryl Renard Atkins, must be upheld because the execution of a person with intellectual disability violates the constitutional prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment.

The Court’s decision in *Atkins* was grounded in a comprehensive analysis of national consensus and evolving standards of decency. The Court observed that, as of 2002, 18 states had enacted laws prohibiting the execution of individuals with intellectual disability, and that the remaining states either had no death penalty or had not carried out such executions. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318. The Court emphasized that “the overwhelming weight of international opinion” also opposes the execution of individuals with intellectual disability. Id. at 320. This national and international consensus reflects a growing recognition that individuals with intellectual disability are less culpable than average offenders due to their diminished capacity for rational decision-making, impulse control, and understanding of consequences. Id. at 319.

The Court in *Atkins* rejected the argument that the Eighth Amendment permits the death penalty for individuals with intellectual disability, even if they are convicted of capital crimes. The Court held that “the execution of individuals with intellectual disability is grossly disproportionate and excessive punishment” in light of their diminished personal responsibility. Id. at 321. The Court reasoned that such individuals are “less able than average persons to understand and process information, to communicate, to learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, to control impulses, and to understand the reactions of others.” Id. at 319. These impairments render them less deserving of the most severe penalty the State may impose.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Atkins* in affirming Atkins’ death sentence. The Court recognized that the jury had been presented with evidence of Atkins’ intellectual disability, including testimony from a forensic psychologist who diagnosed him as mildly mentally disabled. Nevertheless, the jury concluded that Atkins was eligible for the death penalty, and the Virginia Supreme Court upheld that determination under *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), which permitted the imposition of the death penalty on individuals with intellectual disability so long as the jury was properly instructed to consider mitigating factors. However, *Atkins* overruled *Penry*’s continued viability in the context of capital punishment for individuals with intellectual disability. The Court in *Atkins* made clear that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment” bars the execution of such individuals, regardless of the jury’s findings or the presence of mitigating evidence. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 321.

The dissenting opinion in *Atkins* argued that the Court was overstepping its role by imposing a national standard on states that had not adopted such a prohibition. But the Court rejected that argument, emphasizing that the Eighth Amendment is not a mere reflection of state law but a constitutional guarantee that must be interpreted in light of “the evolving standards of decency.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 319. The Court noted that the “moral and legal consensus” against executing individuals with intellectual disability had become so strong that it could no longer be ignored. Id. at 320.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court must be affirmed because it is consistent with the constitutional principle established in *Atkins*. The execution of Daryl Renard Atkins, who was diagnosed with intellectual disability, violates the Eighth Amendment. The Court’s holding in *Atkins* is not a departure from precedent but a necessary evolution of constitutional interpretation in response to a maturing society’s understanding of justice, dignity, and human worth. To permit the execution of individuals with intellectual disability would be to disregard the fundamental principle that punishment must be proportionate to the offender’s moral culpability. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 321.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
