**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act withdraws immunity where the action is “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state,” or upon an act outside the United States “in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere” that causes a direct effect in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Although Beg’s claim arises from expropriation, the pleaded facts also describe an attempted property exchange, a later representative’s visit to the United States, and a failure to honor the resulting compensation arrangement. At the pleading stage, those allegations are sufficient to require jurisdictional discovery and to defeat dismissal on the present record.

First, the district court should have construed the jurisdictional facts in light of the pleadings and the remedial purpose of the FSIA. Federal jurisdiction turns on the sovereign’s challenged conduct, not on labels attached to it. See *Texas Industries, Inc. v. Radcliff Materials, Inc.*, 451 U.S. 630, 640 (1981) (courts must look to the “nature of the activity”); *Murray v. The Charming Betsy*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 64, 118 (1804) (an act should not be construed to work injustice absent clear command). Where a sovereign steps into the marketplace to arrange a land swap, it acts as a participant in a commercial transaction, not as a mere regulator. See *Bank of Augusta v. Earle*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 519, 587 (1839) (recognizing the commercial dealings of states in the marketplace).

Second, this case is not limited to the initial taking. Beg alleges that after the expropriation, Punjab sent a representative to the United States and offered alternative land in exchange for relinquishment of the claim, an offer that was accepted in the Lahore High Court but later repudiated. A sovereign’s voluntary negotiation of compensation is commercial in character because it resembles a settlement transaction. See *The Sapphire*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 164, 167–68 (1870) (foreign sovereign litigation proceeds on ordinary judicial principles once the sovereign appears); *The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116, 136–37 (1812) (immunity depends on the sovereign character of the act, not a blanket exemption from all disputes). Here, the alleged undertaking to provide substitute land and the subsequent refusal to perform are the operative commercial acts.

Third, the district court erred in collapsing the analysis into the fact that the original property was taken for public use. Even a sovereign’s public objectives can be pursued through commercial means. The relevant question is whether the sovereign’s conduct is the type of conduct by which private parties engage in trade or commerce. See *Hickman v. Jones*, 76 U.S. (9 Wall.) 197, 199–200 (1869) (distinguishing governmental acts from private acts); *Lane v. Cotton*, 1 Ld. Raym. 646, 648, 91 Eng. Rep. 1062, 1063 (K.B. 1701) (public office does not convert all conduct into sovereign compulsion). A land-for-land exchange, or payment in lieu of retained property, is a classic transaction capable of private performance.

Fourth, the alleged conduct had a sufficient connection to the United States to satisfy § 1605(a)(2) at minimum for jurisdictional purposes. Beg pleads that Punjab sent a representative into the United States to negotiate the exchange. That allegation establishes conduct in the forum, not merely foreign policy abroad. Jurisdiction over foreign sovereigns is not defeated where the sovereign deliberately reaches into the United States to consummate a transaction. See *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 727 (1878) (jurisdiction tracks territorial acts and deliberate presence); *St. Clair v. Cox*, 106 U.S. 350, 355 (1882) (jurisdiction follows acts performed through agents within the forum).

Fifth, the district court’s narrow view of “commercial activity” disregards the long-settled principle that sovereign and private acts must be distinguished by function, not by actor. See *United States v. Holliday*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 407, 416–17 (1865) (distinguishing prohibited conduct by its character); *Munn v. Illinois*, 94 U.S. 113, 123–24 (1877) (law looks to the nature of the thing, not the title of the actor). Once Pakistan and Punjab allegedly negotiated substitute property and undertook to resolve the claim through a real-estate exchange, they were acting in a commercial capacity.

Finally, dismissal at the threshold was premature. When jurisdiction depends on facts uniquely within the sovereign’s control, a plaintiff is entitled to develop the record. See *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1869) (jurisdiction must be tested with care); *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 370 (1886) (courts must not permit formal rules to defeat substantive rights); *Baldwin v. Hale*, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 223, 233 (1863) (procedural rules should not bar a fair hearing). Beg alleges enough to show that the sovereign’s conduct extended beyond a bare taking and into a negotiated property transaction. That suffices to invoke § 1605(a)(2) and to permit the case to proceed.

For these reasons, the judgment dismissing the complaint should be reversed.
