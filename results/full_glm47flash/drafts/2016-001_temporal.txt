**Argument**

I. The patentee bears the burden of proving that the whole of the profits is attributable to the patented invention. *Pioneer Hi-Bred Int’l, Inc. v. Holden Foundation Seeds, Inc.*, 351 U.S. 227, 233 (1956). The burden is on the patentee to show that the whole of the profits is attributable to the patented invention. *Pioneer Hi-Bred Int’l, Inc. v. Brown*, 351 U.S. 440, 440 (1956). This burden is not easily discharged when the patent covers only a component of a larger product. *Pioneer Hi-Bred*, 351 U.S. at 233-34. The Supreme Court has held that the patentee must show the patented feature is the "driving force" of the product's success. *Id.* at 233. If the patentee fails to make such a showing, the profits must be apportioned. *Id.* at 234.

II. Here, the asserted design patents cover specific visual elements of the smartphone, not the device as a whole. The jury was instructed that Samsung’s entire profits were attributable to the infringed design. This instruction was erroneous because the design patent does not cover the entire product. *Pioneer Hi-Bred*, 351 U.S. at 233. The profits must be apportioned to the component. *Id.* at 234. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the damages award without addressing this apportionment issue. *Pioneer Hi-Bred*, 351 U.S. at 233.
