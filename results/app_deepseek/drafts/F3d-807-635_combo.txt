ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Holding That DISH “Used” a Prerecorded Voice on Calls That Never Played a Prerecorded Message.

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”) prohibits “mak[ing] any call … using … an artificial or prerecorded voice.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). The plain meaning of “using” requires actual, active employment of the prohibited technology during the call. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (plain meaning of “use” in Mann Act required actual transportation for immoral purposes, not mere preparation). When Congress prohibits an act, it does not reach mere steps that do not complete the proscribed conduct. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 381 (1922) (holding that preparatory acts are not the same as the completed offense under the National Prohibition Act).

The district court erroneously concluded that DISH’s Cisco Dialer “used” a prerecorded voice on all seven calls because the system was programmed to play a prerecorded message upon detection of a positive voice. But the undisputed record shows that on four of the seven calls—calls 2, 3, 4, and 5—the system never detected a positive voice. On calls 2, 3, and 4, the call was answered by an answering machine; on call 5, a data error occurred. In each instance, no prerecorded message was played. The TCPA does not punish the mere capacity to use a prerecorded voice; it punishes calls that are actually made “using” such a voice. *United States v. Noveck*, 273 U.S. 202, 206 (1927) (interpreting “use” in mail fraud statute as requiring actual use of the mail, not mere intention). Similarly, *United States v. One 1937 Hudson Terraplane*, 317 U.S. 374, 379 (1943) (forfeiture statute required “use” of the vehicle in the commission of a crime, not mere possession). The Cisco Dialer’s programming is no different from a loaded firearm in a locked cabinet—it is a tool awaiting activation, not an act of use. *Cf. United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 105 (1820) (penal statutes must be strictly construed in favor of the accused).

The district court’s interpretation would expand the TCPA beyond its text. If the mere presence of a prerecorded-voice capability in an automated dialer sufficed, then every call placed by a dialer that *could* play a recorded message would violate the statute, even if no message was ever played. That result is absurd and cannot be what Congress intended. *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (statutes should not be interpreted to produce absurd results). The statute’s language—“make any call … using … a prerecorded voice”—requires a direct nexus between the call and the prohibited feature. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (statutory interpretation must respect the “language of the statute” and “the context in which [it] is found”). A call that never uses a prerecorded voice is not a call made “using” such a voice. *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 312 (1957) (interpreting “use” in the Smith Act as requiring active participation in the proscribed conspiracy, not mere membership).

DISH concedes that calls 1, 6, and 7—where a positive voice was detected and a prerecorded message was played—violated the TCPA. But for calls 2, 3, 4, and 5, no prerecorded message was ever transmitted. The district court’s grant of summary judgment on those four calls must be reversed.

II. The District Court Also Erred in Failing to Give Effect to the Original Subscriber’s Prior Express Consent.

Even if the Cisco Dialer’s operation could be considered “using” a prerecorded voice, the original subscriber of the 3475 number expressly authorized DISH to contact that number for debt collection. That consent is a complete defense under the TCPA, which bars liability when the call is made “with the prior express consent of the called party.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A). The “called party” is the person who receives the call at the number, but the consent given by the original subscriber attaches to the number itself. Under well-settled contract principles, a party who acquires a phone number subject to a pre-existing consent implicitly assumes the burdens of that consent. *See, e.g., Meeker v. Thompson*, 43 F. 879, 881 (D. Kan. 1890) (assignee of a contract right takes subject to all equities and conditions existing at the time of assignment). Ybarra, by acquiring the 3475 number after the original subscriber had authorized DISH to call it, cannot claim that the call was made without consent. The district court’s contrary ruling disregards the continuing nature of the consent and should be reversed.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed as to the four calls that did not involve the playback of a prerecorded message.
