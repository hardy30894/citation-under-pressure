ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States by the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Court of Appeal determined that the evidence was insufficient to prove that petitioner Monge personally used a deadly weapon—an essential element of the prior conviction allegation. That determination constitutes an acquittal. Retrial of that allegation is therefore barred.

**I. The Prior Conviction Allegation Is a Separate Offense for Double Jeopardy Purposes**

The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to any proceeding that subjects a defendant to “jeopardy of life or limb.” In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 176 (1874), this Court held that the Clause prohibits multiple punishments for the same offense. A prior conviction allegation under California’s three-strikes law is not a mere sentencing factor; it is a separate offense that must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Under *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299, 304 (1932), two offenses are not the same if each requires proof of a fact the other does not. The substantive drug offenses require proof of a sale or possession of marijuana; the prior conviction allegation requires proof of a prior serious felony conviction and personal use of a deadly weapon. Each requires proof of a fact the other does not. *In re Nielsen*, 131 U.S. 176, 188 (1889) (holding that the Clause protects against successive prosecutions for the same act as a crime and its aggravation). Because the prior conviction allegation is a distinct offense, jeopardy attached when the trial court began to hear evidence on it.

**II. An Insufficient Evidence Finding Is an Acquittal Barring Retrial**

This Court has long held that a finding of insufficient evidence is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896) (acquittal, however erroneous, bars retrial); *Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141, 143 (1962) (directed acquittal bars retrial even if based on erroneous view of law). The Court of Appeal found that “the evidence was insufficient to establish that defendant had acted personally.” That determination resolved the factual issue in Monge’s favor. It is a final judgment on the merits of the prior conviction allegation. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 191 (1957) (holding that implied acquittal on a greater offense bars retrial for that offense). The State may not now force Monge to run the gauntlet again.

**III. The Noncapital Context Does Not Remove the Proceeding from the Double Jeopardy Clause**

The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 796, and its protections are not limited to capital cases. The Clause speaks of “jeopardy of life or limb,” but that phrase encompasses any deprivation of liberty. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717 (Clause protects against “embarrassment, expense, and ordeal” of multiple trials). California’s three-strikes law doubles a defendant’s prison term, a substantial liberty deprivation. The fact that the proceeding is noncapital does not immunize it from double jeopardy scrutiny.

The California Supreme Court erred in relying on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949). *Williams* held only that the Due Process Clause does not require a formal hearing for all sentencing determinations. *Id.* at 251. But here, California has elected to provide a trial-like proceeding with proof beyond a reasonable doubt, a jury trial, and the rights to confront and cross-examine witnesses. *See, e.g., Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 608 (1967) (where a state creates a separate sentencing proceeding that determines facts leading to increased punishment, due process requires certain protections). The State cannot grant the hallmarks of a criminal trial and then deny the Double Jeopardy Clause’s core protection. *Specht* recognized that when a state “makes the commission of a separate crime a basis for an increased sentence,” the defendant is entitled to “the full panoply of the relevant protections.” *Id.* at 610. Double jeopardy is foremost among those protections.

**IV. Retrial Would Subject Monge to Multiple Punishments for the Same Alleged Prior Conviction**

The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits “multiple punishments for the same offense.” *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 176. The Court of Appeal’s reversal of the true finding on the prior conviction allegation was a determination that the State had failed to prove the allegation. Retrial would require Monge to once again face the risk of an enhanced sentence for the same prior conviction. That is precisely the “second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal” that the Clause forbids. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717.

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation. The judgment of the Supreme Court of California should be reversed.
