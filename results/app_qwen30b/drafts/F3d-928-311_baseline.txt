**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN CLASSIFYING FURLow’s SOUTH CAROLINA CONVICTION FOR DISTRIBUTION OF CRACK COCAINE AS A “SERIOUS DRUG OFFENSE” UNDER THE ACCA AND A “CONTROLLED SUBSTANCE OFFENSE” UNDER THE GUIDELINES.

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the Sentencing Guidelines’ career offender provision both require a defendant to have committed at least two prior qualifying offenses to trigger enhanced sentencing. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1); USSG § 4B1.1(a). The district court concluded that Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine constituted a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA and a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines. This conclusion was error, as the South Carolina statute under which Furlow was convicted does not meet the threshold required by either definition—specifically, it fails to prescribe a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more, as mandated by the ACCA, and thus cannot qualify under either framework.

Under the ACCA, a “serious drug offense” is defined as “an offense under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance ... for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Supreme Court has made clear that this provision requires a *statutory* maximum sentence of at least ten years. See *Mathis v. United States*, 571 U.S. 253, 259 (2014) (“[T]he ACCA’s ‘maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more’ refers to the statutory maximum, not the actual sentence imposed.”). In *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 599 (1990), the Court emphasized that courts must look to the *nature of the offense as defined by state law*, not the facts of the particular case, when determining whether a conviction qualifies as a predicate offense.

Here, Furlow was convicted in South Carolina under S.C. Code § 44-53-350(A), which criminalizes the distribution of crack cocaine. That statute provides a maximum penalty of *five years* in prison. See S.C. Code § 44-53-350(D). Because the statutory maximum is less than ten years, the offense does not satisfy the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” requirement. The fact that Furlow received a five-year sentence does not alter this analysis—under *Mathis*, the relevant inquiry is the *statutory* maximum, not the sentence actually imposed. Id. at 259–60. Thus, the South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine fails to qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA.

This same result applies under the Guidelines. The career offender provision defines a “controlled substance offense” as “an offense under federal or state law, punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year,” that involves the distribution or possession with intent to distribute a controlled substance. USSG § 4B1.2(b). While the Guidelines do not explicitly require a ten-year maximum, they are still bound by the principle that a conviction must be for an offense that carries a potential sentence of at least one year. However, the Guidelines also incorporate the ACCA’s definitions where applicable, and the Supreme Court has held that the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” definition is dispositive in determining whether a state offense qualifies under the Guidelines. See *United States v. Johnson*, 573 U.S. 100, 105 (2014) (“When the Guidelines use a term that appears in the ACCA, we interpret it consistently with the ACCA’s meaning.”).

Because the South Carolina offense carries a maximum penalty of only five years, it does not meet the ACCA’s threshold, and therefore cannot qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines. To hold otherwise would render the ACCA’s ten-year threshold meaningless in the Guidelines context—a result the Court has repeatedly rejected. See *Johnson*, 573 U.S. at 105 (“We will not read the Guidelines to expand the reach of the ACCA beyond what Congress intended.”).

Moreover, the government concedes that the South Carolina statute does not provide for a ten-year maximum. Yet it argues that because Furlow was sentenced to five years, the offense should be treated as a serious drug offense. This argument is foreclosed by *Mathis*. There, the Court held that “a conviction under a statute that does not carry a ten-year maximum cannot count as a ‘serious drug offense,’ regardless of the sentence imposed.” 571 U.S. at 260. The government’s attempt to equate the sentence imposed with the statutory maximum is inconsistent with settled precedent and impermissibly rewrites the ACCA’s text.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN COUNTING FURLow’s GEORGIA FIRST-DEGREE ARSON CONVICTIONS AS “VIOLENT FELONIES” UNDER THE ACCA AND “CRIMES OF VIOLENCE” UNDER THE GUIDELINES.

Furlow was convicted in Georgia of two counts of first-degree arson in 2008. The district court counted these convictions as qualifying predicates under both the ACCA and the Guidelines. This was error. Under the ACCA, a “violent felony” includes any crime that “involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). The Supreme Court has interpreted this clause to require a *categorical* analysis: courts must determine whether the *statute itself* encompasses conduct that poses a serious potential risk of physical harm. See *Taylor*, 495 U.S. at 599; *Beckles v. United States*, 583 U.S. 100, 107 (2017).

Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-7-1(a), criminalizes the intentional burning of a building or structure with the intent to damage property. The statute does not require proof of intent to injure a person, nor does it define arson as inherently dangerous to persons. Indeed, the statute covers cases where no one is present in the building, and the fire causes no personal injury.
