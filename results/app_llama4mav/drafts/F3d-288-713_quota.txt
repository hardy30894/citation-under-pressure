The district court's denial of Riddle's petition for writ of habeas corpus was improper. Riddle's conviction and sentence were tainted by prosecutorial misconduct, ineffective assistance of counsel, and the district court's failure to hold an evidentiary hearing.

First, Riddle's due process rights were violated when the prosecutor, Hill, testified as a witness and continued to prosecute the case. This Court has held that a prosecutor's testimony can be so prejudicial that it requires reversal. See, e.g., United States v. Johnston, 690 F.2d 638, 643-44 (8th Cir. 1982) (en banc) (holding that a prosecutor's testimony can create an appearance of impropriety that undermines the fairness of the trial). The Supreme Court has also recognized that a prosecutor's role is not only to present evidence but also to ensure that justice is served. See Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) ("The United States Attorney is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty whose obligation to govern impartially is as compelling as its obligation to govern at all."). Hill's testimony and continued prosecution of the case created an appearance of impropriety that undermined the fairness of Riddle's trial.

Second, Riddle's trial counsel was ineffective for failing to object to Hill's testimony and continued prosecution. To establish ineffective assistance of counsel, Riddle must show that his counsel's performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced his defense. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). Riddle's counsel failed to object to Hill's testimony, which allowed the prosecutor to vouch for his own credibility and impugn Riddle's. See United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1985) (holding that a prosecutor's vouching for his own credibility can be improper). This failure to object was deficient and prejudiced Riddle's defense.

Third, the district court erred in denying Riddle's request for an evidentiary hearing. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2), a district court may hold an evidentiary hearing if the petitioner has not failed to develop the factual basis of a claim in state court. See Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 420, 437 (2000) (holding that a petitioner has not failed to develop the factual basis of a claim if he was diligent in seeking to develop the facts). Riddle was diligent in seeking to develop the facts underlying his claims, and the district court should have held an evidentiary hearing to resolve the factual disputes. See Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 313 (1963) (holding that a district court must hold an evidentiary hearing if the petitioner did not receive a full and fair hearing in state court), overruled in part on other grounds by Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, 504 U.S. 1 (1992).

Furthermore, the district court's grant of summary judgment was improper. Summary judgment is only appropriate if there is no genuine issue of material fact. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986). Riddle raised several claims that were not fully developed in state court, and the district court should have held an evidentiary hearing to resolve these factual disputes. See Harris v. Nelson, 394 U.S. 286, 300 (1969) (holding that a district court has broad discretion to hold an evidentiary hearing in a habeas corpus proceeding).

The district court's denial of Riddle's petition for writ of habeas corpus was also improper because it failed to consider the cumulative effect of the errors that occurred during Riddle's trial. See Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 298 (1973) (holding that the cumulative effect of errors can be considered in determining whether a defendant received a fair trial). The prosecutor's testimony and continued prosecution, combined with the ineffective assistance of Riddle's trial counsel, undermined the fairness of Riddle's trial.

In conclusion, the district court's denial of Riddle's petition for writ of habeas corpus was improper. Riddle's conviction and sentence were tainted by prosecutorial misconduct, ineffective assistance of counsel, and the district court's failure to hold an evidentiary hearing. This Court should reverse the district court's judgment and remand for an evidentiary hearing. See, e.g., Dobbs v. Zant, 506 U.S. 357 (1993) (reversing and remanding for an evidentiary hearing).
