**ARGUMENT**

**I. Since Rule 52(b)’s inception, plain-error review has required examination of the whole record, not merely the evidence submitted to the jury.**

The plain-error doctrine was designed to permit appellate courts to correct injustice when the ordinary rules of preservation would otherwise bar relief, and this Court has always defined that inquiry by reference to the record as a whole rather than to the trial evidence in isolation. In *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), the Court explained that appellate courts, “in the public interest, may, of their own motion, notice errors to which no exception has been taken, if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” 297 U.S. at 160. That standard—later codified in Rule 52(b)—necessarily calls for an assessment of the proceeding’s overall fairness, a judgment that cannot be made by artificially confining the reviewing court’s gaze to the four corners of the trial transcript.

This Court confirmed as much in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), holding that an error affects “substantial rights” only if, “after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole,” the court cannot say “with fair assurance . . . that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error.” 328 U.S. at 765. “All that happened” is not synonymous with “all that the jury heard.” The very metaphor of the whole and its stripped-down parts presupposes that the reviewing court has before it more material than what was placed before the jury—otherwise there would be nothing to “strip away” or “ponder” beyond the verdict itself.

*United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), likewise instructs that an error’s effect on substantial rights is to be judged by comparing what occurred with what the record shows would otherwise have occurred, an inquiry that reaches beyond the trial evidence to the undisputed facts of the case. And in *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607 (1946), this Court did not evaluate an erroneous instruction in a vacuum; it considered the entirety of the circumstances surrounding the conviction to determine whether the error was consequential. These decisions share a common premise: reviewing courts must look at the case as it actually stood, not merely at the slice of it presented to the jury.

**II. The same logic governs the fourth prong of plain-error review—whether the error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceeding.**

The very phrase used to describe this final inquiry originates in *Atkinson* itself, where the Court framed the exception for “exceptional circumstances” in terms of the proceeding’s public reputation. 297 U.S. at 160. That inquiry is inescapably record-wide: a court cannot assess whether the *public* would view a proceeding as fair or reputable while blinding itself to facts that were undisputed, obvious, or conceded, simply because they were not formally read to the jury. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court exercised its discretion to notice an unpreserved error only after considering the full course of the proceedings—including matters not strictly confined to the evidentiary presentation—to determine whether the defendant had suffered real prejudice. 325 U.S. at 107. And in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), the Court measured the effect of prosecutorial misconduct not by isolating the offending remarks but by assessing “the whole” of the proceeding and the strength of the case against the defendant. 295 U.S. at 89.

*Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), reinforces the point in a closely related context: in determining whether a defendant’s rights were impaired, this Court looked to the entire circumstances of the case, not merely the formal trial record, because “substantial rights” cannot be evaluated by artificial evidentiary blinders. 304 U.S. at 464. Likewise, *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), examined the record broadly to determine whether an asserted violation had actually prejudiced the defendant, rather than presuming prejudice from the bare fact of error. And in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the Court reiterated that harmless-error review must proceed “beyond a reasonable doubt” by reference to the entire record, not a truncated version of it. 386 U.S. at 24.

**III. Applying these settled principles, the court of appeals properly considered the full record—including Greer’s own undisputed prior felony convictions—in concluding that the Rehaif error did not affect his substantial rights or the reputation of the proceeding.**

Consistent with *Atkinson*, *Kotteakos*, *Socony-Vacuum*, *Bollenbach*, *Screws*, *Berger*, *Zerbst*, *Glasser*, and *Chapman*, the court of appeals did precisely what eighty years of precedent instructs: it examined the record as a whole, including the undisputed fact—readily apparent from Greer’s own history—that he had been convicted of multiple felonies before the offense at issue. Nothing in that record suggests any dispute as to Greer’s knowledge of his status. Confining review to the trial transcript alone, while ignoring undisputed facts of record, would exalt formalism over the very purpose plain-error review serves: ensuring that convictions rest on proceedings that are, in substance and reputation, fair. The judgment below should be affirmed.
