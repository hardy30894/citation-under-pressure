**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of §523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only debts “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, *to the extent obtained by* … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. §523(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” unambiguously limits nondischargeability to the value of the benefit the debtor fraudulently acquired. Treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not “obtained by” fraud; they are punitive sanctions imposed by state law after the fact. Because the debtor never “obtained” those sums through his fraud, they fall outside the statutory exception and are dischargeable.

This Court has repeatedly instructed that exceptions to discharge must be narrowly construed in favor of the debtor. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991) (exceptions “should be confined to those plainly expressed”). The “fresh start” policy of the Bankruptcy Code “affords the honest but unfortunate debtor a new opportunity in life.” *Id.* at 286-87. That policy would be undermined if a debtor who committed fraud could be saddled with punitive awards far exceeding the actual loss—a result Congress did not intend.

The Third Circuit erred by following *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which held that punitive damages are nondischargeable under §523(a)(2)(A). *St. Laurent* ignored the statute’s limiting language. The better-reasoned approach is *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), which held that only compensatory damages actually obtained by fraud are excepted. *Levy* correctly observed that punitive damages “do not constitute a debt obtained by fraud” because they are not “money, property, services, or credit” that the debtor received. *Id.* at 198. The Ninth Circuit’s reading respects the statutory text and the narrow-construction canon.

The Third Circuit also misapplied *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59 (1995), which addressed the reliance element of §523(a)(2)(A). *Field* did not consider whether punitive damages are “obtained by” fraud. And *Kelly v. Robinson*, 479 U.S. 36 (1986), involved a different exception—§523(a)(7) for restitution—and is inapposite. *Kelly* expressly noted that the “obtained by” language of §523(a)(2) was not at issue. *Id.* at 49.

The treble damages here are not compensation for actual pecuniary loss. They are a penalty imposed by the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. This Court has recognized that statutory treble damages serve punitive purposes. *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 302, 304 (1943) (treble damages under Emergency Price Control Act are “punitive in nature”). Because they are not “obtained by” fraud, they are dischargeable.

The judgment below should be reversed.
