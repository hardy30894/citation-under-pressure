**ARGUMENT**

**I. Johnson Announced a Substantive Rule, and Substantive Rules Apply Retroactively on Collateral Review.**

This Court has long distinguished between new rules of criminal procedure, which generally do not apply retroactively to cases already final, and substantive rules, which always do. *See Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310–11 (1989) (adopting Justice Harlan’s framework from *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)). *Teague* recognized that one category of rule is categorically excepted from the general bar on retroactivity: rules that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” 489 U.S. at 311. That exception exists because “[i]t is thus not surprising that we have applied Teague’s new-rule bar only to rules of procedure.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). Substantive rules—those that “alter the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes”—apply retroactively because a conviction or sentence obtained under an invalid substantive rule is one that “no one may be” subjected to. *Id.* at 353.

The Court applied this same logic in *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998), holding that a decision narrowing the scope of a criminal statute applies retroactively on collateral review because such a decision “necessarily carr[ies] a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of ‘an act that the law does not make criminal.’” *Id.* at 620 (quoting *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974)). The same risk—of a defendant suffering punishment the law does not authorize—is present here.

**II. Johnson’s Invalidation of the Residual Clause Narrowed the Class of Persons Subject to ACCA’s Mandatory Minimum, Making It a Substantive Rule.**

In *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), this Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s violent-felony definition—covering conduct that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii)—“is unconstitutionally vague.” 576 U.S. at 597. The Court explained that the residual clause “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges,” *id.* at 596–97, and that “[i]nvoking so shapeless a provision to condemn someone to prison for 15 years to life” could not be squared with due process. *Id.* at 602.

That holding did not merely change how courts evaluate prior convictions; it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses that had previously exposed offenders to ACCA’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. Before *Johnson*, individuals like Mr. Welch could be classified as armed career criminals based on convictions—such as Florida strong-arm robbery—deemed to present a “serious potential risk of physical injury” under the now-void residual clause. After *Johnson*, no one may be sentenced under that clause at all. This is precisely the kind of rule that “alter[s] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353, because it removes an entire class of offenders from ACCA’s reach. Like the statutory-narrowing rule in *Bousley*, *Johnson*’s rule means that defendants sentenced under the residual clause now “stand[] convicted”—or, more precisely, sentenced—for conduct the law no longer proscribes as ACCA-qualifying. 523 U.S. at 620.

By contrast, this case is unlike *Chaidez v. United States*, 133 S.Ct. 1103 (2013), where the Court held that a new rule regarding the scope of the Sixth Amendment right to effective assistance of counsel was procedural because it concerned only “how” a defendant’s claim would be evaluated, not the substantive reach of any criminal prohibition. *Id.* at 1107. *Johnson*, unlike *Chaidez*, does not regulate the procedures for adjudicating ACCA claims; it eliminates a substantive basis for imposing an enhanced sentence altogether. That is a rule about the “range of conduct” the law punishes, squarely within *Schriro*’s substantive-rule category. 542 U.S. at 353.

**III. This Court’s Recent Decision in Montgomery Confirms That Substantive Rules Must Be Given Retroactive Effect.**

In *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190 (2016), decided just weeks before oral argument in this case, the Court reaffirmed that “[a] penalty imposed pursuant to an unconstitutional law is no less void because the prisoner’s sentence became final before the law was invalidated,” and held that “the Constitution requires substantive rules to have retroactive effect regardless of when a conviction became final.” *Id.* at 206, 212. *Montgomery* recognized that the substantive-rule exception is not a matter of judicial grace but reflects a constitutional command: courts have no authority to enforce a punishment that the Constitution forbids. That reasoning applies with equal, if not greater, force to a federal prisoner proceeding under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 than to a state prisoner on collateral review, since the source of the retroactivity requirement is the Constitution itself, not the procedural vehicle through which relief is sought.

**CONCLUSION**

Because *Johnson* announced a substantive rule under *Teague*, *Bousley*, *Schriro*, and *Montgomery*, it applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, including Mr. Welch’s. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed.
