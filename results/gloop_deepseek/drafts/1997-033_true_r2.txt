## ARGUMENT

The question presented is whether a federal district court conducting pretrial proceedings under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial. The answer is no. Section 1407(a) commands that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation shall remand each transferred action to its originating district at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings. This mandatory language admits of no exception. The transferee court’s authority is limited to pretrial proceedings; it possesses no power to retain a case for trial under § 1404(a) in derogation of the Panel’s exclusive remand authority.

Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity. The verb “shall” is the language of command, not suggestion. *See Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949) (recognizing that statutory language controls absent clear contrary intent). The Panel’s duty to remand is unconditional. Nothing in § 1407 authorizes a transferee court to convert its limited pretrial assignment into a permanent trial venue. The statute’s design reflects a deliberate policy choice: consolidation for pretrial purposes only, preserving plaintiffs’ right to trial in the original forum. *See Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960) (holding that § 1404(a) transfer is permissible only to a district where the action “might have been brought” originally); *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616-17 (1964) (emphasizing that § 1404(a) does not alter the substantive law applicable to transferred cases).

The practice of self-transfer under § 1404(a) subverts the statutory scheme. Section 1404(a) allows a district court to transfer a case “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. at 56. But that power presupposes that the case is properly before the court for all purposes, including trial. A transferee court under § 1407(a) exercises only limited jurisdiction over pretrial matters. It cannot “transfer” the case to itself because the case was never properly assigned to it for trial. *See Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co.*, 375 U.S. 71, 73 (1963) (per curiam) (holding that § 1404(a) discretion must be exercised consistent with governing law). The transferee court’s use of § 1404(a) to retain a case exceeds the scope of its authority and effectively amends the statute by judicial fiat.

The cases relied upon by the lower courts do not support this practice. *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), are both post-1970 decisions that cannot overcome the plain language of § 1407 and this Court’s pre-1970 precedent. The dissenting judge below correctly observed that this practice ignores the statutory mandate and is illicit. This Court should not permit a widespread practice to override the clear command of Congress.

The policy justifications for self-transfer—judicial efficiency and convenience—cannot justify ignoring the statute. The same arguments were rejected in *Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R.*, 345 U.S. 379, 383 (1953), where this Court held that § 1404(a) “addresses itself only to that federal forum” and does not authorize evasion of venue rules. *See also Kilpatrick v. Texas & Pac. Ry. Co.*, 337 U.S. 75, 78 (1949) (emphasizing the importance of plaintiff’s choice of forum under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act). Congress balanced efficiency against plaintiffs’ forum choice in § 1407; courts must respect that balance.

Moreover, the transferee court’s self-transfer is inconsistent with the exclusive role of the Panel in assigning cases for trial. The Panel alone determines whether consolidation is appropriate and when to remand. *See Koehring Co. v. Hyde Constr. Co.*, 382 U.S. 362, 364-65 (1966) (recognizing that transfer authority is carefully circumscribed). A district judge cannot appropriate that authority. *Herb v. Pitcairn*, 325 U.S. 77, 88-89 (1945) (holding that federal law governs the commencement of federal actions, not state rules). The transferee court’s action usurps the Panel’s function.

Finally, the plain language of § 1407(a) controls. The Panel shall remand. This Court has repeatedly held that statutory commands must be followed. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. at 58. The practice of self-transfer under § 1404(a) is a clear error that this Court should correct. The judgment below should be reversed.
