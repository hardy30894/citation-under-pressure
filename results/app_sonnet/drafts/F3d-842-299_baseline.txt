# ARGUMENT

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this action, and the case must be remanded to the Circuit Court of Franklin County, Virginia.

## I. The Well-Pleaded Complaint Asserts Only State-Law Claims, and Federal Question Jurisdiction Does Not Exist Under 28 U.S.C. § 1331.

Federal-question jurisdiction exists only when a federal question appears on the face of a plaintiff's well-pleaded complaint. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908) (holding that "a suit arises under the Constitution and laws of the United States only when the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action shows that it is based upon those laws or that Constitution"). It is not enough that a federal issue may arise as a defense, or that resolving the case may require reference to federal law tangential to the plaintiffs' claim. *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987) ("[A] case may not be removed to federal court on the basis of a federal defense.").

The Pressls' complaint seeks a declaration of their property rights under a private easement, governed entirely by Virginia contract and property law. The complaint asks the state court to declare "that APCO has no regulatory authority over the plaintiffs' property... beyond those rights defined by the flowage easement, the contemporaneous expressions of the parties, and vested rights to build and own structures." Nowhere does the complaint invoke the Federal Power Act, FERC's licensing authority, or any other federal statute. The claim is a garden-variety easement dispute of the sort Virginia courts routinely adjudicate.

That APCO intends to defend its conduct by reference to its FERC license does not transform the Pressls' state-law claim into a federal one. As the Supreme Court has explained, "the presence of a disputed federal issue" as an anticipated defense "does not overcome the paramount policies embodied in the well-pleaded complaint rule." *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust*, 463 U.S. 1, 12 (1983). It is APCO, not the Pressls, that raises the FERC license and its regulatory implications; the Pressls' cause of action rests solely on the four corners of the 1960 easement instrument, interpreted under Virginia law.

## II. 16 U.S.C. § 825p Does Not Supply Jurisdiction Because This Suit Does Not Arise Under the Federal Power Act.

Section 825p vests federal district courts with jurisdiction over suits "brought to enforce any liability or duty created by" the Federal Power Act or "to enjoin any violation" of the Act. 16 U.S.C. § 825p. This provision does not create an independent grant of jurisdiction over every dispute that happens to touch a FERC-licensed project; it reaches only claims that assert rights or duties arising from the Act itself.

The Pressls' complaint does neither. It does not allege that APCO violated any provision of the Federal Power Act, nor does it seek to enforce any duty the Act creates. It seeks only a declaration of the parties' respective rights under a privately negotiated easement executed in 1960 and construed under state property law principles. The mere fact that APCO's dam operates pursuant to a federal license does not convert every controversy about the scope of an associated private easement into a federal cause of action. Cf. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005) (federal jurisdiction over state-law claims exists only where a federal issue is "necessarily raised," "actually disputed," "substantial," and capable of resolution in federal court "without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities"). The balance Congress struck in enacting the Federal Power Act does not contemplate that every landowner's easement dispute with a hydroelectric licensee must be litigated in federal court merely because a FERC license exists in the background. See *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006) (federal jurisdiction requires more than a "federal element" lurking in a state-law claim).

## III. Removal Jurisdiction Must Be Strictly Construed, and Doubts Must Be Resolved in Favor of Remand.

The removal statutes are to be strictly construed against removal jurisdiction, "because of the significant federalism concerns implicated." *Mulcahey v. Columbia Organic Chemicals Co.*, 29 F.3d 148, 151 (4th Cir. 1994); see also *Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets*, 313 U.S. 100, 108–09 (1941) (removal statutes must be "strictly construed" in light of the limits Congress placed on federal jurisdiction). The burden of establishing federal jurisdiction rests with the removing party—here, APCO. *Mulcahey*, 29 F.3d at 151. Any doubts about the propriety of removal must be resolved in favor of remand. *Id.*

APCO cannot carry that burden. Its removal petition rests entirely on the theory that the FERC license and project boundary designation somehow inject a federal question into a dispute framed exclusively in terms of a private flowage easement and Virginia property law. That theory conflates the existence of a federal regulatory backdrop with the existence of a federal claim—precisely the confusion the well-pleaded complaint rule forecloses. *Mottley*, 211 U.S. at 152.

## CONCLUSION

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, this Court should vacate the judgment below and remand with instructions to return the case to the Circuit Court of Franklin County, Virginia.
