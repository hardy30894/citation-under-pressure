**ARGUMENT**

The statute authorizing post-removal-period detention, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), must be construed to avoid the serious constitutional questions that would arise from permitting indefinite detention of resident aliens. This Court has long recognized that “if a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter.” *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909). The plain text of § 1231(a)(6) does not expressly authorize indefinite confinement; it provides only that an alien “may be detained beyond the removal period.” Read in light of the Constitution’s demands, this language must mean detention for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, not perpetual imprisonment.

Resident aliens such as petitioner are “persons” within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment and are entitled to due process of law. *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (holding that a resident alien “retains due process rights”); *The Japanese Immigrant Case (Yamataya v. Fisher)*, 189 U.S. 86, 101 (1903) (holding that deportation proceedings must comport with due process). Deportation itself is not punishment, *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 730 (1893), but detention that is indefinite and divorced from any realistic prospect of removal becomes punitive in effect. *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922) (recognizing that deportation may involve loss of liberty where “the life of one who is a resident of this country” is at stake). The Court has long held that imprisonment without the protections of a criminal trial violates the Constitution except in narrow circumstances. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896) (striking down statute authorizing imprisonment at hard labor for aliens without judicial trial, because “it is not consistent with the Constitution of the United States”).

The Government’s interpretation—that § 1231(a)(6) permits detention until removal becomes “impossible”—would allow confinement that could last for years, decades, or a lifetime. Such indefinite detention would be “punitive in character” and would “spot the very permanence” that due process forbids. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237. This Court has never authorized the civil detention of a resident alien for an unlimited period when removal is not reasonably foreseeable. In *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 215 (1953), this Court upheld the detention of an excludable alien *at the border*, but expressly distinguished aliens who have “effected an entry” into the United States. *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 600. Petitioner, who entered as a lawful permanent resident, is not subject to the plenary border power that governed *Mezei*.

Where a statute would otherwise raise “grave doubts” about its constitutionality, this Court has repeatedly adopted a narrowing construction. *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 201 (1957) (construing a statute requiring aliens to provide information to immigration authorities to require only information “having a reasonable relation” to the purpose of the statute, thereby avoiding constitutional questions). Similarly, here, § 1231(a)(6) should be read to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *See also Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 348 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (courts should “avoid the necessity of considering a constitutional question” if an alternative construction is available).

The constitutional limits on civil detention are clear. As this Court stated in *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237, “no person” may be “imprisoned or subjected to other punishment” without due process. Indefinite detention—without any connection to a pending removal—is punishment. The only legitimate purpose of post-removal detention is to ensure the alien’s presence for removal. *cf. Carlisle v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 537 (1952) (recognizing that detention pending deportation is justified to prevent flight). When removal is not reasonably foreseeable, that purpose evaporates, and continued detention ceases to serve any non-punitive governmental interest.

In *Ludecke v. Watkins*, 335 U.S. 160, 169 (1948), this Court upheld the detention of enemy aliens during wartime, but only because the war had not ended and removal was still a possibility. Here, petitioner’s removal is not reasonably foreseeable—no country will accept him, and no treaty exists. Under such circumstances, “the continued custody of the alien, after all possibility of his deportation has been exhausted, cannot be sustained.” *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 305 (1944) (holding that detention of loyal Japanese-American citizens could not continue when the stated purpose of relocation no longer existed). Although *Endo* involved citizens, its principle applies with equal force to resident aliens who have the same due process rights. *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596.

The only reasonable construction of § 1231(a)(6) is that it authorizes detention for a period reasonably necessary to achieve removal, and no longer. Once it becomes clear that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Due Process Clause. The district court correctly ordered petitioner’s release, and this Court should affirm.
