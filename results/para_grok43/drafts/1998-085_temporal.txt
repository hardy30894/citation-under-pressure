The common law has long confined punitive damages to cases involving conduct that is itself outrageous or egregious, quite apart from the defendant's state of mind. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851), the Court explained that such awards are proper only where the defendant's acts exhibit "gross fraud, or where the defendant has acted from wanton, reckless, or malicious motives." The same limitation appears in Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858), which permitted exemplary damages solely upon proof of "such misconduct as would imply a sense of high-handed outrage."

That requirement of outrageous conduct was reiterated in Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), where the Court held that punitive damages could not rest on ordinary negligence or even intentional breach, but demanded "such a degree of negligence as the law deems equivalent to a reckless disregard of the plaintiff's rights" rising to the level of "oppression or fraud." Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), confirmed the rule: exemplary damages serve "to punish the offender" only when the act is "aggravated by evil motive, or by a disregard of the rights of others equivalent to actual malice."

These authorities establish that malice or reckless indifference, standing alone, has never sufficed. The defendant's mental state must be accompanied by conduct that is independently outrageous. Because the record here contains no evidence of such aggravated misconduct, the district court's refusal to submit the issue of punitive damages was required by settled precedent.
