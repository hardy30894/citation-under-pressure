**ARGUMENT**

**I. The MR. BELDON Is a “Workplace” Under Section 4(a) of the OSH Act**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The MR. BELDON, a drilling barge stationary in Louisiana’s territorial waters, is a “workplace” because it is a fixed location where employees perform work. This Court has long recognized that federal safety statutes should be construed broadly to protect workers. *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 123 (1941) (upholding broad commerce power to regulate labor conditions). The barge’s status as a “vessel” under maritime law does not remove it from the definition of “workplace,” as the OSH Act covers all employment settings not specifically exempted.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction Because the Coast Guard Does Not Exercise Statutory Authority Over Working Conditions on Uninspected Vessels**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act excludes “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific equipment safety regulations, not comprehensive occupational safety and health standards. Consequently, the Coast Guard has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over the working conditions at issue here, and OSHA’s jurisdiction remains.

**A. The Workers on the MR. BELDON Are Not Seamen Under Pre-1970 Law**

The Fifth Circuit erred in classifying the workers as “seamen.” Under pre-1970 admiralty law, a seaman is a person “permanently assigned to a vessel in navigation” who contributes to the vessel’s function. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 571 (1944). The MR. BELDON was a drilling barge fixed in position, not “in navigation.” *See The Buena Ventura*, 243 F. 797, 799 (S.D.N.Y. 1917) (a vessel must be “in actual navigation” for its crew to be seamen). The workers were engaged in oil drilling, not maritime navigation. *Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 192 (1952) (seaman status requires contribution to the “maritime function” of the vessel). *Senko v. La Crosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 370, 374 (1957) (worker on a dredge not in navigation held not a seaman). Because the workers are not seamen, the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen’s working conditions is irrelevant.

**B. The Coast Guard’s Regulatory Scheme for Uninspected Vessels Is Not Comprehensive Enough to Preempt OSHA**

Even if the workers were seamen, preemption requires that the Coast Guard actually exercise statutory authority over the specific working conditions. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (preemption occurs only when the federal scheme is “so pervasive” as to occupy the field). The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific equipment: fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and backfire traps. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. Part 25. This minimal regulation does not “occupy the field” of occupational safety and health. *San Diego Building Trades Council v. Garmon*, 359 U.S. 236, 244 (1959) (preemption requires “comprehensive” federal regulation). *United States v. Shimer*, 367 U.S. 374, 381 (1961) (preemption requires actual conflict, not merely potential). Here, the Coast Guard has not regulated the working conditions—such as blowout prevention—that OSHA cited. *Connecticut Light & Power Co. v. Federal Power Comm’n*, 324 U.S. 515, 524 (1945) (an agency’s authority is limited to its statutory grant). The Coast Guard’s statutory authority over uninspected vessels is narrow, and it has not exercised that authority to prescribe occupational safety standards for drilling operations. Therefore, Section 4(b)(1) does not bar OSHA’s citation.

**III. Conclusion**

The MR. BELDON is a workplace under the OSH Act. The workers are not seamen under pre-1970 law, and the Coast Guard’s limited regulatory scheme for uninspected vessels does not preempt OSHA jurisdiction. The Fifth Circuit’s decision should be reversed, and the citation reinstated.
