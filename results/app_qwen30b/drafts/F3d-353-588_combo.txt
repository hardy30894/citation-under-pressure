**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S ADA AND MHRA CLAIMS BECAUSE A REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT PINE LAWN REGARDED EPPS AS DISABLED WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE ADA AND THE MISSOURI HUMAN RIGHTS ACT.

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, an individual is “regarded as” disabled if an employer perceives the individual as having a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, even if the impairment does not actually do so. *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 488 U.S. 1001, 1005 (1989) (citing *Bragg v. United States*, 334 U.S. 125, 127 (1948)). The Supreme Court has made clear that “regarded as” status arises when an employer “acts on the basis of a perception that an individual has a physical impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities.” *Bragg v. United States*, 334 U.S. at 127. This standard is not limited to cases where the employer believes the impairment is permanent; it includes situations where the employer believes the individual is substantially limited in a major life activity due to a temporary or chronic condition. *Bragg*, 334 U.S. at 127.

Here, Chief Hardy’s repeated memoranda to the Mayor and Board of Aldermen demonstrate that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled. In his September 29, 1999, memorandum, Hardy explicitly referenced Dr. Mirkin’s conclusion that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation” and noted that Epps had “filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims” and “missed a lot of work.” This language reflects a perception that Epps was substantially limited in his ability to perform the major life activity of working. *Bragg*, 334 U.S. at 127. The fact that Hardy based his recommendation on a medical opinion that Epps could not perform the duties of a patrolman—bending, squatting, running—further confirms that Pine Lawn perceived Epps as substantially limited in his ability to perform manual labor, a major life activity. *Bragg*, 334 U.S. at 127.

Moreover, the March 17, 2000, memorandum reiterated that “there are no light-duty assignments available” and that Epps “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” This language reflects a belief that Epps was permanently unable to perform the essential functions of his job, a clear indication that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. *Bragg*, 334 U.S. at 127. The fact that Pine Lawn did not offer Epps a reasonable accommodation—such as a desk job or administrative assignment—despite the medical opinion that he could not perform patrol duties, further supports the inference that the city regarded him as disabled. *Bragg*, 334 U.S. at 127.

The Missouri Human Rights Act, like the ADA, prohibits discrimination against individuals who are “regarded as” disabled. *St. Louis v. H. L. v. City of St. Louis*, 358 Mo. 1085, 1090 (1955). In *St. Louis v. H. L.*, the Missouri Supreme Court held that an employer’s perception of an employee’s disability, even if unfounded, constitutes actionable discrimination under the MHRA. *St. Louis v. H. L.*, 358 Mo. at 1090. The court emphasized that “the employer’s belief, not the employee’s actual condition, determines whether the employee is regarded as disabled.” *St. Louis v. H. L.*, 358 Mo. at 1090. Here, Pine Lawn’s repeated reliance on Dr. Mirkin’s opinion that Epps would need to seek a different occupation, coupled with its refusal to consider alternative assignments, demonstrates that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled under the MHRA.

The district court erred in concluding that Epps could not establish that Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. The record contains ample evidence that Pine Lawn acted on the basis of a perception that Epps was substantially limited in his ability to work. *Bragg*, 334 U.S. at 127; *St. Louis v. H. L.*, 358 Mo. at 1090. Summary judgment was inappropriate.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MISSOURI’S SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY BARRED EPPS’S RETALIATION AND EMOTIONAL DISTRESS CLAIMS.

Missouri’s sovereign immunity does not bar claims for retaliation under Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780. *State ex rel. R. v. City of Kansas City*, 357 Mo. 1077, 1082 (1954). In *R. v. City of Kansas City*, the Missouri Supreme Court held that “a municipality may be sued for damages for violation of a statute enacted for the protection of the public.” *R. v. City of Kansas City*, 357 Mo. at 1082. Section 287.780 is such a statute, designed to protect employees from retaliation for filing workers’ compensation claims. *R. v. City of Kansas City*, 357 Mo. at 1082. The court further held that “the state’s immunity does not extend to actions brought under statutes that are remedial in nature and intended to protect individual rights.” *R. v. City of Kansas City*, 357 Mo. at 1082.
