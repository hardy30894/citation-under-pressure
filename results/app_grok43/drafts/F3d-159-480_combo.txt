A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Congress lacked power to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951. The statute reaches robbery that occurs wholly within a single State and that bears at most an indirect relation to interstate commerce. Such legislation exceeds the authority granted by the Commerce Clause.

The power to regulate commerce “among the several States” extends only to commercial intercourse that is national in character. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 189-190 (1824). It does not reach “internal” activities whose effects remain local. Id. at 194-195. Manufacturing, mining, and retail operations conducted inside one State are not interstate commerce, even when the goods later cross state lines. United States v. E.C. Knight Co., 156 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1895); Kidd v. Pearson, 128 U.S. 1, 20-21 (1888). A criminal act committed during such an operation therefore lies outside federal regulatory authority. Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 303-304 (1936).

Federal power may not be invoked merely because an intrastate event might reduce the volume of goods later shipped across state lines. A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 548-549 (1935); Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad Co., 295 U.S. 330, 368-369 (1935). The same limitation applies to attempted regulation of local violence. Hammer v. Dagenhart, 247 U.S. 251, 271-272 (1918). Congress cannot convert an ordinary robbery into an interstate offense by declaring that the proceeds might have been used to purchase out-of-state supplies. Paul v. Virginia, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 168, 183 (1868); Coe v. Town of Errol, 116 U.S. 517, 527-528 (1886).

Because § 1951 criminalizes conduct that the Commerce Clause does not reach, the statute is void. The conviction resting upon it must be reversed.
