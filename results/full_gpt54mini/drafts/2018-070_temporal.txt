The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not invite the wholesale importation of state law whenever a state rule concerns the same subject matter as a federal rule. It adopts adjacent state law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That text makes state law surrogate federal law only when federal law is silent on the point. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach, under which state law applies unless it affirmatively conflicts with federal law, cannot be squared with the Act’s structure, purpose, or this Court’s decisions.

OCSLA was enacted to create a federal body of law for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf, not to subject them to a patchwork of state regulation. Congress provided that the Constitution and laws of the United States extend to those structures “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). The point was to supply a comprehensive federal regime for a unique federal domain. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969). But Congress also recognized that federal law would not resolve every local incident arising on fixed platforms, so it borrowed adjacent state law only as surrogate federal law, and only where “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The statute’s built-in limitation would be unnecessary if state law automatically applied whenever it was merely compatible with federal law.

Rodrigue confirms that OCSLA fills federal enclaves with state law only when federal law does not already govern the point at issue. In Rodrigue, the Court explained that the Act was designed to replace maritime law as the principal source of rules for fixed platforms and to assimilate state law as federal law for those structures. 395 U.S. at 355-66. The Court emphasized that OCSLA’s purpose was “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” Id. at 355. Because the platforms were to be treated like “artificial islands” rather than vessels, federal maritime law did not apply of its own force, and state wrongful death law was incorporated instead. Id. at 366. Rodrigue thus illustrates OCSLA’s gap-filling function: state law is borrowed where federal law does not itself supply the rule.

That understanding is reinforced by the Court’s treatment of federal enclaves. In James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), the Court held that state law continues in force in a federal enclave only so far as it is not displaced by federal law. Id. at 99-100. The enclave doctrine assimilates local law to avoid a legal vacuum; it does not authorize dual regulation where Congress has already spoken. See id. at 100-01. OCSLA is structured the same way. It declares adjacent state law to be federal law only “to the extent” it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That language makes sense only if federal law’s coverage matters first, and state law fills the remaining gap.

The government and respondent rely on the idea that state law applies whenever it does not conflict with federal law. But that formulation reads “applicable” out of the statute. Congress did not say state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent.” It said both that state law must be “applicable” and that it must be “not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The ordinary meaning of “applicable” requires that the state rule fit the federal gap. See In re Neagle, 135 U.S. 1, 75-76 (1890) (recognizing that statutory terms should be given operative effect, not rendered surplusage). If federal law already regulates the issue, a contrary state rule is not “applicable” as surrogate federal law, even if it is not directly inconsistent.

That reading also accords with the Constitution’s command that federal law be supreme. Where Congress has occupied the field for a federal enclave, state law cannot supplement federal regulation simply because it is nonconflicting. See Sadrakula, 309 U.S. at 100-01. OCSLA borrows state law as federal law only as a means of filling “gaps” in the federal regime. Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 366. Once Congress has enacted a federal rule governing a subject, there is no gap to fill.

Here, the relevant subject is wages and meal periods for OCS workers. Congress has enacted a federal wage-and-hour code in the Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. Whether that statute is more or less protective than California law is beside the point. The question under OCSLA is whether federal law already provides the governing rule. If it does, state law is not assimilated merely because it is consistent. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule would convert OCSLA into an open-ended choice-of-law statute, allowing adjacent States to regulate whenever they offer more or different protections than Congress chose to supply.

This Court should reject that approach and hold that OCSLA permits the application of state law only when there is a genuine gap in federal law. That is the only reading faithful to the text of § 1333(a)(2)(A), to Rodrigue, and to the federal enclave principles on which OCSLA rests. Because federal law governs the wage-and-hour issues here, California law was not assimilated as surrogate federal law, and the judgment below should be reversed.
