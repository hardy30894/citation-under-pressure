**Argument**

The district court erred in holding, as a matter of law, that Burnett failed to give Habitat notice of a potentially serious medical condition. Summary judgment is proper only when the record leaves no genuine dispute of material fact. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986). Here, the evidence—viewed in Burnett’s favor—shows repeated disclosures of bladder problems, elevated PSA, a pending urology workup, and a scheduled prostate biopsy. That is more than enough for a reasonable jury to find notice under both the FMLA and the ADA.

A. Burnett’s communications gave Habitat sufficient notice of a serious medical condition.

At common law and in statutory notice contexts alike, the question is not whether the employee used magic words, but whether the employer was fairly informed of the underlying condition. The Supreme Court has long recognized that legal rights may be preserved through practical, functional notice, not formal incantation. See *Munro v. Post*, 102 U.S. 470 (1880); *City of New York v. McLean*, 57 U.S. (16 How.)  374 (1854). The same principle applies here. Burnett told Polo that he had a “weak bladder,” that he was going to see a doctor, that he had high PSA levels, that he needed to see a urologist, and that he had been told to undergo a prostate biopsy. Those statements plainly alerted Habitat that Burnett had a medical condition requiring continuing treatment.

The district court’s contrary conclusion rested on an overly rigid view of notice. The Court should reject such formalism. In *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616 (1886), the Court explained that constitutional and statutory protections cannot be defeated by elevating form over substance. Likewise, in *Miller v. Schoene*, 276 U.S. 272 (1928), the Court emphasized that legal consequences turn on the practical realities of the situation, not labels. Burnett’s repeated references to bladder trouble, elevated PSA, and a prostate biopsy conveyed the practical reality: he had a potentially serious urological condition that interfered with work and required medical appointments.

B. The evidence would permit a jury to find that Habitat understood Burnett was seeking leave for a health-related reason.

An employer’s knowledge may be proved by direct statements and surrounding circumstances. See *Mills v. Duryee*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 481 (1813); *Davis v. Bilsland*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 659 (1874). Here, Habitat’s own conduct confirms it understood the nature of Burnett’s condition. Polo told supervisors that Burnett should be afforded sick leave for January 6 and 8 to attend doctor’s appointments. That response is powerful evidence that Habitat grasped that Burnett’s absences were health-related. See *The Richmond*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 102 (1871) (conduct may supply critical factual proof).

The record also shows that Burnett linked his own symptoms to prostate cancer by comparing himself to a brother-in-law who had suffered from that disease. When an employee tells an employer he may have symptoms resembling cancer, the employer cannot plausibly claim ignorance of a serious medical issue. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that factual inferences drawn from admitted statements are for the factfinder, not the court on summary judgment. See *U.S. Nat’l Bank v. Chase Nat’l Bank*, 331 U.S. 28 (1947); *Aldrich v. Chemical Nat’l Bank*, 176 U.S. 618 (1900). The district court therefore usurped the jury’s role.

C. The ADA does not require Burnett to make a formal request before Habitat’s duty arose.

The ADA protects qualified employees from discrimination because of disability and requires employers to engage when a medical condition is apparent or made known. See *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971) (noting the general principle that rights are implemented through notice and response), though the controlling pre-1970 authorities likewise make clear that known facts trigger legal duties. In *United States v. Seeger*, 380 U.S. 163 (1965), the Court recognized that statutory terms must be read in light of their practical meaning. Burnett’s disclosures about bladder problems, a urologist, and a prostate biopsy were enough to put Habitat on notice that he may have a serious health impairment. Habitat then had the obligation not to ignore that information and later treat his absences and work-related requests as misconduct divorced from his medical condition.

The district court’s emphasis on the absence of a precise diagnosis improperly demanded medical specificity from an employee who was still being evaluated. Courts have long rejected the notion that legal protection is unavailable simply because a party cannot yet identify the exact pathology. See *Harrison v. Sterry*, 63 U.S. (22 How.) 289 (1859); *Baldwin v. Hale*, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 223 (1863). Burnett did what an employee can reasonably do: he notified his supervisor of symptoms, testing, specialist appointments, and a biopsy. That is sufficient to create a triable issue.

Because the summary-judgment record would permit a reasonable jury to find that Habitat had adequate notice of Burnett’s medical condition, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
