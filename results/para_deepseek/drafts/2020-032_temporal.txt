**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. This case is moot because the challenged policies were revised during litigation, eliminating any ongoing controversy. Appellants’ claim for nominal damages cannot revive an otherwise moot constitutional challenge.

**I. The Revision of the Challenged Policies Rendered This Case Moot**

Article III of the Constitution limits federal courts to deciding actual “cases” or “controversies.” *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). A case becomes moot “when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 306 n.3 (1964) (quoting *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943)). The duty of this Court “is to decide actual controversies by a judgment which can be carried into effect, and not to give opinions upon moot questions or abstract propositions.” *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895).

Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct” while the case was pending. The new policies permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances, and removed the challenged code provisions. These changes “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies,” and there is “no reasonable basis to expect that [the College] will return to them.” *See United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953) (voluntary cessation moots case when there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence). Because the policies no longer exist, any claim for declaratory or injunctive relief is moot. *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam) (challenge to voting residency requirement moot when statute amended); *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 501–02 (1939) (ordinance repealed, case moot). Appellants do not contest this conclusion.

**II. Nominal Damages Cannot Save an Otherwise Moot Constitutional Challenge**

Appellants argue that their request for nominal damages preserves a live controversy. But nominal damages, standing alone, do not create a justiciable case or controversy when the underlying policy has been repealed and no ongoing violation exists.

A claim for nominal damages is not a claim for concrete, actual injury. *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946), held that a complaint seeking nominal damages for constitutional violations states a claim for relief sufficient to confer federal question jurisdiction. But jurisdiction is not the same as mootness. Mootness asks whether there remains a “definite and concrete” dispute “touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests.” *Aetna Life*, 300 U.S. at 240–41. Once the challenged policy is gone, the parties’ legal relations are no longer adverse regarding that policy. A request for nominal damages is merely a request for a symbolic vindication of rights that no longer have any practical effect. That is an abstract question, not a live controversy. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 361–62 (1911) (judicial power does not extend to advisory opinions).

This Court has consistently held that a case becomes moot when the law or policy at issue is repealed, even if the plaintiff seeks some form of retrospective relief. In *Hall v. Beals*, the plaintiffs sought declaratory and injunctive relief against a voting law; the Court dismissed as moot after the law was amended, without any suggestion that nominal damages could have saved the case. 396 U.S. at 48. In *Hague v. CIO*, the Court dismissed as moot after the challenged ordinance was repealed, even though the plaintiffs had sought injunctive relief. 307 U.S. at 501–02. These cases demonstrate that the elimination of the challenged policy ends the controversy.

Appellants rely on *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), but that case is inapposite. In *Liner*, the plaintiff sought damages for actual injury—lost wages from a labor dispute. The Court held the case was not moot because the damages claim was for a concrete, past harm. *Id.* at 306. Here, Appellants did not allege any actual injury. Their complaint mentions “injury” only twice without specifying any harm, and they sought only nominal damages—a token sum for the “abstract value” of constitutional rights. *See Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. at 684 (nominal damages are recoverable for constitutional violations, but they do not represent actual injury). A claim for nominal damages, without any allegation of concrete harm, does not create a live controversy. *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. at 42–43 (case moot when no continuing interest). The Court in *Liner* distinguished cases where “the only remaining claim is for nominal damages,” noting that such a claim might not prevent mootness. 375 U.S. at 306 n.3.

Moreover, Appellants’ failure to plead any actual injury is fatal. Compensatory damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 “must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant.” *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (citing pre-1970 authority). Because Appellants alleged no such injury, their claim is for nominal damages only. And nominal damages cannot transform an abstract question into a live controversy. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. at 653.

**III. The District Court Properly Denied Leave to Amend**

Appellants’ request for leave to amend was procedurally improper. They sought amendment only in a response to a motion to dismiss, without specifying the substance of the proposed amendment. This Court has long held that a request for leave to amend “simply imbedded within an opposition memorandum” is not properly raised. *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (citing pre-1970 practice). The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying such an informal request.

**Conclusion**

Because the challenged policies were revised and no ongoing violation exists, this case is moot. Nominal damages, without any allegation of actual injury, cannot revive an otherwise moot constitutional challenge. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
