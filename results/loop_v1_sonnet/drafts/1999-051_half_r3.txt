**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedural Default That Is Not the Product of the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Bar Reliance on the Underlying Constitutional Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

This Court has long recognized that the writ of habeas corpus is not to be shackled by mechanical procedural rules where a petitioner's own conduct did not produce the default. In *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266 (1948), the Court emphasized that habeas corpus is an equitable remedy whose "historic purpose" is to guard against unlawful detention, and that its procedures must be flexible enough to serve that end rather than becoming an instrument of forfeiture through no fault of the prisoner himself. *Id.* at 269. The touchstone of that flexible approach is the petitioner's own considered choice to forgo a remedy—not the tactical or negligent omissions of counsel. Carpenter did not himself choose to abandon his sufficiency claim; his appellate lawyer did. Under *Price*, that distinction is decisive.

This principle rests on the settled understanding, articulated in *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), that a waiver of a fundamental constitutional right must be a knowing and intelligent act of the accused himself, and that courts will not lightly presume such a waiver from a silent or ambiguous record. *Id.* at 723–24. A defendant cannot be said to have relinquished a constitutional claim merely because appointed counsel failed to raise it. Attributing counsel's default to the petitioner as though it were his own considered waiver inverts the very premise of *Von Moltke*—that fundamental rights are surrendered only by the client's own knowing act, not by counsel's oversight.

**The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a mere procedural nicety; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. This Court held in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), that "any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him. This seems to us to be an obvious truth." *Id.* at 344.

The following Term, this Court extended that guarantee to the first appeal as of right. *See Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353, 357–58 (1963) (holding that a State may not condition the vindication of federal rights on the fortuity of retained counsel while indigent appellants go unrepresented). Carpenter's claim—that his appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the kind of constitutional deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A default of that claim, itself born of the same disabling failure of counsel, should not extinguish its capacity to explain and excuse the companion default it caused.

**Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require an Independently Exhausted Cause Claim Where the Default Flows from Counsel's Own Failure**

The exhaustion requirement exists, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), to give state courts the first opportunity to consider and correct alleged violations of a prisoner's federal constitutional rights before a federal court will entertain a habeas corpus application. *Id.* at 116–17. It is a rule of comity, not a rule of forfeiture. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise grounded the exhaustion doctrine in considerations of comity between the state and federal sovereignties, allowing state courts the first opportunity to correct their own asserted errors before federal intervention. *Id.* at 203–04. Ohio had every opportunity to correct counsel's failure when Carpenter sought reopening of his appeal; the state court declined only on a timeliness ground unrelated to the merits. Nothing in the comity rationale of *Hawk* or *Darr* requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself first survive a second, independent gauntlet of exhaustion before it may serve its traditional function of explaining a related default.

**This Court's Broader Habeas Corpus Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Rules**

The great writ has always been treated with a flexibility that guards against the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), this Court recognized that where a state proceeding is so fundamentally deficient that it amounts to no real trial at all, federal courts sitting in habeas retain the power and the duty to inquire into the substance of the constitutional violation notwithstanding the formal posture of the state proceedings. *Id.* at 91–92.

This Court reaffirmed that flexible approach in *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), cautioning that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake and infringement of constitutional rights is alleged." *Id.* at 8. The *Sanders* Court further explained that such bars must be applied with equitable discretion rather than rigid formalism. *Id.* at 17–18.

Separately, in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963), this Court confirmed that federal courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims presented in habeas rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive of the underlying federal right.

Taken together, *Price*, *Von Moltke*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Townsend* establish a consistent principle: procedural defaults attributable to counsel's own failings, rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice, should not be permitted to compound themselves into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient constitutional substance to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
