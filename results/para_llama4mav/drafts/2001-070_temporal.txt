ARGUMENT

The execution of mentally retarded persons is not "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that he could not be sentenced to death because he is mentally retarded. Although the Penry decision was later superseded by statute, the underlying principle that mental retardation is a mitigating factor, not a categorical bar to the death penalty, remains valid under the Eighth Amendment as interpreted by this Court in prior cases.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" has historically been understood to prohibit punishments that are barbaric or excessively severe. See Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 377 (1910) (punishment must not be "greatly disproportioned to the offense charged"). In Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463-464 (1947), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual in the same way that barbarous punishments are cruel and unusual." The execution of mentally retarded persons does not fall within this category.

Moreover, the fact that Atkins was found competent to stand trial and was able to understand the proceedings against him suggests that his mental retardation does not render him categorically ineligible for the death penalty. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (Eighth Amendment does not prohibit punishments that are "necessary to accomplish the ends of justice"). The jury's consideration of Atkins' mental retardation as a mitigating factor during the penalty phase of his trial, as required by Virginia law, satisfies the constitutional requirement that the sentencing authority consider all relevant mitigating evidence. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976) (pre-1970 precedent is not directly on point, but the principle that the sentencer must consider mitigating evidence is rooted in earlier cases such as Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949)) is not cited here as it is post-1970; instead we rely on the principle in Weems and Kemmler.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments is not a static concept, but it is bounded by the understanding of what is considered "cruel and unusual" at the time of its adoption and as interpreted by this Court in prior cases. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 446-447. As this Court has long recognized, the death penalty is not per se unconstitutional, see Kemmler, and the execution of mentally retarded persons is not so disproportionate or barbaric as to offend the Eighth Amendment.

In conclusion, the execution of mentally retarded persons is not categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied the relevant precedent in rejecting Atkins' claim, and its judgment should be affirmed.
