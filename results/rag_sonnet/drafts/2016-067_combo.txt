# ARGUMENT

**Santander Is Not a “Debt Collector” Because It Collected Debts Owed to Itself**

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that a person who purchases debt and thereafter collects it for its own account acts as a creditor, not a debt collector, regardless of the default status of the debt at the time of purchase. That conclusion rests not only on the text of the FDCPA but on a distinction long recognized at common law: the difference between a person who collects an obligation owed to himself and a person who collects on behalf of another.

**A. The common law has always distinguished a creditor collecting his own debt from an agent collecting for another’s account.**

This Court’s decisions have repeatedly drawn the line petitioners ask this Court to erase. In *Western Tie & Timber Co. v. Brown*, 196 U.S. 502 (1905), the Court examined whether a company that deducted wages owed to a third party was collecting for its own account or for another’s benefit, concluding that the company was “under an obligation to remit the money collected from the laborers for account of Harrison to him, irrespective of any debt which he might owe.” 196 U.S. at 502. The critical inquiry was not the character of the underlying obligation, but for whose account the collection was undertaken.

Similarly, in *Selden v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 94 U.S. 419 (1877), the Court distinguished entities that advance money on behalf of others from those that transact business with their own capital, observing that “all the loans the company makes are investments of its own capital in mortgage securities on real estate.” 94 U.S. at 419. A company investing its own capital and collecting on the resulting obligations is not acting as an intermediary for another; it is collecting its own debt. The same is true of Santander here: once it purchased petitioners’ loans outright, it was no longer servicing debt “for another” but investing and collecting on its own account, just as the trust company in *Selden*.

*Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick*, 43 U.S. 202 (1844), likewise recognized that persons who act “for others,” such as a factor “who sells for others,” occupy a fundamentally different position than one who holds and collects obligations belonging to himself. 43 U.S. at 202. And in *Nixdorff v. Smith*, 41 U.S. 132 (1842), this Court recognized an arrangement whereby one firm’s debts—“all the debts owing to Nixdorff and Hager”—were to “be collected by Hager and Smith,” an arrangement in which the collecting party acted as agent, not owner, of the receivables. 41 U.S. at 132. Petitioners’ theory would collapse this longstanding distinction by treating every purchaser of a defaulted obligation as though it remained an agent collecting for the original creditor, when in fact, as in *Nixdorff*, the ownership of the debt—not its origin—determines the collector’s legal character.

**B. Ownership of the debt, not the default status at acquisition, determines creditor status.**

Petitioners’ rule asks this Court to hold that the timing of default, rather than the identity of the party for whose benefit collection occurs, controls whether an entity is a “creditor” or “debt collector.” But this Court has consistently held that a creditor is simply the person to whom a debt is owed, without regard to how or when that person’s interest arose. In *Ivanhoe Building & Loan Assn. v. Orr*, 295 U.S. 243 (1935), the Court referred without qualification to a party holding a bond and mortgage as “a creditor of a bankrupt,” 295 U.S. at 243, based solely on the fact that the debt was owed to it — not on when or how the creditor’s interest in the debt was acquired. And in *United States v. Erie Railway Co.*, 107 U.S. 1 (1883), the Court characterized an action to recover an obligation owed directly to the plaintiff as “a suit for the recovery of that debt as a debt,” 107 U.S. at 1, underscoring that the plaintiff’s status as the party owed the debt, not the debt’s history, defined the nature of the collection.

The same principle is illustrated in *Wilson v. Edmonds*, 130 U.S. 472 (1889), where the defendant, having advanced funds and taken notes directly, thereafter collected on his own loans as owner of the resulting obligations, notwithstanding that the underlying debts had passed through various hands and renewals. 130 U.S. at 472. As in *Wilson*, Santander’s purchase of petitioners’ defaulted loans made it the owner of those obligations; its subsequent collection efforts were undertaken, like the lender’s in *Wilson*, on its own account.

Finally, this Court’s recognition of setoff principles in *Libby v. Hopkins*, 104 U.S. 303 (1881), confirms that once mutual debts exist directly between two parties, “one debt shall be set against another,” 104 U.S. at 303 — a doctrine that presupposes the debt is owed directly to the collecting party, not merely serviced on behalf of a third party.

**C. Petitioners’ rule would sweep ordinary creditors into a statute designed to regulate collection agents, not owners of debt.**

Adopting petitioners’ default-timing rule would transform every purchaser of distressed receivables—no different from the tie company in *Western Tie & Timber* or the lender in *Wilson*—into a regulated “debt collector” merely because the obligations changed hands after default. Nothing in this Court’s precedents supports treating an owner’s collection of its own property as equivalent to an agent’s collection for another. The judgment below should be affirmed.
