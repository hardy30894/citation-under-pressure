ARGUMENT

I. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies the Sixth Amendment.

Ring v. Arizona held that, “[b]ecause Arizona’s enumerated aggravating factors operate as ‘the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,’ the Sixth Amendment requires that they be found by a jury.” 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002) (quoting Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)). Florida’s statute, however, assigns the jury the role of rendering an advisory verdict on whether aggravating circumstances exist and whether those circumstances outweigh mitigating ones. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2)–(3) (2012). The trial judge then independently determines the sentence. This division of labor is consistent with the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s findings are necessary predicates to any death sentence. See Hildwin v. Florida, 490 U.S. 638, 640–41 (1989) (per curiam) (jury’s advisory recommendation satisfies constitutional fact-finding requirement).

Ring does not mandate that the jury’s determination be unanimous. The Sixth Amendment’s jury-trial guarantee incorporates no unanimity requirement in state prosecutions. See Apodaca v. Oregon, 406 U.S. 404, 411–12 (1972) (plurality opinion). Florida’s practice of permitting a non-unanimous advisory verdict therefore comports with settled precedent.

The same analysis governs mental retardation. Atkins v. Virginia left “to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon [their] execution of sentences.” 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002) (quoting Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 416–17 (1986)). Nothing in Atkins or Ring requires that the factual question of intellectual disability be submitted to a jury, much less decided unanimously. Florida’s provision for a judicial determination, Fla. Stat. § 921.137, satisfies the Eighth Amendment floor recognized in Atkins.

II. The Eighth Amendment imposes no additional requirement of jury unanimity or jury adjudication of mental retardation.

The Eighth Amendment forbids only those practices that are both cruel and unusual. See Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957, 994–95 (1991). At the time of the Framing, capital sentencing was routinely entrusted to judges or non-unanimous juries. See Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 463–64 (1984). Contemporary practice confirms that Florida’s scheme is not unusual: although most States now require unanimous jury findings, the Eighth Amendment does not demand nationwide conformity. See Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 370–71 (1989), abrogated on other grounds by Roper v. Simmons, 543 U.S. 551 (2005). Because Florida’s procedures are neither arbitrary nor contrary to “evolving standards of decency,” they withstand Eighth Amendment scrutiny. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion).

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
