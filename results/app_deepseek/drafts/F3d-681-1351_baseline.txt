ARGUMENT

The Board erred in holding that Harari’s ’880 application is entitled to the benefit of the April 13, 1989 filing date of the ’566 application. The Board misinterpreted this Court’s prior decision in *Harari v. Hollmer*, 602 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (“*Harari I*”), and applied the wrong legal standard to the intervening applications in the priority chain. Because the incorporation-by-reference language in the ’838 and ’768 applications is ambiguous and was never clarified at the time of their filing, those applications do not satisfy the continuity requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 120. The Board’s contrary conclusion must be reversed.

**A. The Board Applied the Wrong Standard to the Intervening Applications**

In *Harari I*, this Court articulated a narrow rule for evaluating ambiguous incorporation statements “at the initial filing stage” of an application. *Id.* at 1352-53. The Court held that the proper inquiry is “whether the identity of the incorporated reference is clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented.” *Id.* at 1353. Critically, that standard was applied only to the ’880 application itself, where the transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment provided the necessary clarification. *Id.* at 1353-54. The Court did not hold that the same standard applies retroactively to earlier applications in the priority chain that were filed without any such clarifying documents.

On remand, the Board extended *Harari I*’s holding to the ’838 and ’768 applications, concluding that a reasonable examiner reviewing those applications would understand the incorporation statement to refer to the ’579 application. That was error. The incorporation statement in the ’566 application—copied verbatim into the ’838 and ’768 applications—states that the ’579 application was “filed on the same day as the present application.” J.A. 1204. But the “present application” in the ’838 and ’768 applications is not the ’566 application; it is the application being filed at that time. The ’579 application was not filed on the same day as the ’838 or ’768 applications. The statement is therefore facially inaccurate and ambiguous when read in the context of those later filings.

Unlike the ’880 application, the ’838 and ’768 applications were not accompanied by any transmittal sheet or preliminary amendment that could resolve the ambiguity. The Board’s reliance on the fact that an examiner could “figure out” the intended reference by reviewing the entire priority chain is precisely the kind of speculation that *Harari I* rejected. *See Harari I*, 602 F.3d at 1352 (criticizing the Board for “fill[ing] in the gaps” in an ambiguous incorporation statement). The proper standard requires that the identity of the incorporated reference be clear from the documents presented *at the time of filing*. *See Advanced Display Sys., Inc. v. Kent State Univ.*, 212 F.3d 1272, 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2000) (en banc) (incorporation by reference must be “clear and explicit” to satisfy the written description requirement). The Board’s after-the-fact reconstruction does not meet that standard.

**B. The Intervening Applications Fail to Satisfy § 120’s Continuity Requirements**

Under 35 U.S.C. § 120, a later-filed application is entitled to the benefit of an earlier filing date only if the earlier application “discloses the invention in the manner provided by the first paragraph of section 112.” That requirement includes adequate written description, which in turn requires that any incorporated material be clearly identified. *See Advanced Display*, 212 F.3d at 1282. Because the incorporation statement in the ’838 and ’768 applications is ambiguous, those applications do not effectively incorporate the ’579 application. As a result, the ’579 application’s disclosure is not part of the written description of the intervening applications, and the priority chain is broken.

The ’880 application is undisputedly entitled only to the filing date of the ’708 application (December 20, 1996), which clearly discloses the ’579 application. That date is later than Hollmer’s date of conception for the ’601 patent. The Board’s contrary finding that the ’880 application can claim the 1989 filing date of the ’566 application is therefore unsupported by the law and the record.

**C. The Board’s Decision Conflicts with the Purpose of the Written Description Requirement**

The written description requirement ensures that an applicant “convey[s] with reasonable clarity to those skilled in the art that, as of the filing date sought, he or she was in possession of the invention.” *Vas-Cath Inc. v. Mahurkar*, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563-64 (Fed. Cir. 1991). Allowing an applicant to cure an ambiguous incorporation statement years later, through documents filed in a subsequent application, undermines this purpose. The Board’s approach would permit applicants to retroactively fix defective priority chains, contrary to the statutory scheme. This Court should reject that result and hold that the ’880 application is not entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Board’s decision should be reversed, and judgment should be entered in favor of Hollmer.
