Respondent should prevail because the Internal Revenue Code extends the collection period only when “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the limitations period, 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), and an assessment must identify the taxpayer whose liability is being recorded. The partnership here was assessed; the individual partners were not. That is fatal to the government’s claim.

The Code treats a partnership and its partners as distinct taxpayers. A “taxpayer” is “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes both “an individual” and “a partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). The Supreme Court has long recognized that tax enforcement turns on the statutory identity of the assessed taxpayer. In Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282 (1929), the Court held that tax liabilities must be pursued in the manner Congress prescribed, not by administrative shortcut. Likewise, in Graham v. Goodcell, 282 U.S. 409 (1931), the Court emphasized that statutory tax procedures are to be strictly applied, especially where they define the government’s power to collect. And in Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88 (2004), the Court reiterated the importance of the Internal Revenue Code’s structural limits on federal tax enforcement, confirming that the government’s authority is wholly statutory.

The government’s position also conflicts with the settled rule that an assessment is the formal act that fixes the taxpayer and starts the collection period. In United States v. John Barth Co., 279 U.S. 370 (1929), the Court described assessment as the event that authorizes collection measures. In Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), the Court explained that taxes are collected through the specific mechanisms Congress created, not through broad equitable notions of liability. And in United States v. Rindskopf, 105 U.S. 418 (1881), the Court treated assessment as a distinct and necessary step in tax enforcement. Those cases defeat the notion that an assessment against one taxpayer automatically becomes an assessment against another.

The Court’s decisions on tax liens point the same way. In Price v. United States, 269 U.S. 492 (1926), the Court held that a federal tax lien depends on compliance with the statutory conditions that create it. In Glass City Bank v. United States, 326 U.S. 265 (1945), the Court again recognized that federal tax collection rights arise only upon the statutory predicates Congress imposed. Those decisions underscore that the IRS cannot enlarge a partnership assessment into an assessment against separate individuals without a new statutory act.

Nor does state partnership law save the government. A federal tax assessment is a federal act governed by federal law. In United States v. Mitchell, 403 U.S. 190 (1971), the Court held that federal tax consequences turn on federal law, not merely on state-law characterizations. Earlier cases say the same. In Poe v. Seaborn, 282 U.S. 101 (1930), the Court allowed state property law to influence federal tax treatment only because Congress had not supplied a contrary rule; here Congress did supply one by requiring assessment of the taxpayer. And in Burnet v. Harmel, 287 U.S. 103 (1932), the Court made clear that federal tax statutes control federal tax consequences even where state law defines underlying rights. Thus, California partnership law cannot substitute for the federal assessment Congress required.

The government’s reliance on the partners’ substantive liability is misplaced for one more reason: substantive liability is not the same as enforceable collection authority. In Flint v. Stone Tracy Co., 220 U.S. 107 (1911), the Court distinguished tax incidence from the mechanism of collection. In Eisner v. Macomber, 252 U.S. 189 (1920), the Court similarly recognized that tax statutes impose precise legal consequences, not free-floating obligations. Here the IRS may well be able to show that partners are liable in some substantive sense, but without an individual assessment or timely judgment, the agency cannot collect from them after the three-year period has expired.

Finally, this Court has repeatedly rejected efforts to treat separate parties as interchangeable where Congress has required individualized treatment. See Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926); United States v. Stowell, 133 U.S. 1 (1890). The same principle controls here. Because the IRS never assessed Abel Galletti or the other individual partners, § 6502(a)’s 10-year extension never began to run as to them. The claim is time-barred, and the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed it under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).
