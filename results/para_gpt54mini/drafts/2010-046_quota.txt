California’s challenge fails because the Prison Litigation Reform Act does not forbid a population-reduction order when that order is the necessary means to remedy proven Eighth Amendment violations. Congress limited, but did not eliminate, federal courts’ traditional equitable power to cure unconstitutional prison conditions. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), (a)(3); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12 (2011). The order here fits squarely within the PLRA’s text and this Court’s precedents.

First, the record supports the finding that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations and that no less intrusive remedy would suffice. Under the PLRA, a prisoner release order may issue only after a court has “entered an order for less intrusive relief” that “has failed to remedy the deprivation” and after the court gives “substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). This Court has made clear that remedial deference does not require abdication: once a constitutional violation is established, the federal court may order the relief necessary to cure it. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978); Milliken v. Bradley, 433 U.S. 267, 280-81 (1977); Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971). The district court’s order followed exactly that path. It was entered only after years of lesser remedies failed, repeated findings of persistent violations, and a factual determination that overcrowding drove the inability to provide constitutionally adequate care.

Second, the PLRA expressly authorizes a “prisoner release order” when the statutory predicates are met, and the statute does not permit courts to ignore the reality that population reduction may be the only effective remedy. In Brown v. Plata, this Court held that the PLRA “allows a district court to issue a prisoner release order” when the statute’s stringent requirements are satisfied, and that “[p]risoners retain the essence of human dignity inherent in all persons.” 563 U.S. at 511, 520. The Court further explained that where overcrowding creates unconstitutional medical and mental-health conditions, population reduction can be a proper remedy notwithstanding its institutional consequences. Id. at 525-28. That reasoning controls here. The order is not an open-ended intrusion into prison administration; it is a tightly cabined remedial measure designed to bring conditions within constitutional bounds.

Third, California’s argument that the order exceeds federal remedial authority ignores the long-established principle that equitable relief must be broad enough to restore constitutional compliance. In Swann, this Court held that once a constitutional violation is shown, courts have “broad power to fashion a remedy that will assure a unitary school system.” 402 U.S. at 15. In Milliken, the Court confirmed that a remedy may be tailored to “eliminate the condition that offends the Constitution.” 433 U.S. at 282. And in Hutto, the Court approved population-related remedies in the prison context because courts need not “accept remedial measures that are wholly ineffective.” 437 U.S. at 687-88. The same principle applies here: if overcrowding is the source of unconstitutional deprivation, a reduction in population is not an abuse of power but an application of it.

Fourth, California’s reliance on federalism principles does not change the result. Although prison administration is ordinarily a state function, federal courts must enforce the Constitution when a state system inflicts cruel and unusual punishment. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 511-12; Ruiz v. Estelle, 679 F.2d 1115, 1145-46 (5th Cir. 1982); Gates v. Collier, 501 F.2d 1291, 1320-21 (5th Cir. 1974). The PLRA reflects that balance by requiring narrow tailoring, public-safety findings, and findings that prior relief failed. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), (a)(3); Benjamin v. Fraser, 343 F.3d 35, 52-53 (2d Cir. 2003). Those safeguards were met here.

Finally, California’s suggestion that the order is invalid because it may require releases into the community mistakes the remedy for the violation. A court may not leave prisoners in unconstitutional conditions simply because the necessary cure is difficult or politically sensitive. See Hutto, 437 U.S. at 687; Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 527-28. The PLRA demands a remedy that is no broader than necessary, not one that is ineffective. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); Cason v. Seckinger, 231 F.3d 777, 784-85 (11th Cir. 2000).

The order below satisfies the PLRA because it was the product of repeated failures of lesser relief, was narrowly aimed at curing Eighth Amendment violations, and was entered with due regard for public safety. The judgment should be affirmed.
