ARGUMENT

I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION PRECLUDE STATES FROM IMPOSING TERM LIMITS ON MEMBERS OF CONGRESS

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in the U.S. House of Representatives and the U.S. Senate, respectively. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency, and it is well-established that these provisions are exhaustive.

In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), this Court held that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that Congress may not add to or alter the qualifications specified therein. The Court reasoned that the framers of the Constitution intentionally limited the qualifications for membership in Congress to those enumerated in the Constitution, and that any additional qualifications would be inconsistent with the constitutional design.

Similarly, in U.S. v. Smith, 286 U.S. 6 (1932), this Court observed that the Constitution "fixes the qualifications of members of Congress" and that "the power of Congress to prescribe additional qualifications is not supported by the Constitution." These precedents make clear that states, like Congress, are precluded from imposing additional qualifications, including term limits, on members of Congress.

The term limits provision in Amendment 73, Section 3, which prohibits individuals who have served a certain number of terms from appearing on the ballot for election to Congress, is an impermissible attempt to add to the qualifications specified in the Qualifications Clauses. By rendering certain individuals ineligible for election to Congress based on their prior service, Amendment 73 effectively imposes an additional qualification that is not authorized by the Constitution.

II. THE PROVISIONS OF AMENDMENT 73 ARE INSEVERABLE

Even if Section 3 of Amendment 73 is deemed unconstitutional, the remaining provisions of the Amendment are not severable. The test for severability is whether the measure would have been adopted without the unconstitutional provision. Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964).

In this case, the sponsors of Amendment 73 presented the term limits provision as a unified package, lumping together state and federal officials. The Preamble to Amendment 73 refers to "elected officials" generally, without distinguishing between state and federal officeholders. Section 6 of Amendment 73 states that the provisions of the Amendment shall be applicable to "the offices specified in this Amendment," which includes both state and federal offices.

Given the intertwined nature of the provisions, it is impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the provision limiting terms for federal officeholders. The presence of a severability clause is insufficient to save the remaining provisions, as the uncertainty created by the sponsors of Amendment 73 cannot be resolved through speculation.

The judgment of the circuit court should be affirmed, as Amendment 73 is unconstitutional in its entirety.
