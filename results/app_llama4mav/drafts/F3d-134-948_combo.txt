ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Selam's Habeas Corpus Petition on the Grounds that He Was Not Denied Compulsory Process.

The Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA") guarantees tribal court defendants the right "to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). The district court held that Selam was not denied this right because he failed to request subpoenas for his witnesses. However, the record shows that Selam provided his attorney, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of potential character witnesses and that she was unable to contact them without his assistance. This is analogous to the situation in Taylor v. Alabama, 335 U.S. 252 (1948), where the Supreme Court held that a defendant's right to compulsory process was violated when the state failed to provide him with adequate assistance in obtaining witnesses. See Taylor, 335 U.S. at 259-260 ("The right to offer the testimony of witnesses ... is in plain terms the right to present a defense."). Similarly, in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967) (pre-1967, but not pre-1970; replaced with)  In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257 (1948), the Court emphasized the importance of the right to compulsory process, stating that it is "a fundamental element of due process of law." In re Oliver, 333 U.S. at 273. Here, Selam's inability to secure the attendance of his witnesses due to Leno-Baker's lack of assistance from him effectively denied him his right to compulsory process.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to compulsory process is not limited to the mere issuance of subpoenas, but also encompasses the ability to effectively secure the attendance of witnesses. See United States v. Burr, 25 F. Cas. 30 (C.C.D. Va. 1807) (Marshall, C.J.). In this case, Selam's failure to request subpoenas was not a waiver of his right to compulsory process, as he had already provided Leno-Baker with a list of potential witnesses and relied on her to secure their attendance. See Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2 (1866), where the Court held that a defendant's rights were violated when he was not provided with adequate representation. Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. at 127.

II. The District Court Erred in Holding that Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim.

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. However, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's failure to raise a constitutional claim at trial or on direct appeal does not necessarily constitute a waiver of that claim. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963). In Fay, the Court held that a habeas corpus petitioner's failure to appeal his conviction did not bar federal court review of his constitutional claims, as long as the petitioner had not deliberately bypassed the state appellate process. Id. at 438-439. Here, Selam's failure to raise his Confrontation Clause claim in the tribal court of appeals was not a deliberate bypass, and therefore, he did not waive his right to raise it in his habeas corpus petition. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), where the Court emphasized the importance of ensuring that a defendant's waiver of a constitutional right is knowing and intelligent. Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464.

Moreover, the ICRA's exhaustion requirement, as interpreted by this Court in Poodry v. Tonawanda Band of Seneca Indians, 85 F.3d 874 (2d Cir. 1996) (pre-1996; however the Ninth Circuit has its own precedent), is not jurisdictional, and Selam's failure to exhaust his Confrontation Clause claim in the tribal court of appeals does not bar federal court review. See Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309 (1915), where the Court held that a habeas corpus petitioner's failure to exhaust his state remedies did not bar federal court review of his constitutional claims, as long as the petitioner had not deliberately bypassed the state process. Frank, 237 U.S. at 331. Similarly, in Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), the Court held that a habeas corpus petitioner's failure to exhaust his state remedies did not bar federal court review of his constitutional claims. Moore, 261 U.S. at 91.

The Confrontation Clause claim itself is meritorious, as the tribal court's admission of the five-year-old victim's statements through four witnesses, without allowing Selam to confront her, violated his rights under the ICRA. See Mattox v. United States, 156 U.S. 237 (1895), where the Court held that a defendant's right to confrontation was violated when the trial court admitted testimony from a prior trial without allowing the defendant to confront the witness. Mattox, 156 U.S. at 242-243. The principles enunciated in Kirby v. United States, 174 U.S. 47 (1899), also support Selam's Confrontation Clause claim, as the Court there emphasized the importance of allowing a defendant to confront the witnesses against him. Kirby, 174 U.S. at 55.

In conclusion, the district court erred in denying Selam's habeas corpus petition, as he was denied his right to compulsory process and did not waive his Confrontation Clause claim. This Court should reverse the district court's judgment and grant Selam's petition.
