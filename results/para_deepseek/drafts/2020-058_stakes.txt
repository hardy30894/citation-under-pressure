## ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) cannot constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The text, context, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the opposite conclusion: when the Secretary confers TPS, the alien receives a “lawful entry . . . after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer,” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A), and thus is “admitted” for purposes of adjustment of status.

### I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Requires Treating TPS as an Admission

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” The INA defines “nonimmigrant” as an alien who is “admitted” into the United States for a specific purpose. See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15). Every nonimmigrant must be inspected and admitted. By directing that TPS recipients be “considered” as being in nonimmigrant status, Congress necessarily required that they be treated as having been admitted. The process of obtaining TPS itself involves inspection and authorization: applicants must submit biometrics, undergo background checks, and receive affirmative approval from immigration authorities. That process satisfies the definition of “admission” in § 1101(a)(13)(A).

The Third Circuit’s rigid distinction between “admission” and “status” ignores that § 1254a(f)(4) operates as a legal fiction. As the Ninth Circuit explained in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), “by the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.” The statute does not merely grant a status; it deems the alien to have maintained lawful status since entry, which necessarily implies a lawful admission at the time of entry. The Sixth Circuit similarly held that TPS recipients “obtain a lawful entry when they are granted TPS.” *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F. App’x 340, 346 (6th Cir. 2017) (unpublished). These interpretations respect the statutory text; the Third Circuit’s does not.

### II. The Context of § 1255 Confirms That TPS Satisfies the Admission Requirement

Section 1255(a) requires an alien to have been “inspected and admitted or paroled.” Section 1255(c)(2) separately bars aliens who have failed to maintain lawful status. If admission and lawful status were entirely distinct, as the Third Circuit held, then § 1255(c)(2) would be superfluous for aliens who entered illegally—they could never satisfy § 1255(a) regardless of status. Congress instead structured § 1255 to allow aliens who obtain lawful status after entry (including through TPS) to meet the admission requirement through that status. The Third Circuit’s reading would make § 1255(c)(2) meaningless for TPS recipients, contrary to the canon that “no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

Moreover, Congress expressly provided exceptions to the admission requirement for certain aliens, see 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i), but did not similarly exclude TPS recipients. That silence does not imply exclusion; it reflects that TPS already satisfies the requirement through the legal fiction of § 1254a(f)(4). The Third Circuit’s reliance on the “disparate inclusion” canon from *I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987), is misplaced because that canon applies only when Congress has provided a clear alternative in the same statute. Here, the alternative is the very status that § 1254a(f)(4) creates.

### III. The Purpose of TPS Supports This Interpretation

TPS is a humanitarian program designed to shield aliens from removal during crises. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Congress intended TPS recipients to be able to “make use” of the adjustment process. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 961. Denying adjustment to those who entered illegally forces them to choose between remaining in the United States with temporary status (and never becoming permanent residents) or leaving the country and reentering lawfully—often impossible because their home countries remain unsafe. That result undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. The Third Circuit’s reliance on the supermajority requirement of § 1254a(h) is unavailing; that provision governs future legislative adjustments, not the interpretation of the existing statutory scheme.

### IV. The Third Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with Sister Circuits and the BIA

The Ninth and Sixth Circuits have held that TPS constitutes an admission. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960; *Flores*, 718 F. App’x at 346. The Board of Immigration Appeals has also recognized that adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission. *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). The Third Circuit’s contrary holding creates a circuit split that only this Court can resolve. It also improperly denies relief to TPS recipients who entered illegally, even though Congress intended them to have the same opportunity to adjust as those who entered lawfully.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the holding that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission under § 1255(a).
