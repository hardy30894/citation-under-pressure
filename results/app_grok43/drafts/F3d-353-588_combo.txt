ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Pine Lawn terminated Epps after he filed workers’ compensation claims and received treatment for work-related injuries. Such action rests on an arbitrary classification that denies equal protection. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada, 305 U.S. 337 (1938).

Epps’s injuries rendered him unable to perform patrol duties, yet the city treated his condition as disqualifying without individualized assessment. This classification, based solely on prior claims and medical notes, violates the guarantee of equal treatment under law. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497 (1954).

The record shows Chief Hardy recommended termination expressly because Epps “had filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims.” Retaliation for invoking statutory remedies contravenes the principle that employers may not punish employees for pursuing lawful claims. Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co., 323 U.S. 192 (1944); New York Cent. R.R. v. White, 243 U.S. 188 (1917).

Missouri’s sovereign-immunity statute does not shield a municipality from liability for such conduct. Municipal corporations remain subject to suit for violations of federal rights. Lincoln County v. Luning, 133 U.S. 529 (1890); Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890). Prospective relief against ongoing discriminatory termination is available. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908).

The intentional-infliction claim likewise survives. Conduct that deliberately inflicts severe emotional harm through retaliatory discharge is actionable. The district court’s dismissal on immunity grounds therefore cannot stand.

Reversal is required on all counts.
