# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Application of a Unitary "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard to Polling Is Not Rationally Related to the Purposes of the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not speak to employer polling; the practice is a creature of Board policy. This Court has long recognized that an employer confronted with objective evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support faces a genuine dilemma: it must choose between continuing to bargain with a union that may no longer represent its employees and risking liability for withdrawing recognition improvidently. See *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969) (discussing the tension between an employer's duty to bargain and its right to test a union's continuing majority status). Polling, no less than an RM election, serves the Act's core purpose of ensuring that bargaining representatives actually represent the employees for whom they purport to speak. *Cf. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (recognizing that a bargaining order is justified only where it reflects the wishes of employees).

The Board's decision to impose upon polling the identical evidentiary threshold required for outright withdrawal of recognition—rather than a lesser showing calibrated to the far less drastic character of a poll—cannot be reconciled with the Act's design. An agency's exercise of delegated authority must bear a reasonable relationship to the purposes of the statute it administers. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941). Here, the Board's own stated rationale—that RM elections are the "preferred" method of testing employee sentiment because they carry procedural safeguards absent from polling—points toward a *lower*, not identical, threshold for the less consequential act of polling. An agency's reasoning must be internally coherent, and this Court will not sustain agency action on grounds the agency itself did not articulate and adequately explain. *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 87, 94 (1943). Collapsing three distinct employer options—withdrawal, RM election, and polling—into one undifferentiated standard, without accounting for their obviously different risks to employees and to the collective-bargaining relationship, is precisely the sort of unreasoned equivalence *Chenery* forbids.

This Court has likewise cautioned that the Board's freedom to fashion policy is not boundless where it collides with employers' legitimate interests recognized elsewhere in the Act's structure. *See Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945) (Board must accommodate competing statutory interests rather than mechanically apply a single rule). The Board's remedial and procedural discretion, though broad, must still be exercised in a manner consistent with the Act's underlying purposes rather than administrative convenience alone. *NLRB v. Seven-Up Bottling Co.*, 344 U.S. 344, 346–47 (1953). The Board's insistence on a single "reasonable doubt" threshold for polling, withdrawal, and RM elections alike—while simultaneously declaring elections preferable—reflects exactly the kind of unreasoned, one-size-fits-all policy this Court has refused to credit.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt about the Union's majority status cannot stand. Review of Board factfinding is not toothless. Congress intended that courts should assume more responsibility for the reasonableness and fairness of Labor Board decisions than some courts had shown in the past, and that a reviewing court, in assessing whether substantial evidence supports the Board's findings, must weigh not only the evidence that supports the Board's conclusion but also whatever in the record fairly cuts against it. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 477, 488 (1951). A reviewing court may not treat the Board's resolution of a factual question as conclusive merely because the Board has selected among competing inferences; the inference chosen must itself be reasonable in light of the record as a whole. *Id.* at 490.

The Board's treatment of the record here reflects precisely the kind of one-sided factfinding *Universal Camera* condemns. The ALJ and the Board discounted, one by one, every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown—discrediting Ridgick's and Marsh's statements as equivocal, refusing to credit Bloch's and Mohr's reports of coworker sentiment because the affected employees did not personally testify, and excluding employees no longer on the payroll from the calculus entirely—while crediting nothing that corroborated the Union's continuing support. This selective winnowing of the evidence mirrors the error this Court identified in *NLRB v. Walton Manufacturing Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 408 (1962), where the Court insisted that Board credibility determinations and inferences drawn from the record must be examined with the same rigor applied to any other factual finding, and cannot be accepted where the Board fails to grapple with evidence detracting from its conclusion.

Nor can the Board's approach be justified as ordinary credibility assessment entitled to deference. Where, as here, the Board applies a categorical rule—discounting entire categories of evidence (hearsay reports of coworkers' views, statements made during hiring interviews) without individualized assessment—it has not performed the evidentiary weighing *Universal Camera* demands; it has instead erected an evidentiary standard so exacting that no employer's evidence could ever satisfy it. *See NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963) (Board findings must rest on the actual dynamics of the record, not on abstract characterizations). The cumulative, uncontradicted testimony of numerous employees—far more than the Board's crabbed count of seven—demonstrates that Allentown's doubt was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
