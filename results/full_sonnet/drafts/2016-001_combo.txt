**ARGUMENT**

**Section 289's "Total Profit" Remedy Attaches Only to the "Article of Manufacture" to Which the Patented Design Has Been Applied, Which May Be Less Than the Entire End Product**

The statute Congress enacted to remedy design-patent infringement authorizes recovery of total profit only on "any article of manufacture to which such design or colorable imitation has been applied." 35 U.S.C. § 289. Congress chose that phrase deliberately, against a backdrop of this Court's decisions holding that a design-patent owner could not recover an infringer's entire profits absent proof connecting those profits to the design itself. See *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885); *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886). The 1887 Act, from which § 289 descends, relieved patentees of the burden of proving *causation* between infringement and profit, but it did not relieve them of identifying the correct "article" to which those profits must be tied. *Dunlap v. Schofield*, 152 U.S. 244 (1894) (recognizing that the statute permits recovery of "total profit" only "on the article" to which the design has been applied).

Lower courts applying that statute have consistently recognized that the "article of manufacture" is not automatically the whole commercial product as sold to consumers, but rather the component or article that actually embodies the patented design. In *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916), the Second Circuit measured profits by reference to the piano case—the article bearing the ornamental design—rather than the entire piano with its internal mechanical works, because the design patent covered only the case. Likewise, in *Untermeyer v. Freund*, 58 F. 205 (2d Cir. 1893), the court's inquiry into whether the whole watchcase or only a portion of it constituted the relevant article turned expressly on how much of the physical object the design covered. These decisions confirm that identification of the "article of manufacture" is a factual inquiry keyed to the scope of the design, not a rule of thumb that automatically sweeps in an entire multicomponent device merely because the design appears somewhere on it. Where, as here, a design patent covers only a discrete component—a rounded corner, a bezel, a grid of icons—the "article of manufacture" under § 289 is that component, not the smartphone as a whole with its thousands of unpatented, independently functional elements.

This construction also harmonizes § 289 with the broader apportionment principle that has governed patent-damages law since the middle of the last century. In *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480 (1854), this Court held that a patentee whose invention improved only part of a machine could not recover profits attributable to the whole machine, because the patentee's monopoly extends only to the improvement itself. That principle was reaffirmed and refined in *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), where the Court held that a patentee "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features," unless the entire value of the marketed article is properly attributable to the patented feature. *Id.* at 121. This Court applied the same logic to profits recoverable for utility-patent infringement in *Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co.*, 225 U.S. 604 (1912), rejecting recovery of an infringer's entire profit where the patented feature contributed only part of the value of the whole machine. There is no principled reason design patents should escape this settled apportionment logic merely because § 289 speaks of "total profit"—that phrase describes the *measure* of recovery once the correct article is identified, not license to disregard the article's actual scope. Congress's use of "total profit" eliminated the patentee's burden of apportioning *causation*, as the Dobson cases had required, but it did not eliminate the antecedent step of correctly identifying the relevant article, a step that lower courts, including *Bevin Bros. Manufacturing Co. v. Starr Bros. Bell Co.*, 114 F. 362 (C.C.D. Conn. 1902), have long treated as necessarily component-specific when the patented design does not extend to the entire commercial product.

Respondent's position—that "total profit" must always mean total profit on the entire multicomponent device regardless of how small a fraction of that device the patented design covers—cannot be squared with the statutory text, its historical origins in the Dobson line, or the apportionment principles this Court has applied to patent remedies for over a century. A smartphone comprises many thousands of components embodying independent engineering and functionality wholly unrelated to a design patent covering, for example, a rounded rectangular shape or icon arrangement. To award the infringer's entire profit on the finished smartphone, including profit attributable to the processor, memory, wireless radios, and countless other unpatented elements, untethers the remedy from the article the design patent actually covers.

Because the asserted design patents claim only components of Samsung's smartphones, the "article of manufacture" under § 289 is limited to those components, and damages must likewise be limited to the profit attributable to them. This Court should so hold and remand for a damages calculation consistent with that limitation.
