**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious groups when doing so would violate the Establishment Clause. The Milford Central School’s decision to deny the Good News Club access to its after-school facilities was not a violation of the Club’s free speech rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. Rather, it was a reasonable and constitutionally permissible application of a policy that prohibits the use of public school facilities for religious instruction and worship—activities that are inherently religious in nature and purpose. The Club’s activities, as detailed in the record, are not merely expressive of a religious viewpoint on secular subjects; they constitute religious worship and instruction, which the school may lawfully exclude from its limited public forum.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a public school may maintain a limited public forum for non-governmental groups while preserving its authority to exclude speech that is religious in character. In *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993), the Court held that a school district may deny access to religious groups if the denial is based on the nature of the speech—specifically, religious worship or instruction—rather than on the viewpoint expressed. The Court emphasized that “the government may exclude religious groups from a limited public forum only if the exclusion is justified by a legitimate, content-neutral policy.” Id. at 394. Here, Milford’s Community Use Policy is content-neutral: it prohibits “religious purposes” and requires applicants to certify that their use complies with that restriction. The policy applies equally to all groups, regardless of viewpoint, and is not designed to suppress religious expression per se, but to prevent the use of public school facilities for religious worship and instruction.

The Good News Club’s activities are not merely “teaching morals from a religious perspective,” as the Second Circuit suggested in its majority opinion. The Club’s meetings are structured around religious worship: they begin with a prayer, include singing hymns with explicitly Christian lyrics, involve the recitation of scripture, and culminate in a direct invitation to children to accept Jesus Christ as their personal Savior. These are not incidental religious elements; they are central to the Club’s purpose and structure. As the Court explained in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), “there is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’” The Club’s activities are not simply expressive of a religious viewpoint on secular topics; they are themselves acts of religious worship and instruction, which fall outside the bounds of protected speech in a public forum.

The Club’s argument that its message is “moral and character development” is disingenuous. The record shows that the Club’s lesson plans explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children, and that the “challenge and invitation” segment is designed to convert children to Christianity. The lesson titled “Israel Demands a King” includes a direct invitation to “believe on Jesus” and to “pray this prayer to be saved.” This is not moral instruction; it is religious conversion. The Club’s own materials, including the *Daily Bread* and lesson plans, repeatedly refer to salvation, the second coming of Christ, and the necessity of personal faith in Jesus. These are not secular moral lessons; they are doctrinal teachings of a specific religion.

The Second Circuit’s distinction between “religious instruction” and “teaching values from a religious viewpoint” is legally unsound. The Supreme Court has rejected such a distinction in *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 533 U.S. 98, 108 (2001), where the Court held that “the government may not exclude religious groups from a limited public forum solely because their speech is religious in nature.” But the Court also made clear that “a school may exclude religious groups from a limited public forum if the group’s activities are religious in nature and purpose.” Id. at 110. The Club’s activities are not merely religious in content; they are religious in function. The Club’s meetings are not forums for discussion or debate; they are structured religious services designed to teach and convert children to Christianity.

Finally, the school’s policy is justified by a compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. The Establishment Clause prohibits government endorsement of religion, *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971), and the use of public school facilities for religious worship or instruction creates a substantial risk of such endorsement. The Court has recognized that “the appearance of government sponsorship of religious activity is as troubling as actual sponsorship.” *Board of Education v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990). By allowing the Good News Club to meet in the school building, Milford would risk conveying the message that the school endorses Christian religious beliefs. This is precisely the kind of entanglement the Establishment Clause was designed to prevent.

The judgment of the Second Circuit is affirmed. The school’s denial of access to the Good News Club was not a violation of the First Amendment. The Club’s activities are religious in nature and purpose, and the school’s policy is content-neutral and justified by a compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations. The Club’s free speech rights were not infringed.
