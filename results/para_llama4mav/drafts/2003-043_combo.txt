The government's argument that assessing the partnership is sufficient to extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from individual partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, an "individual" is a distinct "taxpayer" from a "partnership." Galletti v. United States (In re Galletti), 298 F.3d 1107 (9th Cir. 2002).

The regulation interpreting § 6203 requires that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. This implies that the IRS must assess each taxpayer separately. The government's failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limit bars it from collecting the unpaid taxes from them. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), supports this interpretation. In Coson, the court held that an assessment against a partnership did not create a valid lien against an individual partner's property because the partner had not been assessed individually. Id. at 458. Similarly, in this case, the assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from the individual partners.

The district court's decision in Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D.Cal.1959), aff'd 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960), is distinguishable. The court in Young held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. However, the court also noted that the government would have had to resort to court action or individually assess the taxes against the partner to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050.

Other cases have also held that an assessment against a partnership is not sufficient to collect taxes from individual partners. In United States v. Kaufman, 267 F. Supp. 351 (S.D.N.Y. 1967), the court held that the government could not collect taxes from individual partners without first assessing them individually. See also United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995) (distinguishable because the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners); United States v. Galletti is consistent with other pre-1970 cases, such as United States v. Shapiro, 159 F.2d 890 (2d Cir. 1947), and United States v. Russell, 155 F. Supp. 143 (D.C. Mich. 1957).

Furthermore, the government's reliance on state partnership law is misplaced. Under California law, a creditor must first obtain a judgment against a partner to collect a partnership debt from the partner's assets. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c); see also, e.g., First Nat'l Bank of Ann Arbor v. Farson, 226 F.2d 570 (6th Cir. 1955); Bank of Topeka v. Eaton, 100 F. 8 (2d Cir. 1900); Liberty Nat'l Bank of N.Y. v. Bear, 265 N.Y. 544 (1934); and Bank of California v. Connolly, 36 Cal. App. 3d 350 (Cal. Ct. App. 1934).

In conclusion, the government's failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limit bars it from collecting the unpaid taxes from them. The assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from the individual partners. The Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with pre-1970 case law, and the government's reliance on state partnership law is misplaced. Therefore, the court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's decision.
