### Argument

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state law applies on the Outer Continental Shelf whenever it concerns the subject matter of the dispute and is not inconsistent with federal law. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not incorporate adjacent-state law as freestanding law that coexists whenever it is merely “not inconsistent” with federal law. Rather, state law enters the OCSLA framework only as surrogate federal law, and only when federal law does not already supply the rule of decision. That reading follows the statutory text, the Act’s structure, and this Court’s OCSLA precedent.

Section 1333(a)(2)(A) provides that adjacent-state law is “the law of the United States” on the OCS only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has repeatedly explained that the OCSLA was enacted to create “a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the shelf. Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). In Rodrigue, the Court rejected the premise that ordinary maritime law governed fixed platforms, emphasizing that Congress intended the OCS to be treated like a federal enclave and that state law would apply only through incorporation under the Act. Id. at 359-66. The statute therefore asks not whether state law touches the same subject matter, but whether it is a proper surrogate for missing federal law.

That is why this Court in Rodrigue described state law as applicable where “there is no admiralty law to apply.” Id. at 357. The key point was not abstract consistency, but whether federal law already occupied the field of the particular issue before the Court. When federal law did not furnish the governing rule, state law could be borrowed to fill the gap. Id. at 365-66. Likewise, in Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court treated adjacent-state law as filling interstitial space in the federal scheme, not as a parallel, independent source of rights. Id. at 104-05. The Ninth Circuit’s decision departs from that understanding by allowing state law to operate even when federal law already addresses the question.

This Court’s more recent OCSLA cases confirm the same principle. In Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court held that the OCSLA’s “special relationship with the shore community” does not itself expand state law beyond the Act’s terms. Id. at 217-19. And in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Court explained that OCSLA jurisdiction extends federal law to fixed structures “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” Id. at 477-78. That federal-enclave analogy matters: in an enclave, state law applies only as assimilated law, and only when federal law is deficient. See United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958); Lewis v. United States, 523 U.S. 155, 160-61 (1998).

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), on which respondent relies, is not to the contrary. Continental Oil stated that OCSLA state law may be adopted when necessary “to fill a significant void or gap.” Id. at 1036. That is the correct approach. A “not inconsistent” standard alone cannot do the work respondent asks of it, because inconsistency is a necessary condition for displacing borrowed state law, not a sufficient condition for importing it. Where federal law already provides the rule, there is no gap to fill. See Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 357, 366; Tallentire, 477 U.S. at 218-19.

Here, federal law supplies the relevant wage-and-hour rules. The Fair Labor Standards Act establishes a comprehensive federal scheme governing minimum wage and overtime, including a savings provision preserving more protective state laws in their own spheres. 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207, 218(a). But the question under OCSLA is not whether California law is valid in California; it is whether California law is needed as surrogate federal law on the OCS. Because the FLSA already governs wage-and-hour obligations there, California meal-period and related wage claims are not incorporated by OCSLA. See Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355-66; Gulf Offshore, 453 U.S. at 477-78.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule would convert OCSLA into a broad incorporation statute for all adjacent-state labor regulation whenever Congress has not expressly forbidden it. That is inconsistent with the Act’s design and this Court’s cases. The proper test is whether federal law leaves a gap that state law can fill as surrogate federal law; if federal law already covers the issue, state law does not apply. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
