**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s appointed state judges fall within the ADEA’s exception for “appointees on the policymaking level.” 898 F.2d at 602-03. This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the statute, the weight of judicial authority, and the traditional role of judges in our constitutional system.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Supreme Court has instructed that statutory exemptions must be construed narrowly, *see, e.g.,* *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Echazabal*, 536 U.S. 73, 80 (2002), but it has also recognized that the policymaking-level exception sweeps broadly. In *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), the First Circuit—whose analysis this Court has repeatedly found persuasive, *see, e.g., United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 267 n.7 (1997)—held that judges are policymakers because their decisions “necessarily resolve issues previously unsettled and thus create law.” *Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55. This is consistent with the Supreme Court’s observation that judges “exercise discretion in decisionmaking” and “engage in thoughtful judgment,” *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam), and with this Court’s own recognition that judges perform “important policymaking functions,” *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 603.

Other courts have reached the same conclusion. The Fourth Circuit, reversing a district court decision that had held Virginia judges exempt from the ADEA, nonetheless acknowledged that judges are policymakers when it noted that the policymaking-level exception “would apply to judges in many, if not most, instances.” *Schlitz v. Virginia*, 854 F.2d 43, 45 (4th Cir. 1988) (citing *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313). The Pennsylvania Supreme Court likewise held that judges are policymakers under state law, *In re Stout*, 521 Pa. 571, 559 A.2d 489, 492 (1989), and the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court reached the same result under the ADEA, *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145 (1988). These decisions confirm that the policymaking-level exception is not limited to executive or legislative appointees but extends to judges who, by the nature of their office, shape legal policy.

The legislative history cited by Petitioners is not controlling. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the authoritative source for finding the Legislature’s intent lies in the words of the statute.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The statute’s text is unambiguous: judges who exercise policymaking authority fall outside the ADEA’s coverage. *See Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55. The joint explanatory statement on which Petitioners rely merely reflects Congress’s intent to construe the exception narrowly—not to exclude judges from its reach. *See id.* at 56.

**II. EVEN IF THE ADEA WERE APPLICABLE, IT DOES NOT PREEMPT MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION.**

The ADEA does not expressly preempt state constitutional provisions governing judicial tenure. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 601. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that federal statutes do not preempt state law unless Congress has made its intent “clear and manifest.” *Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr*, 518 U.S. 470, 485 (1996) (quoting *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947)). The ADEA contains no such express preemption clause, and its structure—particularly the policymaking-level exception—suggests that Congress did not intend to intrude upon state control over judicial qualifications. *See Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 54-55.

This Court has recognized that “traditionally sensitive areas,” such as the regulation of state judges, require a “clear statement” of congressional intent to preempt. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 601 (quoting *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971)). The absence of such a statement here is dispositive. *See also Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992) (plurality opinion) (“Congress’ intent to supersede state law must be ‘clear and manifest.’”).

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Eighth Circuit correctly upheld Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision as rationally related to legitimate state interests. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604. The Supreme Court has long held that age-based classifications are subject only to rational-basis review, *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313, and that states may adopt mandatory retirement rules for judges to ensure “the prompt administration of justice” and to avoid the “embarrassment” of removing judges for incapacity. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir.) (citing *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313-14), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979).

Other courts have upheld similar provisions on rational-basis grounds. *See, e.g., Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980) (upholding Pennsylvania’s mandatory retirement age for judges); *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146 (same). The Seventh Circuit has explained that mandatory retirement “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 627, and the Third Circuit has noted that such rules “eliminate the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges,” *Malmed*, 621 F.2d at 572.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Gregory* is misplaced. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Gregory* did not address the merits of the equal protection claim because it concluded that the judges were exempt from the ADEA as policymakers. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602-03. Even if this Court were to reach the equal protection issue, however, the provision easily survives rational-basis review. *See Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent respectfully submits that the judgment below should be affirmed.
