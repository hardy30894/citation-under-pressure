ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. The constitutional test is the same one that governs competency to stand trial: whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); see also Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966).

This Court has never imposed a more demanding standard for the waiver of constitutional rights that necessarily accompanies a guilty plea or self-representation. A guilty plea must be voluntary, knowing, and intelligent, Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969), and a waiver of counsel must likewise be knowing and intelligent, Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975). Those requirements presuppose only that the defendant possesses the mental capacity measured by the Dusky standard; they do not demand an additional inquiry into whether the defendant’s ultimate choice is “reasoned.” See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (competency to waive counsel judged by capacity to understand rights and consequences).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule, derived from Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), rests on the premise that waiving counsel or pleading guilty requires “a higher level of mental functioning” than standing trial. That premise finds no support in this Court’s decisions. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly applied the Dusky formulation to determine whether a defendant may validly waive counsel or enter a plea. See Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam) (remanding for competency determination under Dusky where defendant sought to waive counsel); Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 398 (1993) (holding that the Dusky standard governs both decisions).

Requiring a separate, heightened inquiry would also be inconsistent with the practical administration of criminal justice. Trial courts already must ensure that waivers are knowing and voluntary; layering an additional “reasoned choice” assessment would invite endless collateral litigation without advancing any constitutional interest. See Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 453 (1992) (recognizing that competency procedures must not “impose an undue burden” on the States).

Because the Nevada courts applied the correct Dusky standard and the record supports their competency finding, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
