The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held that the insanity defense is a separate and distinct issue from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. The Court noted that "the law is administered in the interests of the community" and that "the state may rightfully take measures to guard against the commission of crime." Id. at 488.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. The due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all evidence he desires, as noted in Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893). The state has the right to regulate the presentation of evidence in its courts, a principle recognized in cases such as  Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591 (1896).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. The Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In  Holmes v. United States, 168 U.S. 533 (1897), the Court considered the admissibility of certain evidence and noted the importance of state rules of evidence. Similarly, in  Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Court discussed the role of presumptions in trial proceedings.

In addition, the Court has recognized that the insanity defense is a "substantive" issue that is separate from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). The respondent argues that the Arizona rule is consistent with this principle, and with the general principle that states have broad authority to regulate their own judicial procedures, as noted in  Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908).

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
