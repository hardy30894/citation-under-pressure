ARGUMENT

I. THE ALASKA NATIONAL INTEREST LANDS CONSERVATION ACT OF 1980 (ANILCA) AUTHORIZES THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE (NPS) TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES ON STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS (CSUs)

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs. The plain text of the statute supports this interpretation.

A. The Plain Meaning of ANILCA § 103(c)

When interpreting a statute, courts must first look to the plain meaning of the text. United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). ANILCA § 103(c) provides that "only those lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] which are public lands ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). However, this provision does not limit NPS's regulatory authority to public lands only.

The statute's definition of "public lands" is not a limitation on NPS's authority, but rather a clarification of what lands are included within CSUs. See Katie John III, 720 F.3d at 1228 (noting that ANILCA's definition of "public lands" is not a limitation on federal authority). NPS's regulatory authority is derived from its general authority to regulate activities within national parks, which includes the authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within park boundaries. See 54 U.S.C. § 100751(a); see also United States v. Brown, 552 F.3d 854, 860 (9th Cir. 2008) (holding that NPS has authority to regulate activities within national parks).

B. NPS's Regulatory Authority is Not Limited to Public Lands

The Ninth Circuit's decision in Katie John III is instructive on this point. In that case, the court held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not preclude federal regulation of activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. 720 F.3d at 1228. The court noted that the statute's definition of "public lands" is not a limitation on federal authority, and that NPS's regulatory authority is derived from its general authority to regulate activities within national parks. Id.

Similarly, in United States v. Lindsey, the Ninth Circuit held that NPS has authority to regulate activities within national parks, including activities on non-federal lands. 782 F.3d 1064, 1073 (9th Cir. 2015). The court noted that NPS's regulatory authority is not limited to public lands, but rather extends to all lands within park boundaries. Id.

C. The Hovercraft Ban is a Valid Exercise of NPS's Regulatory Authority

The hovercraft ban at issue in this case is a valid exercise of NPS's regulatory authority. The ban is a reasonable regulation aimed at protecting the resources and values of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. See 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e); see also United States v. Garfield Cnty., 122 F. Supp. 2d 1201, 1224 (D. Utah 2000) (noting that NPS has broad authority to regulate activities within national parks).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal agencies have broad authority to regulate activities within national parks. See, e.g., Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 540-41 (1976) (holding that the federal government has authority to regulate activities on public lands); see also United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927) (noting that the federal government has authority to regulate activities within national forests).

In this case, the hovercraft ban is a reasonable regulation aimed at protecting the resources and values of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. The ban is not limited to public lands, but rather applies to all lands within the preserve, including state-owned lands and waters. See 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e).

D. Sturgeon's Claims are Without Merit

Sturgeon's claims are without merit. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs. The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of NPS's regulatory authority, and Sturgeon's use of a hovercraft within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is subject to the ban.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal agencies have broad authority to regulate activities within national parks. See, e.g., Kleppe, 426 U.S. at 540-41; see also Alford, 274 U.S. at 267. In this case, NPS's regulatory authority is not limited to public lands, but rather extends to all lands within the preserve, including state-owned lands and waters.

II. ALASKA LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE NPS'S REGULATORY AUTHORITY

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Alaska lacks standing to challenge NPS's regulatory authority. Alaska's claims are based on its alleged sovereign and proprietary interests in state-owned lands and waters within CSUs. However, Alaska has failed to demonstrate a concrete and particularized injury-in-fact.

To establish standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury-in-fact that is concrete and particularized, as well as actual or imminent. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). Alaska has failed to meet this requirement. Alaska's alleged injuries are speculative and hypothetical, and do not demonstrate a concrete and particularized harm.

In addition, Alaska's claims are not ripe for review. The ripeness doctrine is designed to prevent courts from deciding cases that are not yet fit for judicial resolution. See Abbott Labs. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 148-49 (1967). Alaska's claims are not yet ripe, as they are based on hypothetical and speculative injuries.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that states have limited standing to challenge federal regulations. See, e.g., Massachusetts v. EPA, 549 U.S. 497, 520 n.17 (2007) (noting that states have limited standing to challenge federal regulations). In this case, Alaska has failed to demonstrate a concrete and particularized injury-in-fact, and its claims are not ripe for review.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
