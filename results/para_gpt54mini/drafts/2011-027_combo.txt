The Court should affirm. The constitutional test for navigability for title turns on whether the segment of river was navigable at the time of statehood, because the equal-footing doctrine vests title in the States only to the beds of waters that were then “navigable in fact.” Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64 (1931).

Under the equal-footing doctrine, a new State enters the Union “with the same rights, sovereignty, and jurisdiction” as the original States, including title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders. Pollard’s Lessee, 44 U.S. at 228-29. But that title depends on the condition of the waterway at the moment statehood is conferred. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894), explained that the beds of navigable waters pass to the State “to the extent that the waters are navigable in fact.” Likewise, United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co., 229 U.S. 53, 69-70 (1913), recognized that the controlling inquiry is whether the stream was navigable as a matter of fact at the relevant time. The Court has repeatedly treated that historical condition as the touchstone of title.

United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), is especially clear. There, the Court held that navigability for title “is to be determined as of the time of statehood,” id. at 75, and that “the question is whether the waters were navigable in fact at the time of statehood,” id. at 76. The same principle appears in Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922), where the Court tied title to the condition of the river when the State entered the Union. So too in Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 87-88 (1922), the Court held that the State’s title depended on whether the stream was navigable at statehood, not on later changes in usage or condition.

That is why later improvements, obstructions, or altered water conditions cannot rewrite title. United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940), distinguished the broader federal commerce power from the title inquiry and emphasized that title depends on navigability in fact at statehood. The Court in Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 124-25 (1921), likewise held that post-statehood evidence may illuminate historical conditions, but only to resolve the river’s navigability when title vested. And United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926), reiterated that title to submerged lands turns on whether the water was navigable at the time the State was admitted.

This rule is also consistent with the Court’s treatment of evidence. Because the relevant condition is historical, courts may consider reports, maps, testimony, and other proof bearing on the river’s character at statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 82-83; United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. at 55-56. But the evidence serves one end only: reconstruction of the river’s condition at the moment of statehood. The test is not whether the river was later useful for commerce, hydroelectric generation, or some other purpose. See United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. at 408-09; United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 83-84.

PPL’s contrary approach would untether title from the constitutional moment when sovereignty and ownership passed to the State. That would contradict Pollard’s Lessee, Shively, Utah, Holt State Bank, Brewer-Elliott, Chandler-Dunbar, Oklahoma v. Texas, Economy Light, and Appalachian Electric Power. The State’s title cannot be divested or expanded by later events, and the trial court therefore acted correctly in focusing on whether the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable when Montana entered the Union in 1889. Because that is the governing constitutional standard, the judgment should be affirmed.
