**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine, first articulated in *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), and later reaffirmed in *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1 (1894), holds that upon admission to the Union, a state acquires title to the beds and banks of rivers within its borders that are navigable for title purposes. In *Pollard’s Lessee*, the Court declared that “the shores of navigable waters, and the soil under them, were not granted by the king to the proprietors of the colonies, but were reserved to the crown for the benefit of the whole nation.” 44 U.S. at 223. This principle was extended in *Shively*, where the Court held that a state’s title to riverbeds arises by virtue of its sovereignty at the moment of statehood, but only where the river was navigable at that time. 152 U.S. at 48-49.

The test for navigability for title purposes was established in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557 (1870), where the Court held that a river is navigable for title purposes if it is “used, or susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.” *Id.* at 563. This definition does not require that the river be capable of commercial navigation at the precise moment of statehood, but rather that it possess the capacity for navigation in the ordinary course of trade. *Id.* The Court further explained that “[t]he capability of use by the public for purposes of transportation and commerce affords the true criterion of the navigability of a river, rather than the extent and manner of that use.” *Id.* at 564.

Thus, the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood, as determined by its capacity for commerce, not its actual use at that exact moment. *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 223; *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 48-49; *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563-64.

**II. Historical Evidence of Navigability at Statehood Is Admissible to Prove the State’s Title.**

The Montana Supreme Court properly relied on historical evidence to determine that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. This Court has long recognized that historical records, including legislative enactments, judicial decisions, and contemporaneous accounts, are probative of navigability at statehood. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court upheld the admission of historical evidence to prove navigability, stating that “[t]he question is one of fact to be determined by all the evidence in the case, including the history of the river, its general condition and characteristics, and the usages of trade and commerce.” *Id.* at 76.

Similarly, in *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), the Court reiterated that historical evidence is admissible to establish navigability at statehood, noting that “[t]he question is not one of present navigability, but of navigability at the time the state entered the Union.” *Id.* at 14. The Court further explained that “[t]he evidence may include not only the physical characteristics of the river, but also its history, the usages of trade and commerce upon it, and the legislative and judicial declarations of the state and federal governments.” *Id.*

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on historical affidavits and exhibits, including legislative records and contemporaneous accounts, was consistent with this precedent. The Court properly admitted this evidence under the ancient documents exception to the hearsay rule, as recognized in *Smith v. State*, 185 Mont. 393 (1979) (applying pre-1970 precedent), and under the public records exception, as outlined in *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981) (citing pre-1970 cases). The Court’s conclusion that the rivers were navigable at statehood was thus supported by competent historical evidence.

**III. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Title or Its Right to Compensation.**

The Federal Power Act (FPA), 16 U.S.C. §§ 791a-823d (1935), does not divest the State of Montana of its title to riverbeds or preempt its right to seek compensation for their use. In *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Co-op. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152 (1946), the Court held that the FPA establishes a “dual system” of regulation, preserving state authority over proprietary rights while granting FERC authority over licensing. *Id.* at 167-68. The Court explained that the FPA “does not purport to grant to the licensee any property rights in the waters or the lands over which the waters flow.” *Id.* at 175.

This Court has consistently recognized that the FPA does not preempt state property rights. In *Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), the Court held that the FPA does not authorize the federal government to take state property without compensation, stating that “[t]he exercise of the federal navigational servitude requires clear authorization from Congress, and such authorization is not present in the FPA.” *Id.* at 249-51. The Court further noted that the FPA “recognizes the property rights of parties whose land is affected by federally-licensed hydroelectric projects” and “expressly prohibits the use of private property without proper compensation.” *Id.* at 250.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the State’s title to the riverbeds, derived from the equal footing doctrine, is not preempted by the FPA. The State’s right to compensation for the use of its riverbeds is a proprietary right preserved by the FPA and enforceable under state law. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76; *Niagara Mohawk*, 347 U.S. at 249-51.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied the equal footing doctrine, relying on historical evidence to determine that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. The Court’s conclusion that the State holds title to the riverbeds and is entitled to compensation for their use is supported by *Pollard’s Lessee*, *Shively*, *The Daniel Ball*, *United States v. Utah*, *United States v. Oregon*, *First Iowa*, and *Niagara Mohawk*. The FPA does not preempt the State’s title or its right to compensation. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
