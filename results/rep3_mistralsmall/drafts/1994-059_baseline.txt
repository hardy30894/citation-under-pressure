**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION BECAUSE IT IMPOSES ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESS THAT ARE NOT SPECIFIED IN THE CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly alters the qualifications for federal office set forth in the Constitution. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 of the United States Constitution provide the exclusive qualifications for service in the United States House of Representatives and Senate, respectively. These clauses specify age, citizenship, and residency requirements, and do not permit states to add or alter any qualifications for federal office. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 804 (1995) (“The qualifications for service in Congress established in the text of the Constitution are fixed until changed by the amending process.”); *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person duly elected by his constituents who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.”).

The Arkansas Amendment attempts to impose term limits on Arkansas’s federal delegation by barring candidates from appearing on the ballot after they have served a specified number of terms. This is an unconstitutional qualification because it effectively prevents otherwise qualified candidates from serving in Congress. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 804 (“[A]llowing the several States to formulate qualifications for Congress would undermine the uniformity and the national character that the Framers sought to ensure.”). The Court has recognized that ballot access restrictions can operate as qualifications when they foreclose the opportunity to serve. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788 n.9 (1983) (“[A]n early voting deadline might ‘amount to a qualification’ of candidates if it were to operate to exclude a ‘substantial number’ of potential candidates.”).

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the Amendment violates the Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 794-95 (citing *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 522). The fact that the Amendment uses ballot access as a mechanism to impose term limits does not evade the constitutional prohibition. *Id.* at 804 (“The fact that the Arkansas provision is framed in terms of a ballot access restriction does not immunize it from scrutiny under the Qualifications Clauses.”).

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT PROPERLY DECLINED TO SEVER THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL PORTIONS OF THE AMENDMENT BECAUSE THEY WERE INEXTRICABLY LINKED TO THE VALID PORTIONS.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly refused to sever the unconstitutional federal term limits from the valid state term limits provisions. The court found that the Amendment was presented to the voters as a single, indivisible package and that the severability clause could not cure the constitutional infirmity. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964) (severability clause is effective only if the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion).

The court’s conclusion is supported by the structure and presentation of the Amendment. The Preamble referred to “elected officials” collectively, and the Amendment applied to both state and federal offices in a single vote. The voters were not given the opportunity to approve state term limits independently of federal term limits. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 838 (dissenting opinion) (“The Arkansas initiative did not present the voters with two separate questions—one on term limits for state officials and one on term limits for federal officials—but with a single question that bundled the two together.”).

This Court should affirm the Arkansas Supreme Court’s holding that the unconstitutional federal term limits provisions cannot be severed from the valid state term limits provisions. *Id.* at 837-38 (majority opinion acknowledging the “fusion” of state and federal term limits in the Amendment).

**III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE AMENDMENT WAS INVALID UNDER THE ARKANSAS CONSTITUTION FOR FAILING TO INCLUDE AN ENACTING CLAUSE.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court properly invalidated the Amendment for its failure to include the statutorily required Enacting Clause. *Ark. Const. amend. 7*. The Enacting Clause serves a critical function by clearly stating the source of the people’s authority to enact the measure. *Plugge v. McCuen*, 310 Ark. 654, 670, 841 S.W.2d 139, 147 (1992) (Hays, J., dissenting) (“The Enacting Clause is the people’s voice, declaring that they, and not the legislature, are enacting the law.”).

The circuit court’s finding that the Amendment lacked an Enacting Clause was supported by the record. The absence of the required language rendered the Amendment procedurally invalid under Arkansas law. *Id.* at 669, 841 S.W.2d at 147. This Court should defer to the Arkansas Supreme Court’s interpretation of its own constitution. *See Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 691 (1975) (“[W]e must ultimately decide for ourselves whether these state procedural rulings are so lacking in justification that they deny rights guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment violates the Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution and is invalid under the Arkansas Constitution.
