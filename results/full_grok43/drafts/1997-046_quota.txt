ARGUMENT

I. Clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest.

"Pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). The Due Process Clause therefore does not attach to Ohio's clemency procedures. See Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976). State-created procedures alone do not generate a liberty interest absent an independent substantive right. Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472, 483-84 (1995); Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex, 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979). Because the Governor's clemency power remains "almost unfettered," State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994), no protected interest arises from the mere availability of review.

Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), does not alter this conclusion. Evitts required effective counsel on a first appeal as of right because the state had made appeal "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." Id. at 393 (quoting Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956)). Clemency lies far beyond that system. Ross v. Moffitt, 417 U.S. 600, 610-11 (1974) (no right to counsel on discretionary review). Extending Evitts to executive clemency would contradict Dumschat's explicit refusal to treat commutation as part of the adjudicatory process.

II. Voluntary participation in a clemency interview imposes no Fifth Amendment compulsion.

The regulation permits, but does not require, an interview. A prisoner "remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes." No statement is compelled. Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316-17 (1976) (no violation where inmate could remain silent without automatic penalty). Any risk of self-incrimination is therefore "premature" for judicial resolution. Id. at 324.

The "unconstitutional conditions" doctrine does not apply. A state may condition a benefit on the relinquishment of a right only when the condition is coercive. See, e.g., Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974). Here the interview is optional; declining it carries no automatic adverse consequence in any pending criminal proceeding. Because the Fifth Amendment protects only against compelled testimony, the voluntary option creates no constitutional violation.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
