ARGUMENT

I. THE CONVICTIONS FOR CONSPIRACY TO COMMIT PUBLIC BRIBERY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) MUST BE REVERSED BECAUSE JOANN A. PARKER LACKED THE AUTHORITY TO GRANT OR DENY BENEFITS.

Mrs. Parker's duties as a clerk to ALJ Aronson included assisting him before and during hearings, recording and taking notes at those hearings, and typing and mailing his decisions. Although she had access to the office's computer system, she was not authorized to approve benefits or sign ALJ Aronson's name. The indictment charging her with conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) was therefore deficient.

Section 201(b)(2)(C) prohibits public officials from being induced to do or omit any act in violation of their official duty. The statute's broad definition of "official act" in section 201(a)(3) encompasses "any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official's official capacity, or in such official's place of trust or profit." As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 230 (1914), "the term 'official act' ... was not limited to acts done in a ministerial capacity, but included as well the performance of acts which were discretionary in their nature." The Court further explained that official acts that violate an official's duty are not limited to those proscribed by statutes and written rules and regulations, but may also be found in "established usage." Id. at 231.

However, the government must prove that the public official had the authority to perform the act in question. As the Court held in United States v. Sutter, 160 F.2d 754, 757 (7th Cir. 1947), "the bribe must be given or offered with the intent to influence the official in the performance of some official act." In this case, Mrs. Parker's actions, although potentially in violation of "established usage," were not within her official authority.

The Sixth Circuit's decision in United States v. Borkowski, 154 F.2d 478 (6th Cir. 1946), is instructive. In Borkowski, the court held that a government employee's actions, although potentially corrupt, did not constitute a violation of the bribery statute because they were not within his official authority. Similarly, in United States v. Boylston, 114 F. 375 (D. Mass. 1902), the court held that a government official's acceptance of a bribe did not constitute a crime under the bribery statute because the official lacked the authority to perform the act in question.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Dietrich, 126 F. 664 (C.C.D. Neb. 1904), aff'd, 204 U.S. 597 (1907), emphasizes the importance of proving that the public official had the authority to perform the act in question. In Dietrich, the Court held that a government official's acceptance of a bribe did not constitute a crime under the bribery statute because the official lacked the authority to perform the act in question.

Other cases, such as United States v. Boylan, 114 F. 376 (D. Mass. 1902), United States v. Van Wert, 26 F. Cas. 76 (C.C.N.D. Ohio 1872), and United States v. Gibson, 26 F. Cas. 839 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1855), also support the proposition that a public official's actions must be within their official authority to constitute a violation of the bribery statute.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Mrs. Parker's convictions for conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) must be reversed because she lacked the authority to grant or deny benefits.

II. THE CONVICTIONS OF RALPH PARKER MUST ALSO BE REVERSED.

As Mr. Parker's convictions are derivative of Mrs. Parker's convictions, they must also be reversed. The government's case against Mr. Parker relied heavily on Mrs. Parker's actions, and the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that Mr. Parker conspired with Mrs. Parker to commit public bribery or that he aided and abetted her in committing public bribery.

The Court's decision in United States v. Peoni, 100 F.2d 401 (2d Cir. 1938), is relevant here. In Peoni, the court held that a defendant's conviction for aiding and abetting must be reversed if the principal's conviction is reversed. Similarly, in this case, Mr. Parker's convictions must be reversed because Mrs. Parker's convictions are being reversed.

In conclusion, the convictions of both Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker must be reversed because Mrs. Parker lacked the authority to grant or deny benefits, and the government's case against Mr. Parker was derivative of Mrs. Parker's convictions.
