**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Court's Order Recalling the Mandate Was Reasonably, Though Ultimately Incorrectly, Understood to Restart the Certiorari Clock.**

Supreme Court Rule 13.3 provides that "if the lower court appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing … the time to file the petition for a writ of certiorari for all parties … runs from the date of the denial of rehearing." This Court's June 12, 2007 order recalling the mandate to entertain Buckles's Rule 4-1(e) motion functioned, from Buckles's vantage point, exactly as a reopened rehearing proceeding: the mandate was withdrawn, the matter was again pending before this Court, and a new mandate issued only on June 20, 2007. Buckles reasonably read that sequence — confirmed by what the Clerk's office told his sister — to mean that his certiorari clock began anew. While this Court may ultimately conclude that a Rule 4-1(e) motion for appointment of counsel is not the functional equivalent of a petition for rehearing under Rule 13.3, the ambiguity Buckles confronted is itself relevant to the equitable tolling analysis below.

**II. Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling of the § 2255 Limitations Period.**

**A. The Governing Standard.**

This Court applies the same equitable tolling principles to § 2255 motions that govern habeas petitions under § 2254. *United States v. Battles*, 362 F.3d 1195, 1197 (9th Cir. 2004). A petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling only if he shows "(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way." *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (quoting *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005)). The doctrine is not confined to cases of adversarial misconduct; the Supreme Court has long recognized that limitations periods may be tolled where a litigant "has been induced or tricked by his adversary's misconduct into allowing the filing deadline to pass," *Irwin v. Dep't of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990), and this Court has extended that logic to erroneous guidance from government officials charged with administering the very deadline at issue. In *Socop-Gonzalez v. INS*, 272 F.3d 1176 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc), this Court held that equitable tolling was warranted where a petitioner reasonably relied on an official's mistaken representation about a filing deadline, explaining that tolling is appropriate when a party's delay results from reasonable reliance on misleading official conduct rather than his own lack of diligence.

**B. Misinformation from This Court's Own Clerk's Office Constitutes an Extraordinary Circumstance.**

Buckles has alleged, and the record does not contradict, that on June 4, 2007, his sister telephoned this Court's Clerk's office and was told that "the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued." If true, that statement is precisely the sort of "extraordinary circumstance" contemplated by *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 649, because it did not merely fail to assist Buckles — it affirmatively misled him about the operation of a jurisdictional deadline administered by the very court whose order he was relying upon. A pro se prisoner cannot be faulted for trusting information relayed from the Clerk of the very Court of Appeals presiding over his case. Cf. *Socop-Gonzalez*, 272 F.3d at 1193 (tolling appropriate where reliance on official misinformation was reasonable).

Separately, Buckles's retained counsel abandoned him at the critical juncture. Buckles timely wrote to counsel on April 24, 2007 — the twenty-first and final day under Circuit Rule 4-1(e) — "officially requesting" assistance in preparing a motion for appointment of certiorari counsel, precisely as Rule 4-1(e) obligates retained counsel to do. Counsel neither filed the motion nor withdrew; he simply went silent despite Buckles's "many" subsequent attempts to reach him. This Court has recognized that an attorney's failure to fulfill basic professional obligations at a critical filing juncture can itself constitute an extraordinary circumstance warranting equitable tolling. *Spitsyn v. Moore*, 345 F.3d 796, 800–02 (9th Cir. 2003) (egregious attorney misconduct, including failure to communicate and prepare a filing, may warrant tolling). The combination of counsel's abandonment and the Clerk's office's erroneous guidance left Buckles — a pro se, incarcerated litigant — with no reliable source of accurate information about his filing deadline.

**C. Buckles Diligently Pursued His Rights.**

Buckles satisfies the diligence prong as well. He wrote to counsel on the very last day permitted under Rule 4-1(e); he forwarded that letter to the Clerk the same day; when counsel proved unresponsive, he personally filed a "Supplemental Motion" within days; and once his certiorari petition was flagged as untimely, he promptly filed a declaration with the Supreme Court explaining the confusion. Each of these steps reflects the kind of "reasonable diligence" *Holland* requires — not "maximum feasible diligence," but diligence under the circumstances. 560 U.S. at 653.

**CONCLUSION**

Because Buckles has alleged facts that, if true, establish both extraordinary circumstances and diligence, this Court should vacate the dismissal of his § 2255 motion and remand for an evidentiary hearing on equitable tolling.
