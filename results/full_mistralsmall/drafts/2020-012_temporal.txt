**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under the plain text of the Railroad Retirement Act and this Court’s precedents.

The Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”) grants the federal courts of appeals jurisdiction to review “[a]ny order of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) (1964). The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s prior application is an “order” within the meaning of § 355(f). In *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972 (10th Cir. 1995), the Tenth Circuit held that a refusal to reopen is an “order” subject to review under the analogous statute governing Social Security benefits, reasoning that “[a] refusal to reopen is an order that finally disposes of a claimant’s request for relief.” *Id.* at 974 (citing *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253, 255 (2d Cir. 1966)). This Court has long recognized that the term “order” in the RRA is to be construed broadly to effectuate the remedial purposes of the statute. *See, e.g., United States v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 221 F.2d 523, 526 (5th Cir. 1955) (holding that the RRA’s jurisdictional grant is to be liberally construed to ensure review of Board actions).

The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is also a “final decision” under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”), 5 U.S.C. § 704 (1966), which applies to the Board’s proceedings. *See* 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) (incorporating APA standards). The Supreme Court has held that an agency’s refusal to reopen a prior proceeding is a final decision subject to judicial review where it “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and “determine[s] rights or obligations.” *FCC v. Schreiber*, 382 F.2d 279, 281 (D.C. Cir. 1967) (quoting *Abbott Labs. v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967)). The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application meets this standard. It conclusively denied Salinas’s request for relief and resolved his claim for purposes of the RRA’s jurisdictional grant. *See Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 255 (holding that a refusal to reopen is a final decision where it “finally disposes of the claimant’s request for relief”).

II. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is reviewable under the RRA’s jurisdictional grant, and this Court’s precedents do not foreclose review.

The Fifth Circuit has held that § 355(f) permits review of “Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted.” *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139, 140 (5th Cir. 2003). However, this statement does not exclude review of Board decisions refusing to reopen prior claims. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has recognized that the RRA’s jurisdictional grant encompasses review of all Board orders, including those refusing to reopen prior claims. *See R.R. Retirement Bd. v. Alton R.R. Co.*, 295 U.S. 330, 343 (1935) (holding that the RRA’s predecessor statute permitted review of Board orders “in respect of any order made by [the Board]”).

This Court’s precedents do not foreclose review. In *Roberts*, the Fifth Circuit relied on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), to hold that it lacked jurisdiction to review a refusal to reopen. *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141. However, *Sanders* was decided after the Fifth Circuit’s earlier precedents, which permitted review of refusals to reopen. *See, e.g., Gutierrez v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990) (noting that *Sanders* did not disturb earlier precedents permitting review). Moreover, *Sanders* itself did not address the RRA or the Board’s refusal to reopen claims. *Sanders*, 430 U.S. at 107-08. Thus, *Roberts* is inapposite, and this Court should follow its earlier precedents permitting review of refusals to reopen.

III. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is arbitrary and capricious under the APA.

The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application was arbitrary and capricious because it failed to consider relevant evidence and misapplied the Board’s regulations. The APA requires that agency decisions be “based on consideration of the relevant factors.” *Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe*, 401 U.S. 402, 416 (1968). The Board’s refusal to reopen was not based on consideration of the relevant factors because it ignored evidence of administrative error in the denial of Salinas’s 2006 application. *See* 20 C.F.R. § 261.2 (1965) (permitting reopening for administrative error). The Board’s refusal to reopen was therefore arbitrary and capricious under the APA.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Fifth Circuit and remand with instructions to review the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application.
