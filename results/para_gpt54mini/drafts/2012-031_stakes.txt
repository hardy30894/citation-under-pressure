**Argument**

The court of appeals correctly held that the district court may certify this antitrust class only if Plaintiffs proved, by admissible evidence, that damages are susceptible to classwide measurement. Rule 23 does not permit certification on speculation. It requires a “rigorous analysis” of whether the prerequisites are actually satisfied, and that analysis may “entail some overlap with the merits.” Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 351, 354 (2011); In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). If a plaintiff cannot show that damages are capable of common proof, predominance fails. Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013); Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623-24 (1997).

I. **Rule 23 requires proof, not assumptions, that damages are measurable on a classwide basis.**

This Court has long required that the Rule 23(b)(3) predominance inquiry be grounded in evidence. In Hydrogen Peroxide, the Third Circuit held that “the requirements set out in Rule 23 are not mere pleading rules” and that the district court must resolve factual disputes relevant to certification by a preponderance of the evidence. 552 F.3d at 320. Likewise, Wal-Mart explained that a district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to determine whether Rule 23’s requirements have been met. 564 U.S. at 351. These decisions foreclose class certification where the proponent offers no admissible, classwide damages model tied to the theory of liability actually certified.

That is precisely what this Court later held in Comcast. The Court reversed certification because the plaintiffs’ damages model “did not isolate damages” attributable to the sole theory of antitrust impact that remained in the case. 569 U.S. at 36. Where the model “identif[ies] damages resulting from” different theories, it cannot establish that damages are “capable of measurement on a classwide basis.” Id. at 35-36. The same principle governs here: Plaintiffs must show that a common method can measure only the damages resulting from the certified antitrust theory. Id. at 34-35.

II. **The district court may not certify a class when the damages showing depends on inadmissible or unreliable expert evidence.**

Rule 23’s evidentiary burden is not satisfied by an expert report that would be inadmissible or unreliable at trial. In Hydrogen Peroxide, the Third Circuit held that the district court must “resolve all factual or legal disputes relevant to class certification,” even if they overlap with the merits. 552 F.3d at 307, 319-20. This Court in Comcast likewise required a damages model capable of proving classwide injury through common proof, not one resting on generalized assumptions. 569 U.S. at 34-35. And in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993), the Court made clear that expert testimony must be both relevant and reliable to be admissible.

The certification inquiry thus demands more than a proposed methodology in the abstract. If the proffered damages model is not admissible or does not fit the theory of liability, it cannot carry the plaintiffs’ Rule 23 burden. See Wal-Mart, 564 U.S. at 350-51; Comcast, 569 U.S. at 34-36. A district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class on the basis of a model that fails to measure the damages attributable to the common injury alleged. Comcast, 569 U.S. at 36.

III. **The respondent’s theory is consistent with antitrust and class-action precedent.**

Antitrust class actions are not exempt from Rule 23’s demands. Although antitrust injury may often be proved with common evidence, the plaintiffs must still show that the injury and damages are susceptible to classwide proof. See Amchem, 521 U.S. at 623-24; Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 311-12. The Third Circuit’s approach respects that distinction: it permits certification only when common evidence establishes both impact and a methodology for damages. 552 F.3d at 323-24. Comcast later confirmed that a class cannot be certified where the damages model is “indisputably not limited to” the certified theory. 569 U.S. at 36.

The rule urged by Respondent therefore does not heighten the merits burden beyond Rule 23. It simply enforces the settled requirement that plaintiffs show, with admissible evidence, that damages can be measured on a classwide basis. Wal-Mart, 564 U.S. at 351; Comcast, 569 U.S. at 34-36. Without that showing, certification would convert Rule 23(b)(3) from a threshold inquiry into a presumption of manageability, contrary to the Supreme Court’s decisions in Amchem, 521 U.S. at 623-24, and Comcast, 569 U.S. at 34-36.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
