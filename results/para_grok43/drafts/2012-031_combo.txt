The district court’s certification order cannot stand. Under Rule 23(b)(3), predominance demands that questions common to the class predominate, a requirement that cannot be satisfied by speculation that damages may someday be measured on a class-wide basis. See Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921) (class treatment proper only when “the questions at issue are common to all”); Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302 (1853) (same). Plaintiffs bear the burden of demonstrating, by admissible evidence, that such common proof exists. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 44-45 (1940).

That burden was not met. Antitrust damages must be established with reasonable certainty and may not rest on conjecture. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931); Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). Where, as here, the proposed methodology fails to isolate the effects of the challenged conduct from other market variables, individual inquiries necessarily predominate. See Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp., 370 U.S. 690, 697 (1962); Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962).

Expert evidence offered to bridge this gap must itself satisfy threshold standards of admissibility. Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). The district court’s acceptance of an untested regression model that concededly could not quantify damages attributable solely to the alleged overbuilder deterrence violated this rule. Without competent proof that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement, certification necessarily rested on an erroneous legal standard. See Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) (due process requires adequate demonstration that representative litigation will fairly adjudicate the claims presented).

The same defect appears in the court’s treatment of the geographic market. Absent evidence that the asserted market definition can be proved through common facts rather than individualized proof, predominance fails. United States v. Yellow Cab Co., 338 U.S. 338, 341 (1949); United States v. Trenton Potteries Co., 273 U.S. 392, 395 (1927). Because plaintiffs supplied no such evidence, the certification order must be reversed.
