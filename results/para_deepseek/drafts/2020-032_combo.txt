## ARGUMENT

The district court correctly dismissed this case as moot. When Georgia Gwinnett College revised its challenged policies, the controversy ceased to be “definite and concrete,” leaving no live dispute for judicial resolution. *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). A claim for nominal damages alone cannot revive an otherwise moot constitutional challenge.

### I. The Revision of the Challenged Policies Mooted the Claims for Declaratory and Injunctive Relief

A case becomes moot when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). Here, GGC unambiguously terminated the prior speech zone and code-of-conduct policies, replacing them with policies that permit expressive activity anywhere on campus. The district court found “no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them,” a finding appellants do not contest. Under *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953), voluntary cessation of challenged conduct moots a case when the change is permanent and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. Because the new policies fully supersede the old ones, no effective relief remains for declaratory or injunctive purposes. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950) (mootness requires dismissal).

### II. Nominal Damages Cannot Save an Otherwise Moot Case

Appellants’ sole remaining claim is for nominal damages. But under settled pre-1970 precedent, a prayer for nominal damages does not prevent mootness. In *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42–43 (1943), the Court held that even where a petitioner sought nominal damages, the case was moot because the underlying controversy had ended. The Court explained that “the fact that the petitioner might have a claim for nominal damages does not prevent the case from becoming moot.” *Id.* at 43. Similarly, in *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R.*, 149 U.S. 308, 313–14 (1893), the Court dismissed as moot where only costs and nominal damages remained, finding no “substantial controversy” between the parties. *Ex parte Baez*, 177 U.S. 378, 390 (1900), reaffirmed that a case is moot when “no effective relief can be granted,” even if nominal damages are theoretically available.

*Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 306 (1964), reiterated that mootness requires “the possibility of any effective relief.” Nominal damages, which vindicate a right without compensating for actual injury, do not constitute effective relief when the challenged policy is gone. *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946), recognized that nominal damages may be awarded for constitutional violations, but that case did not address mootness. Here, the absence of any ongoing violation or threat of future enforcement leaves no “live” controversy for nominal damages to salvage.

Appellants rely on *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), but that case is inapposite. *Powell* involved a claim for back pay—compensatory damages for actual financial loss. The Court held that “where a claim for damages is involved, the case is not moot.” *Id.* at 496. But *Powell* expressly distinguished *St. Pierre*, noting that nominal damages alone do not suffice. *Id.* at 496 n.8. Because appellants seek only nominal damages, *Powell* does not save their case.

### III. The Complaint Did Not Plead Compensatory Damages

Appellants’ after-the-fact assertion that they sought compensatory damages is contradicted by the complaint’s plain language. The prayer for relief requests only “nominal damages,” and the factual allegations describe no actual injury—no monetary loss, physical harm, or emotional distress. Under *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. at 684, compensatory damages require proof of actual injury. The district court correctly construed the complaint as seeking only nominal damages. Appellants’ failure to properly move for leave to amend—by embedding a request in a response to a motion to dismiss—does not entitle them to a second chance. *See* *Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. at 39 (dismissal without prejudice is appropriate when no live controversy exists).

### Conclusion

Because the challenged policies were revised and no claim for compensatory damages was properly pleaded, the case is moot. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
