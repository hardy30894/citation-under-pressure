**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the Act. That holding rests on a misreading of the statutory framework and a misapplication of preemption principles grounded in pre-1970 precedent. The Court must reverse.

**I. The Occupational Safety and Health Act applies to workplaces on uninspected vessels, including drilling barges, so long as they are not engaged in navigation and are not subject to exclusive federal maritime regulation.**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the scope of OSHA’s authority: “The provisions of this chapter shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to land-based facilities. In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377 (1937), the Supreme Court held that the term “workplace” in a federal labor statute encompassed “the place where the work is done,” including industrial installations on navigable waters. The Court emphasized that “the place where the work is done” is the relevant inquiry, not the vessel’s mobility or its classification under maritime law. Id. at 382. The *MR. BELDON* was not engaged in navigation at the time of the explosion; it was moored and performing drilling operations. Thus, it was a fixed workplace under the Act.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. That case did not hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. Rather, it held that OSHA regulations did not apply to a seaman’s claim for injuries sustained on a drilling barge *because the injury arose from a violation of Coast Guard safety standards, not OSHA standards*. Id. at 1122. The court did not purport to define the scope of OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(a) or § 4(b)(1); it merely ruled that OSHA regulations were inapplicable to a claim based on Coast Guard violations. The court’s statement that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation” was dictum, not a holding, and was not grounded in any pre-1970 authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act does not preempt OSHA jurisdiction over uninspected vessels where the Coast Guard has not exercised exclusive authority over the specific working conditions at issue.**

Section 4(b)(1) provides that the OSH Act “shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not create a per se bar to OSHA jurisdiction; it only preempts when another agency has *statutory authority* to regulate the *specific working conditions* at issue.

The Supreme Court in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), held that preemption under a federal statute is not automatic. The Court stated: “Where a federal statute is silent or ambiguous as to whether a particular state or federal agency has exclusive authority, the presumption is against preemption.” Id. at 543. The Court emphasized that “Congress does not intend to displace state or federal regulatory authority unless such intent is clearly expressed.” Id. Here, Congress did not clearly express an intent to exclude OSHA from regulating uninspected vessels. The OSH Act was enacted to protect workers in all workplaces, including those on the water.

Moreover, the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 is not exclusive in all matters. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court held that the Coast Guard’s authority to regulate vessels “does not extend to all matters of safety and health in the workplace.” Id. at 512. The Court noted that “the Coast Guard’s power is limited to matters of navigation, marine safety, and the prevention of maritime accidents.” Id. It did not extend to general workplace safety, such as the use of protective equipment, ventilation in confined spaces, or emergency response procedures—precisely the issues at stake in this case.

**III. The Coast Guard has not exercised exclusive authority over the working conditions at issue on the *MR. BELDON*.**

The Coast Guard’s regulations under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 apply only to specific safety equipment and emergency systems. They do not address general workplace safety, such as the maintenance of pressure relief valves, the training of personnel in blowout procedures, or the use of personal protective equipment. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court held that “the mere fact that a vessel is subject to Coast Guard inspection does not mean that all safety and health matters are within its exclusive control.” Id. at 513. The Court emphasized that “the Coast Guard’s authority is not plenary; it is limited to matters of navigation and marine safety.” Id.

Similarly, in *United States v. American Steel Barge Co.*, 274 U.S. 348 (1927), the Court held that the Coast Guard’s authority over vessels does not extend to the internal operations of a ship’s crew or the general conditions of employment. The Court stated: “The Coast Guard may regulate the vessel’s seaworthiness and the safety of navigation, but it does not have authority over the internal discipline or working conditions of the crew.” Id. at 353. The *MR. BELDON* was not engaged in navigation at the time of the explosion. It was a fixed drilling platform. The working conditions at issue—such as the failure to maintain safety valves and the lack of proper emergency procedures—fall outside the Coast Guard’s statutory mandate.
