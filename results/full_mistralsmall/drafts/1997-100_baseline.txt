**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE PROTECTS PETITIONER FROM RETRIAL OF THE PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IN THIS NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDING.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This constitutional protection bars not only successive prosecutions but also multiple punishments for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the Clause’s core concern is preventing the State from “mak[ing] repeated attempts to convict” a defendant, thereby subjecting him to “embarrassment, expense and ordeal” and compelling him to live in a “state of suspense” regarding his fate. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187–188 (1957).

In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial of a capital sentencing proceeding after the State failed to persuade the jury to impose the death penalty. The Court reasoned that the capital sentencing hearing bore “the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” because it was a “bifurcated proceeding” in which the State bore the burden of proof and the defendant enjoyed the right to remain silent and to confront witnesses. *Id.* at 438–440. The Court later extended this principle to noncapital sentencing proceedings in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial of a sentencing factor after the State failed to persuade the jury to impose the death penalty. *Id.* at 209–210.

The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* and *Rumsey* is unavailing. The Court below erroneously suggested that these cases are limited to capital sentencing proceedings, *Monge v. California*, 16 Cal.4th 826, 836 (1997), but the Supreme Court has never so held. *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994) (noting that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases but declining to “hold” that their reasoning was so limited). Indeed, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protections are not confined to capital cases. *See, e.g., United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 154 (1997) (applying double jeopardy principles to a noncapital sentencing proceeding).

The Court below also erred in suggesting that the procedural protections afforded in a Penal Code § 1025 trial are merely statutory and therefore do not implicate the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Monge*, 16 Cal.4th at 837. The Supreme Court has made clear that the Clause’s protections are not dependent on the source of the procedural safeguards. *See, e.g., Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438–439 (applying double jeopardy protections despite the fact that the procedural safeguards were statutory). Moreover, the Court below’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held only that the Due Process Clause does not require a formal sentencing hearing in noncapital cases. *Id.* at 245–246. It did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause, nor did it suggest that the State may retry sentencing allegations after an initial acquittal.

Finally, the Court below’s suggestion that the mental anguish caused by retrial of a prior conviction allegation is insufficient to warrant double jeopardy protection is unsupported by precedent. *Monge*, 16 Cal.4th at 838. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the “embarrassment, expense and ordeal” of retrial is precisely the harm the Double Jeopardy Clause seeks to prevent. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187–188.

II. THE CALIFORNIA COURT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH SUPREME COURT PRECEDENT AND THREATENS TO UNDERMINE THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE’S PROTECTIONS.

The California Supreme Court’s decision creates a direct conflict with the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*. By holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings, the Court below has eviscerated the Clause’s protections in a class of cases that the Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized as warranting protection. This Court should grant review to resolve this conflict and to reaffirm that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings.

Moreover, the Court below’s decision threatens to undermine the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protections in other contexts. If the State may retry a prior conviction allegation after an initial acquittal, it may also retry other sentencing factors after an initial acquittal, thereby subjecting defendants to repeated attempts to enhance their sentences. This is precisely the harm the Double Jeopardy Clause seeks to prevent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should grant the petition for a writ of certiorari and reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
