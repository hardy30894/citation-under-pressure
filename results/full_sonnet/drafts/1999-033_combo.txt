ARGUMENT

I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE PROTECTS AGAINST DISCRIMINATION AMONG SIMILARLY SITUATED CLASSES, NOT AGAINST ISOLATED DECISIONS AFFECTING A SINGLE INDIVIDUAL

From its inception, this Court's equal protection jurisprudence has been concerned with classifications that treat groups of similarly situated persons differently, not with the treatment of a single litigant in isolation. In Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), the Court explained that the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee is directed against unequal and partial legislation "which, in carrying out a public purpose, is limited in its application" to some persons and not others "under like circumstances." Id. at 32. That formulation presupposes a comparison between classes of persons, not a comparison between a lone plaintiff and every other individual who ever received different treatment from government.

This Court reaffirmed that structure in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), where the offending administrative practice was condemned because it worked "a practical denial" of equal protection to "the class of persons" to which the petitioner belonged. Id. at 373-74. Yick Wo did not hold that any individual disparity in municipal permitting decisions offends the Constitution; it held that systematic discrimination against an identifiable class did so.

The classification requirement was made explicit in F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia, 253 U.S. 412 (1920), where the Court stated the enduring test: a classification "must be reasonable, not arbitrary, and must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike." Id. at 415 (emphasis added). The phrase "similarly circumstanced" identifies the relevant unit of equal protection analysis as a class of persons sharing a characteristic, not a single property owner comparing herself to her neighbors on an ad hoc basis.

This Court's later cases confirm that intentional, class-based discrimination is the touchstone of an equal protection violation. In Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court held that the erroneous or even unequal application of a facially valid law "is not a denial of equal protection of the laws unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." Id. at 8. Snowden makes clear that a plaintiff must show more than a single instance of differential treatment; she must show a purposeful discriminatory design of the kind historically associated with classifications such as race, alienage, or other group characteristics. Absent an alleged class, there is no framework within which to test whether treatment was "unequal" in the constitutional sense.

II. RECOGNIZING A "CLASS OF ONE" THEORY WOULD ERASE THE DEFERENCE THIS COURT HAS AFFORDED TO ROUTINE GOVERNMENT LINE-DRAWING

Even where classifications indisputably exist, this Court has repeatedly instructed that the Equal Protection Clause tolerates substantial line-drawing by government bodies, particularly in matters of economic and regulatory judgment such as the terms on which a municipality extends utility service. In Railway Express Agency v. New York, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court upheld a traffic regulation against an equal protection challenge, explaining that "it is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." Id. at 110. If government may address one problem while ignoring others of the same kind without violating equal protection, it follows a fortiori that government may impose a particular easement requirement on one property without thereby committing a constitutional violation merely because another parcel received a different requirement in the past.

The Court applied the same deferential principle in Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc., 348 U.S. 483 (1955), holding that a legislature "may select one phase of one field and apply a remedy there, neglecting the others," and that such a choice offends neither due process nor equal protection. Id. at 489. And in Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464 (1948), the Court cautioned against using equal protection to override the "specific instances of injustice" that inevitably arise from any general regulatory scheme. Id. at 466. Tigner v. Texas, 310 U.S. 141 (1940), likewise recognized that equal protection "does not require identity of treatment" but only that classifications be free from invidious discrimination. Id. at 147.

These principles carry particular force in the land-use and municipal-services context. In Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365 (1926), this Court held that municipal regulation of property use is entitled to a strong presumption of validity, sustainable "unless it is clearly arbitrary and unreasonable, having no substantial relation to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare." Id. at 395. A municipality's determination of the easement width necessary to safely extend water service is precisely the sort of discretionary, technical judgment Euclid instructs courts not to second-guess through constitutional litigation.

III. A "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION WOULD CONVERT EVERY DISCRETIONARY MUNICIPAL DECISION INTO A FEDERAL CONSTITUTIONAL CASE

The cumulative teaching of Barbier, Yick Wo, Royster Guano, Snowden, Railway Express, Williamson, Goesaert, Tigner, and Euclid is that equal protection doctrine has always operated through the lens of classes and classifications. To permit a plaintiff who disclaims membership in any class to proceed solely on the theory that she, individually, received different treatment than some other individual would untether equal protection from its historical and doctrinal moorings. It would transform every discretionary decision by every local official—every easement, every permit condition, every zoning variance—into a potential federal question, contrary to the deference this Court has consistently accorded to local governmental line-drawing. The judgment below should be reversed.
