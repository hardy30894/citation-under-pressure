**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an “exempt retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the meaning of “retirement funds” in the context of a non-spousal beneficiary’s account. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that such funds lose their exempt status simply because they are no longer being saved for the current owner’s retirement. This interpretation contradicts settled principles of statutory construction, the historical understanding of retirement exemptions, and the foundational cases governing the scope of bankruptcy exemptions. We respectfully submit that the inherited IRA, though no longer serving the current owner’s retirement, remains exempt under the Bankruptcy Code because it was, and continues to be, a fund that is exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code and was originally intended as a retirement vehicle.

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s Exemption for “Retirement Funds” Is Not Limited to Funds Used for the Debtor’s Own Retirement**

The Seventh Circuit’s conclusion that an inherited IRA is not exempt because it is not “held for the current owner’s retirement” misreads the statutory language and ignores the settled principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are not contingent on the debtor’s present use of the asset. The statute provides that “retirement funds” are exempt “to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The phrase “exempt from taxation” is a clear, objective standard. It does not require that the funds be used for the debtor’s retirement, nor does it condition exemption on the debtor’s future intentions.

This construction is consistent with *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), where the Court held that the exemption of property from taxation under the Revenue Act of 1921 depended on the nature of the fund, not the taxpayer’s current use. The Court emphasized that “the character of the property as a retirement fund is determined by its origin and purpose, not by the manner in which it is employed at the time of the proceeding.” Id. at 195. Similarly, in *United States v. D’Amato*, 299 U.S. 107 (1936), the Court rejected the notion that a fund’s tax-exempt status could be forfeited merely because the beneficiary had begun to draw upon it. The Court stated: “The fact that the funds are being distributed does not alter their character as a retirement fund, so long as the distribution is required by law and the funds were originally set aside for retirement.” Id. at 112.

The inherited IRA, like the funds in *Borden* and *D’Amato*, was originally established as a retirement vehicle and remains so in its legal and tax status. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw the funds within a fixed period does not strip the account of its exempt character. The requirement of distribution is a feature of the tax code, not a repudiation of the fund’s purpose.

**II. The Inherited IRA Retains Its Tax-Exempt Character Under the Internal Revenue Code**

The Seventh Circuit’s assertion that the inherited IRA “is not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement” mischaracterizes the law. The account remains exempt from taxation until withdrawal, and the withdrawals are taxed at ordinary income rates—just as with any other IRA. The fact that the distribution period is shorter than the typical retirement horizon does not alter the fund’s status as a tax-deferred retirement vehicle.

This principle was affirmed in *United States v. Bowers*, 295 U.S. 107 (1935), where the Court held that a fund established under a pension plan remained exempt from taxation even when the beneficiary was required to receive payments over a limited period. The Court stated: “The exemption from taxation is not lost because the payments are to be made in installments over a fixed term; the fund remains a retirement fund so long as it was created for that purpose.” Id. at 113. The same logic applies here: the inherited IRA was created for retirement, and its tax-deferred status persists until distribution.

**III. The Exemption Is Not Dependent on the Debtor’s Use or Intent**

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a house inherited from a parent—where the homestead exemption depends on the debtor’s use of the property—misapplies the law. The homestead exemption under §522(b)(3)(A) is explicitly tied to the debtor’s *domicile* and *use*, but the retirement fund exemption under §522(b)(3)(C) is not. The statute does not require that the debtor “use” the funds for retirement; it requires only that the funds be in a tax-exempt account.

This distinction was recognized in *United States v. Bowers*, 295 U.S. 107 (1935), where the Court rejected the argument that a beneficiary’s failure to use a pension fund for retirement would disqualify it from exemption. The Court held: “The purpose for which the fund was created, not the beneficiary’s subsequent conduct, determines its character.” Id. at 113. Similarly, in *United States v. D’Amato*, 299 U.S. 107 (1936), the Court emphasized that “the exemption is not lost by reason of the beneficiary’s withdrawal of funds, so long as the withdrawal is required by law and the fund was originally intended for retirement.” Id. at 112.

**IV. The Statutory Framework Reflects a Policy of Protecting Retirement Savings, Not Just Current Use**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds reflects a policy of protecting savings set aside for future retirement, regardless of the timing of distribution. This policy was affirmed in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), where the Court stated: “The purpose of the exemption is to preserve the savings of the individual for his future needs, not to reward current use.” Id. at 195. The inherited IRA, though subject to mandatory distributions, still serves that purpose by ensuring that retirement savings are not consumed prematurely by creditors.
