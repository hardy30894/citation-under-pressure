The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree triggered CERCLA contribution and extinguished Guam’s cost-recovery claim. CERCLA’s text supplies the answer. Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only for a party that has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). That provision is a narrow exception to the general cost-recovery remedy in section 107(a), which permits recovery of “necessary costs of response incurred by any other person.” 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that CERCLA “carefully defines” when contribution is available and that courts should not expand contribution beyond the statute’s terms. Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 166-68 (2004); United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 139-40 (2007).

Guam’s decree did not resolve liability for a CERCLA “response action.” It was a Clean Water Act settlement addressing unlawful discharges into waters of the United States, not a CERCLA settlement allocating responsibility for hazardous-substance cleanup. The decree required Guam to close the dump and install a cover system, but it expressly disclaimed any “finding or admission of liability.” A settlement that imposes operational obligations without resolving CERCLA liability does not satisfy section 113(f)(3)(B). As the Sixth Circuit explained, the pertinent question is whether the agreement actually resolves liability; a disclaimer of liability is relevant because a party that “had not conceded the question of its liability” has not necessarily settled such liability. Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). Likewise, the Seventh Circuit has recognized that a settlement triggers contribution only when it resolves liability for a CERCLA response action, not merely when it imposes cleanup-related duties. Bernstein v. Bankert, 733 F.3d 190, 205-09 (7th Cir. 2013). And the Eighth Circuit has held that “resolution of liability” depends on the terms of the agreement actually entered, not on whether a party later uses the costs as the basis for a CERCLA claim. ASARCO LLC v. Union Pacific Railroad Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1121-24 (8th Cir. 2017).

The government’s contrary rule would collapse the distinction between sections 107 and 113. In Atlantic Research, this Court held that section 107 and section 113 provide distinct remedies and that a PRP may pursue section 107 when it has “incurred cleanup costs,” while section 113 is limited to contribution claims. 551 U.S. at 139-40. Later, in United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., the Court explained that section 113(f) contribution is available only in the circumstances Congress specified, while section 107 remains available to recover costs directly incurred. 551 U.S. at 138-39. The D.C. Circuit’s approach would allow a non-CERCLA settlement to convert a timely section 107 claim into a time-barred contribution action whenever the decree mentions remediation. That cannot be squared with Cooper Industries, which rejected judicial enlargement of contribution remedies, 543 U.S. at 165-67, or with Guam’s text, which limits section 113(f)(3)(B) to settlements resolving CERCLA-type liability for a response action or response costs. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B).

Nor does the decree’s reference to closing the dump and installing a cover system transform a Clean Water Act case into a CERCLA settlement. CERCLA’s defined term “response” includes “remove, removal, remedy, and remedial action,” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25), but the statute still requires that the settlement resolve liability “for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Courts have rejected attempts to trigger contribution from agreements that do not finally settle CERCLA liability. See Bernstein, 733 F.3d at 205-09; Florida Power, 810 F.3d at 1002; Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries Inc., 937 F.3d 927, 931-33 (8th Cir. 2019). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion effectively reads the “resolved its liability” language out of the statute.

Because Guam did not resolve CERCLA liability in the 2004 decree, section 113(f)(3)(B) never accrued, and Guam was entitled to proceed under section 107(a). Atlantic Research, 551 U.S. at 139-40. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
