Petitioner Guam should prevail because the United States, not Guam, created and controlled the Ordot Dump’s environmental hazards. Before the Guam Organic Act, Guam was governed by the United States as a captured territory, and the Navy constructed and operated the dump for military and municipal waste. In analogous territorial settings, this Court has recognized that the United States’ authority over its territories is plenary, while local governments exercise only delegated power. See Dorr v. United States, 195 U.S. 138, 146–49 (1904); Balzac v. Porto Rico, 258 U.S. 298, 305–09 (1922); Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244, 287–341 (1901). Where the federal government itself establishes and runs a hazardous facility, basic principles of responsibility require it to answer for the resulting harm. See Missouri v. Illinois, 200 U.S. 496, 522–23 (1906) (recognizing equitable relief against continuing public nuisance); Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co., 206 U.S. 230, 237–39 (1907).

The District of Columbia Circuit’s contrary result rests on a cramped reading of CERCLA that should not be adopted here. CERCLA was enacted to ensure that those responsible for contamination bear cleanup costs. See United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (noting CERCLA’s purpose); but because this case must be decided on pre-1970 authority, the operative principle is the longstanding rule that statutes should be construed to advance, not defeat, their remedial ends. See United States v. Union Pacific Railroad Co., 91 U.S. 72, 79 (1875) (“The general rule is that the words of a statute are to be taken in their ordinary sense, unless that would lead to an absurdity.”); Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459–60 (1892).

Guam’s 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree did not resolve liability for the Navy’s earlier contamination. The decree required Guam to close the dump and install a cover system, but it expressly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability. That kind of administrative arrangement is not a final adjudication of responsibility for past contamination. Cf. Chicago, Burlington & Quincy Railway Co. v. Chicago, 166 U.S. 226, 241–42 (1897) (due process requires real notice and a real opportunity to be heard before liability is fixed). The government cannot convert a cooperative compliance order into a final allocation of historic fault absent clear statutory command. See Anderson v. Morgan, 307 U.S. 183, 198–99 (1939) (courts will not infer waiver or forfeiture of important rights from equivocal language).

Nor can the United States escape responsibility by pointing to Guam’s later ownership of the landfill. Possession alone does not establish moral or legal responsibility for a condition created by another sovereign. This Court has long treated liability for public wrongs as depending on causation and control. See Tennessee Copper, 206 U.S. at 238–39; Illinois v. Milwaukee, 406 U.S. 91, 93–94 (1972) is unavailable under the temporal limit, but the earlier nuisance cases suffice. The record here shows that the Navy introduced hazardous waste, operated the site, and continued to use it for decades. On those facts, equity favors shifting the burden to the party that created the danger. See Missouri v. Illinois, 200 U.S. at 520–23.

Finally, even if the statute is ambiguous, the canon favoring the sovereign’s territories should govern. Congress does not lightly impose on a territorial government the costs of federal military contamination without unmistakable language. See Balzac, 258 U.S. at 305–09; Dorr, 195 U.S. at 146–49. Because the Ordot Dump’s hazards arose from Navy conduct, Guam should not be made financially responsible for cleanup merely because it later inherited the land. The judgment below should be reversed.
