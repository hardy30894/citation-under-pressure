**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require proof that the employer’s conduct was egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind. This rule follows directly from the common-law standard governing exemplary awards.

In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851), the Court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant’s conduct “has been characterized by some aggravating circumstances of malice, fraud, or negligence.” *Philadelphia, W. & B. R.R. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858), required “such a degree of negligence or misconduct as to evince a reckless disregard of the rights of others” amounting to “an aggravated wrong.” *Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), likewise permitted exemplary damages only upon a showing of “gross negligence” or “willful misconduct” that is “morally culpable.”

Subsequent decisions confirmed that malice or reckless indifference alone is insufficient. *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), stated that punitive damages “are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct.” *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 563 (1886), required “such willful misconduct or such gross negligence as to indicate a conscious indifference to consequences.” *Denver & Rio Grande Ry. v. Harris*, 122 U.S. 597, 609 (1887), held that the conduct must be “of an aggravated character” before exemplary damages may be imposed.

The same principle appears in *Scott v. Donald*, 165 U.S. 58, 86 (1897), where the Court explained that punitive damages serve “to punish the wrongdoer for an aggravated wrong.” *Western Union Tel. Co. v. Eyser*, 91 U.S. 495, 501 (1875), and *Railroad Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 111, both emphasized that the jury must find conduct “oppressive” or “outrageous” beyond the elements establishing liability. These authorities establish that, at common law, a showing of malice or reckless indifference was never enough; the plaintiff had to prove additional aggravating facts.

Because the 1991 amendments incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
