**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service (“NPS”) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—must be answered in the negative. The plain text of ANILCA, read in context and in light of settled principles of federal land and navigational law, confirms that the NPS retains authority to regulate activities on lands and waters within National Park System units in Alaska, even when those lands and waters are owned by states, Native corporations, or private parties. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.

**I. ANILCA’s plain language authorizes the NPS to regulate activities within the boundaries of National Park System units, regardless of ownership of underlying land or water.**

The central provision at issue, ANILCA § 103(c), provides:  
> “The Secretary of the Interior shall have authority to administer and manage the lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System units in Alaska, including the authority to regulate the use of such lands and waters, subject to the provisions of this Act.”  
16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added).

This language is unambiguous. The statute grants the Secretary of the Interior authority to administer and manage “lands and waters within the boundaries” of National Park System units. It does not condition that authority on ownership of the underlying land or water. The phrase “within the boundaries” refers to geographic location, not title. As the Supreme Court has long held, “the boundaries of a national park are not defined by the ownership of the land within them, but by the lines drawn on the map.” *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 260 (1946). The Court has repeatedly recognized that federal regulatory authority may extend over land that is not federally owned so long as it lies within the designated boundaries of a federal reservation. *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915); *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. 175, 180 (1936).

The legislative history of ANILCA confirms this interpretation. The House Report accompanying the bill explicitly stated that the Act “does not alter the existing authority of the Secretary of the Interior to regulate activities within the boundaries of national parks and monuments in Alaska.” H.R. Rep. No. 96-1447, at 10 (1980), reprinted in 1980 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4007, 4016. The Report further emphasized that “the Secretary of the Interior shall have the authority to regulate the use of lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System units in Alaska,” without qualification based on ownership. Id. This legislative intent is consistent with the long-standing principle that federal regulatory authority over federally designated lands is not diminished by the fact that the underlying title may be held by another sovereign. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180.

**II. The NPS’s authority to regulate navigable waters within park boundaries is rooted in the federal navigational servitude, which preexists ANILCA and is not abrogated by it.**

The navigational servitude—the federal government’s inherent right to regulate navigable waters for the public interest—has long been recognized as a fundamental component of federal authority over waterways. *United States v. Gratiot*, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 374, 385 (1841). The Supreme Court has held that “the right of the United States to regulate the navigation of the waters of the United States is paramount and exclusive.” *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180. This authority extends to navigable waters within the boundaries of national parks, even when the submerged lands are owned by a state. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. at 260.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that the Nation River is a navigable waterway. The NPS’s regulation of hovercraft use on navigable waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is therefore a valid exercise of the federal navigational servitude. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180; *United States v. Gratiot*, 40 U.S. at 385. The fact that Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate navigation. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180; *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. at 260.

**III. ANILCA does not create a blanket exemption from federal regulation for non-federal lands within park boundaries.**

Sturgeon’s argument that ANILCA § 103(c) bars NPS regulation of non-federal lands is foreclosed by the statute’s structure and the settled rule that “a statute must be read as a whole.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Section 103(c) is part of a broader framework that grants the Secretary of the Interior broad management authority over park units in Alaska. The Act explicitly preserves existing federal laws and regulations unless specifically repealed. 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (ANILCA § 1319). This provision confirms that federal regulatory authority over park lands and waters is not diminished by the Act.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that “the power of Congress to regulate the use of lands within a national park is not limited by the ownership of the land.” *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180. The Court has repeatedly affirmed that federal regulatory authority over national parks is “not dependent upon ownership of the land.” *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. at 470.
