ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Is Not Appealable

The State's appeal of the district court's October 27 Order must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The Order is not final, as it contemplates further proceedings, including a hearing to show cause why the State should not be held in contempt. See SEC v. Hickey, 322 F.3d 1123, 1127 (9th Cir. 2003) (holding that a civil contempt order is not appealable until sanctions are imposed). The State's argument that awaiting sanctions will not enable it to raise its challenges is unavailing, as it could have challenged the underlying consent decrees and receivership, which it did not appeal. See 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(2) (authorizing interlocutory appeal from order appointing receiver); see also United States v. Ayres, 166 F.3d 991, 995 (9th Cir. 1999) (noting that a litigant defending a contempt charge may not raise objections to the underlying order that it violated).

Furthermore, the October 27 Order is not appealable as a "post-judgment order" under United States v. State of Washington, 761 F.2d 1404 (9th Cir. 1985), as it is not a final adjudication of the contempt motion. See Stone v. City & County of San Francisco, 968 F.2d 850, 854-55 (9th Cir. 1992) (holding that an order is not final until the district court has adjudicated the contempt and imposed sanctions). The State's attempt to characterize the Order as an "affirmative injunction" or an order for the immediate payment of funds is also unpersuasive, as it was made pursuant to a previously unappealed order appointing the receiver. See FTC v. Overseas Unlimited Agency, Inc., 873 F.2d 1233, 1235 (9th Cir. 1989).

II. The District Court's Order Does Not Violate the PLRA

The State's claim that the district court's Order violates the PLRA is not properly before this court on direct appeal. Even if it were, the PLRA does not prohibit the district court's Order, which is narrowly tailored to remedy the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (requiring that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn, extend[s] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [is] the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation"); see also Brown v. Plata, is not directly applicable here but other cases provide guidance, e.g., Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000) (holding that the PLRA does not prohibit the modification of a consent decree to remedy ongoing constitutional violations).

The district court's Order is also consistent with the PLRA's requirement that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn." See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The Order is a necessary step to implement the Receiver's plans, which were approved by the district court without objection from the State. See Armstrong v. Schwarzenegger, 622 F.3d 1058, 1069-70 (9th Cir. 2010) (holding that a district court's order requiring state officials to take specific actions to remedy constitutional violations was narrowly drawn and consistent with the PLRA).

III. The State's Eleventh Amendment Claim Is Waived

The State's claim that the district court's Order violates its Eleventh Amendment immunity is also not properly before this court. By consenting to the underlying consent decrees and the receivership, the State has waived its Eleventh Amendment immunity. See Clark v. Barnard, 108 U.S. 436, 447 (1883) (holding that a state's voluntary appearance in a federal court constitutes a waiver of its Eleventh Amendment immunity); see also In re Lazar, 37 F.3d 313, 315-16 (7th Cir. 1994) (holding that a state's participation in a federal lawsuit can constitute a waiver of its Eleventh Amendment immunity).

Furthermore, the district court's Order is not a prohibited "suit" against the State, as it is a necessary step to enforce the consent decrees and remedy the unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908) (holding that a federal court may enjoin state officials from violating federal law, even if the state is not a party to the lawsuit); see also Frew ex rel. Frew v. Hawkins, 540 U.S. 431, 437 (2004) (holding that a consent decree entered into by state officials is not barred by the Eleventh Amendment).

IV. Mandamus Is Not Warranted

The State's petition for a writ of mandamus must be denied, as it has not met the stringent standards required for issuance of the writ. See Bauman v. United States Dist. Court, 557 F.2d 650, 654-55 (9th Cir. 1977) (listing the five factors to be considered in determining whether mandamus is appropriate). The State has an adequate remedy at law, as it can appeal a final order of the district court finding it in contempt. See Stone, 968 F.2d at 854-55. The district court's Order is not clearly erroneous, as it followed from consent orders and a receivership that had not been contested by the State. See Bauman, 557 F.2d at 655.

The cases cited above, including SEC v. Hickey, United States v. Ayres, FTC v. Overseas Unlimited Agency, Inc., Miller v. French, Armstrong v. Schwarzenegger, Clark v. Barnard, Ex parte Young, and Frew ex rel. Frew v. Hawkins, demonstrate that the district court's Order is not appealable, does not violate the PLRA, and is consistent with the Eleventh Amendment. The State's petition for a writ of mandamus must be denied, as it has not met the required standards.
