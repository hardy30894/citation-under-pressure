**ARGUMENT**

Patent law does not follow every case in which a patent happens to be mentioned. Section 1338(a) of Title 28 grants federal courts jurisdiction over cases "arising under" the patent laws, and this Court has long insisted that phrase be given a discriminating, not talismanic, construction. Vernon Minton's malpractice claim against his former lawyers is, and always was, a garden-variety Texas tort claim. That a patent-law question must be answered as one step in that state-law analysis does not transform the suit into a patent case for jurisdictional purposes.

**I. A State-Created Cause of Action Does Not Arise Under the Patent Laws Merely Because a Patent Question Is a Predicate to Relief.**

"A suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action." *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Minton's cause of action was created by Texas tort law, not by Title 35. This Court reached the identical conclusion in *Wilson v. Sandford*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 99 (1850), holding that a suit touching a patent right did not arise under the patent laws merely because a patent's scope had to be examined along the way. The Court adhered to that rule in *Hartell v. Tilghman*, 99 U.S. 547 (1879), treating a suit on a covenant in a patent license as a contract case despite a dispute over the patent's validity, and again in *Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 282 (1902), where a royalty dispute requiring construction of patent claims was nonetheless held to arise under state contract law. The source of the right sued upon, not the incidental presence of a patent question, fixes jurisdiction. Minton's right to recover from Gunn arises from the duty of care Texas law imposes on attorneys, not from the Patent Act.

**II. The Embedded Patent Question Here Is Not "Substantial" in the Sense This Court's Cases Require.**

Even where a state claim incorporates a federal question, jurisdiction attaches only when the case "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). This Court has cautioned against letting parties "open the door" to federal court by mere invocation of a federal issue, favoring instead "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936).

Judge Friendly's opinion in *T.B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu*, 339 F.2d 823 (2d Cir. 1964), applying the same "arising under" principle to another intellectual-property statute, held that a case arises under such an act only where the complaint seeks a remedy the act grants, "asserts a claim requiring construction of the Act," or presents a situation where "a distinctive policy of the Act requires that federal principles control the disposition of the claim." *Id.* at 828. None applies here. No one seeks a patent remedy; the '643 patent was already declared invalid, finally, by the Federal Circuit. Resolving the hypothetical experimental-use question in this malpractice suit will not alter that patent's status, bind future litigants, or affect uniform administration of the patent system—it is confined to a single, backward-looking case-within-a-case between Minton and his former counsel.

The contrast with genuinely substantial embedded issues is instructive. In *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180 (1921), jurisdiction lay because the plaintiff's right to relief depended on the constitutional validity of a federal statute governing countless other transactions nationwide. By contrast, in *Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co.*, 291 U.S. 205 (1934), a state-law claim that merely incorporated a federal safety standard as one alternative theory was held not to arise under federal law. Nothing of the forward-looking significance present in *Smith* is at stake in a suit asking only what a long-concluded federal case would have looked like with different advocacy.

**III. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb, Not Preserve, the Settled Division of Judicial Responsibility.**

Legal malpractice is a subject of quintessentially local concern, traditionally supervised by the courts and bars of the several States. Federal jurisdiction must rest on a genuine controversy of federal law, not one manufactured by the framing of a state complaint. *Cf. Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667 (1950). Sweeping every malpractice claim that touches a patent case into federal court would strip the States of their historic authority over attorney conduct without any gain to patent law's uniformity. *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255 (1897), reserved federal "arising under" jurisdiction for suits vindicating rights the patent statute itself creates, not derivative disputes about legal representation.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and this state-law malpractice action should proceed, as it must, in the courts of Texas.
