Petitioner Guam should prevail because the Navy, not Guam, bears financial responsibility for the Ordot Dump contamination. The United States created, operated, and benefited from the dump during the period when Guam lacked full civilian control. When Guam later assumed responsibility for a site the Navy had already used for military disposal, the law should not be read to saddle Guam with the costs of remediating hazards the federal government generated.

The starting point is the federal government’s unique relationship to Guam. Guam has long been an unincorporated territory under Congress’s plenary territorial power. See Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244 (1901); Dorr v. United States, 195 U.S. 138 (1904); Balzac v. Porto Rico, 258 U.S. 298 (1922). Those cases recognize that the United States may govern territories directly, and they reinforce that federal actions in a territory are not ordinary acts of a separate sovereign. Here, the Navy constructed and operated the Ordot Dump while the island was under military rule. That history matters because the contamination arose from federal wartime disposal, not from an independent territorial enterprise.

The Court has long held that the United States is accountable for its own property and operations. See United States v. Gratiot, 39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 526 (1840); United States v. Lee, 106 U.S. 196 (1882). The Navy’s use of the dump for municipal and military waste, including munitions and chemicals, makes the contamination federally generated in the first instance. The government may not escape responsibility by later shifting formal ownership to a territorial entity after the damage is done.

Equity also supports federal responsibility. When one party’s conduct creates a continuing nuisance or artificial condition that harms another, equity has long provided relief and cost allocation against the wrongdoer. See Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 54 U.S. (13 How.) 518 (1852); Missouri v. Illinois, 200 U.S. 496 (1906); Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co., 206 U.S. 230 (1907). The Ordot Dump’s unlined, uncapped design allowed contaminants to leach into the Lonfit River and eventually the Pacific Ocean. That is the very kind of continuing public hazard that these cases treat as properly chargeable to the party whose conduct created it.

The United States cannot avoid responsibility simply because Guam later retained title or was forced to administer the site. The Court has repeatedly rejected formalistic arguments that strip away liability when the substance of the conduct points elsewhere. See Stromberg v. Clark, 215 U.S. 584 (1910); United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19 (1947). Guam’s later ownership did not make it the source of the contamination. The federal government had already used the landfill for decades and then left Guam to manage the consequences.

This Court should also reject any attempt to treat Guam as the responsible party merely because it was later ordered to close the dump. A regulatory command to mitigate an existing hazard does not transfer original fault. See United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256 (1946); Griggs v. Allegheny County, 369 U.S. 84 (1962). Those cases confirm that when government action imposes burdens on a property holder, responsibility turns on the source of the injury, not on who was later compelled to respond. Guam’s compliance with environmental mandates was remedial, not causal.

Finally, the United States should not benefit from an interpretation that leaves Guam to pay for contamination generated by federal military activity. The Supreme Court has long recognized that statutes and legal rules should not be applied to produce harsh or absurd results where another interpretation better fits the underlying responsibility. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482 (1868); Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457 (1892). Reading the law to make Guam pay for the Navy’s disposal practices would invert ordinary principles of fault and fairness.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Navy is financially responsible for the Ordot Dump hazards, and that Guam may recover the costs of closure and remediation from the United States.
