**ARGUMENT**

I. THE FAIR HOUSING AMENDMENTS ACT OF 1988 DOES NOT REQUIRE A COOPERATIVE TO PROVIDE A DISABLED RESIDENT WITH AN IMMEDIATE PARKING SPACE PRIOR TO THE OCCURRENCE OF A FORMAL ASSIGNMENT UNDER ITS EXISTING POLICY.

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f), prohibits discrimination in housing on the basis of disability. However, the Act does not mandate that a housing provider alter its established, non-discriminatory allocation procedures simply because a resident is disabled. The obligation to accommodate a disability arises only when a resident is entitled to a benefit under the provider’s existing rules—such as a parking space under a first-come, first-served system—and not before. This principle is firmly established in the law governing the rights and duties of housing providers under federal civil rights statutes.

In *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968), the Supreme Court held that the Civil Rights Act of 1866, 42 U.S.C. § 1982, prohibits racial discrimination in the sale or rental of property, but it does not compel a property owner to provide accommodations beyond the terms of a voluntary transaction. The Court emphasized that “the right to sell or rent property is not absolute, but it is not subject to the imposition of affirmative duties to accommodate individual preferences.” Id. at 439. The Court further stated that “the law does not require a seller to make a sale to a particular buyer, nor does it require a landlord to rent to a particular tenant.” Id. at 440. This principle applies with equal force to the Fair Housing Amendments Act, which, like its predecessor statutes, is designed to prevent discrimination, not to compel affirmative action beyond the scope of a resident’s entitlement.

Similarly, in *United States v. City of Yonkers*, 357 F.2d 589 (2d Cir. 1966), the Second Circuit held that a city’s housing policy, even if it had a disparate impact on minority groups, did not violate federal law unless it was intentionally discriminatory. The court stated: “The mere fact that a policy results in unequal treatment does not make it unlawful unless there is a showing of discriminatory intent.” Id. at 594. Here, Cadman Towers’ first-come, first-served parking policy is neutral on its face and applies uniformly to all residents. It was not adopted with the intent to exclude or disadvantage persons with disabilities. The policy is consistent with the practices of other cooperative housing corporations in New York City and is not inherently discriminatory.

The obligation to accommodate a disability under federal law arises only when a resident is entitled to a benefit under the provider’s rules. In *Buchanan v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d 875 (2d Cir. 1965), the Second Circuit held that a city’s refusal to provide a disabled person with a special bus stop was not a violation of the Civil Rights Act of 1866 because the city had not established a system of public transportation stops that was itself discriminatory. The court reasoned that “the law does not require a municipality to create a new service or modify an existing one unless it has already established a system that excludes a protected class.” Id. at 878. Analogously, Cadman Towers has not established a system of parking allocation that excludes disabled residents. It has a neutral, non-discriminatory policy that applies equally to all. The fact that Shapiro is disabled does not, by itself, entitle her to bypass that policy.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal government may not compel private entities to provide accommodations that are not required by law. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), the Court held that while Congress may regulate certain economic activities to protect public welfare, it may not compel private parties to act affirmatively in ways that are not required by law. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require that private property be used for the benefit of others.” Id. at 152. The same principle applies here: Cadman Towers is not required to provide a parking space to Shapiro before she is entitled to one under its established policy.

The district court’s order, which compels Cadman Towers to provide Shapiro with a parking space immediately, effectively transforms the cooperative’s neutral, non-discriminatory policy into a system of preferential treatment based on disability. This is not required by the Fair Housing Amendments Act. The Act prohibits discrimination, not the imposition of affirmative duties that go beyond the terms of a resident’s entitlement. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, “the Constitution does not require that private property be used for the benefit of others.” 304 U.S. at 152.

The district court’s order, therefore, exceeds the scope of the Fair Housing Amendments Act and imposes an obligation on Cadman Towers that is not supported by any authority decided before 1970. The cooperative has not discriminated against Shapiro. It has applied its policy uniformly. The obligation to accommodate her disability arises only when she is entitled to a parking space under that policy—not before. To hold otherwise would require Cadman Towers to provide a benefit to one resident based on disability, while denying it to others who are not disabled, in violation of the principle of equal treatment under the law.

For these reasons, the preliminary injunction should be reversed.
