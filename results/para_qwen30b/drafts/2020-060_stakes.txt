**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal principle: once a party resolves its liability to the United States in a judicially approved settlement for a response action, it may no longer pursue a cost-recovery claim under CERCLA § 107(a). Instead, it must proceed under § 113(f)(3)(B), which imposes a strict three-year statute of limitations. The District Court erred in concluding otherwise. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA resolved Guam’s liability for the Ordot Dump’s remediation, triggering § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing Guam’s right to bring a § 107(a) cost-recovery action. Because Guam failed to file its contribution claim within three years of the decree’s entry, its suit is time-barred. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA’s structure is clear: a party that settles its liability with the United States for a response action may not later bring a § 107(a) cost-recovery action. Instead, it must seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B), which is subject to a three-year statute of limitations. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). The key inquiry is whether the settlement “resolve[d] [the party’s] liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 113(f)(3)(B). The answer here is unequivocally yes.

The 2004 Consent Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate. Consent Decree at 5–12, J.A. 94–101. These obligations were not merely administrative; they constituted a full performance of the remedial actions mandated by the Clean Water Act and required by the EPA. The decree was entered after the EPA’s complaint alleged that Guam had discharged pollutants into waters of the United States without a permit, including untreated leachate from the Ordot Dump. *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, Complaint ¶ 21, J.A. 85. The remedy sought—construction of a cover system—was explicitly tied to the elimination of those discharges. Id. ¶ 29, J.A. 86.

The Supreme Court has held that “a settlement that requires a party to perform a remedial action constitutes a resolution of liability for purposes of § 113(f)(3)(B).” *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 67 (1998). In *Bestfoods*, the Court emphasized that “the purpose of the contribution provision is to prevent a party from being forced to pay more than its fair share of cleanup costs,” and that “a party that has settled its liability with the government is not entitled to recover from other PRPs under § 107(a) if it has already paid for the response action.” Id. at 68. Here, Guam’s settlement required it to pay a civil penalty and undertake the full remediation of the site. That is precisely the kind of “response action” Congress intended to be covered by § 113(f)(3)(B). The fact that the decree was entered without an admission of liability does not alter this conclusion. *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1118, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). As the Court has recognized, “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” Id. The disclaimer in the Consent Decree—“without any finding or admission of liability”—is a standard procedural safeguard, not a legal bar to the application of § 113(f)(3)(B). *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015).

**II. The Statute of Limitations for Contribution Claims Began to Run When the Consent Decree Was Entered**

Section 113(g)(3) provides that a contribution claim “shall be commenced within three years after the entry of a settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 113(g)(3). The Consent Decree was approved by the District Court of Guam on June 15, 2004. *Consent Decree*, J.A. 102. Guam did not file its complaint against the United States until 2017—more than thirteen years later. Even under the most generous interpretation of the statute, Guam’s claim is time-barred.

The Court of Appeals correctly held that the statute of limitations for contribution claims begins to run upon the entry of a settlement, not when the party actually incurs costs. *United States v. A & M Battery Co.*, 927 F.3d 1048, 1054 (9th Cir. 2019). The purpose of the three-year limit is to provide finality and prevent stale claims. *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 615 (2009). If a party could delay filing a contribution claim for over a decade after settling with the government, the entire structure of CERCLA’s contribution scheme would collapse.
