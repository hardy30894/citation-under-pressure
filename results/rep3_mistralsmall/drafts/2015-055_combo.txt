**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Gregory Welch respectfully submits that the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate his sentence under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The Eleventh Circuit erred in refusing to apply *Johnson* retroactively because the Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson* announced a new substantive rule of constitutional law that must be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004) (holding that a new rule applies retroactively if it places “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the State’s power to proscribe”); *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion) (stating that a new rule applies retroactively if it “places a class of private conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law” or “requires the observance of those procedures that are ‘implicit in the concept of ordered liberty’”).

The Supreme Court has long held that a rule is substantive—and thus retroactive—if it alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes. *See Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 353 (1964) (holding that a judicial enlargement of criminal liability violates due process when applied retroactively); *Welch v. United States*, 356 U.S. 353, 356 (1958) (holding that a decision interpreting a criminal statute to exclude certain conduct from its scope applies retroactively). *Johnson* did precisely that by invalidating the residual clause of the ACCA as unconstitutionally vague. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Court held that the residual clause failed to provide fair warning of the conduct it proscribed and thus violated the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 2556. Because *Johnson* narrowed the scope of the ACCA by excluding certain conduct from its reach, it announced a substantive rule that must be applied retroactively. *See Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 353.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively—rests on an erroneous application of *Teague*. The court below reasoned that *Johnson* announced a procedural rule because it did not “place[] certain conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law.” *Welch v. United States*, 802 F.3d 1202, 1206 (11th Cir. 2015) (quoting *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351). But this misapprehends *Teague*’s framework. A rule is substantive if it alters the legal consequences of past conduct by narrowing the scope of a criminal statute. *See Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998) (holding that a decision interpreting a statute to require proof of a specific intent applies retroactively). *Johnson* did exactly that by invalidating the residual clause, which had been used to enhance sentences for conduct that did not clearly fall within the ACCA’s ambit. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556. The rule announced in *Johnson* thus applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that decisions interpreting criminal statutes to exclude certain conduct from their scope apply retroactively. *See United States v. Peltier*, 422 U.S. 531, 542 (1975) (plurality opinion) (holding that *Almendarez-Torres v. United States*, 523 U.S. 224 (1998), applied retroactively because it narrowed the scope of a criminal statute); *Welch*, 356 U.S. at 356 (holding that a decision interpreting a criminal statute to exclude certain conduct applies retroactively). *Johnson* did precisely that by invalidating the residual clause as unconstitutionally vague. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556. The rule announced in *Johnson* thus applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Peltier*, 422 U.S. at 542.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *Chaidez v. United States*, 568 U.S. 340 (2013), is misplaced. *Chaidez* held that *Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010), did not apply retroactively because *Padilla* announced a new procedural rule. *Chaidez*, 568 U.S. at 348. But *Johnson* announced a substantive rule because it narrowed the scope of the ACCA by invalidating the residual clause. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556. The rule announced in *Johnson* thus applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351.

In sum, the rule announced in *Johnson* applies retroactively to invalidate Petitioner’s sentence under the ACCA. The Eleventh Circuit erred in refusing to apply *Johnson* retroactively, and this Court should reverse the judgment below.
