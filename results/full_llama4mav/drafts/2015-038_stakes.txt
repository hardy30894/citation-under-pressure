The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit the National Park Service (NPS) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) supports the NPS's authority to regulate activities within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including non-federal lands.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon's interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute. The court found that the hovercraft ban is not a regulation that applies "solely to public lands within such units," as it affects activities on both federal and non-federal lands within CSUs. This interpretation is consistent with the statutory language, which does not limit the NPS's regulatory authority to federal lands within CSUs.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is also supported by the Supreme Court's precedent on statutory interpretation. In Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984), the Court established that when a statute is ambiguous, an agency's reasonable interpretation is entitled to deference. Here, the NPS's interpretation of § 103(c) is reasonable, as it is consistent with the statutory language and the legislative history of ANILCA.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with its prior holdings in Katie John III, 720 F.3d 1214 (9th Cir.2013), and Katie John I, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir.1995), which addressed the scope of federal regulatory authority under ANILCA. While these cases did not directly address the issue presented here, they demonstrate the court's understanding of the complex regulatory framework established by ANILCA.

Sturgeon's claim that the NPS's regulatory authority is limited to federal lands within CSUs is unpersuasive. The plain text of § 103(c) does not support this interpretation, and the legislative history of ANILCA does not suggest that Congress intended to limit the NPS's regulatory authority in this way. In fact, the House Committee report on the bill that became ANILCA indicates that the Secretary of the Interior was authorized to promulgate and enforce regulations applicable to CSUs, without limiting this authority to federal lands.

In addition, the NPS's regulatory authority is not limited by the fact that some lands within CSUs are owned by the State of Alaska or private parties. The Supreme Court has recognized that the federal government retains significant authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within national parks, even if the lands are owned by the state or private parties. See, e.g., United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc., 474 U.S. 121 (1985).

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that the NPS has regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs in Alaska, including the Yukon-Charley River National Preserve. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) and the legislative history of the statute support this interpretation, which is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent on statutory interpretation and the Ninth Circuit's prior holdings on the scope of federal regulatory authority under ANILCA.
