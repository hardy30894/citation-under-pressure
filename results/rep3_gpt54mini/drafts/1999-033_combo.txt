**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not authorize every disappointed property owner to convert a local permit dispute into a federal constitutional case. Its central command is that the government must govern alike persons who are similarly situated. *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885); *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886). That protection has long been understood to reach discrimination directed at a person because of membership in an identifiable class, or at least discrimination that is the product of an arbitrary classification. *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 112–13 (1949); *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147–48 (1940). It does not create a general license to challenge isolated acts of alleged governmental unfairness untethered to class-based discrimination.

This Court’s precedents have consistently rejected the notion that the Equal Protection Clause is a roving commission to second-guess all unequal outcomes. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944), the Court explained that unequal application of a law, without more, does not violate equal protection; there must be purposeful discrimination. Likewise, *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425–26 (1961), held that a statutory classification survives so long as it rests on a rational basis, confirming that equal protection concerns the validity of classifications, not individual grievances. And *Borden’s Farm Products Co. v. Baldwin*, 293 U.S. 194, 209 (1934), emphasized that a challenger must negate “every conceivable basis” that might support the governmental distinction. Those decisions presuppose an actual distinction drawn by the government, not a freestanding federal tort for spite.

Olech’s claim falls outside that settled doctrine. She does not allege that Willowbrook classified her by race, nationality, or any other protected status. She alleges only that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from some others. But “[m]ere errors of judgment by officials do not make a decision constitutionally infirm.” *Brail v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co.*, 256 U.S. 445, 449 (1921). Nor does the Constitution forbid all unequal treatment among individual applicants; local government necessarily exercises discretion in dealing with distinct parcels and circumstances. The Equal Protection Clause tolerates line-drawing if any rational basis exists. *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955); *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78–79 (1911).

The court below erred by relying on an alleged “spiteful effort” theory. That standard has no basis in the pre-1970 Equal Protection decisions. This Court has recognized that “the Constitution does not assure uniformity of decisions or immunity from merely erroneous action.” *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60, 80 (1917). Where the complaint amounts to nothing more than a claim that officials treated one person less favorably than another in a single instance, without alleging membership in a protected class or the creation of a cognizable classification, the claim is, at most, a dispute over arbitrary administration—not an equal protection violation.

The contrary rule would constitutionalize ordinary land-use and permitting disagreements. Local governments routinely impose parcel-specific conditions based on drainage, access, engineering, traffic, or safety. If a plaintiff may proceed merely by alleging that one applicant received a different demand than another, every individualized municipal decision becomes subject to federal litigation whenever a disappointed owner can plead resentment or bad motive. That is inconsistent with the Equal Protection Clause as construed in *Tigner*, 310 U.S. at 147–48, *Railway Express*, 336 U.S. at 112–13, and *McGowan*, 366 U.S. at 425–26, all of which permit governmental line-drawing so long as it is not invidious and has a rational basis.

Nor does the fact that Olech alleges a differential easement demand change the analysis. The Equal Protection Clause protects against intentional discrimination between similarly situated persons, but it does not require identical treatment of persons whose properties, histories, or municipal relationships may differ in material respects. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 8; *Lindsley*, 220 U.S. at 78–79. To state a constitutional claim, a plaintiff must identify a suspect classification or demonstrate that the government adopted an arbitrary classification lacking any rational justification. *Borden’s Farm Products*, 293 U.S. at 209; *Williamson*, 348 U.S. at 489. Olech has done neither.

Because the complaint alleges only that the Village imposed a heavier burden on one owner than on others, and because it disclaims any class-based discrimination, the Equal Protection Clause affords no cause of action. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
