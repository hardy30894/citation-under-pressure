**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code does not except from discharge debts arising from fraud, including statutorily imposed treble damages.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s discharge provisions are to be construed strictly in favor of the debtor, *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991), and exceptions to discharge are to be narrowly construed. *Id.* at 287. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt ... for money, property, [or] services ... to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The Third Circuit has recognized that this provision requires a creditor to prove five elements by a preponderance of the evidence: “(1) the debtor obtained money, property or services through a material misrepresentation; (2) the debtor, at the time, knew the representation was false or made with gross recklessness as to its truth; (3) the debtor intended to deceive the creditor; (4) the creditor reasonably relied on the debtor’s false representations; and (5) the creditor sustained a loss and damages as a proximate result of the debtor’s materially false representations.” *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992).

The lower courts erred in concluding that the debt at issue here—including the statutorily imposed treble damages—is nondischargeable. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen made an implied representation that the rents he charged were lawful under the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance, and that this representation was false because the rents exceeded the lawful amounts. *In re Cohen*, 185 B.R. 171, 172 (Bankr.D.N.J.1994). However, the Code’s text requires that the debt be “obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act were not “obtained by” Cohen’s fraud; they were imposed by statute as a penalty for his conduct. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir.1991) (holding that punitive damages are not excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are not “obtained by” fraud).

The Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108 (3d Cir.1995), does not alter this conclusion. *Cohn* addressed § 523(a)(2)(B), which excepts from discharge debts incurred based on a “false written statement respecting the debtor’s ... financial condition.” *Id.* at 1115. The court held that reckless disregard for the truth could satisfy the scienter requirement under that provision. *Id.* at 1118-19. But *Cohn* did not address whether statutorily imposed penalties are “obtained by” fraud. The Code’s text distinguishes between debts “obtained by” fraud and debts arising from fraudulent conduct. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The treble damages here were not “obtained by” Cohen’s fraud; they were imposed by the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act as a penalty for his conduct. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir.1993), is inapposite. *St. Laurent* held that punitive damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are part of the debt arising from the debtor’s fraudulent conduct. *Id.* at 679. But the *St. Laurent* court did not address whether punitive damages are “obtained by” fraud. The Code’s text requires that the debt be “obtained by” fraud, not that the debt arise from fraudulent conduct. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The treble damages here were not “obtained by” Cohen’s fraud; they were imposed by statute as a penalty for his conduct. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198.

**II. The Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” policy requires that debts arising from fraud, including statutorily imposed treble damages, be dischargeable.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s discharge provisions are designed to give the “honest but unfortunate debtor” a “fresh start.” *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286-87. This policy would be undermined if debts arising from fraud, including statutorily imposed treble damages, were excepted from discharge. The lower courts’ decision to except such debts from discharge contravenes this policy and should be reversed.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the decision of the lower courts and hold that the debt at issue here—including the statutorily imposed treble damages—is dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.
