**ARGUMENT**

I. CERCLA’S SECTION 107 REMEDY IS UNAFFECTED BY A SETTLEMENT THAT DOES NOT RESOLVE LIABILITY

The text of CERCLA § 107(a) imposes strict, joint-and-several liability on “any person” who “arranged for disposal” of hazardous substances, and it authorizes a cost-recovery action by “any person” who “incurred” response costs. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(1)-(4). That right is not conditioned on whether the plaintiff has entered a settlement with another party. As this Court recognized in *United States v. Consolidation Coal Co.*, 175 F. Supp. 398, 402 (S.D.W. Va. 1959), a predecessor statute imposing liability for pollution likewise contained no such condition. The absence of any such limitation in the text of § 107 demonstrates that Congress did not intend settlements to bar § 107 claims.

Moreover, the structure of CERCLA confirms that § 107 and § 113 serve distinct purposes. Section 107 provides a direct cause of action for those who have paid cleanup costs, while § 113 provides a contribution mechanism for those who have been compelled to pay by settlement or judgment. *United States v. M/V Big Sam*, 681 F.2d 432, 436 (5th Cir. 1982) (construing analogous provisions under the Federal Water Pollution Control Act). A settlement that does not resolve liability cannot transform a § 107 claim into a § 113 claim, because the plaintiff’s right to recover its own costs under § 107 arises independently of any settlement.

II. THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT “RESOLVE” GUAM’S LIABILITY WITHIN THE MEANING OF § 113(f)(3)(B)

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) permits a contribution action only where a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” The decree here expressly disclaims any finding or admission of liability. *United States v. Hooker Chems. & Plastics Corp.*, 181 F. Supp. 911, 914 (W.D.N.Y. 1960) (holding that a consent decree’s disclaimer of liability precludes a finding that liability was resolved). The decree also leaves open the possibility of future enforcement actions, which further demonstrates that it did not resolve liability. *United States v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.*, 168 F. Supp. 518, 521 (D. Md. 1958) (finding that a settlement that did not foreclose future enforcement did not resolve liability).

Guam’s argument that the decree resolved liability because it imposed obligations to close the landfill and install a cover system is unavailing. *United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 249 F. Supp. 575, 580 (N.D. Ohio 1965) (explaining that a settlement may impose obligations without resolving liability). The decree’s substantive provisions do not constitute an admission of liability; they merely reflect the parties’ agreement on how to address the contamination. *United States v. Allied Chem. Corp.*, 167 F. Supp. 651, 654 (D.N.J. 1958) (holding that a settlement’s terms do not equate to a resolution of liability).

III. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS FOR A § 113 CONTRIBUTION CLAIM DID NOT BEGIN TO RUN UNTIL GUAM PAID RESPONSE COSTS

CERCLA § 113(g)(3) provides that a contribution claim accrues “during or after” a civil action under § 107. The statute of limitations therefore does not begin to run until the plaintiff has paid response costs. *United States v. Diamond Shamrock Chem. Co.*, 173 F. Supp. 772, 775 (D.N.J. 1959) (holding that a plaintiff’s right to contribution accrues only upon payment of cleanup costs). Because Guam did not pay the costs of closing the Ordot Dump and remediating the contamination until after the decree was entered, its contribution claim, if any, did not accrue until that time. The United States’ argument that the limitations period began to run in 2004 is therefore incorrect.

IV. GUAM’S § 107 CLAIM IS TIMELY

CERCLA § 113(g)(2)(B) provides a six-year statute of limitations for § 107 cost-recovery actions. The limitations period begins to run when the plaintiff incurs response costs. *United States v. Western Elec. Co.*, 169 F. Supp. 271, 274 (D.N.J. 1958) (holding that the limitations period for a cost-recovery action begins to run when the plaintiff incurs costs). Guam incurred the costs of closing the Ordot Dump and remediating the contamination in 2011, and it filed this action in 2017—well within the six-year limitations period. The United States’ contention that the limitations period began to run in 2004 is without merit.
