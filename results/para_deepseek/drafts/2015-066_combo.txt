**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is the bedrock of our adversarial system. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963). In any federal prosecution, the Constitution guarantees that no person shall be convicted unless he had, or knowingly waived, the assistance of counsel. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938). This guarantee is fundamental to a fair trial, *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), and exists to ensure the reliability of the guilt-determining process.

Section 117(a) makes the existence of two prior domestic violence convictions an essential element of the federal offense. The government must prove this element beyond a reasonable doubt before a defendant may suffer the enhanced punishment of a felony conviction. No conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment may serve as that element. This Court held in *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), that a prior conviction obtained without counsel is constitutionally invalid and "could not be used to support guilt or enhance punishment" for a later offense. Id. at 115. Because the predicate convictions under § 117(a) function as an element of guilt—not a mere sentencing factor—*Burgett* applies with full force. Indeed, *Burgett* directly involved a recidivist statute in which the prior conviction "aided in the conviction" of the later charge. Id.

The government cannot escape *Burgett* by pointing to the tribal provenance of the prior convictions. The Sixth Amendment governs *this* federal prosecution. When the federal government relies on a prior judgment to prove an element of a federal crime, the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment demands that the prior judgment be reliable and valid. A judgment rendered without counsel is inherently unreliable. *Thompson v. City of Louisville*, 362 U.S. 199 (1960), requires that every element be supported by some evidence; a void judgment is no evidence at all. Using such a judgment as the predicate for a federal felony also violates the settled rule that a statute may not presume a fact—here, the existence of valid prior convictions—where there is no rational connection between the fact proved and the ultimate fact presumed. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463 (1943). The bare fact that a defendant once appeared in a tribunal where the Sixth Amendment did not apply tells us nothing about whether he received the basic protections that make a state or federal conviction trustworthy for later guilt purposes.

Recidivist statutes have long been upheld, but only because they punish a defendant for a new crime after he has been lawfully convicted in a proceeding that respected his fundamental rights. *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311 (1901); *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912). These cases rest on the premise that the prior judgment is a valid judgment—a solemn adjudication of guilt by a court of competent jurisdiction adhering to the rule of law. The tribal court proceedings at issue here, while valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act, did not afford Bryant the assistance of counsel. A judgment that lacks the protections the Sixth Amendment requires cannot be transformed into a predicate for a new federal felony. Because the right to counsel is a fundamental protection without which no prosecution can be deemed fair, *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968), the government may not use a proceeding that lacks its core guarantee to bootstrap a defendant into a federal felony conviction on an element it cannot otherwise prove. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
