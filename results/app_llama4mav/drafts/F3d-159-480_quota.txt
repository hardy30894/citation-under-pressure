## ARGUMENT

### A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Mr. Pearson challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, arguing that Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause in enacting this statute. The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states, but this power is not unlimited. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549 (1995). In Lopez, the Supreme Court held that a federal statute regulating the possession of a firearm near a school was unconstitutional because it did not have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Id. at 561.

The Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, prohibits robbery or extortion that "obstructs, delays, or affects commerce or the movement of any article or commodity in commerce." To determine whether Congress had the authority to enact this statute, we must examine whether the regulated activity has a substantial effect on interstate commerce. United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 609 (2000). The government argues that the robbery of Mr. Goodcents affected interstate commerce because the store purchases goods from out-of-state companies. However, this connection is too attenuated to justify federal regulation under the Commerce Clause. United States v. Wang, 222 F.3d 234, 239-40 (6th Cir. 2000).

The Supreme Court has held that Congress may regulate activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce, even if the activity is not itself interstate in nature. Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 125 (1942). However, the Court has also emphasized that there are limits to Congress's power under the Commerce Clause. Lopez, 514 U.S. at 558-59. In this case, the government presented evidence that Mr. Goodcents purchases goods from out-of-state companies, but this is not sufficient to establish a substantial effect on interstate commerce. United States v. Collins, 40 F.3d 95, 100-01 (5th Cir. 1994).

Furthermore, the jurisdictional element of § 1951 requires only a de minimis effect on interstate commerce. United States v. Bolton, 68 F.3d 396, 399 (10th Cir. 1995). However, this does not mean that Congress has the authority to regulate any activity that has some connection to interstate commerce. The Court has held that the Commerce Clause requires a "substantial effect" on interstate commerce, not just a minimal or incidental effect. Morrison, 529 U.S. at 613.

In United States v. McCoy, 323 F.3d 1114 (9th Cir. 2003), the Ninth Circuit held that the Hobbs Act was constitutional as applied to a robbery that affected a business that purchased goods from out-of-state companies. However, the court emphasized that the government must show a "substantial effect" on interstate commerce, not just a minimal or incidental effect. Id. at 1118-19. In contrast, the Tenth Circuit has held that the Hobbs Act is constitutional because it regulates economic activity. United States v. Farr, 536 F.3d 1174, 1186 (10th Cir. 2008) (citing Gonzales v. Raich, 545 U.S. 1 (2005)).

Given the evidence presented at trial, it is clear that the robbery of Mr. Goodcents had some effect on interstate commerce. However, under the reasoning of Lopez and Morrison, this effect is not substantial enough to justify federal regulation under the Commerce Clause. Therefore, we urge this Court to hold that 18 U.S.C. § 1951 is unconstitutional as applied to Mr. Pearson.

### B. Tardy Alibi Defense

The district court erred in refusing to allow Mr. Pearson to submit a tardy alibi defense. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12.1, a defendant must provide notice of an alibi defense within the time period specified by the court. However, the rule also allows the court to permit a late notice for good cause. Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1(b). Mr. Pearson's counsel provided a reasonable explanation for the delay in providing the alibi notice, and the government did not demonstrate any prejudice resulting from the delay. Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 415 (1988). Therefore, the district court should have allowed Mr. Pearson to present his alibi defense.

### C. Sufficiency of the Evidence

The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support Mr. Pearson's conviction. The government's key witness, Courtney Martin, was an accomplice who had a motive to lie. United States v. Hill, 627 F.3d 1178, 1183 (10th Cir. 2010). Additionally, the in-court identification of Mr. Pearson by Angela Starks was unreliable because she had seen his picture on television before the trial. United States v. Wade, 388 U.S. 218, 228-29 (1967). The government failed to present sufficient evidence to prove Mr. Pearson's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

### D. Felony Murder Instruction

The district court erred in instructing the jury on the elements of felony murder. The instruction did not accurately reflect the law, and the government failed to present sufficient evidence to prove the elements of the offense. United States v. Nguyen, 493 F.3d 613, 619 (5th Cir. 2007).

### E. Lesser Included Offense Instructions

The district court erred in refusing to submit Mr. Pearson's requested lesser included offense instructions to the jury. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 31(c), a defendant is entitled to a lesser included offense instruction if the elements of the lesser offense are a subset of the elements of the greater offense. Schmuck v. United States, 489 U.S. 705, 716 (1989). Mr. Pearson's requested instructions met this standard, and the district court should have submitted them to the jury. United States v. Duran, 127 F.3d 911, 914-15 (10th Cir. 1997).

For the foregoing reasons, we urge this Court to reverse Mr. Pearson's conviction and remand for a new trial.
