**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE STATUTE**

A criminal defendant may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge a federal statute on federalism grounds. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) (“[T]he [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.”); *see also United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004) (rejecting a defendant’s Tenth Amendment challenge for lack of standing), *cert. denied*, 543 U.S. 874 (2004); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006) (same), *cert. denied*, 548 U.S. 904 (2007). The Tenth Amendment “is not a source of rights enforceable by an individual.” *Bond v. United States*, 564 U.S. 211, 221 (2011) (citing *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992)). Rather, it “leaves to the several States a residuary and inviolable sovereignty,” *Bond*, 564 U.S. at 221 (quoting *New York*, 505 U.S. at 156), and “establishes a constitutional plan for the distribution of power between the Federal Government and the States,” *id.* (quoting *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 457 (1991)). Because the Amendment protects “the States as States,” *New York*, 505 U.S. at 181 (quoting *Texas v. United States*, 788 F.2d 1512, 1518 (D.C. Cir. 1986)), only a State may assert a Tenth Amendment claim. *Bond*, 564 U.S. at 221.

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. The Seventh Circuit’s dicta in *Gillespie* is inconsistent with the weight of authority and has not been followed by this Court. *See, e.g., Parker*, 362 F.3d at 1284–85; *Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d at 234–35; *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005), *cert. denied*, 548 U.S. 904 (2006). Moreover, the Supreme Court has since clarified that the Tenth Amendment is not a font of individual rights. *Bond*, 564 U.S. at 221.

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER**

Congress may enact legislation to implement a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (“If [a] treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”); *see also United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (recognizing that the treaty power “is itself a power of the National Government”); *United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998) (same). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing it. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 223 (noting that the Convention “aims to eliminate the use of chemical weapons” and that § 229 “criminalizes precisely the type of locally motivated chemical warfare that respondent waged”).

Bond’s argument that § 229 lacks a federal interest element is unavailing. The Necessary and Proper Clause permits Congress to regulate intrastate activity when doing so is “conducive to the ends” of an enumerated power. *United States v. Comstock*, 560 U.S. 126, 133 (2010) (quoting *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421 (1819)). Here, the treaty power provides the enumerated authority, and § 229 is plainly adapted to effectuate the Convention’s objectives. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432.

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

A statute is void for vagueness if it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited.” *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). Section 229 provides clear notice: it prohibits the knowing use of toxic chemicals as weapons. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1). Bond’s conduct—repeatedly spreading toxic chemicals on her victim’s property—falls squarely within the statute’s terms. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 223.

Bond’s overbreadth argument fails because § 229 is not substantially overbroad. *See United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 292 (2008) (requiring a statute to be “substantially overbroad” to support a facial challenge). The statute targets only the knowing use of toxic chemicals as weapons, a narrow class of conduct. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
