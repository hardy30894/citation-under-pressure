The Eighth Amendment does not forbid the death penalty for mentally retarded offenders. This Court’s precedents have long held that punishment must be judged by the Constitution’s text and this Nation’s historical practice, not by a freestanding judicial revision of capital sentencing. See Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130 (1879); McElvaine v. Brush, 142 U.S. 155 (1891); Kelley v. Oregon, 273 U.S. 589 (1927).

In Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely rejected the categorical Eighth Amendment claim Atkins renews here. Penry held that execution of the mentally retarded was not then barred by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” because there was no national consensus and because the Constitution left room for individualized capital sentencing. Id. at 330-35. Penry also explained that the Eighth Amendment analysis looks to objective indicia of societal values and to the Court’s own judgment about proportionality. Id. at 331-34. Under that framework, Atkins cannot prevail unless he can show that the Constitution itself now compels a different categorical rule.

He cannot. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the Constitution permits capital punishment for the gravest offenses. Wilkerson upheld a sentence of death by firing squad and rejected the proposition that the Eighth Amendment constitutionalized a particular mode of execution. 99 U.S. at 135-36. Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), and Good Shot v. United States, 179 U.S. 87 (1900), likewise confirm that where the law authorizes death, the Constitution does not negate the punishment simply because a jury may in a particular case recommend mercy. See Andres, 333 U.S. at 748-49; Good Shot, 179 U.S. at 95-96. These decisions underscore that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically exempt whole classes of offenders absent a clear constitutional command.

Nor does Atkins identify such a command. The historical cases he relies upon involve procedure, not a substantive bar to execution. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), required due process before a defendant could be subjected to a new indeterminate sentencing scheme, but it did not suggest that mentally impaired defendants are constitutionally immune from severe punishment. Id. at 608-10. Likewise, Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134 (1951), addressed habeas pleading and competency allegations, not the substantive limits of capital punishment. Id. at 134-36. Those authorities confirm only that criminal sentencing must comport with due process; they do not establish a special Eighth Amendment exemption for mental retardation.

The better reading of this Court’s capital cases is that mental retardation is a factor for the sentencer, not a constitutional bar. In Penry itself, the Court recognized that evidence of mental retardation may be highly relevant mitigation because it may reduce culpability and bear on moral blameworthiness. 492 U.S. at 319-20. That principle fits comfortably within the longstanding constitutional requirement of individualized sentencing in capital cases. See Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978); Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280 (1976). But individualized consideration is different from categorical immunity. The Virginia courts followed that settled approach by allowing Atkins to present his mental condition and by submitting punishment to the jury.

Atkins also cannot show the sort of national consensus that would justify overturning Penry. The Court in Penry relied on the absence of a clear legislative trend and the then-existing number of jurisdictions permitting execution of the mentally retarded. 492 U.S. at 334. That reasoning still controls unless and until this Court concludes that objective evidence has crossed the constitutional threshold. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 173-74 (1976); Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977). But the Constitution does not permit the Court to convert a policy debate into a categorical prohibition without the objective indicia that Penry requires.

Finally, this case is a poor vehicle for creating a new constitutional rule. Atkins was found guilty of a brutal capital murder. The record shows repeated contradictory statements, flight, and evidence that the victim suffered a sustained and savage beating. The jury heard the mental-retardation evidence and still imposed death. The Eighth Amendment does not mandate that the Court override that determination absent a settled constitutional rule. See Rakes v. United States, 212 U.S. 55, 60-61 (1909) (the test is what punishment may be imposed under the statute); Kelley, 273 U.S. at 593-94.

Penry remains controlling. Because the Constitution does not categorically forbid the execution of mentally retarded persons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
