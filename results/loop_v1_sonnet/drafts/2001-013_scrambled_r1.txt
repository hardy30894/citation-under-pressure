# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Exempts OSHA Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Authority Over the Working Conditions at Issue

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA jurisdiction only for "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards." The statute's use of the active verb "exercise" requires more than the bare existence of regulatory power; it requires that the power actually have been brought to bear on the working conditions in question. This Court has long insisted that displacement of an otherwise applicable federal regulatory scheme be measured by what the delegated agency has actually done, not by what it might do. See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (displacement of an existing regime requires "the clear and manifest purpose of Congress").

The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels—covering fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire arrestors, and signaling lights, 46 U.S.C. § 4102—address navigational and life-saving equipment. They do not "exercise" authority over the occupational hazards of oil and gas exploration, such as blowout-prevention procedures, that caused the injuries here. Because the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over these working conditions, Section 4(b)(1) does not apply, and OSHA's citation stands on solid statutory ground.

## II. Field-Preemption Principles Do Not Extend to Minimal, Unexercised Coast Guard Authority

Petitioner's reliance on a field-occupation theory is misplaced. Even where this Court has found federal regulation preemptive of concurrent regulation, it has done so only when the federal scheme was actually complete and occupied the field—not merely potential. See Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 66–67 (1941) (federal law displaces concurrent regulation only where Congress has enacted a "complete scheme of regulation" leaving "no room" for other rules). No comparable comprehensive exercise of authority exists here. The Coast Guard's uninspected-vessel rules are narrow and equipment-specific; they leave untouched the drilling and well-control practices at issue in this case. Absent the kind of pervasive regulatory occupation demanded by Hines, there is no basis to infer that Congress intended silent, unexercised Coast Guard authority to oust OSHA.

## III. Concurrent Regulation of Vessel Safety Is Permissible Absent Actual Conflict

This Court has long recognized that not every subject touching maritime commerce demands exclusive federal control; only matters that are "in their nature national, or admit only of one uniform system" require exclusive regulation, while other subjects remain open to concurrent treatment. Cooley v. Board of Wardens, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 299, 319 (1852). Consistent with that principle, this Court sustained state regulation of tug-and-barge towing operations notwithstanding federal inspection laws in Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1 (1937), because Congress had not manifested an intent to exclude such regulation. The occupational safety of drilling crews aboard a stationary barge is not the sort of subject demanding "one uniform system" of the kind reserved to the Coast Guard's navigational authority; it is precisely the type of local, workplace-specific concern that Cooley's concurrent-power doctrine leaves open to concurrent regulation. The Coast Guard's failure to regulate drilling-related occupational hazards thus leaves room for OSHA's complementary regulation without producing any conflict Congress sought to avoid.

## IV. OSHA's Remedial Purpose Supports Broad Construction of "Workplace" Coverage

The Act is remedial legislation, and this Court has long construed such statutes generously to achieve their protective purpose. See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 124 (1944) (construing "employee" broadly to serve the statute's remedial objectives in light of "the mischief at which the Act is aimed"). At the same time, where a statute's language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 4(a)'s broad reference to "employment performed in a workplace" plainly reaches a barge actively engaged in drilling operations on state waters, staffed by employees performing industrial tasks. The Secretary's interpretation of her own statute, reached through reasoned application to these facts, warrants respect. Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (agency interpretations warrant deference commensurate with "the thoroughness evident in [their] consideration").

## V. The Workers' Functional Role, Not the Vessel's Documentation Status, Governs

To the extent petitioner suggests that its employees' mere presence aboard a vessel automatically triggers exclusive maritime regulation, this Court's seaman-status precedents establish that coverage turns on the function performed, not on the vessel's Coast Guard classification. See South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260 (1940) (status depends on "the nature of the service" and its relationship to navigation, not the mere fact of being aboard a vessel); Norton v. Warner Co., 321 U.S. 565, 571–72 (1944) (assessing status by actual duties performed). Whether an employee qualifies as a seaman is ordinarily a question to be resolved on the facts of the particular employment. Senko v. La Crosse Dredging Corp., 352 U.S. 370, 374 (1957) ("the determination of who is a seaman … should be left to the finder of fact"). Employees engaged in oil and gas extraction from a stationary drilling barge perform industrial, not seafaring, functions, reinforcing that the barge was a "workplace" subject to OSHA under Section 4(a) and that the Coast Guard's unexercised authority over uninspected vessels does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1).

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
