**ARGUMENT**

The execution of mentally retarded persons does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Court’s holding in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), remains controlling and compels affirmance of the Virginia Supreme Court’s judgment. Petitioner cannot demonstrate that a national consensus has emerged against this practice, nor that evolving standards of decency require overruling *Penry*.

**I. *Penry v. Lynaugh* Controls and Petitioner Has Not Shown That Its Reasoning Has Been Undermined**

In *Penry*, this Court squarely held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically bar the execution of mentally retarded persons. *Id.* at 340. The Court declined to exempt mentally retarded offenders from capital punishment, reasoning that “mental retardation is a constitutionally relevant characteristic” that may be considered as a mitigating factor in the penalty phase. *Id.* at 338, 340. This conclusion reflected the principle that “the Eighth Amendment does not require the adoption of a bright-line rule prohibiting the execution of mentally retarded offenders.” *Id.* at 338.

*Penry* remains good law, and this Court should not lightly reconsider it. Since *Penry*, the number of states that prohibit capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders has increased, but no national consensus has coalesced. As of 2002, only thirteen states and the federal government had enacted such bans. That is far fewer than the “super-majority” this Court required when it prohibited execution of offenders under age sixteen in *Thompson v. Oklahoma*, 487 U.S. 815, 826-29 (1988) (plurality opinion). In *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989), the Court declined to extend *Thompson* to offenders under eighteen, noting that only fifteen states prohibited capital punishment for sixteen-year-olds. The same reasoning applies here: the absence of a uniform national consensus undermines petitioner’s claim.

**II. No Evolving Standards of Decency Support Categorical Exemption**

The Eighth Amendment’s meaning is determined by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). The primary evidence of such standards is “legislation enacted by the people’s representatives,” *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 175 (1976) (plurality opinion), and “the actions of sentencing juries,” *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 596 (1977) (plurality opinion). Here, the legislative trend is insufficient to establish a national consensus. Only thirteen states and the federal government have banned execution of the mentally retarded. That is a minority of the thirty-seven states that currently impose capital punishment. This Court has repeatedly declined to find a consensus where the number of states adopting a prohibition falls short of a clear majority. *See, e.g., Edmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782, 792-96 (1982) (finding no consensus where only seven states prohibited felony murder liability); *Tison v. Arizona*, 481 U.S. 137, 154-57 (1987) (finding no consensus where only three states prohibited capital punishment for major participants in felony murder with reckless indifference). Petitioner’s reliance on the “diminishing frequency” of such executions is similarly unavailing. As *Gregg* recognized, “the Eighth Amendment is not violated every time a State reaches a conclusion different from a majority of its sisters.” 428 U.S. at 176.

**III. The Death Penalty Serves Legitimate Penological Goals Even for Mentally Retarded Defendants**

The Eighth Amendment does not prohibit punishments that serve legitimate penological objectives. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 183. Capital punishment advances the goals of retribution and deterrence. *Id.* at 183-84. Mental retardation does not categorically deprive a defendant of the cognitive or moral capacity to be held fully responsible for his actions. As *Penry* recognized, mentally retarded offenders may still act with “deliberation and premeditation,” 492 U.S. at 338, and may pose a future danger warranting execution. Accordingly, the categorical exemption petitioner seeks would unduly limit the states’ ability to impose just punishment in appropriate cases. *Compare Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986) (prohibiting execution of the insane, who lack understanding of the punishment). This Court has consistently declined to expand categorical exemptions beyond those required by the text and tradition of the Eighth Amendment. *See Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 372; *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 340.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
