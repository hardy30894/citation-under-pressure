## ARGUMENT

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency triggered a contribution claim under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s timely cost-recovery claim under § 107. The decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for a CERCLA “response action,” and Guam therefore retains the right to proceed under § 107.

### I. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action

Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 decree was a Clean Water Act settlement that required Guam to install a cover system to address leachate discharges. It did not address the release of hazardous substances, nor did it require any CERCLA “response action” as defined in § 101(25). See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25) (defining “response” as “remove, removal, remedy, and remedial action”). The cover system was a Clean Water Act remedy, not a CERCLA remedial action. *Cf. United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA targets “industrial pollution” and hazardous substance releases). Because the decree did not resolve liability for a CERCLA response action, it cannot trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) (contribution right arises only from a § 107 action or a settlement resolving CERCLA liability).

### II. The Decree’s Disclaimer of Liability Precludes a Finding That Liability Was “Resolved”

The consent decree expressly states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” J.A. 92. Such a disclaimer is dispositive. *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (consent decree did not resolve liability where “the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability”). While some circuits have held that a disclaimer alone is not enough to avoid § 113(f)(3)(B), *see Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017); *Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, Inc.*, 937 F.3d 926, 931 (7th Cir. 2019), those cases involved decrees that expressly addressed CERCLA liability. Here, the decree never mentions CERCLA, and the underlying EPA complaint alleged only Clean Water Act violations. A settlement of non-CERCLA claims cannot “resolve” CERCLA liability. *See United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007) (CERCLA’s contribution provision applies only to “liability under CERCLA”).

### III. Guam May Properly Bring a § 107 Cost-Recovery Claim

A party that has incurred response costs may sue under § 107(a)(4)(B) for recovery from other potentially responsible parties, even if that party is itself a PRP. *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 141. The Supreme Court in *Cooper Industries* held that a PRP that has not been sued under § 107 or settled under § 113 may bring a § 107 claim. 543 U.S. at 167. Guam was never sued under CERCLA; the EPA’s 2002 action was under the Clean Water Act. Guam’s costs—closing the dump and installing a cover—were incurred under compulsion of that non-CERCLA action. Nothing in CERCLA requires Guam to forgo a § 107 claim simply because it entered a consent decree with EPA. *See Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 818 (1994) (response costs recoverable under § 107 include costs of identifying hazardous substances and negotiating with regulators).

### IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with CERCLA’s Remedial Purpose

CERCLA was enacted to “promptly clean up hazardous waste sites and to ensure that responsible parties foot the bill.” *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). The Navy operated the Ordot Dump for decades, disposing of munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange. Guam, as the current owner, bore the cleanup costs. The D.C. Circuit’s decision immunizes the Navy—the primary polluter—from liability, contrary to CERCLA’s goal of holding responsible parties accountable. *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (CERCLA imposes joint and several liability on PRPs). The practical effect is that Guam, an unincorporated territory with limited resources, must bear the entire $160 million cost while the United States escapes liability. *See O’Neil v. Picillo*, 883 F.2d 176, 178–79 (1st Cir. 1989) (CERCLA’s liability scheme ensures that “responsible parties rarely escape joint and several liability”).

### V. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Bar Guam’s § 107 Claim

Guam filed suit in 2017, within the six-year statute of limitations for § 107 remedial actions. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). The D.C. Circuit’s holding that Guam’s claim is time-barred under § 113’s three-year limit rests on the erroneous conclusion that the consent decree triggered contribution. Because the decree did not resolve CERCLA liability, Guam’s § 107 claim remains timely. *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 141 (PRP may choose between § 107 and § 113 when both are available).

### VI. The Consent Decree Did Not Provide Notice to Other PRPs

Allowing a Clean Water Act decree to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) would silently extinguish the contribution rights of other PRPs without due process. *See Florida Power*, 810 F.3d at 1003 (settlement must clearly resolve CERCLA liability to provide notice). The decree here gave no notice to other potentially responsible parties, undermining CERCLA’s settlement incentives. *Cf. Cooper Industries*, 543 U.S. at 168 (settlement under § 113(f)(2) protects settling parties from contribution claims).

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s judgment and hold that Guam may proceed under § 107.
