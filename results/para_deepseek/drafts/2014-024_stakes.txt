**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below must be reversed. Section 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of a “threat,” and both the statute’s background principles of mens rea and the First Amendment require the government to prove that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat. The district court’s instruction, which required only that a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat, permitted conviction without any finding of the defendant’s culpable state of mind.

**I. Section 875(c) requires subjective intent to threaten**

“The existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence.” _Morissette v. United States_, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Although §875(c) does not specify a mental state, “the presumption in favor of a mens rea requirement is strong.” _United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc._, 513 U.S. 64, 69 (1994). That presumption is especially powerful here because §875(c) is a serious felony, not a public-welfare offense, and because it criminalizes speech. Nothing in the statute’s text suggests Congress intended to impose strict liability for the utterance of words that a listener might find alarming.

For a communication to be a “threat,” the speaker must intend it to be one. A threat is an intentional communication of an intent to cause harm; it is not an unintended effect on a listener. Thus, the “mens rea” required by the statute is the subjective intent to communicate a threat. That is the natural reading of the word “threaten,” and it is the reading this Court applied in _Virginia v. Black_: “‘True threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). The language “the speaker means to communicate” requires a subjective inquiry into the speaker’s purpose, not an objective inquiry into a reasonable listener’s perception.

The Third Circuit’s contrasting “reasonable speaker” standard—asking whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as a threat—collapses the distinction between a threat and a statement that merely sounds threatening. That approach effectively punishes the speaker for the listener’s interpretation, not for the speaker’s culpable intent.

**II. The First Amendment independently requires proof of subjective intent**

Because §875(c) criminalizes speech, the constitutional background against which Congress legislated narrows the statute’s reach to unprotected “true threats.” In _Watts v. United States_, the Court reversed a §875(c) conviction because the statute must be limited to a “true threat” and “what is a threat must be distinguished from what is constitutionally protected speech.” 394 U.S. 705, 707-08 (1969). That distinction cannot turn on how a reasonable listener reacts without impermissibly chilling protected expression.

The objective test gives the speaker no way to know whether his words will be deemed criminal. A joke, a boast, a line of fiction, or a rap lyric may frighten a reasonable listener, even though the speaker intended no threat. If conviction follows from that misperception, the speaker pays for the listener’s misunderstanding. The result is self-censorship: a speaker who wants to discuss a violent topic, or express frustration in hyperbole, must anticipate the most fearful listener in his audience. That is precisely the harm the First Amendment forbids. “The threat of sanctions may deter their exercise almost as potently as the actual application of sanctions.” _NAACP v. Button_, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). A subjective-intent requirement ensures that the government may punish only those who deliberately choose to use the language of threat, not those who are inadvertently misunderstood.

_Black_ reinforces this conclusion. There, the Court invalidated a statutory presumption that allowed a jury to infer intent to intimidate from the act of cross burning. 538 U.S. at 363-64. The Court reasoned that the presumption “would create an impermissible risk of error” because it might “send a message” that the speaker had an intent he did not hold. _Id._ The same risk inheres in an objective standard that substitutes the jury’s reaction for the defendant’s intent. It is constitutionally insufficient to convict a speaker of a threat unless he “mean[t] to communicate” one. _Id._ at 359.

**III. The error was not harmless**

At trial, the district court instructed the jury that a true threat exists when “a reasonable person would interpret the statement as a threat.” That instruction expressly rejected the subjective-intent standard. The jury was never asked to determine whether Elonis intended his posts to be threatening. Indeed, his entire defense was that the posts were rap lyrics and therapeutic expressions, not genuine communications of intent. The jury’s verdict therefore did not resolve the dispositive question under the correct legal standard. Because Elonis was convicted without proof of the required subjective intent, his conviction must be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
