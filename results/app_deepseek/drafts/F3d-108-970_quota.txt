**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in entering judgment for Feltmann because she failed to prove that Sieben’s legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons for its employment actions were pretextual. Under the McDonnell Douglas burden-shifting framework, once the employer articulates a legitimate reason, the plaintiff must show that reason is false and that discrimination was the real cause. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973); *Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). Feltmann did not meet that burden.

**I. Sieben had legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons for its actions.**

Sieben placed Feltmann on probation due to her low sales performance and low gross profit margins—objective metrics that also led to the probation of male consultant Rick Beutel. Such performance-based decisions are classic legitimate reasons. *Kier v. F. & M. Bank*, 646 F.2d 360, 363 (8th Cir. 1981). Rich’s memo regarding Feltmann’s “work ethic” and her misuse of the demo car—specifically, driving 300 miles weekly outside the St. Louis area despite a dealership-wide restriction—was a neutral, business-driven response to her specific conduct. No male consultant received a similar memo because no male consultant engaged in the same repeated violation. *Henson v. Union Pac. R.R. Co.*, 415 F.3d 847, 851 (8th Cir. 2005) (comparators must be similarly situated in all relevant respects). The decision not to send Feltmann to the Chicago sales competition was based on her not being among the top two sales consultants at the time; the two top consultants declined, and no other consultant was offered the opportunity. These are all legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons.

**II. Feltmann failed to show pretext.**

To establish pretext, a plaintiff must show that the employer’s stated reasons are unworthy of credence. *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993). Feltmann offered no evidence that the performance data were fabricated or that Sieben treated similarly situated male employees more favorably. Indeed, she was the only woman, but she was also the only consultant who repeatedly violated the demo-car policy. Her sales decline after the incentive program ended affected both male and female consultants—Beutel was also placed on probation. The fact that she rebounded in March does not retroactively invalidate the legitimate concerns about her earlier low profits and work ethic. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000) (plaintiff must show that the employer’s explanation is “unworthy of credence” and that discrimination was the real reason).

Feltmann’s reliance on the vulgar comment by Anzalone (a non-supervisor at a different franchise) is insufficient. Sieben promptly investigated, reprimanded Anzalone, and offered to fire him—which Feltmann declined. This swift response negates any inference of discriminatory animus. *Cf. Clark County Sch. Dist. v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 271 (2001) (isolated, stray remarks unrelated to employment decisions do not establish pretext). The later comment by Barnstead about a “thick personnel file” was vague and not shown to be tied to her complaint.

**III. The retaliation claim fails for lack of causation.**

Feltmann’s retaliation claim requires proof that her protected activity (the November 1992 complaint) was a but-for cause of the adverse actions. *Univ. of Tex. Sw. Med. Ctr. v. Nassar*, 570 U.S. 338, 362 (2013). The probation and memo occurred in March 1993, four months later. Temporal proximity alone is insufficient to establish causation, especially where the employer had ongoing performance concerns predating the complaint. *Breeden*, 532 U.S. at 273–74. Feltmann’s low profits and demo-car misuse began before the complaint and continued after. Sieben’s actions were consistent with its legitimate business judgment, not retaliatory motive.

**IV. The evidence was insufficient to support the verdict.**

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to Feltmann, a reasonable jury could not conclude that Sieben’s reasons were pretextual. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 149–50. The record shows only that Feltmann was a capable salesperson who had performance issues, was treated no differently than male colleagues, and received a fair investigation of her complaint. No reasonable jury could find that gender or retaliation played any role in Sieben’s decisions. The judgment must be reversed.
