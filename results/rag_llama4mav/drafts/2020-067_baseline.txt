## Argument

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that when reviewing a defendant's claim of plain error, it may consider the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, to determine whether the error affected the defendant's substantial rights or impacted the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This conclusion is supported by the Supreme Court's precedent on plain error review.

In United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), the Court established the framework for plain error review, requiring a defendant to show that an error occurred, that it was plain, and that it affected their substantial rights. If these conditions are met, the Court may exercise its discretion to correct the error if it "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." Id. at 736 (quoting United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985)).

When assessing whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights, the Court has consistently looked beyond the trial record. For example, in Lockhart v. Nelson, 488 U.S. 33 (1988), the Court considered evidence outside the sentencing hearing to determine whether the defendant's sentence was valid. Similarly, in Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), the Court examined the validity of a prior conviction, even though it was not part of the trial record.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision to review the entire record, including Greer's prior convictions, was consistent with this precedent. By considering this evidence, the court could determine whether Greer was prejudiced by the Rehaif error, which omitted the knowledge-of-status element from the jury's consideration. The court's review of the record revealed that Greer had multiple prior convictions, making it likely that he knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm.

Greer's argument that the Eleventh Circuit should have limited its review to the trial record is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has never imposed such a restriction on plain error review. Instead, the Court has allowed appellate courts to consider relevant evidence, even if it was not presented to the jury. See, e.g., Washington v. Recuenco, 548 U.S. 212 (2006) (considering evidence outside the trial record to determine whether a sentencing error was harmless).

Furthermore, limiting plain error review to the trial record would undermine the fairness and integrity of the judicial process. In cases where a defendant fails to object to an error at trial, it is often because they believe it is not prejudicial. However, on appeal, the defendant may argue that the error was plain and affected their substantial rights. By considering the entire record, the appellate court can make an informed decision about whether to correct the error.

In conclusion, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it may review the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, when assessing whether a plain error affected a defendant's substantial rights or impacted the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This conclusion is supported by the Supreme Court's precedent on plain error review, and it ensures that appellate courts can make informed decisions about whether to correct errors that occurred at trial.
