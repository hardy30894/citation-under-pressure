ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 171 (1976). A punishment is unusual if it is contrary to “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). When the Virginia Supreme Court affirmed petitioner’s death sentence, thirty-eight jurisdictions authorized capital punishment and only two barred its imposition on mentally retarded offenders. Atkins v. Commonwealth, 260 Va. 375, 385, 534 S.E.2d 312, 319 (2000). That legislative pattern demonstrates that the practice is not unusual. Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 334 (1989).

Petitioner contends that clinical definitions of mental retardation supply an independent Eighth Amendment limitation. Clinical standards, however, “do not establish the constitutional rule.” Id. at 335. The Eighth Amendment inquiry turns on objective indicia of societal values, not on professional nomenclature. Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 370 (1989). Because those indicia continue to permit capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders who commit murder, the Constitution does not forbid the sentence imposed here.

Nor does the punishment fail the requirement of proportionality. The Eighth Amendment demands only that the sentence be graduated and proportioned to the offense. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). Mental retardation may lessen culpability, yet it does not render every such offender incapable of the moral blameworthiness required for capital punishment. Penry, 492 U.S. at 338. Virginia’s procedures permit the sentencer to consider mental retardation as a mitigating factor, thereby satisfying the constitutional demand for individualized assessment. Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 113-114 (1982).

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
