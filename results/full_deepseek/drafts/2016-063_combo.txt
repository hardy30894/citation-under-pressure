**ARGUMENT**

The D.C. Circuit erred in transferring this case to the Federal Circuit. Under the governing statutory framework, a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing a mixed-case appeal on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court. This conclusion follows from the plain text of the Civil Service Reform Act, as reinforced by long-settled principles of administrative law and judicial review.

**I. The Statutory Text Places Mixed-Case Review in District Court, Regardless of the Board’s Jurisdictional Ruling**

The Act provides that an employee who “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the [MSPB]” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination” has filed a “mixed case.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Judicial review of such a case “shall be filed” in district court. *Id.* § 7703(b)(2). The statute defines the “action” by reference to the employee’s *ability* to appeal it to the Board, not the Board’s *ultimate determination* of its own jurisdiction. When an employee like Perry identifies an adverse action of a type that is generally appealable—here, a removal and suspension—and alleges discrimination, the case qualifies as a mixed case at the time of filing. The Board’s later dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not retroactively undo that classification.

This interpretation is consistent with the canon of statutory construction that courts must give effect to the plain meaning of the words used. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The phrase “may appeal” looks to the employee’s right, not the Board’s eventual decision. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary reading—that a jurisdictional dismissal strips the case of its mixed-case status—improperly imports an ambiguity into unambiguous language. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts should not “search for a meaning that is not plainly expressed”).

**II. Pre-1970 Precedent Requires De Novo Judicial Review of Jurisdictional Facts in District Court**

Even if the statutory text were ambiguous, pre-1970 Supreme Court precedent compels the conclusion that the Board’s jurisdictional determination—particularly one resting on the voluntariness of the employee’s action—must be reviewed in district court. The Board’s ruling that Perry’s retirement and suspension were voluntary is a “jurisdictional fact” that, under the Constitution, requires independent judicial review. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 60 (1932) (holding that when an administrative agency’s jurisdiction depends on a fact, the court must determine that fact for itself). *Accord Ohio Valley Water Co. v. Ben Avon Borough*, 253 U.S. 287, 289 (1920) (due process requires “independent judicial review” of jurisdictional facts); *St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 38, 51 (1936) (same). The Board’s conclusion that Perry’s settlement was voluntary is a factual predicate for its jurisdiction, and the district court—not the Federal Circuit—is the proper forum for reviewing that fact, because the discrimination claim is intertwined with it.

Moreover, the purpose of the mixed-case provisions is to ensure that discrimination allegations receive the benefit of district court review, where the employee can obtain a trial de novo or broader administrative record. The Board’s jurisdictional ruling here—that the settlement was voluntary—directly overlaps with the merits of Perry’s discrimination claim, because the same coercion that renders the settlement involuntary supports his argument that the agency’s actions were discriminatory. To channel such a case to the Federal Circuit, which reviews MSPB decisions under a deferential standard, would frustrate that purpose. See *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 487-88 (1951) (scope of review affects the reality of judicial protection). The district court’s broader review authority is necessary to vindicate the employee’s substantive rights. See *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938) (substantial evidence review requires considering the whole record).

**III. The Presumption of Reviewability Favors District Court Jurisdiction**

The Supreme Court has long recognized a strong presumption that agency actions are subject to judicial review. *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 140 (1967) (“[J]udicial review of a final agency action by an aggrieved person will not be cut off unless there is persuasive reason to believe that such was the purpose of Congress.”). The D.C. Circuit’s rule—that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit—effectively creates a jurisdictional dead end for employees who, like Perry, bring mixed cases in good faith. The Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over non-mixed MSPB appeals, but if the Board erroneously dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction, the employee may be left without a meaningful forum. The district court is the only court that can provide both review of the jurisdictional determination and full adjudication of the discrimination claim. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. 137, 163 (1803) (the judiciary must provide a remedy for violations of legal rights). The D.C. Circuit’s rule undermines this fundamental principle.

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Reliance on a Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals Is Unsupported**

The D.C. Circuit drew a line between procedural dismissals (which go to district court under *Kloeckner*) and jurisdictional dismissals (which go to the Federal Circuit under *Powell*). But that distinction has no basis in the statutory text or in pre-1970 precedent. As the Court recognized in *Crowell*, jurisdictional facts are not inherently different from procedural facts; both require judicial review. *Crowell*, 285 U.S. at 60. Moreover, the Board’s authority to waive procedural defects does not justify treating jurisdictional dismissals differently. The Board cannot waive its own statutory jurisdiction, but that only reinforces the need for a district court to review the jurisdictional ruling independently. *Estep v. United States*, 327 U.S. 114, 120 (1946) (when an agency’s jurisdiction is challenged, the reviewing court must determine the jurisdictional question).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the Court should hold that a Merit Systems Protection Board decision dismissing a mixed-case appeal on jurisdictional grounds is subject to judicial review in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit should be reversed.
