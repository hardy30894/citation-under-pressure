**Argument**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) bars discharge of “any debt” for money, property, services, or credit “to the extent obtained by” false pretenses, false representation, or actual fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The question is not whether the Bankruptcy Code gives a dishonest debtor a partial amnesty for the amounts he managed to retain while stripping away everything else. It does not. The statute excepts from discharge the entire debt traceable to the fraud, including statutory and punitive components that arise from the fraudulent scheme itself. *Cohen v. de la Cruz* should therefore be affirmed.

First, this Court has already recognized that the fraud exceptions to discharge are construed to prevent a debtor from using bankruptcy as a shield for misconduct. In *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991), the Court explained that the Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” is reserved for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” That principle is fatal to petitioner’s attempt to discharge treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs generated by his fraudulent overcharges. The debt existed because of fraud, and Congress chose to make such debts nondischargeable.

Second, the statutory text is broader than petitioner’s reading. Section 523(a)(2)(A) bars discharge of “any debt” for money or property obtained by fraud “to the extent obtained by” fraud. In *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. 213, 218-23 (1998), this Court held that the phrase “any debt” encompasses all liability arising from the fraud, not merely the value initially received. The Court rejected the view that the provision limits nondischargeability to the amount of the fraudulently obtained property, explaining that once money, property, or services are obtained by fraud, “any debt” arising from that fraud is excepted from discharge. *Id.* at 218-19.

Third, the Court’s discharge cases confirm that exceptions to discharge turn on the nature of the underlying conduct, not on artful relabeling of damages. In *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59, 63-64 (1995), the Court construed § 523(a)(2)(A) against a background of common-law fraud principles and held that actual fraud triggers the exception. *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286-87, likewise emphasized that Congress intended to prevent discharge of debts incurred by culpable fraud. Those cases make clear that the focus is on the fraudulent procurement of the debt, not on whether a portion of the award is compensatory, statutory, or punitive.

Fourth, petitioner’s argument fails because the debt here was not a separate penalty divorced from the fraud. The Court in *Cohen* held that treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act were nondischargeable because they were part of the liability “obtained by” fraudulent conduct. 523 U.S. at 223-24. The same is true of attorney’s fees and costs, which are likewise part of the debt created by the fraudulent transaction. *Id.* at 223 (“[O]nce it is established that specific money or property has been obtained by fraud, ‘any debt’ arising therefrom is excepted from discharge.”).

Fifth, petitioner’s reliance on a narrow, compensatory-only approach conflicts with the Court of Appeals decisions the Court found unpersuasive in *Cohen*. In *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198-99 (9th Cir. 1991), the Ninth Circuit held punitive damages dischargeable because they were not the amount “obtained by” fraud. The Court rejected that reasoning in *Cohen*, 523 U.S. at 219-23. By contrast, the Eleventh Circuit in *St. Laurent v. Ambrose*, 991 F.2d 672, 680-81 (11th Cir. 1993), correctly held that punitive damages awarded on account of fraud are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). That reading aligns with the statutory text and with the Court’s teaching that bankruptcy relief is not for debtors who commit fraud. *Cohen*, 523 U.S. at 223-24.

Sixth, this Court has repeatedly refused to permit form to override substance where federal statutes allocate consequences of wrongful conduct. In *McNary v. Haitian Refugee Center, Inc.*, 498 U.S. 479, 494 (1991), the Court recognized the importance of giving federal remedial statutes practical effect rather than allowing evasion through procedural maneuvers. And in *United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989), the Court instructed that statutory interpretation begins with the text and its ordinary meaning. Here, the ordinary meaning of “any debt” and the Court’s construction in *Cohen* compel nondischargeability.

Seventh, bankruptcy policy cuts against discharge. The Court has long recognized that bankruptcy is meant to afford an honest debtor a fresh start, not to confer a fraud-based windfall. *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286-87; *Cohen*, 523 U.S. at 217-18. Allowing petitioner to discharge the treble-damage component would undercut deterrence and reward the very conduct Congress singled out for special treatment.

Finally, nothing in the Bankruptcy Code suggests that a debtor who commits actual fraud can cleanse the resulting liability by filing Chapter 7. To the contrary, *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. 213 (1998), resolves the issue: debts arising from fraud, including statutory multiple damages, attorney’s fees, and costs, are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The judgment should be affirmed.
