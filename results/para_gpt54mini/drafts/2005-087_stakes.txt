## ARGUMENT

Arizona’s rule limiting Clark’s psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense does not violate due process. The Constitution does not require a State to allow a defendant to introduce all relevant evidence in whatever manner he prefers. Rather, a State may structure the admissibility of mental-health evidence so long as it does not prevent the defendant from presenting a meaningful defense. Arizona satisfied that standard here.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the Due Process Clause leaves States considerable room to define criminal responsibility and the evidentiary consequences of mental disease. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state insanity rule that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court explained that “the Fourteenth Amendment does not require” a State to adopt any particular allocation of the insanity burden. *Id.* at 798. If due process permits a State to require a defendant to prove insanity at the highest standard, it necessarily permits a State to confine expert psychiatric evidence to that insanity inquiry.

Likewise, in *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), the Court rejected a due process challenge to a state scheme that assigned the defendant the burden of proving extreme emotional disturbance. The Court emphasized that “the Due Process Clause, as now interpreted, does not put New York to the choice of abandoning those portions of the criminal law in order to affect a procedural rule of evidence.” *Id.* at 205. *Patterson* confirms that a State may define the elements of the offense and channel defensive proof in ways that do not relieve the prosecution of its burden on those elements. Arizona’s rule does exactly that: it still requires the State to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt, but it limits the use of psychiatric opinion evidence to the separately defined insanity defense.

That approach is consistent with *Clark’s* own logic at the Supreme Court level. The Court later upheld Arizona’s scheme in *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006), holding that due process does not compel a State to admit evidence of mental illness on the issue of mens rea when the State reasonably limits such evidence to insanity. The Court explained that “there is no single common-law understanding” of insanity or mental disease evidence that binds the States. *Id.* at 749. It further recognized that evidence of mental illness can be “confusing,” “misleading,” and capable of inviting jurors to conflate capacity with intent. *Id.* at 774-75. Arizona’s restriction was therefore a permissible effort to keep the jury focused on the prosecution’s burden to prove the defendant’s actual mental state at the time of the offense, rather than on broad psychiatric speculation about impairment.

The Court’s earlier precedent points in the same direction. In *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), a plurality rejected a due process challenge to a statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication on the question of mens rea. Justice Scalia’s opinion stressed that “the definition of the mental element of a crime is for the States,” *id.* at 58, and that due process does not constitutionalize every common-law rule of exculpation. Although *Egelhoff* involved intoxication rather than mental illness, the principle is the same: States may decide what kinds of mental-state evidence are sufficiently reliable and probative to be considered by the jury.

Nor does *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683 (1986), help Clark. *Crane* held that a defendant must be permitted to present evidence about the circumstances of his confession to challenge its credibility. But *Crane* expressly recognized that the right to present a defense is subject to “reasonable restrictions.” *Id.* at 689-90. Arizona’s rule is reasonable because it does not bar Clark from contesting the prosecution’s proof in general; it merely assigns psychiatric-disease evidence to the insanity question, where it is most properly evaluated.

Clark’s argument also fails because the Constitution does not require a State to allow an expert to give a conclusion that effectively tells the jury what result to reach on intent. In *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303 (1998), the Court upheld a categorical exclusion of polygraph evidence and reiterated that “state and federal rulemakers have broad latitude under the Constitution to establish rules excluding evidence from criminal trials.” *Id.* at 308. Arizona’s limitation is far less drastic than the rule in *Scheffer*; it does not exclude mental-health evidence altogether, but merely channels it to the issue of legal insanity.

Finally, the state rule is especially defensible because Arizona still left Clark with a full opportunity to argue lack of mens rea through nonexpert evidence and through the State’s own proof. Due process protects the ability to present a meaningful defense, not the ability to present evidence in whatever form the defendant prefers. See *Crane*, 476 U.S. at 690; *Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 308. Arizona’s evidentiary line preserved that core right while avoiding the substantial risks that psychiatric testimony on intent would create.

In short, due process does not give Clark a free-standing right to use expert mental-disease evidence to negate mens rea apart from his insanity defense. Under *Leland*, *Patterson*, *Egelhoff*, *Crane*, *Scheffer*, and *Clark v. Arizona*, Arizona’s rule is constitutionally permissible, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
