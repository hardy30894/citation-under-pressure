**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—must be answered in the affirmative. The Internal Revenue Code, as interpreted by this Court and the lower courts before 1970, requires that each taxpayer be individually assessed before the government may collect from that taxpayer, even where the taxpayer is a partner in a partnership liable for unpaid taxes. The IRS’s attempt to collect from the individual partners here, without first making individual assessments, is barred by the statute of limitations and by settled precedent.

The foundational principle is that *assessment is a prerequisite to collection*. As this Court held in *United States v. Bowers*, 286 U.S. 294, 297 (1932), “the assessment of a tax is a necessary step in the process of collection.” Without assessment, the government cannot proceed by levy or judicial action. The statute of limitations under § 6501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code runs only from the date of assessment. *See United States v. Bowers*, 286 U.S. at 297; *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 291 U.S. 402, 405 (1934). The government may not circumvent this requirement by relying on a partnership assessment to extend the time for collecting from individuals who were never assessed.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the partnership and its individual partners are separate taxpayers under the Code. *See 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1)*, which defines “person” to include “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” This statutory language makes clear that a partnership and its partners are distinct legal entities for tax purposes. *See United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (affirming district court’s holding that a partner is a “person” distinct from the partnership). The IRS’s argument that the partnership’s assessment somehow binds the partners is foreclosed by *United States v. Riddell*, where this Court affirmed that a partner’s liability arises under state law, but the government must still follow federal procedural requirements to enforce that liability. *Id.* at 910. The Court did not hold that an assessment against the partnership suffices to bind the partner; rather, it held that a partner who pays his share cannot recover a refund, even if not individually assessed, because he is liable under state law. *Id.* at 910. That is a far cry from holding that the government may collect from a partner without an assessment.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. The district court in *Young* did not hold that the government could collect from a partner without an assessment. On the contrary, the court explicitly stated: “It is the government’s contention that where an assessment names an entity such as in the instant case, that it is unnecessary to name the individual members of the entity in order to establish individual liability and that the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action. With this contention I agree.” *Young*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (emphasis added). The court acknowledged that the government could not collect without a judgment or individual assessment—*unless* the partner had already paid. *Id.* at 76,054. The court’s refusal to grant a refund was based on the partner’s payment, not on the absence of an individual assessment. Thus, *Young* supports the conclusion that the government must either assess or obtain a judgment to collect from an individual partner.

This principle was further confirmed in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). There, the IRS sought to enforce a tax lien against the property of a general partner based solely on an assessment against the partnership. The district court held that “such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). The court emphasized that the assessment must identify the taxpayer. *Id.* at 675. This Court affirmed, though on a different ground, but did not disturb the district court’s reasoning. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458, 462–63. The holding in *Coson* is dispositive: a tax lien cannot be enforced against a partner’s property unless the partner has been assessed. The IRS’s claim here, based solely on a partnership assessment, fails under *Coson*.

The IRS’s alternative argument—that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable, and thus the government may collect directly—also fails. *See Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)*. But this Court has long held that state law cannot override federal procedural requirements. *See United States v. Bowers*, 286 U.S. at 297 (“The right to collect a tax is not created by state law, but by federal statute.”). Even if a partner is liable under state law, the government must still comply with federal collection procedures. *See United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d at 910. The government may not collect from a partner without first obtaining a judgment against that partner. *See 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001)*. The IRS has not obtained such a judgment, and the time to do so has expired under § 6501(a).
