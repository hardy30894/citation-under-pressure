# ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit held that the Due Process Clause requires a heightened "reasoned choice" standard of competency—more demanding than the standard this Court articulated for competency to stand trial—before a defendant may plead guilty or waive counsel. That holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's settled competency jurisprudence, which recognizes a single constitutional standard of mental competence applicable throughout criminal proceedings.

## I. The Constitutional Standard of Competency Is Uniform and Does Not Vary with the Decision to Be Made.

This Court has long defined the competency inquiry in functional terms: whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and possesses "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960). That formulation was not limited to the decision whether to stand trial; it defines the threshold of mental competence a defendant must possess to participate meaningfully in *any* aspect of the proceeding. *See Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) (applying the *Dusky* understanding of competency and holding that "the failure to observe procedures adequate to protect a defendant's right not to be tried or convicted while incompetent to stand trial deprives him of his due process right to a fair trial").

Nothing in *Dusky* or *Pate* suggests that a defendant's mental capacity fluctuates according to the particular right he chooses to exercise or forgo. The competency inquiry asks whether the defendant's mind is capable of rational understanding and communication with counsel—a single, unitary question. A defendant either possesses that capacity or does not; the question does not become more demanding merely because the defendant elects to plead guilty rather than proceed to trial, or to represent himself rather than accept appointed counsel.

## II. This Court's Waiver Cases Establish a Knowing-and-Voluntary Inquiry, Not a Heightened Competency Standard.

The Ninth Circuit's error stems from conflating two analytically distinct due process inquiries: (1) whether the defendant is *competent*, and (2) whether his waiver of rights was *knowing, voluntary, and intelligent*. This Court's decisions addressing waiver of the right to counsel have consistently framed the inquiry as the latter, not as a separate, elevated competency threshold. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), the Court explained that waiver of the constitutional right to counsel requires "an intelligent and competent waiver," determined by examining "the total circumstances" surrounding the particular case—not by asking whether the defendant met some heightened psychiatric standard beyond ordinary competence.

Similarly, in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942), the Court held that a defendant "may waive his Constitutional right to assistance of counsel" so long as he does so "competently and intelligently," reasoning that the same defendant who is competent to stand trial is competent to make the choices trial entails, including forgoing counsel. And in *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708, 723-24 (1948), the plurality's searching inquiry concerned whether the record demonstrated the defendant *understood* the consequences of waiver—not whether he satisfied some independent, more rigorous competency test.

*Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962), reaffirmed that the waiver inquiry turns on "the ability to understand the proceedings" and required only that courts not "presume waiver from a silent record." Likewise, *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954), addressed the competency of a defendant to waive counsel by asking whether he had "the mental capacity" to make an "understanding" choice—again invoking the same rational-understanding standard, not a distinct, higher bar.

Guilty pleas receive the same treatment. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969), this Court held that a valid guilty plea requires "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," and that the record must affirmatively disclose that the defendant entered his plea understandingly and voluntarily. The *Boykin* inquiry protects against pleas entered without adequate understanding of the rights being relinquished—precisely the concern already addressed by the knowing-and-voluntary waiver standard, without need for a separate competency threshold keyed to the "reasoned choice" language the Ninth Circuit imported from its own precedent.

## III. A Bifurcated Standard Is Unworkable and Unnecessary to Protect the Values Underlying Due Process.

The competency standard exists to ensure the defendant can rationally comprehend the proceedings and communicate with counsel; the knowing-and-voluntary inquiry exists to ensure that, given such comprehension, the defendant's choice was not the product of ignorance, coercion, or misunderstanding of the rights at stake. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 307 (1963) (distinguishing factual competency determinations from the legal question of whether constitutional standards were properly applied). These two inquiries, together, fully vindicate due process without requiring courts to invent a third, undefined tier of "reasoned choice" competency untethered to *Dusky*'s functional test.

Requiring trial courts to divine and apply a heightened competency standard—one this Court has never defined with the precision *Dusky* provides—would inject intolerable uncertainty into proceedings already adequately safeguarded by the *Dusky* competency test and the *Johnson v. Zerbst* / *Boykin* waiver inquiry. The Ninth Circuit's judgment should be reversed.
