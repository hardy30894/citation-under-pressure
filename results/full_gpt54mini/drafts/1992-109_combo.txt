The Due Process Clause does not require a special “reasoned choice” competency test for a defendant who pleads guilty or waives counsel. The constitutional minimum is the familiar standard of whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This Court has never held that a separate, heightened competency inquiry applies simply because the defendant chooses to plead guilty or proceed without counsel.

That conclusion follows from the Court’s competency cases. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that due process forbids trial of an incompetent defendant and requires a hearing when the evidence raises a bona fide doubt about competence. Id. at 385-86. Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), later reaffirmed the same constitutional baseline for competency to stand trial: rational understanding and the ability to consult with counsel. Id. at 171-72. And Bishop v. United States, 350 U.S. 961 (1956) (per curiam), vacated a conviction where competency had not been properly addressed, underscoring that the Constitution’s concern is with competence in the Due Process sense, not with the wisdom of a defendant’s choice. But none of these decisions announced a heightened “reasoned choice” requirement for plea or waiver decisions.

To the contrary, the Court’s waiver cases reject the notion that competency turns on whether the choice is strategically sound. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that waiver of counsel must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” Id. at 464. But Zerbst addressed the validity of waiver, not a special mental-capacity standard beyond competence. In Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court recognized that a defendant may waive counsel when the waiver is “competently and intelligently” made. Id. at 279. Adams did not impose a distinct psychiatric standard for defendants who elect to make their own defense. Nor did Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), which required a searching judicial inquiry into whether the waiver was knowing and intelligent. Id. at 723-24. Those decisions protect the autonomy interests inherent in waiver, but they do not create a separate constitutional test of “reasoned choice” competence.

Indeed, the Court has consistently distinguished between competence and the voluntariness or intelligence of a waiver. In Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105 (1954) (per curiam), the Court set aside a conviction where the accused was not afforded counsel, but again the focus was on the validity of waiver, not on a special degree of mental functioning. Id. at 106. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court remanded for inquiry into whether the defendant could validly waive counsel, but it did not suggest that due process demands a higher competency threshold than Dusky. Id. at 150-51. And in Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), the Court emphasized that waiver of counsel cannot be presumed from a silent record. Id. at 516. Again, the inquiry was whether the waiver was knowing and voluntary, not whether the defendant could make a “reasoned choice” in some enhanced sense.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on a heightened standard cannot be squared with this Court’s treatment of guilty pleas. In Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), the Court explained that a guilty plea is valid when it is entered voluntarily and intelligently, with awareness of the relevant circumstances and likely consequences. Id. at 748. That is a waiver doctrine, not a separate competency doctrine. The Constitution asks whether the defendant is competent under Dusky and whether the waiver is knowing and voluntary under Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), which requires that the record affirmatively show an intelligent and voluntary plea. Id. at 242-44. Boykin protects against uninformed waiver; it does not recast competence into a “reasoned choice” test.

Petitioner’s position is especially strong here because the trial court had already found Moran competent to stand trial under the settled standard recognized in Dusky and Pate. The Constitution does not require a second, heightened competency finding merely because the defendant later elects to plead guilty or discharge counsel. The relevant constitutional safeguards are the competency determination itself and the separate inquiry into whether the waiver is knowing and voluntary. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938); Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942); Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969); Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970).

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed. The Due Process Clause requires a valid competency determination and a knowing, voluntary waiver; it does not constitutionalize a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for guilty pleas or self-representation.
