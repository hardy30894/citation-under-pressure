### Argument

Section 875(c) should be construed to require proof that the defendant **subjectively intended** to issue a threat, or at least knew that his communication would be understood as a threat. That construction follows the statute’s text, the default presumption that criminal statutes require mens rea, and this Court’s true-threat precedents.

First, § 875(c) contains no express strict-liability command. It criminalizes transmitting “any communication containing any threat,” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), but Congress’s silence on mens rea does not eliminate that element. This Court has repeatedly held that “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal,” and that where a statute is silent, courts ordinarily read in “only that mens rea which is necessary to separate wrongful conduct from otherwise innocent conduct.” Carter v. United States, 530 U.S. 255, 269-70 (2000); Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 605-06 (1994). The Court has cautioned that “the familiar presumption” favoring scienter applies even when a statute is silent. Liparota v. United States, 471 U.S. 419, 426 (1985). Because § 875(c) criminalizes speech, that presumption has особенной force. See Elonis v. United States, 575 U.S. 723, 734-35 (2015).

Second, this Court’s “true threats” doctrine is rooted in the speaker’s culpable intent, not merely an objective listener reaction. In Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam), the Court distinguished political hyperbole from a true threat and emphasized the context of the statement. In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), the Court explained that “true threats” encompass statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit an act of unlawful violence. Black further observed that “[i]ntimidation in the constitutionally proscribable sense of the word is a type of true threat where a speaker directs a threat to a person . . . with the intent of placing the victim in fear of bodily harm or death.” Id. at 360. That language is incompatible with a pure negligence standard.

Third, an objective-only standard wrongly collapses § 875(c) into a strict-liability speech offense and risks chilling protected expression. The First Amendment permits punishment of “true threats,” but it does not permit the government to convict speakers merely because a reasonable person might misread ambiguous, crude, or artistic speech. See Watts, 394 U.S. at 707-08; Black, 538 U.S. at 359-60. As this Court later explained, “[t]he crucial element separating legal innocence from wrongful conduct is not the fear that the threat causes, but the intent of the speaker.” Elonis, 575 U.S. at 737. A subjective-intent requirement preserves the boundary between criminal threats and protected speech.

The Third Circuit relied on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), but Kosma predates Black and cannot control the meaning of “true threats” after Black’s statement that a true threat involves a speaker who “means to communicate” a serious expression of violence. 538 U.S. at 359. To the extent Kosma permits conviction without proof of subjective intent, it is inconsistent with Black and with the general mens rea principles this Court has repeatedly applied. See Carter, 530 U.S. at 269-70; Staples, 511 U.S. at 605-06.

The government may argue that a subjective-intent requirement would make prosecution too difficult. But Congress chose to criminalize only “threats,” not negligent misunderstandings. See 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). And a knowledge-or-purpose standard still permits conviction where the defendant knew his words would be understood as threatening. See Elonis, 575 U.S. at 737-38. That standard adequately protects victims while honoring the statute’s text and the Constitution.

Because the jury here was instructed on an objective standard alone, Elonis’s conviction cannot stand. The Court should reverse and hold that a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended, or at minimum knew, that his communication would be taken as a threat.
