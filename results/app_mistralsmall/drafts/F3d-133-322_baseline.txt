**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE INDICTMENT FAILED TO STATE AN OFFENSE BECAUSE MRS. PARKER LACKED AUTHORITY TO PERFORM "OFFICIAL ACTS" UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).**

Mrs. Parker’s conviction rests on the erroneous legal premise that her conduct constituted "official acts" under 18 U.S.C. § 201. Section 201(b)(2)(C) criminalizes bribery where a public official is induced to perform or omit an "official act." An "official act" is defined broadly in § 201(a)(3) as "any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy" pending before or within the official’s purview. However, this definition is not limitless. The Supreme Court has long held that an "official act" must be one that the defendant was actually authorized to perform. *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914) ("[A]n act is not official unless it be done under color of office and in the exercise of official authority."); *United States v. Cincotta*, 689 F.2d 238, 241 (1st Cir. 1982) ("[A]n act is not ‘official’ unless the defendant had authority to perform it.").

Mrs. Parker’s duties were clerical and ministerial—assisting ALJ Aronson by typing decisions, recording hearings, and mailing correspondence. She had no authority to approve or deny SSI benefits, nor to sign ALJ Aronson’s name. Her actions in altering and forging letters were unauthorized and criminal under state law, but they were not "official acts" within the meaning of § 201. *See United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983) (finding no § 201 violation where defendant lacked authority to perform the acts in question). The government’s reliance on *Birdsall* is misplaced; that case involved a customs official who had authority to issue permits but issued them corruptly. Here, Mrs. Parker lacked any such authority.

The government’s argument that "established usage" or "settled practice" expands the scope of "official acts" is unavailing. While *Birdsall* acknowledged that duties may be established by custom, it did not extend § 201 to acts entirely outside an official’s authority. 233 U.S. at 231. Mrs. Parker’s conduct—altering letters and forging signatures—was not part of her official duties, nor was it ratified or condoned by the SSA. The indictment therefore failed to state an offense because it did not allege that Mrs. Parker performed an "official act" as defined by § 201(a)(3).

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT MR. PARKER’S CONVICTION FOR CONSPIRACY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY.**

Mr. Parker’s conviction rests on the testimony of Mrs. Parker, who implicated him during a custodial interrogation after waiving her *Miranda* rights. However, her statements were not corroborated by independent evidence linking Mr. Parker to the bribery scheme. "[A] conviction cannot be based solely on the uncorroborated testimony of an accomplice." *United States v. Williams*, 809 F.2d 1072, 1081 (5th Cir. 1987) (citing *United States v. Williams*, 734 F.2d 685, 687 (8th Cir. 1984)). Mrs. Parker’s testimony that Mr. Parker "assisted" her in the scheme was vague and unsupported by any documentary or testimonial evidence. The government presented no evidence that Mr. Parker received or demanded money, drafted forged letters, or communicated with any of the alleged bribe payors.

Moreover, the government failed to prove that Mr. Parker had the requisite intent to join the conspiracy. "[A] conspiracy conviction requires proof of a specific intent to agree and to commit the substantive offense." *United States v. Dillman*, 986 F.2d 101, 104 (5th Cir. 1993). Mrs. Parker’s unsupported allegations do not suffice. The evidence showed only that Mr. Parker was present during some of Mrs. Parker’s interactions with Simmons, but presence alone is insufficient to establish conspiracy. *United States v. Garcia*, 986 F.2d 1005, 1010 (5th Cir. 1993). The conviction must be reversed for lack of sufficient evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the convictions of Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker should be reversed. The indictment failed to state an offense as to Mrs. Parker, and the evidence was insufficient to support Mr. Parker’s conviction.
