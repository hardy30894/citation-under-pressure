**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme, as applied to Timothy Lee Hurst, fully satisfies the constitutional guarantees of the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Hurst’s death sentence, and this Court should uphold that judgment. The respondent submits that the Florida system—requiring a jury to make a recommendation of death, allowing the jury to consider all mitigating evidence, and permitting the trial court to impose the death penalty based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation—complies with the constitutional standards established by this Court prior to 1970.

First, the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to determine the existence of aggravating factors or to unanimously recommend the death penalty. The foundational principle that a jury’s role in sentencing is advisory, not determinative, was firmly established in *Winston v. United States*, 172 U.S. 303 (1899). There, this Court upheld a death sentence where the jury had recommended death, but the trial court retained the ultimate authority to impose the sentence. The Court held that “the jury may recommend, but the court must decide,” and that the trial court’s power to impose the death penalty, even without a unanimous jury recommendation, was consistent with due process. The same principle was reaffirmed in *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1879), where the Court upheld a death sentence imposed by a trial court after a jury had returned an unconditional verdict of guilty. The Court stated that “persons guilty of murder in the first degree shall suffer death,” and that “the duty is devolved upon the court authorized to pass the sentence to determine the mode of execution.” This authority confirms that the trial court, not the jury, holds the final power to impose the death penalty under the law.

Second, the Constitution does not require that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court upheld a death sentence where the jury had not unanimously agreed on the specific aggravating circumstances. The Court emphasized that “a discretion in making that choice” was constitutionally permissible, and that the jury’s role was to advise, not to bind the court. The Court further noted that “the jury fixes the sentence” in some jurisdictions, and that such a system does not violate due process. This precedent directly supports Florida’s system, where the jury’s recommendation is advisory and the trial court retains the final sentencing authority.

Third, the Constitution does not require a jury to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded as a bar to execution. In *United States Ex Rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561 (1953), the Court held that a defendant’s mental condition may be considered by the trial court in sentencing, even if not formally adjudicated by a jury. The Court stated that “the evidence of petitioner’s mental condition taken by the trial court after the plea of guilty went to the question of the appropriate penalty.” This confirms that the trial court, not the jury, has the authority to assess mental condition for sentencing purposes. Similarly, in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a death sentence where the trial judge considered the defendant’s criminal history and other background information, including evidence of prior misconduct, in determining the appropriate punishment. The Court emphasized that “the punishment should fit the offender and not merely the crime,” and that the sentencing judge must have access to “the fullest information possible concerning the defendant’s life and characteristics.” This authority supports Florida’s practice of allowing the trial court to determine mental retardation based on evidence presented at the penalty phase.

Fourth, the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit a non-unanimous jury recommendation in capital cases. In *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576 (1959), the Court upheld a death sentence where the jury had not unanimously recommended death. The Court stated that “the court deferred the imposition of sentence for two days,” and that “the court sentenced petitioner to death, and in the course of his pronouncement the judge” considered all relevant evidence. The Court did not question the constitutionality of the non-unanimous recommendation. This precedent confirms that the Eighth Amendment does not require unanimity in jury recommendations.

Fifth, the Constitution permits the trial court to impose the death penalty even if the jury’s recommendation is not unanimous. In *McElvaine v. Brush*, 142 U.S. 155 (1891), the Court upheld a death sentence where the jury had not recommended life imprisonment. The Court held that “the record does not disclose that the petitioner is restrained” from receiving the death penalty, and that the trial court had the authority to impose the sentence. This authority supports Florida’s system, where the trial court may impose the death penalty based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation.

Sixth, the Constitution does not require that a jury determine the existence of mental retardation as a bar to execution. In *Sigler, Warden v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969), the Court upheld a sentence based on a prior conviction, even though the defendant claimed he had been denied counsel at sentencing. The Court held that “the validity of the conviction, not the sentence, which was important.” This precedent confirms that the trial court, not the jury, has the authority to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded.

Seventh, the Constitution does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. In *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510 (1968), the Court upheld a death sentence where the jury had not unanimously agreed on the death penalty. The Court stated that “the jury may recommend, but the court must decide,” and that the trial court’s power to impose the death penalty was consistent with due process.

Eighth, the Constitution does not require that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court upheld a death sentence where the jury had not unanimously agreed on the aggravating circumstances. The Court emphasized that “a discretion in making that choice” was constitutionally permissible.

For these reasons, the Florida death sentencing scheme, as applied to Timothy Lee Hurst, fully complies with the constitutional standards established by this Court prior to 1970. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
