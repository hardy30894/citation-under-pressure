**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 VIOLATE THE FIRST AMENDMENT BY CHILLING PROTECTED SPEECH AND ARE NOT JUSTIFIED BY A SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST.

The First Amendment protects the right of individuals and associations to engage in political expression, including the communication of ideas to government officials in an effort to influence legislation. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to petition the government for redress of grievances is a fundamental component of the First Amendment. *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875). The disclosure requirements imposed by Chapter 93-121—mandating the public reporting of all lobbying expenditures, including those for food, entertainment, travel, and lodging—do not merely regulate conduct; they compel the disclosure of the very content and context of protected political speech.

The statute compels lobbyists to reveal not only *what* they spent, but *how* they spent it, thereby exposing the nature and timing of their communications with public officials. This level of detail creates a chilling effect on political expression. As the Court observed in *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 361 U.S. 516, 524 (1960), “The First Amendment protects the right to speak, and the right to speak includes the right to speak without fear of retribution or public exposure that may deter the expression.” The Florida statute, by requiring the public disclosure of expenditures categorized as “entertainment” or “food and beverages,” forces lobbyists to reveal the very forums in which they may have engaged in constitutionally protected advocacy. Such disclosures may deter individuals from participating in political discourse altogether, particularly when the nature of the communication is sensitive or controversial.

Moreover, the statute’s requirement that *all* expenditures—regardless of whether they are directly related to lobbying—be disclosed, including those made by the principal, creates a broad and indiscriminate burden on speech. The Supreme Court has consistently held that regulations that burden speech must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 530 (1945). Here, the state’s interest in transparency is not sufficiently compelling to justify such a sweeping mandate. The mere possibility of corruption or the appearance of impropriety does not justify the suppression of protected speech. *United States v. Associated Press*, 326 U.S. 1, 15 (1945). The state has not demonstrated that the disclosure of routine expenditures such as meals or travel is necessary to prevent corruption, nor has it shown that less intrusive alternatives—such as targeted reporting of large or suspicious expenditures—would be ineffective.

The statute’s administrative mechanism, which allows for advisory opinions, does not cure the constitutional defect. *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 402 (1930). The mere availability of guidance does not render a law constitutional if the law itself is overbroad and chills protected speech. The requirement to disclose expenditures in categories such as “entertainment” and “research” is inherently vague and invites arbitrary enforcement. *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). The lack of clear standards for what constitutes a “lobbying expenditure” renders the statute unconstitutionally vague, violating the due process component of the Fifth Amendment and the First Amendment’s protection against overbroad laws.

II. THE BAN ON CONTINGENCY-FEE LOBBYING IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE IT DIRECTLY RESTRICTS THE CONTENT OF POLITICAL SPEECH AND IS NOT JUSTIFIED BY A SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST.

The prohibition on contingency-fee arrangements—whereby a lobbyist receives compensation only if a desired legislative outcome is achieved—constitutes a direct restriction on the content of political speech. The First Amendment protects not only the right to speak but also the right to structure the terms of one’s speech. *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968). The state may not regulate the form or method of expression unless it serves a compelling governmental interest and is narrowly tailored. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. at 530.

The Florida statute’s ban on contingency fees is not narrowly tailored. It applies equally to all lobbyists, regardless of the nature of the issue, the size of the principal, or the likelihood of corruption. This blanket prohibition sweeps within its reach legitimate and non-corrupt lobbying activities. The state has not demonstrated that contingency fees are inherently corrupt or that they pose a greater risk than other forms of compensation. Indeed, the mere fact that a lobbyist is paid based on results does not imply that the speech is false, coercive, or otherwise unprotected. *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. 644, 650 (1929). The state’s interest in preventing corruption cannot justify the suppression of all such arrangements, especially when the statute applies to small, non-profit, or public-interest lobbying efforts.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may not regulate speech based on its content or the speaker’s motive. *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. at 650. The contingency-fee ban targets a specific method of financing political expression—payment based on results—without regard to the substance of the speech or the integrity of the speaker. This is precisely the kind of content-based regulation that the First Amendment forbids. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574. The state’s interest in preventing corruption is not sufficient to justify such a broad restriction on the form of political expression.

Furthermore, the statute’s ban on contingency fees is not supported by any historical precedent. The Court has never upheld a law that prohibited a speaker from receiving compensation based on the success of their message. *United States v. Associated Press*, 326 U.S. at 15. The Florida statute, by contrast, penalizes the very mechanism by which many individuals and organizations—particularly those with limited resources—can afford to engage in political advocacy.
