# ARGUMENT

The question presented is which court possesses jurisdiction to review a Board decision that disposes of a mixed case—one combining an appealable adverse action with a claim of discrimination—on the ground that the Board lacked jurisdiction over the underlying action. Petitioner submits that review belongs in district court, because the classification of a case as "mixed" must be fixed by the claim the employee actually presented, and because sound principles of judicial administration and the remedial character of civil-rights legislation counsel against a rule that shunts discrimination claims to the Federal Circuit merely because the Board, after full adjudication, concluded it lacked jurisdiction.

## I. The forum for judicial review must be determined by the nature of the claim as presented, not by the tribunal's ultimate disposition of a threshold question.

This Court has long held that the character of a controversy for jurisdictional purposes is fixed by what the claimant actually alleges, not by what a tribunal later decides about the merits of that allegation. In *Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908), the Court explained that the existence of federal-question jurisdiction "must be determined from what necessarily appears in the plaintiff's statement of his own claim," without regard to matters raised in defense. The same logic governs the amount-in-controversy inquiry: in *St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 303 U.S. 283 (1938), the Court held that jurisdiction, once properly invoked on the face of the pleading, is not defeated by subsequent developments that reveal the claim to be weaker than alleged.

That principle finds fullest expression in *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 (1946), which distinguished a tribunal's power to hear a case from its ultimate ruling on the merits. The Court explained that "the failure to state a proper cause of action calls for a judgment on the merits and not for a dismissal for want of jurisdiction." *Id.* at 682. A claim does not become jurisdictionally nonexistent merely because the tribunal, after considering it, rules against the claimant.

Perry's petition presented precisely such a mixed case: he alleged that his suspension and retirement were coerced through discrimination and retaliation. That the Board, after considering the evidence, concluded that the retirement was voluntary and therefore beyond its jurisdiction does not retroactively transform Perry's mixed case into something else. Under *Mottley*, *St. Paul Mercury*, and *Bell v. Hood*, the classification of Perry's case—and hence the forum for review—must be assessed by reference to the claim as presented, not by reference to the Board's ultimate disposition of it.

## II. Splitting a single discrimination controversy between two courts contravenes the settled policy against fragmenting one controversy into piecemeal proceedings.

This Court has consistently disfavored rules that fracture a unitary controversy into disconnected proceedings before different tribunals. In *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949), the Court cautioned against permitting appellate mechanisms to produce "piecemeal" litigation of matters properly resolved together, confining any exception to claims "separable from, and collateral to, rights asserted in the action." *Id.* at 546. A rule making the forum for review of Perry's discrimination and retaliation claims depend on the fortuity of the Board's jurisdictional ruling would produce exactly the fragmentation these precedents disfavor: an employee whose claim of coerced retirement is rejected would be routed to the Federal Circuit, while an employee whose identical claim is credited would proceed to district court. The discrimination claim is not "separable" or "collateral" from the voluntariness question; the two are inextricably intertwined, since the very discrimination and retaliation Perry alleges are what he says rendered his agreement involuntary in the first place. *Cohen*'s policy against splitting inseparable questions between tribunals therefore counsels for a single forum—district court—regardless of the ground on which the Board disposed of the appeal.

## III. The remedial purpose of extending civil-rights protections to federal employment requires a construction that secures, rather than forecloses, meaningful judicial access.

This Court has repeatedly construed remedial civil-rights and labor statutes to favor accessible judicial recourse for the individuals they were designed to protect. In *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400 (1968), the Court explained that a claimant vindicating rights under civil-rights legislation acts "not for himself alone" but "as a 'private attorney general,' vindicating a policy that Congress considered of the highest priority." *Id.* at 402. Likewise, in *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co.*, 323 U.S. 192 (1944), the Court construed the Railway Labor Act to guarantee an aggrieved employee a judicial remedy sufficient to make the statutory right effective, rather than allow that right to be defeated by a restrictive reading of the statute's procedural machinery.

The same interpretive commitment governs here. A construction under which Perry's discrimination claim is diverted to the Federal Circuit—a tribunal without plenary authority to try discrimination claims on the merits—would substantially impair the effective judicial remedy Congress meant to guarantee federal employees. District court review, by contrast, preserves the full, trial-type proceeding that claims of discrimination and retaliation of this kind require, consistent with the remedial purposes this Court has recognized in *Newman* and *Steele*.

For these reasons, the decision below should be reversed, and Perry's case should proceed to review in the United States District Court.
