**ARGUMENT**

The 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability to the United States for a response action or response costs within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). See 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Guam therefore retains its right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). See Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004).

Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” The Decree expressly states that it was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. This disclaimer precludes any resolution of liability. See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015).

The Decree’s substantive obligations likewise fail to satisfy the statutory trigger. It arose from a Clean Water Act enforcement action addressing pollutant discharges, not CERCLA hazardous-substance liability. See Complaint for Injunctive Relief ¶ 26, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam). CERCLA’s contribution provision applies only to settlements resolving CERCLA liability. See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009).

Because the Decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), the three-year limitations period of § 113(g)(3) never began to run. Guam’s timely § 107(a) action therefore remains available. See United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998). The court of appeals erred in holding otherwise.
