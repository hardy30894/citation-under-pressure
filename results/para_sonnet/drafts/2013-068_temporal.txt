# ARGUMENT

## I. THE MASSACHUSETTS ACT IS A VALID, CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION

The First Amendment does not guarantee speakers the right to any particular forum or method of communication; it guarantees that ample means of communication remain open. This Court has long recognized that government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech on public streets and sidewalks so long as the regulation is content-neutral, serves a significant governmental interest, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576 (1941) (upholding a licensing scheme for parades because it was "a valid exercise of the police power" that left speakers "free to communicate their ideas"). The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute satisfies each element of this settled test.

**A. The Act Is Content- and Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The statute draws no distinctions based on the content of speech. It restricts entry into a defined zone by anyone—counselor, protester, or curious bystander—except those falling within narrow, functionally defined categories: patients, employees, and those merely passing through. This Court has consistently upheld regulations of this kind where, as here, "the regulation is applicable to all who ... would'" occupy the same physical space regardless of what they intend to say. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 554-55 (1965) (upholding a statute restricting picketing near a courthouse because it protected the "administration of justice" without regard to the picketers' message). The exemptions in the Act—for patients, employees, and passersby—are keyed to *function*, not viewpoint; a pro-life sidewalk counselor and a pro-choice escort are treated identically. Cf. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1966) (sustaining a trespass conviction for demonstrators on jailhouse grounds because the State could regulate access to the property "for security reasons" without regard to the demonstrators' message).

**B. The Act Serves a Substantial Governmental Interest.**

Massachusetts enacted the buffer zone to ensure the safe, unobstructed passage of patients and staff into healthcare facilities. This interest in unimpeded ingress and egress from a building is precisely the sort of interest this Court has recognized as legitimate and substantial. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 306 (1940), the Court acknowledged that the State retains "power to regulate the times, the places, and the manner" of speech-related activity to protect "public safety, peace, comfort, or convenience." Likewise, in *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 398 (1953), the Court sustained a licensing ordinance because "[t]he right to speak freely" does not carry with it "the right to use every possible method of communication" without regard for the safety and convenience of others using the same public space. The buffer zone protects exactly that: the physical safety and unimpeded movement of women seeking medical care.

**C. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels of Communication.**

Critically, the Act does not silence petitioners. It does not restrict what they may say, only where, within a narrow thirty-five-foot zone, they may stand while saying it. As the trial record demonstrates, petitioners retain every traditional mode of communication: loud oral speech, amplified prayer, handheld signs, distribution of literature, and personal appeals from just outside the buffer's edge. This is the same protection this Court demanded in *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939), where it distinguished between an ordinance that "prohibits the distribution of literature" outright and one that merely regulates the manner of distribution while preserving "other appropriate means" of reaching an audience. The Massachusetts statute falls into the latter category: it channels speech away from clinic doorways while preserving the entirety of petitioners' message and their proximity to the flow of pedestrian traffic on the sidewalk itself.

Nor does the buffer zone deprive petitioners of the "quintessential" public forum the streets and sidewalks represent. *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (plurality opinion) (streets and parks "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" for "assembling, communicating thoughts ... and discussing public questions"). Petitioners remain on the public way; they are merely required to stand a short distance from the clinic entrance. That modest spatial adjustment, imposed uniformly on all persons regardless of viewpoint, is a far cry from the total exclusions this Court has struck down. Compare *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946) (invalidating total prohibition on distribution of literature in company-owned town).

## II. THE ACT IS NOT AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL PRIOR RESTRAINT OR IMPERMISSIBLY VAGUE

Petitioners' remaining challenges fare no better under settled doctrine. A regulation is not a prior restraint merely because it conditions access to a forum on compliance with neutral, generally applicable rules. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574 (a licensing requirement for use of public streets is not a prior restraint where it is administered "without unfettered discretion" and applied evenhandedly). The Massachusetts statute contains no discretionary licensing scheme at all; it is a flat, generally applicable spatial rule that any person can read and comply with in advance. Nor is the statute vague: its thirty-five-foot boundary, marked with painted lines and posted signage at each facility, gives "persons of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited." *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308 (quoting general due process vagueness principles).

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
