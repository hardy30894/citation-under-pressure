**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll only when it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status is contrary to the Act’s structure and purpose. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 612 (1969) (recognizing that the Act contemplates prompt resolution of bargaining disputes through elections or voluntary recognition). The Board’s insistence on the same standard for polling as for withdrawal of recognition ignores the distinct functions of each. *Amalgamated Ass’n of St. Elec. Ry. & Motor Coach Employees v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Bd.*, 340 U.S. 383, 396 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) (noting that the Act permits multiple avenues for testing employee support). The Board’s approach renders polling a “marginally useful” tool, *see NLRB v. Tex-Tan Welder, Inc.*, 363 F.2d 235, 238 (5th Cir. 1966), when it should serve as a legitimate means for employers to ascertain employee sentiment without the delay and formality of an RM election. *NLRB v. Kohler Co.*, 220 F.2d 380, 383 (7th Cir. 1955) (holding that polling is permissible where the employer acts in good faith to test employee support).

The Board’s rule is also inconsistent with the Act’s deference to employer good faith. *NLRB v. Burnup & Sims, Inc.*, 379 U.S. 21, 23 (1964) (holding that an employer’s good-faith belief in the truth of its statements is a defense to an unfair labor practice charge). The Board’s standard effectively penalizes employers for acting in good faith to verify union support, a purpose Congress expressly contemplated. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 477 (1941) (noting that the Act seeks to prevent industrial strife by ensuring fair representation). The Board’s approach thus frustrates the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 345 (1938) (holding that the Act aims to promote industrial peace through collective bargaining).

II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

The Board erred in discounting the statements of employees who expressed anti-union sentiments. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (holding that statements by employees evidencing opposition to a union are probative of their sentiments). The Board’s reliance on the timing of certain statements is misplaced. *NLRB v. Reed & Prince Mfg. Co.*, 205 F.2d 131, 134 (1st Cir. 1953) (holding that employee statements made in the ordinary course of employment are admissible as evidence of their sentiments). The Board’s refusal to credit the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr—who reported that a majority of employees opposed the union—was arbitrary. *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940) (holding that hearsay may be considered if it is the type of evidence upon which a reasonable mind might rely).

The Board also erred in disregarding the poll results themselves. *NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n*, 310 U.S. 318, 339 (1940) (holding that the Board must give due weight to evidence of employee sentiment). The poll was conducted under strict safeguards, supervised by a neutral third party, and reflected the free choice of employees. *NLRB v. Somerset Classics, Inc.*, 193 F.2d 613, 616 (2d Cir. 1952) (holding that a secret ballot poll is a reliable method for ascertaining employee sentiment). The Board’s refusal to credit the poll’s results undermines the Act’s goal of ensuring that bargaining relationships reflect the true wishes of employees. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42 (1937) (holding that the Act seeks to protect the right of employees to organize and bargain collectively).

The Board’s determination is further undermined by the fact that the bargaining unit was newly formed, with many employees hired after the sale of the dealership. *NLRB v. Armco Drainage & Metal Prods. Co.*, 182 F.2d 335, 338 (6th Cir. 1950) (holding that the presumption of majority support is weaker in a newly formed unit). The Board’s refusal to consider the statements of employees who were no longer in the unit as of January 25, 1991, was arbitrary. *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 597 (1941) (holding that the Board must consider all relevant evidence in determining employee sentiment).

For these reasons, the Board’s order should be reversed.
