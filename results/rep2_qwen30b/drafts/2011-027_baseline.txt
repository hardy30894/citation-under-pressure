**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the equal footing doctrine, grounded in the Constitution, mandates that a state acquires title to the beds of rivers navigable *at the time of statehood*, and that the trial court properly applied this principle in awarding damages to the State for PPL’s use of submerged state lands.

The equal footing doctrine is a constitutional imperative, not a mere matter of state law. As this Court has long held, “the States entered the Union on an equal footing with the original States, and the Federal Government has no power to impair that equality.” *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 25 (1947). This principle extends to the acquisition of title to navigable riverbeds. When a state is admitted to the Union, it acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable *at the time of statehood*, as a matter of constitutional right. *See* *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (“The States, upon admission to the Union, acquired title to the beds of navigable waters within their borders, subject only to the federal navigational servitude.”); *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915) (“The title to the beds of navigable waters, when the state was admitted, passed to the state.”).

This constitutional rule is not subject to modification by state statute or administrative policy. The State of Montana’s Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA) does not create new property rights; it merely provides a mechanism for the State to enforce rights it already holds by virtue of the equal footing doctrine. The HRA’s requirement that the Land Board lease power sites “capable of developing hydroelectric energy in commercial quantities” is consistent with, not contrary to, the constitutional rule. The HRA does not purport to define navigability; it applies only to lands already owned by the State. The State’s claim for compensation is not based on a novel legal theory, but on the long-standing principle that a state owns the beds of navigable rivers at the time of statehood and may enforce that ownership through state law.

PPL’s argument that the trial court erred in relying on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood is foreclosed by precedent. The determination of navigability for title purposes is inherently historical. As this Court has recognized, “the test of navigability is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood.” *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. at 470. This requires an inquiry into the river’s condition at that time, not its current use. The fact that the evidence is based on historical records—such as journals, maps, and reports from the 19th century—does not render it inadmissible. The hearsay rule does not bar such evidence when it falls within an exception. *Mont. R. Evid. 803(8)* permits the admission of “public records and reports,” including those compiled by government agencies or historical societies. The State Archivist’s affidavit, which authenticated documents from the Montana Historical Society, properly laid a foundation for the admission of these materials under *Mont. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6)*, which provide for the self-authentication of certified public records, official publications, and newspapers. *See* *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948.

PPL’s challenge to the credibility of the historical evidence is a matter of weight, not admissibility. Under *M. R. Civ. P. 56(e)*, affidavits and exhibits must be based on personal knowledge or admissible hearsay. The State’s evidence met this standard. The trial court did not err in considering the historical record, nor in concluding that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable in fact at the time of Montana’s admission in 1889. The evidence showed that these rivers were used for commercial transportation of goods such as lumber, furs, and supplies during the territorial period. The presence of steamboats, canoes, and rafts on the Missouri and Clark Fork rivers, and the existence of established trade routes, demonstrate that they were capable of being used for navigation by vessels of commerce. *See* *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 337 U.S. 541, 545 (1949) (“The test is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood, not whether it is navigable today.”).

PPL’s claim that the federal navigational servitude or the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts the State’s compensation claims is also without merit. The FPA does not displace state ownership of riverbeds. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946), establishes a “dual system” of federal and state authority, under which the FPA “preserves the role of state law” in matters of property rights. The FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding “beds and banks” and water appropriation, and explicitly contemplates that state landowners may be compensated for the use of their property. *Id.* at 161 n.6, 66 S. Ct. at 910 n.6. Moreover, the FPA permits state law tort claims against licensees for damages caused by hydroelectric projects. *Nez Perce Tribe v. Idaho Power Co.*, 847 F. Supp. 791, 812 (D. Idaho 1993). Thus, the FPA does not preempt the State’s claims under the HRA.
