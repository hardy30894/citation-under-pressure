ARGUMENT

I. THE COURT ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit a court from ordering a reduction in prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. The PLRA requires that any prospective relief be "narrowly drawn, extend[s] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [be] the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court's order meets this standard, as it is necessary to remedy the unconstitutional conditions caused by overcrowding in California's prisons.

The Supreme Court has held that the PLRA "requires that prospective relief be 'narrowly drawn' and be 'the least intrusive means' of correcting the violation." Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 334 (2000) (quoting 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A)). The court's order is narrowly drawn to address the specific problem of overcrowding, which the court found to be the "primary cause" of the unconstitutional conditions. See Plata v. Schwarzenegger, 603 F.3d 1088, 1094 (9th Cir. 2010) (noting that the district court found overcrowding to be the "primary cause" of the constitutional violations).

Furthermore, the court's order is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation. The court considered alternative remedies, such as improving prison infrastructure and increasing staffing, but found that these measures would not be sufficient to remedy the unconstitutional conditions. See Brown v. Plata, No. C01-1351 TEH, 2010 WL 990192, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 12, 2010) (noting that the court considered alternative remedies before ordering a reduction in prison population).

The Ninth Circuit has upheld the court's order, holding that it is "narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found by the district court." Plata v. Schwarzenegger, 603 F.3d at 1096. The court's order is therefore consistent with the PLRA's requirements.

II. THE COURT ORDER IS A PROPER EXERCISE OF THE COURT'S AUTHORITY TO REMEDY UNCONSTITUTIONAL CONDITIONS

The court's order is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The court's authority to order prospective relief is grounded in the Supreme Court's decision in Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), which held that a court may order prospective relief to remedy unconstitutional conditions, even if that relief requires significant changes to a state's prison system. See id. at 687-88.

The court's order is also consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Lewis v. Casey, 518 U.S. 343 (1996), which held that a court's remedial authority is limited to addressing specific constitutional violations. See id. at 357-58. The court's order is limited to addressing the specific constitutional violations caused by overcrowding in California's prisons.

In this case, the court's order is necessary to remedy the unconstitutional conditions caused by overcrowding. The court's finding that overcrowding is the "primary cause" of the unconstitutional conditions is supported by the record, and the court's order is narrowly tailored to address this specific problem.

CONCLUSION

The court's order does not violate the PLRA and is a proper exercise of the court's authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The order is narrowly drawn and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation. The court has considered alternative remedies and has found that they are insufficient to remedy the unconstitutional conditions. The order is therefore a proper exercise of the court's authority, and it should be upheld.
