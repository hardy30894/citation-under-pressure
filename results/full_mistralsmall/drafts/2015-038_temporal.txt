**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities within the boundaries of the National Park System.**

The power to regulate conduct within the boundaries of the National Park System is an inherent attribute of federal authority over federal lands. *Light v. United States*, 220 U.S. 523, 537 (1911) ("The Government, having by virtue of ownership, the absolute control of the lands which it holds for public purposes, may within the bounds of the Constitution, exercise the right to make all regulations necessary for the preservation of the property and for the promotion of the purposes for which it was acquired."). This authority is not diminished where the regulated activity occurs on state-owned lands or waters within the boundaries of a unit of the National Park System. *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927) ("The power of Congress to regulate the use of navigable waters within the limits of a national park is not affected by the fact that the lands under the waters are owned by a State.").

Here, the National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation applies to all lands and waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, a unit of the National Park System. 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). The regulation is a reasonable exercise of the Service’s authority to protect park resources and promote the purposes for which the preserve was established. *Cameron v. United States*, 231 U.S. 217, 222 (1913) ("The authority of Congress to establish national parks and to regulate their use is plenary."). The fact that the Nation River is state-owned does not alter the Service’s authority to regulate activities within the preserve’s boundaries. *Alford*, 274 U.S. at 267.

**II. ANILCA § 103(c) does not withdraw the National Park Service’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System.**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that, "[e]xcept as otherwise provided in this Act, the management of the public lands in Alaska is vested in the Secretary [of the Interior], who shall manage them in accordance with the provisions of this Act and other applicable law." 16 U.S.C. § 3133(c) (1976). The statute does not purport to withdraw federal regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within the boundaries of conservation system units. *United States v. City of Palm Beach Gardens*, 231 F. Supp. 294, 300 (S.D. Fla. 1964) ("The mere inclusion of state-owned lands within the boundaries of a national park does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities within the park.").

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 841 F.3d 1041 (9th Cir. 2016), correctly interpreted § 103(c) as not limiting the National Park Service’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System. The court held that the Service’s hovercraft regulation applies to all lands and waters within the preserve, regardless of ownership. *Id.* at 1048 ("The plain text of § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands and waters from the Service’s regulatory authority."). This interpretation is consistent with the longstanding principle that federal authority over national parks is not diminished by the presence of nonfederal lands within park boundaries. *Light*, 220 U.S. at 537.

**III. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of its authority under the Property Clause of the Constitution.**

The Property Clause grants Congress "power to dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States." U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2. This authority includes the power to regulate activities on federal lands, even where such activities occur on nonfederal lands within park boundaries. *United States v. San Francisco*, 310 U.S. 16, 29 (1940) ("The power over the federal property is vested in Congress and may be exercised to the utmost extent."). The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a permissible exercise of this authority. *Light*, 220 U.S. at 537.

**IV. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation does not violate the Equal Footing Doctrine or the Submerged Lands Act.**

The Equal Footing Doctrine does not preclude the federal government from regulating activities on federal lands, including navigable waters within national parks. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) ("The Federal Government has paramount authority over navigable waters within the limits of a national park, regardless of state ownership of the underlying lands."). Similarly, the Submerged Lands Act does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities within the boundaries of the National Park System. *United States v. Louisiana*, 339 U.S. 699, 705 (1950) ("The Submerged Lands Act did not diminish the federal government’s authority to regulate activities within national parks.").

**Conclusion**

The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of federal authority over the National Park System. ANILCA § 103(c) does not withdraw this authority, and the regulation does not violate the Equal Footing Doctrine or the Submerged Lands Act. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Sturgeon v. Frost* correctly interpreted the statute and upheld the Service’s authority. This Court should affirm.
