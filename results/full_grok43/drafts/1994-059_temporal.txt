The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution fix the exclusive criteria for membership in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; id. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. Those provisions “were intended to be exclusive” and “preclude[] a state from imposing additional qualifications.” Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522, 548 (1969). Because Amendment 73 adds an age-and-service disqualification not found in the text, it is void.

The Framers rejected proposals that would have permitted states to vary the listed qualifications. Id. at 532-34. Uniform national standards were essential to the “national character” of the federal legislature. Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1, 7-9 (1964). Allowing Arkansas to impose its own restrictions would produce the “hodge-podge of qualifications” the Constitution was designed to prevent. Powell, 395 U.S. at 533.

State power over the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections, U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1, does not extend to redefining eligibility. That clause “does not authorize the States to impose additional qualifications.” Powell, 395 U.S. at 547. Nor may a state achieve the same result indirectly by denying ballot access. See United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941) (states may regulate procedures but may not “alter the qualifications” fixed by the Constitution).

The severability clause cannot rescue the state-office provisions. When Congress and the people of Arkansas were presented with a single package that included an unconstitutional federal restriction, the measure as a whole must fall. See, e.g., Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 312-13 (1936). The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Section 3 violates the Qualifications Clauses; the remainder of Amendment 73 therefore cannot stand.

The judgment should be affirmed.
