The respondent, Eugene Woodard, has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings. The Due Process Clause does not apply to clemency proceedings because they are not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. As the Supreme Court held in Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." This is because clemency is a matter of executive discretion, and the decision to grant or deny it is not subject to the same due process requirements as a judicial proceeding. See also, Faulder v. Johnson, 81 F.3d 515, 517 (5th Cir. 1996) ("Clemency proceedings are not part of the trial.").

Moreover, the existence of a clemency procedure does not create a protected liberty interest. As the Court held in Dumschat, a state's clemency procedure does not give rise to a constitutional liberty interest simply because it is available. 452 U.S. at 465. The Court has also held that a state's parole procedures do not create a protected liberty interest unless they contain "explicitly mandatory language" that limits the discretion of the decision-maker. See, e.g., Kentucky Department of Corrections v. Thompson, 490 U.S. 454, 463 (1989). Ohio's clemency procedure, which is governed by Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, does not contain such language, and therefore does not create a protected liberty interest.

Furthermore, the Due Process Clause is not implicated by Ohio's clemency procedure because it is not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. As the Court held in Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985), due process applies to proceedings that are "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." Clemency proceedings, however, are not part of the adjudicatory process, but rather a separate, discretionary proceeding that occurs after a defendant's guilt and sentence have been finally determined. See, e.g., Herrera v. Collins, 506 U.S. 390, 411-12 (1993) ("Clemency is deeply rooted in our Anglo-American tradition of law, and is committed to the sole discretion of the executive.").

The respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is also without merit. The Ohio clemency procedure does not compel a prisoner to waive their Fifth Amendment rights. As the Court held in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 317 (1976), a state may not "penalize" a person's assertion of their Fifth Amendment rights, but it may "draw[] adverse inferences from [their] failure to testify." Ohio's clemency procedure does not require a prisoner to answer any questions, and the prisoner remains free to assert their Fifth Amendment rights at any time. See, Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07; Ohio Department of Rehabilitation and Correction Regulation (providing that a prisoner "may" participate in a personal interview, but is not required to do so).

Additionally, the respondent's claim that the clemency procedure presents a "Hobson's choice" between participating in the procedure and asserting their Fifth Amendment rights is speculative and premature. As the Court held in Baxter, it is "premature" to consider the constitutionality of a state procedure when the alleged harm is speculative and has not yet occurred. 425 U.S. at 324. The respondent has not yet participated in the clemency procedure, and it is unclear whether he will be required to waive his Fifth Amendment rights if he does so.

The Sixth Circuit's decision to remand the case for further proceedings is therefore unwarranted. The court should have affirmed the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims, as they are without merit. See, e.g., Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472, 487 (1995) (holding that a court should not "constitutionaliz[e]" state procedures unless they implicate a protected liberty interest); Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976) (holding that the Due Process Clause is not implicated by state procedures that do not affect a protected liberty interest). 

In conclusion, the Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency procedure does not implicate the respondent's due process or Fifth Amendment rights. The decision of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims should be reinstated.
