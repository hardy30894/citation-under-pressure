## Argument

Tarmac Roofing Systems, Inc. ("Tarmac") respectfully submits that the district court's judgment awarding Stelwagon Manufacturing Company ("Stelwagon") $1,423,392.50 should be vacated because Stelwagon failed to present sufficient evidence of actual antitrust damages under the Robinson-Patman Act.

### I. Stelwagon Failed to Prove Actual Antitrust Damages

To recover damages under the Clayton Act, a plaintiff must prove that it suffered actual antitrust damages as a result of the defendant's anticompetitive conduct. J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp., 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). The Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff must show that it suffered a cognizable injury that was caused by the defendant's violation of the antitrust laws. Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc., 429 U.S. 477, 489 (1977). In this case, Stelwagon failed to present sufficient evidence of actual antitrust damages.

The district court erred in allowing the jury to speculate about the amount of damages suffered by Stelwagon. As the Supreme Court has noted, "damages are not rendered uncertain because they cannot be calculated with absolute exactness." Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931). However, a plaintiff must still provide a reasonable basis for estimating damages. Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). Stelwagon's damages expert failed to provide such a basis, relying on speculative assumptions about the impact of Tarmac's price discrimination on Stelwagon's sales. See Stelwagon Manufacturing Company v. Tarmac Roofing Systems, Inc., 862 F.Supp. 1361, 1368 (E.D.Pa.1994) (noting that Stelwagon's damages expert "failed to account for various factors that could have affected Stelwagon's sales").

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Tarmac's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law

Tarmac moved for judgment as a matter of law at the close of Stelwagon's case and again at the close of all evidence, arguing that Stelwagon had failed to present sufficient evidence of actual antitrust damages. The district court denied both motions, and the case was submitted to the jury. However, as the Third Circuit has held, a district court should grant a motion for judgment as a matter of law if "there is no legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for [the non-moving] party." Lightning Lube, Inc. v. Witco Corp., 4 F.3d 1153, 1166 (3d Cir. 1993) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a)(1)). In this case, Stelwagon's failure to present sufficient evidence of actual antitrust damages rendered judgment as a matter of law in favor of Tarmac appropriate.

The Third Circuit has also held that a plaintiff must prove causation and injury with reasonable particularity. See Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 395 U.S. 100, 114 (1969) (holding that a plaintiff must prove that the defendant's anticompetitive conduct caused the plaintiff's injury). Stelwagon failed to meet this burden, and the district court should have granted Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law.

Furthermore, the district court's denial of Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law was inconsistent with the principles established in Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574 (1986), and Monsanto Co. v. Spray-Rite Service Corp., 465 U.S. 752 (1984). In Matsushita, the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff must present evidence that "tends to exclude the possibility" that the defendant's conduct was lawful. 475 U.S. at 588. In Monsanto, the Court held that a plaintiff must show that the defendant's anticompetitive conduct was the cause of the plaintiff's injury. 465 U.S. at 763. Stelwagon failed to meet these standards, and the district court should have granted Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law.

In addition, the district court's decision was also at odds with the holding in Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp., 370 U.S. 690 (1962), which established that a plaintiff must prove that the defendant's anticompetitive conduct was the proximate cause of the plaintiff's injury. 370 U.S. at 702. Stelwagon's failure to prove causation and injury with reasonable particularity rendered judgment as a matter of law in favor of Tarmac appropriate.

Finally, the district court's remittitur of the damages award does not cure the error. As the Third Circuit has noted, a remittitur is not a substitute for a proper damages calculation. See Gumbs v. Pueblo Int'l, Inc., 823 F.2d 768, 773 (3d Cir. 1987). In this case, the district court's remittitur was based on a finding that the damages awarded by the jury were "unsupported by the evidence and grossly excessive." Stelwagon Manufacturing Company v. Tarmac Roofing Systems, Inc., 862 F.Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D.Pa.1994). This finding underscores the need for a new trial or judgment as a matter of law in favor of Tarmac.

For the foregoing reasons, Tarmac respectfully requests that this Court vacate the district court's judgment and enter judgment in its favor or, alternatively, remand the case for a new trial.
