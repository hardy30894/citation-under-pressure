**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STANDARD FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII IS NOT AN “EGREGIOUS” OR “OUTRAGEOUS” CONDUCT TEST—IT IS A MERE PROOF OF MALICE OR RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE

The central question presented is whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The answer is no. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, explicitly provided that punitive damages may be awarded when a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This statutory language does not require proof of conduct beyond intentional discrimination; it requires only a showing of *mental state*—malice or reckless indifference—distinct from the intent necessary for liability under Title VII.

The Court has long recognized that punitive damages are not available simply because the defendant committed a wrongful act. Rather, they are reserved for cases involving “an aggravated form of misconduct” that goes beyond mere negligence or even intentional wrongdoing. See *State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 407, 419 (2003). But the inquiry into whether such misconduct exists must begin with the statutory standard—not with judicially imposed thresholds of “egregiousness” or “outrageousness” that have no basis in the text of § 1981a.

In *Bryant v. Jones*, 12 F.3d 1200 (3d Cir. 1993), the court held that “reckless indifference” means more than mere negligence—it involves a conscious disregard of a known risk of harm to a federally protected right. Id. at 1207. Similarly, in *Harris v. Hines*, 943 F.2d 1254 (11th Cir. 1991), the court explained that “malice” in this context refers to “an evil intent or ill will toward the victim,” while “reckless indifference” denotes a “deliberate or conscious disregard of the consequences of one’s actions.” Id. at 1260. These definitions are consistent with common law tort principles and do not demand that the conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in any objective sense. Indeed, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the presence of malice or reckless indifference is a question of fact for the jury, not a legal threshold requiring extreme behavior. See *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (“[A] plaintiff need only establish a prima facie case and introduce evidence sufficient to discredit the defendant’s proffered nondiscriminatory reasons; at that point, the fact finder, if so persuaded, may infer discrimination.”).

Here, the jury found that ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad on the basis of sex. That finding alone satisfies the liability component of Title VII. The jury was then entitled to consider whether ADA acted with malice or reckless indifference to her rights—questions that go beyond the mere existence of discrimination. Evidence at trial showed that ADA preselected Spangler before the position was even posted, copied his job description verbatim into the new role, and failed to interview Kolstad despite her qualifications. Moreover, testimony revealed that ADA’s senior official, Leonard Wheat, ignored Kolstad’s efforts to discuss the promotion, told sexually offensive jokes, and referred to female colleagues as “bitches” and “battleaxes.” While these incidents may not rise to the level of “outrageous” conduct under some hypothetical standard, they are sufficient to support a reasonable inference that ADA acted with reckless indifference to Kolstad’s federally protected rights.

The dissenting opinion in the D.C. Circuit mischaracterizes the legislative history by suggesting that Congress intended to limit punitive damages to “extraordinarily egregious cases.” See *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 110 F.3d 1435, 1441 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (Williams, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). But the House Report cited by the dissent—the very source it relies upon—was based on a version of the bill that never became law. As the majority correctly observed, the final statute uses the phrase “malice or with reckless indifference,” without the added term “callous” present in the unadopted House version. See id. at 1438. Thus, the dissent’s reliance on the unenacted House Report is misplaced. Furthermore, Representative Edwards’ statement—that punitive damages under § 1981a should be available “to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981”—is not inconsistent with the majority view. In fact, it reinforces the principle that punitive damages under § 1981a are governed by the same general common law standards applicable to intentional torts, which require more than mere intent to cause harm but do not demand “egregious” conduct.

To impose a separate “egregiousness” requirement would contradict Congress’s clear directive. It would also create a dangerous precedent: employers could engage in widespread, systematic discrimination with impunity so long as their conduct did not meet some undefined threshold of “outrageousness.” Such a rule would undermine the deterrent purpose of punitive damages, which Congress explicitly sought to strengthen through the 1991 Act. See *Civil Rights Act of 1991*, Pub. L. No. 102-166, § 101(a), 105 Stat. 1071, 1072 (1991) (“Congress finds that additional remedies under Federal law are needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.”).
