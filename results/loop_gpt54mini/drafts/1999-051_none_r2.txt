**Argument**

A state prisoner may not rely on a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim unless the ineffectiveness claim itself was fairly presented to the state courts or its default is independently excused. That rule follows from the Court’s exhaustion and procedural-default decisions, which preserve the States’ first opportunity to address alleged constitutional error. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944); *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204-05 (1950); *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 485-86 (1953); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963); *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447-48 (1965).

The habeas doctrine is built around comity and orderly review. Federal courts do not sit to disregard state procedures whenever a petitioner can identify a constitutional label. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947); *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464-65 (1938). Where the State has applied an adequate and independent procedural bar, federal review is unavailable absent a legally sufficient excuse. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87-91 (1977). The “cause” that may lift such a bar must be an external factor that actually explains the default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986).

That is fatal to petitioner’s position. His sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was defaulted in state court. He attempts to excuse that default by asserting that appellate counsel was ineffective for failing to raise the sufficiency issue on direct appeal. But the ineffectiveness claim on which he relies was itself not properly preserved in state court; it was dismissed as untimely. Under *Carrier*, a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot automatically serve as cause for a different defaulted claim. 477 U.S. at 489. To hold otherwise would let a petitioner bypass state procedural rules simply by converting one forfeited claim into the alleged explanation for another.

The Court’s exhaustion precedents confirm the point. In *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971), the Court held that a petitioner must fairly present the same federal claim to the state courts before seeking federal habeas relief. *Anderson v. Harless*, 459 U.S. 4, 6-7 (1982) (per curiam), reiterated that the State must be alerted to the federal nature of the claim itself. Ineffective assistance is not an abstract excuse; it is a separate Sixth Amendment claim governed by *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687, 694 (1984). Because it is a distinct constitutional claim, it must be exhausted like any other before it can do work as “cause.” *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 489.

Older decisions point in the same direction. *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 104-05 (1942), recognized the availability of habeas for constitutional error, but it did not dispense with the requirement that claims be properly presented. *Francis v. Henderson*, 425 U.S. 536, 542-43 (1976), and *Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 493-94 (1976), both reflect the Court’s refusal to turn habeas into an all-purpose forum for claims the State never had a fair chance to resolve. Petitioner’s rule would do exactly that: it would allow a prisoner to present one defaulted claim in federal court as the “cause” for another, even though the asserted cause itself was never properly exhausted.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment alter the analysis. *Strickland* requires both deficient performance and prejudice, 466 U.S. at 687, 694, and *Carrier* makes clear that attorney error amounts to “cause” only when it rises to that constitutional level and has been properly presented in state court. 477 U.S. at 488-89. Otherwise, the federal court would be adjudicating a defaulted ineffectiveness claim in the first instance, contrary to *Darr*, *Picard*, and *Henry*.

Petitioner’s position would also undermine finality and respect for state procedures. If any defaulted ineffectiveness allegation could excuse any other default, then timeliness rules, fair-presentation requirements, and adequate-and-independent state grounds would all be easily evaded. That is not the law. The Court’s precedents instead require a petitioner to clear each procedural hurdle in sequence: the underlying claim must be preserved or excused, and the purported cause must itself be preserved or excused. *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 489; *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 87-91.

Because petitioner’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself procedurally defaulted and not independently excused, it cannot serve as cause to overcome the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment below should be affirmed.
