The Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings that complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act and therefore “do not violate the Sixth Amendment.” Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994) (citing Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). Because the Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes,” Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008), the absence of appointed counsel created no constitutional infirmity that could be imported into a later federal prosecution.

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penalize[s] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747. The government must prove the predicate convictions beyond a reasonable doubt, but that requirement does not transform the predicates into elements whose validity is measured by federal constitutional standards. The same logic that permits an uncounseled misdemeanor to increase a Guidelines range, id. at 748, permits it to satisfy the statutory threshold for felony liability. Every other circuit to address the question has so held. See United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 604 (8th Cir. 2011).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule rests on a distinction between the “guilt phase” and the “sentencing phase” that Nichols itself rejected. The Court there made clear that reliability concerns are satisfied once the prior proceeding is shown to have been “valid” under the Sixth Amendment. 511 U.S. at 746. Tribal-court judgments that conform to the Indian Civil Rights Act meet that test. See United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978) (recognizing retained tribal sovereignty over internal criminal matters). Imposing an additional federal right-to-counsel requirement would “intrude[] needlessly on tribal self-government,” Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978), and would nullify the very enforcement mechanism Congress created in § 117(a) to address domestic violence on Indian lands.

Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), confirms rather than undermines this conclusion. Shelton invalidated only the sentence of imprisonment; it left the underlying conviction intact. Id. at 661-62. Bryant’s tribal-court convictions are therefore usable as predicates even if, hypothetically, they could not have supported a federal term of imprisonment standing alone.

Because the predicates are constitutionally valid and § 117(a) punishes only the instant offense, their use does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
