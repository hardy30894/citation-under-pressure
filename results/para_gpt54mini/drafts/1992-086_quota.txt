**Argument**

The School District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because the proposed films were “church related.” Once a school district opens its facilities for after-hours community use, it may not exclude speech because of its religious viewpoint. The controlling principles come from *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37 (1983), *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985), and *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981). Under those decisions, the District’s selective exclusion was unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.

A forum may be limited in subject matter, but even in a limited public forum the government may not discriminate against speakers on the basis of viewpoint. *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). The District here allowed community use of school property after hours for civic and educational purposes. Having created that access, it could not reserve the forum for secular discussion of family and child-rearing while excluding the same topic from a Christian perspective. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), confirms that when the government opens school property for a class of speakers or subject matter, exclusion of religious perspective on the same subject is impermissible viewpoint discrimination.

This Court’s decision in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), is especially instructive. There, the Court held that a university that generally made its facilities available to student groups could not exclude religious use because “of the religious nature of the speech at issue.” *Id.* at 277. The same principle applies here. The District opened its premises for after-hours use by community groups; it then singled out Lamb’s Chapel because the film series would present family-values themes from a Christian standpoint. That is not content-neutral regulation. It is suppression of one perspective on an otherwise permitted subject.

The District’s reliance on *Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier*, 484 U.S. 260 (1988), and *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is misplaced. *Hazelwood* concerned school-sponsored student expression in a curricular setting, where educators may exercise editorial control over school speech. 484 U.S. at 271-73. Lamb’s Chapel’s proposed film series was private speech in an after-hours forum, not school-sponsored expression. *Mergens* likewise helps petitioner, not respondent. There, the Court held that when a school creates a limited open forum, it may not discriminate against student religious speech. 496 U.S. at 248-50. If a school cannot exclude a student Christian club from a limited open forum, it cannot exclude a church’s after-hours film series simply because the speech is religious.

Nor can the District justify its exclusion under the Establishment Clause. In *Widmar*, the Court rejected the argument that equal access would violate the Establishment Clause, explaining that accommodation of religious speech in an open forum does not amount to endorsement. 454 U.S. at 270-75. *Board of Educ. of Kiryas Joel Village School Dist. v. Grumet*, 512 U.S. 687 (1994), and *County of Allegheny v. ACLU Greater Pittsburgh Chapter*, 492 U.S. 573 (1989), do not support censorship here; they address governmental endorsement, not private speech on equal terms. Allowing Lamb’s Chapel to show films on family life would simply treat religious speech like comparable secular speech, which is what the First Amendment requires. *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 828-31 (1995).

The Second Circuit erred in treating the District’s exclusion as viewpoint neutral. A rule barring use “for religious purposes” may sound neutral, but its application here targeted the religious perspective of otherwise permissible subject matter. That is precisely the kind of discrimination condemned in *Rosenberger*, where the Court held that the government may not “regulate speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction.” *Id.* at 829. The District allowed discussion of family values; it refused the same discussion because it was presented through a Christian lens.

Finally, even if the District’s forum were considered nonpublic, its restriction still would fail because the exclusion was not reasonable in light of the forum’s purpose and was plainly based on hostility to religion. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 809. The District’s refusal to permit Lamb’s Chapel to present religious-oriented films about family values was not a legitimate limitation on access; it was disfavored treatment of religious speech.

The judgment should be reversed.
