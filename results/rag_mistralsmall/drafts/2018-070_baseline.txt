**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) Permits the Application of State Law Whenever State Law Is Applicable and Not Inconsistent with Federal Law, Without Requiring a Gap in Federal Coverage.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), authorizes the application of state law to activities on the Outer Continental Shelf whenever such law is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. The statute provides:

> “To the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States for that portion of the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf ... and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.”

43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the OCSLA incorporates state law as surrogate federal law under this provision. *See Pacific Operators Offshore, LLP v. Valladolid*, 565 U.S. 207, 222 (2012) (“[T]he OCSLA extends various provisions of state and federal law to certain aspects of the OCS.”); *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (holding that state wrongful death laws apply on the OCS under § 1333(a)(2)(A)).

Contrary to Petitioner’s argument, the absence of a “gap” in federal law is not a prerequisite to applying state law under the OCSLA. The Fifth Circuit’s earlier formulation requiring a “significant void or gap” in federal law, *see Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), has been superseded by the plain text of § 1333(a)(2)(A), which contains no such limitation. *See Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473, 482 (1981) (applying state law under § 1333(a)(2)(A) without requiring a gap in federal coverage).

The legislative history of the OCSLA confirms that Congress intended state law to apply broadly. As originally enacted in 1953, the OCSLA incorporated only state laws in existence at the time. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. In 1975, Congress amended the statute to allow for the incorporation of state laws “now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed,” eliminating any temporal limitation. *See Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97, 104 (1971) (discussing the 1975 amendment). This amendment reflected Congress’s intent to ensure that state law could evolve alongside developments on the OCS. *See id.*

Petitioner’s reliance on *Continental Oil* is misplaced. That case predated the 1975 amendment and applied a now-obsolete test. The Fifth Circuit itself has since recognized that the “applicable and not inconsistent” standard governs. *See Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Eng’g, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990) (“State law applies to the OCS if it is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law.”).

**II. California’s Wage and Hour Laws Are Applicable and Not Inconsistent with the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA).**

California’s wage and hour laws apply to Newton’s claims because they are both “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with the FLSA. The FLSA itself contains a savings clause that expressly preserves more protective state wage and hour laws:

> “No provision of this chapter or of any order thereunder shall excuse noncompliance with any Federal or State law or municipal ordinance establishing a minimum wage higher than the minimum wage established under this chapter.”

29 U.S.C. § 218(a). This Court has repeatedly deferred to the Department of Labor’s interpretation of the FLSA’s savings clause. *See Long Island Care at Home, Ltd. v. Coke*, 551 U.S. 158, 171 (2007) (noting that the DOL’s interpretation of the FLSA is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984)).

Petitioner’s argument that the FLSA is a “comprehensive statutory scheme” that preempts state law is incorrect. This Court has rejected the notion that federal labor laws preempt state wage and hour laws absent a clear congressional intent to do so. *See Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 737 (1981) (“The FLSA was not intended to supplant state laws regulating wages and hours of employment.”). The FLSA’s savings clause itself belies any claim of preemption, as it explicitly permits state laws to provide greater protections than the FLSA.

Moreover, the Department of Labor has recognized that state wage and hour laws may apply to workers on the OCS. In *Iowa Beef Packers, Inc. v. Thompson*, 405 U.S. 228 (1972), this Court deferred to the DOL’s interpretation of the FLSA’s meal period regulations, demonstrating that the DOL’s views on the interplay between federal and state wage laws are entitled to deference. *See id.* at 232.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Does Not Create a Conflict with Other Circuits.**

Petitioner cites cases from the Fifth Circuit for the proposition that state wage and hour laws do not apply on the OCS. However, those cases either predate the 1975 amendment to the OCSLA or involve different legal issues. *See, e.g., Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036 (applying pre-1975 law); *Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc.*, 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014) (addressing contract claims, not wage and hour laws). The Fifth Circuit has not squarely addressed whether state wage and hour laws apply under the current text of § 1333(a)(2)(A).

In contrast, the Ninth Circuit’s decision aligns with the plain text of the OCSLA and this Court’s precedents. The decision does not conflict with *Rodrigue* or *Valladolid*, which both affirm the application of state law on the OCS under § 1333(a)(2)(A). *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355; *Valladolid*, 565 U.S. at 222.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the OCSLA permits the application of state law whenever such law is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law. California’s wage and hour laws meet this standard, and the FLSA’s savings clause expressly preserves them. The decision below should be affirmed.
