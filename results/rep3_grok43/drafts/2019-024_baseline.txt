ARGUMENT

The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars respondent’s claims. That provision requires only “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not knowledge that the underlying acts were unlawful. Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985) (“section 1113 is triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law”). The earliest date on which a plaintiff possesses such knowledge starts the period. Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991).

Respondent received detailed disclosures describing the challenged asset allocations, their risks, and their performance. Those documents supplied the facts constituting the alleged imprudence. Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co., 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (limitations triggered when plaintiff received information revealing the transaction later alleged to be improper). Because the disclosures were furnished more than three years before suit, the claims are time-barred.

The court of appeals erred in requiring proof that respondent subjectively appreciated the imprudence of the investments. Section 1113(2) contains no such element. Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (knowledge of the transaction suffices even if plaintiff “may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA”). Imposing an additional awareness requirement would revive the constructive-knowledge provision Congress repealed in 1987 and would render the statute’s text meaningless whenever a plaintiff elects not to examine plan materials.

The same analysis governs the derivative claims. Those claims rest on the same underlying transactions; once respondent had actual knowledge of those transactions, the limitations period began to run. Phillips, 944 F.2d at 520. The district court correctly entered summary judgment.
