**ARGUMENT**

I. An Inmate Has a Protected Liberty Interest in the Clemency Process Under the Fourteenth Amendment

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects not only life and liberty in their ordinary sense but also those interests created by state law when such interests are “fundamental” and “so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as essential to the scheme of ordered liberty.” *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process, though not a judicial proceeding, constitutes a critical stage in the final adjudication of a death sentence and thus implicates a constitutionally protected liberty interest.

In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), the Court held that a state’s failure to provide due process in a criminal proceeding violated the Fourteenth Amendment, even when the proceeding was not a trial. The Court emphasized that “the right to a fair trial is not confined to the formalities of judicial procedure,” but extends to “the right to be free from arbitrary and oppressive action by the state.” Id. at 97. Similarly, in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), the Court struck down a congressional act that removed an individual from federal employment based on political beliefs, holding that “the right to be free from arbitrary governmental action” is a “fundamental” liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause. Id. at 315.

Here, Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere formality. It is a state-created mechanism through which the governor may commute a death sentence, and it is the final opportunity for a condemned inmate to seek mercy. The process is not discretionary in the abstract; it is structured, regulated, and routinely invoked. As the Court recognized in *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243 (1833), the Fourteenth Amendment applies to state action that deprives individuals of life or liberty without due process. The Ohio clemency process, by its very nature, is a state action that directly affects the duration of a prisoner’s life. To deny that such a process implicates a protected liberty interest would render the Due Process Clause meaningless in the context of capital punishment.

Moreover, in *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 268 U.S. 510 (1925), the Court held that the liberty interest in “the right of parents to direct the upbringing and education of their children” is protected by the Due Process Clause. The Court reasoned that “liberty” includes “the right to engage in any of the common occupations of life.” Id. at 534. By analogy, the right to seek clemency—especially in a system where such proceedings are routine and where the state has created a formalized process—is a fundamental aspect of the liberty interest in life. The state’s creation of a clemency process, and its subsequent failure to afford due process in that process, cannot be insulated from constitutional scrutiny.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to life is not absolute,” but that “the state may not deprive a person of life without due process of law.” *Lynch v. Household Finance Corp.*, 382 U.S. 419, 424 (1966). This principle applies with equal force to the final stage of capital punishment. In *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60 (1917), the Court invalidated a city ordinance that restricted where African Americans could live, holding that such a law “deprived a person of liberty without due process of law.” Id. at 75. The same logic applies here: when the state creates a process that determines whether a person lives or dies, and fails to afford due process in that process, it violates the Fourteenth Amendment.

II. The Voluntary Interview Option Violates the Fifth Amendment

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves. *Cummings v. Missouri*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 277, 322 (1845). This protection applies not only in criminal trials but in any situation where a person is compelled to provide testimony that may incriminate them. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965).

Ohio’s clemency procedure, as structured, presents a prisoner with a Hobson’s choice: either participate in a voluntary interview and risk self-incrimination, or forgo the interview and forfeit any opportunity to present mitigating evidence. This is not a true choice. In *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 28 (1953), the Court held that “the privilege against self-incrimination is not a mere technicality,” but a “fundamental right” essential to the integrity of the legal system. The Court emphasized that “the right to remain silent is not a privilege to be lightly forfeited.” Id.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when a state creates a process that effectively compels a person to choose between self-incrimination and a significant disadvantage, the process violates the Fifth Amendment. In *Cummings v. Missouri*, the Court invalidated a state law that required individuals to testify against themselves in matters of political belief, holding that such a law “violates the fundamental principles of justice.” 44 U.S. at 322. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court stated that “the privilege against self-incrimination is not a mere technicality,” but a “basic right” that must be protected even in administrative proceedings.

Here, the clemency interview is not truly voluntary. The state has created a process in which the only meaningful way to present mitigating evidence is through the interview. To participate is to risk self-incrimination; to refrain is to be deemed uncooperative. This is precisely the kind of coercive pressure the Fifth Amendment was designed to prevent.
