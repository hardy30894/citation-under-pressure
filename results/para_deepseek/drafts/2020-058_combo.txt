ARGUMENT

I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Must Be Given Its Full Effect

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted Temporary Protected Status “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This is a legal fiction. When Congress employs the phrase “considered as,” it creates a conclusive presumption that the alien’s status is equivalent to that of a nonimmigrant who was lawfully admitted. *United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905) (holding that a statutory “deeming” provision creates a presumption that is “conclusive” for purposes of the statute). A nonimmigrant is defined as an alien “who is coming into the United States” for a temporary purpose. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15). The only way to achieve “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” is to have been admitted as such. By deeming TPS recipients to be in that status, Congress necessarily deemed them to have been admitted. *See Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (ambiguity in immigration statutes must be resolved in favor of the alien).

II. The Term “Admission” Is Not Mechanically Tied to Physical Entry

The court below erred by rigidly applying the definition of “admission” in § 1101(a)(13)(A) as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization.” That definition governs the physical act of entry, but the context of adjustment of status is different. *Rosenberg v. Fleuti*, 374 U.S. 449, 461–62 (1963), held that the term “entry” in the INA “is not to be mechanically applied” and must be interpreted in light of the statute’s purpose. The same principle applies to “admission” in § 1255. The purpose of TPS is to provide temporary protection to aliens from unsafe countries, and the purpose of adjustment of status is to allow those who have maintained lawful status to become permanent residents. Interpreting “admission” to exclude TPS recipients would frustrate these purposes. *See Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (courts must look beyond literal text to effectuate legislative intent).

III. The TPS Grant Itself Constitutes an Inspection and Authorization

The process of obtaining TPS requires the alien to submit an application, undergo background checks, and receive an affirmative approval from the government. This is analogous to the inspection and authorization that occurs at the border. *United States v. Haggerty*, 116 F.2d 1015, 1017 (9th Cir. 1941) (holding that a reentry permit constitutes an “admission” because it represents an affirmative act of authorization by the government). The sovereign has the power to prescribe the conditions under which it will admit aliens. *Nishimura Ekiu v. United States*, 142 U.S. 651, 660 (1892). By granting TPS, the government authorized petitioners to remain in the United States. That authorization should be treated as an admission for purposes of § 1255.

IV. The Third Circuit’s Interpretation Renders § 1254a(f)(4) Superfluous

If TPS recipients are not deemed admitted, then § 1254a(f)(4)’s statement that they are “in” lawful status as a nonimmigrant is meaningless for adjustment of status, because they still cannot meet the threshold requirement of being “inspected and admitted or paroled.” *Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10 (statutes should be interpreted so that no part is superfluous). Congress would not have included that provision if it intended no effect on adjustment eligibility.

V. The Canon Against Absurd Results Supports Petitioners

The Third Circuit’s interpretation forces TPS recipients who entered illegally to live indefinitely in the United States with no path to permanent residence, even if they have maintained lawful status for decades. This absurd result undermines the very purpose of TPS. *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. 482, 486 (1868) (statutes should not be interpreted to produce “an absurd consequence” that Congress could not have intended).

VI. Constitutional Avoidance Reinforces This Interpretation

If TPS recipients cannot adjust status, they may be forced to remain in the United States without any opportunity to become permanent residents, raising due process concerns. *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (resident aliens are entitled to due process). This Court should avoid an interpretation that raises serious constitutional questions. *See United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 544 (1950) (admission is a privilege, but once granted, the alien has rights). 

For these reasons, this Court should hold that the conferral of TPS constitutes an “admission” under § 1255.
