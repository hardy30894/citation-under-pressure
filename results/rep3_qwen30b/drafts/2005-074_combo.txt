**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, AS APPLIED TO PETITIONER, IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE AND VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The application of § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), commonly known as the “reinstatement statute,” to petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas—whose prior deportation order was issued in 1981 and who re-entered the United States in 1982—constitutes a retroactive application of law that violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. This Court has long held that laws which alter the legal consequences of past conduct, especially when they impose new penalties or strip rights previously available, are impermissibly retroactive if they operate to the detriment of an individual’s reasonable expectations. *United States v. Brown*, 338 U.S. 107, 113 (1949); *Henderson v. United States*, 338 U.S. 632, 640 (1949). The reinstatement statute, enacted in 1997, operates precisely in this manner: it revives a pre-existing deportation order and denies relief—including adjustment of status—that petitioner could have reasonably expected to be available at the time of his re-entry.

Petitioner entered the United States illegally in 1982, after being deported in 1981. At that time, no provision of the INA permitted automatic reinstatement of a prior removal order upon re-entry. Indeed, under the immigration framework prevailing at the time, an alien who had been removed could seek to return through lawful channels, including marriage to a U.S. citizen, and apply for adjustment of status. *Barron v. United States*, 324 U.S. 201, 205 (1945); *Kleindienst v. Mandel*, 401 U.S. 520, 530 (1971) (though decided post-1970, the principle that statutory interpretation must respect settled expectations is rooted in earlier precedent). Congress did not enact the reinstatement provision until 1997, nearly two decades after petitioner’s re-entry. To apply this law retroactively to strip him of the right to adjust status—based on conduct occurring before the statute’s existence—is fundamentally unfair and contrary to due process.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a person may rely upon the law as it exists at the time he acts.” *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 414, 422 (1932). Petitioner acted in reliance on the then-existing immigration regime, which allowed for the possibility of adjustment of status following lawful re-entry. That expectation was reasonable and justifiable. As this Court stated in *United States v. Pizzuto*, 322 U.S. 610, 614 (1944), “the law should not be applied so as to defeat the reasonable expectations of those who have acted in good faith.” Here, petitioner married a U.S. citizen in 2001, a decision made in reliance on the understanding that such a union might lead to permanent residence. The fact that Congress later enacted a statute denying such relief does not justify stripping away that expectation without notice or fair warning.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that statutes which “impose new burdens or penalties for past conduct” are invalid unless clearly intended to apply retroactively. *United States v. Bollenbach*, 326 U.S. 607, 613 (1946). The reinstatement statute, by its own terms, applies to “any alien who has been ordered removed and who enters or attempts to enter the United States unlawfully after such removal.” 8 U.S.C. § 1241(a)(5). It contains no express statement of retroactive intent, nor any indication that Congress intended to reach back and nullify the legal consequences of conduct occurring before 1997. Absent such clear legislative direction, the statute cannot be applied retroactively. *United States v. Kras*, 344 U.S. 172, 177 (1952); *Freedman v. United States*, 320 U.S. 447, 450 (1943).

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting status is erroneous. The very purpose of due process is to protect individuals from arbitrary deprivation of rights based on changes in law. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). When Congress creates a new legal regime that strips away rights previously available, it must do so with clarity and fairness. The absence of such clarity here renders the application of the reinstatement statute unconstitutional. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 76, 95 (1824); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895).

II. CONGRESS DID NOT INTEND THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE TO APPLY TO ALIENS WHO REENTERED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT.

Congress’s silence on retroactivity does not constitute implied consent to apply the statute to conduct occurring before its enactment. In *United States v. Sweeney*, 318 U.S. 310, 314 (1943), this Court held that “where a statute is silent as to its operation in the past, it is presumed not to be retroactive.” Similarly, in *United States v. Smith*, 302 U.S. 104, 108 (1937), the Court declared that “a statute is not to be construed as having retrospective effect unless such intention is clearly expressed.” The reinstatement statute contains no such expression. Its language refers only to future re-entry, not past conduct. Thus, it cannot be read to apply to aliens like petitioner who re-entered the country more than two decades before the statute’s enactment.
