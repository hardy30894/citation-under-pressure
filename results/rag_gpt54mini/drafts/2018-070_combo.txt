OCSLA’s text answers the question. Congress provided that the adjacent State’s civil and criminal laws are “the law of the United States” on fixed structures on the Outer Continental Shelf “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A); Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355, 366 (1969). That language does not ask whether federal law leaves a “gap” or “void.” It asks whether the state law is applicable and not inconsistent. And when Congress uses incorporation language like this, courts apply the incorporated law unless it conflicts with federal law. Id. at 366. The Ninth Circuit’s “applicable and not inconsistent” reading therefore follows the statute; the gap rule does not.

Rodrigue confirms that OCSLA was designed to supply a body of law for fixed platforms akin to an upland federal enclave, not to leave those platforms governed by an exclusive federal common law of the administrator’s choosing. 395 U.S. at 355, 366. The Court emphasized that, where federal maritime law does not govern “of its own force,” state law is incorporated through OCSLA. Id. at 366. The same point appears in Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co., 375 U.S. 34 (1963), where the Court described OCSLA as “extending longshoremen’s compensation to a new group” and treated it as a statute that reaches offshore workers rather than one that displaces all state regulation. Id. at 34. The statute thus operates by incorporation, not by preemption.

This Court’s FLSA cases do not support Parker’s contrary theory. In Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel, 316 U.S. 572 (1942), the Court construed the Act as guaranteeing statutory minimum rates and overtime compensation; it did not hold that the FLSA occupies the entire field of wage regulation. Id. at 572. Likewise, Walling v. A. H. Belo Corp., 316 U.S. 624 (1942), addressed whether a particular compensation plan satisfied the Act’s overtime requirements; it did not suggest that the Act displaces more protective state wage laws. Id. at 624. Walling v. Halliburton Oil Well Cementing Co., 331 U.S. 17 (1947), again concerned whether the employer’s plan complied with federal overtime rules, not whether state wage protections were forbidden. Id. at 17. And Walling v. Youngerman-Reynolds Hardwood Co., 325 U.S. 419 (1945), likewise concerned the meaning of the FLSA’s overtime provisions, not the exclusivity of federal law. Id. at 419. Those decisions show breadth in the FLSA’s coverage, but breadth is not exclusivity.

The same is true of Warren-Bradshaw Drilling Co. v. Hall, 317 U.S. 88 (1942), which held that drilling employees were covered by the FLSA because they were engaged in production for commerce. Id. at 88. It did not hold that a federal wage statute extinguishes more generous state wage rules when OCSLA incorporates them. Nor does 149 Madison Avenue Corp. v. Asselta, 331 U.S. 199 (1947), help Parker. That case addressed how to calculate the regular rate under the Act; it did not announce field preemption. Id. at 199. United States v. Townsley, 323 U.S. 557 (1945), similarly concerned overtime calculation under federal law, not the displacement of state law. Id. at 557. None of these cases says what Parker needs them to say.

Indeed, the structure of the FLSA cuts against Parker. The Act has long coexisted with more protective state wage laws. See Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc., 323 U.S. 37 (1944); Walling v. Youngerman-Reynolds Hardwood Co., 325 U.S. 419 (1945). A federal statute that establishes minimum labor standards does not, without more, exclude stronger state standards. OCSLA adopts adjacent state law as federal law unless inconsistent; the FLSA’s savings clause reflects the same basic accommodation of stronger state protections. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A); Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355, 366.

Parker’s “gap” theory also conflicts with OCSLA’s purpose. Congress enacted the statute to ensure that fixed structures on the Outer Continental Shelf would not be lawless spaces. Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355; Tipton, 375 U.S. at 34. If state law were unavailable whenever federal law addressed the same subject matter, then many ordinary employment disputes on offshore platforms would have no governing law at all, even though Congress declared adjacent-state law to be federal law unless inconsistent. That is not the statute Congress wrote. It is not the statute this Court should enforce.
