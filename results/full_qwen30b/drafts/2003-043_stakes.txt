**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of the Internal Revenue Code and settled precedent. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS may not collect unpaid partnership taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The government’s attempt to circumvent the statutory limitations period by relying solely on an assessment against the partnership fails as a matter of law.

**I. The Statute of Limitations for Assessment and Collection Applies to Each Taxpayer Individually**

Under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the government must assess a tax against a taxpayer within three years after the return is filed or the tax becomes due. *United States v. Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. 321, 328 (1998) (“The statute of limitations for assessment is a jurisdictional bar to the government’s power to assess a tax.”). The statute defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because individual partners are distinct taxpayers under the Code, each is subject to its own three-year assessment period.

The IRS’s argument that a partnership assessment automatically extends the limitations period for individual partners is foreclosed by the plain language of the statute. Section 6502(a) permits collection of a tax “only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” (Emphasis added.) The statute does not say “after the assessment of the partnership,” but “after the assessment of the tax”—a tax assessed against a specific taxpayer. The assessment must be made against the individual taxpayer to trigger the ten-year collection period. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien under § 6321 “does not exist against a particular individual’s property unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203”).

The IRS’s position would render the individual assessment requirement superfluous. If an assessment against the partnership were sufficient to extend the limitations period for partners, Congress would have no reason to require separate assessments. *See United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 563 (7th Cir. 1995) (noting that the statute “does not permit the government to collect from a partner without first assessing the partner”). The Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Wright* is not to the contrary. There, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *Id.* at 562. The court held only that the statute of limitations was tolled for the partners because they had been assessed. *Id.* The court did not address whether a partnership assessment alone could extend the limitations period for partners. Thus, *Wright* is distinguishable and does not support the government’s position.

**II. The Government Cannot Collect from Partners Without a Judgment or Individual Assessment**

Even if the statute of limitations were extended, the government may not collect from individual partners without a valid assessment or a judgment against them. The IRS’s alternative argument—that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable and thus allows collection from partners without individual assessment—fails because it ignores the procedural requirements of federal tax law.

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against a partner. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” *See also* 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (“[A] partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”). This rule is consistent with federal law, which requires a valid assessment or judgment before the government may proceed against a taxpayer’s property. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

The IRS has neither assessed the individual partners nor obtained a judgment against them. The three-year limitations period under § 6501(a) has long expired. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS’s claims because they were “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor” under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The government’s claim is not merely contingent or unmatured—it is barred by statute.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with Precedent and the Structure of the Tax Code**

The Ninth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Galletti* is fully supported by precedent. In *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), we affirmed a district court’s refusal to grant a refund to a partner who had paid his share of a partnership tax. The court held that the partner was liable under state law, but emphasized that the government could not collect the tax without an assessment or judgment. *Young*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050. The government’s argument that the assessment against the partnership was sufficient to create liability ignores the procedural safeguards built into the Code.

Similarly, in *United States v. Coson*, we affirmed the district court’s dismissal of a tax lien against a partner who had never been assessed. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458. The court held that a lien could not attach to a partner’s property without a valid assessment against the partner. *Id.* at 458. The IRS’s attempt to collect from partners without individual assessments or judgments is precisely the kind of procedural overreach that the Code was designed to prevent.

**Conclusion**

The government’s claim against the individual partners is barred by the statute of limitations. The assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for the partners, nor did California law
