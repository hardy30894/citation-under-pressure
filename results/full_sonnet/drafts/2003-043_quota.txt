# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of the Internal Revenue Code Requires Assessment of the Individual Taxpayer Before the Ten-Year Collection Period May Run Against That Taxpayer.

Section 6502(a) extends the limitations period to ten years only where "the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made" against the person from whom collection is sought. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The Code defines "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and "person" includes both an "individual" and a "partnership" as distinct categories. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14). Because a partnership and its partners are separate taxpayers, each possessing its own identifying number, an assessment against one is not an assessment against the other. The implementing regulation confirms that a valid assessment must "provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Where, as here, the government assessed only Marina Cabrillo Partners and never assessed Mr. and Mrs. Galletti or Mr. and Mrs. Briguglio individually, the extended ten-year period never began to run against them, and the ordinary three-year period of § 6501(a) governs.

## II. This Court's Own Precedent Confirms That Individual Assessment Is a Prerequisite to Individual Collection.

This Court's decisions in *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), and *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), foreclose the government's position. In *Young*, the district court—affirmed by this Court—recognized that assessment against a partnership entity does not, by itself, create a mechanism for collecting from an unassessed partner without either "resorting to court action" or "individually assess[ing] the taxes" against him. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959), *aff'd*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). The partner in *Young* was denied a refund only because he had already paid the tax; the court did not hold that the IRS could have compelled payment absent an assessment or judgment against him individually.

*Coson* is even more direct. There, the district court held that "such a lien does not exist against a particular individual's property pursuant to §§ 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203," and this Court affirmed. *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (quoting *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958)). If assessment of the individual is necessary to support even a lien on the individual's property, it is necessarily required to extend the limitations period applicable to collection from that individual.

## III. *United States v. Wright* Does Not Support the Government's Position.

The Seventh Circuit's decision in *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is inapposite. In *Wright*, the IRS had separately assessed both the partnership and the individual partners, and the only question presented was whether the limitations period applicable to the partners was tolled during the partnership's bankruptcy. *Id.* at 562–63. *Wright* therefore assumed, rather than dispensed with, the requirement of individual assessment. The government's reliance on dicta from that case, and on decisions such as *United States v. W. Products, Ltd.*, 168 F. Supp. 2d 84, 91 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), which applied a different state's partnership law to permit collection from a general partner without individual assessment, cannot substitute for the plain text of §§ 6203, 6501, and 6502, nor override this Court's contrary precedent.

## IV. California Law Independently Bars Collection From an Unassessed Partner Absent a Judgment.

Even apart from the federal assessment requirement, California law does not permit a creditor to collect a partnership debt from a partner's individual assets without a separate judgment against that partner. "A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The government obtained no such judgment here, and the time to do so — three years from the filing of respondents' returns, 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) — has long since expired. *See In re Goldston*, 104 F.3d 1198, 1200–01 (10th Cir. 1997) (recognizing that, absent a timely assessment, the government must timely bring a judicial collection action within the three-year period).

## V. The Bankruptcy Court Properly Disallowed the Government's Claims.

A bankruptcy court reviews de novo the legal question whether a claim is enforceable under nonbankruptcy law. *Neilson v. Chang (In re First T.D. & Inv., Inc.)*, 253 F.3d 520, 526 (9th Cir. 2001) (citing *In re Gruntz*, 202 F.3d 1074, 1084 n.9 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc)); *Beaupied v. Chang (In re Chang)*, 163 F.3d 1138, 1140 (9th Cir. 1998). Because the government's claims against respondents were "unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor" for want of a timely assessment or judgment, the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed them under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). This Court should affirm.
