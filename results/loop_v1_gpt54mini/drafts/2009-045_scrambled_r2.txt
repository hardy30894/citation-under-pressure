The Court should answer the question presented in the negative: a fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may not be enhanced solely because counsel performed well and achieved an excellent result. The governing decisions teach that the lodestar method is the “guiding light” in fee calculation, that the lodestar is presumed to be reasonable, and that any departure must be justified by something other than the very factors already reflected in the hourly rate and hours reasonably expended. See, e.g., Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc., 488 F.2d 714, 716-19 (5th Cir. 1974); Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 433-37 (1983); Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 897-901 (1984).

That result follows from the structure of fee-shifting law. Courts awarding fees have long distinguished between compensation for services and a premium for extraordinary performance. See Ex parte Peterson, 253 U.S. 300, 312-13 (1920). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that a “reasonable” fee is one that is sufficient to attract competent counsel, not to create a windfall. Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 565-68 (1986); Blum, 465 U.S. at 897-901; Hensley, 461 U.S. at 433-37.

The quality of representation and the results obtained ordinarily are already subsumed within the lodestar. In Hensley, the Court explained that the “degree of success obtained” is important to the fee inquiry, 461 U.S. at 436, but that does not mean those same considerations may be used twice—first in setting the lodestar and then again to enhance it. Blum held precisely that the “quality of representation” and “results obtained” may not justify an increase where those factors are already reflected in the lodestar. 465 U.S. at 898-900. Delaware Valley reaffirmed that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee.” 478 U.S. at 566.

This Court’s older fee decisions point the same way. In Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp., 487 F.2d 161, 167-69 (3d Cir. 1973), courts rejected open-ended fee premiums untethered to proof that the base calculation was inadequate. Likewise, in Johnson, 488 F.2d at 717-19, the Fifth Circuit identified “skill requisite” and “results obtained” as factors in the initial fee analysis, not as licenses for duplicative enhancement. The same principle appears in King v. Greenblatt, 560 F.2d 1024, 1027-28 (1st Cir. 1977), and Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer, 427 U.S. 445, 456-57 (1976), which both treated fee awards as compensation, not applause.

Allowing enhancement solely for “superior” lawyering and “exceptional” outcomes would collapse the lodestar into a discretionary reward system. But fee statutes do not authorize courts to pay more merely because counsel did an excellent job. See Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society, 421 U.S. 240, 262-71 (1975); Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co., 386 U.S. 714, 720-21 (1967). Fee shifting is an exception to the American Rule, and exceptions are to be strictly confined to the statutory text. Alyeska Pipeline, 421 U.S. at 247-71; Arcambel v. Wiseman, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 306 (1796).

The better reading of the statute is therefore categorical: performance and results may inform whether the lodestar is reasonable, but they cannot support an enhancement when they are the sole basis offered. That is the rule that preserves predictability, prevents double counting, and keeps fee awards anchored to work actually performed. See Hensley, 461 U.S. at 433-37; Blum, 465 U.S. at 897-901; Delaware Valley, 478 U.S. at 565-68.

For those reasons, the Court should reverse and hold that a federal fee-shifting award may not be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained.
