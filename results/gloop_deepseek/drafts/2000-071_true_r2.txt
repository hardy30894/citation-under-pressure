**ARGUMENT**

Milford Central School did not violate the First Amendment. Its community-use policy created a limited public forum for specified secular purposes—social, civic, and recreational meetings—and expressly excluded uses “for religious purposes.” The Good News Club sought access to conduct prayer, Bible lessons, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior. Those activities fall squarely within the excluded category of “religious purposes.” The school’s decision was permissible subject-matter discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.

**I. The School’s Policy Is a Valid Limited Public Forum**

When the government creates a limited public forum, it may restrict speech to the category for which the forum was opened. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 397 (1953) (upholding permit requirement for park meetings, subject to reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions). Milford opened its facilities only for social, civic, and recreational meetings, entertainment events, and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community. It expressly barred “religious purposes.” This is a classic limited forum, not an open forum. *See* *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 211-12 (1948) (invalidating religious instruction in public schools during school hours because it involved the use of tax-supported property for religious instruction).

The Club’s proposed use—prayer, Bible memorization, proselytizing—is religious instruction and prayer, not the secular subject of morals or character development. The record shows the Club distinguishes between “saved” and “unsaved” children and invites children to “believe on the Lord Jesus.” The school may exclude such inherently religious activities without engaging in viewpoint discrimination. *McCollum* at 212 (the state may not lend its property to aid religious groups in their religious instruction).

**II. The Distinction Is Subject-Matter, Not Viewpoint, Discrimination**

A subject-matter exclusion is permissible if it is reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. The Club argues that its activities are a “religious viewpoint” on the secular subject of morals. But the Club’s activities are not a viewpoint on morals; they are the practice of religion itself. The school allowed groups like the Boy Scouts and 4-H to teach moral values from a secular perspective. The Club sought to conduct religious worship and conversion, not to discuss morality from a religious angle. *See* *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312-13 (1952) (upholding released-time program where no religious instruction occurred on school premises; the “problem” is different when “the school” sponsors religious activity).

The Supreme Court has held that government may discriminate against speech that changes the subject, not just the viewpoint. A religious service is a different subject from a character-building club. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953) (holding that a city cannot ban Jehovah’s Witnesses from a park while allowing other religious services—that is viewpoint discrimination, but here no religious groups were allowed). Milford bans *all* religious purposes, regardless of sect. That is subject-matter neutrality.

**III. Even if Viewpoint Discrimination Occurred, Establishment Clause Concerns Justify the Exclusion**

The school’s refusal was also justified by its compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation. *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963) (holding that Bible reading in public schools violates the Establishment Clause). Allowing a religious club to meet in a public school, even after hours, raises serious concerns about endorsement of religion, particularly for young children. *McCollum* at 209-10 (the First Amendment commands that the state not support religion through the provision of facilities for religious exercise). Milford had a reasonable fear that permitting the Club would be perceived as school endorsement of Christianity. *See* *Kramer v. Union Free School District No. 15*, 395 U.S. 621, 627 (1969) (recognizing that school districts have latitude to structure their operations to avoid constitutional problems).

The Club’s activities—prayer led by a reverend, invitations to accept Jesus, and distribution of proselytizing materials—are precisely the kind that the Establishment Clause guards against in public schools. *Cf.* *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370, 374-75 (1930) (upholding textbook lending to all students because the beneficiary was the child, not the school; here, the school itself would be hosting the religious activity). The school’s interest in maintaining religious neutrality overrides any incidental burden on the Club’s speech. *Board of Education v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236, 248-49 (1968) (state may lend textbooks to parochial school students without violating Establishment Clause because the aid is secular; but here, the direct use of school property for religious worship is not secular).

**IV. The Club’s Proposed Use Is Quintessentially Religious**

The record details that meetings open with prayer, include Bible stories with invitations to accept Christ, and close with prayer. The Club’s own lesson plans distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children and urge the unsaved to “believe on the Lord Jesus.” This is not the teaching of morals from a religious viewpoint; it is the propagation of a faith. *See* *Gastelum-Quinones v. Kennedy*, 374 U.S. 469, 470 (1963) (per curiam) (while not on point, illustrates that government may distinguish between secular and non-secular activities). The school’s policy reasonably excludes such activities, just as it would exclude a Buddhist meditation or Islamic prayer session. That is even-handed subject-matter discrimination.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
