## ARGUMENT

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Center Moriches School District could exclude Lamb’s Chapel from using school facilities for a religiously oriented film series on family values. The District opened its facilities as a limited public forum for community expressive activities, and its categorical exclusion of religious speech constitutes impermissible viewpoint discrimination in violation of the First Amendment.

**I. The School District Created a Limited Public Forum for Expressive Activities**

The District’s policy and practice demonstrate that it opened school facilities to a broad range of community groups for expressive purposes. The District allowed groups such as the Boy Scouts, the Girl Scouts, the Southern Harmonizers (a choral group that performed religious music), and a lecture series on parapsychology to use its facilities. By permitting these groups to present their messages, the District created a “limited public forum” — a forum “opened for use by the public for expressive activity.” *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). Once a government creates such a forum, it may impose content-based restrictions only if they are “reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum” and “viewpoint neutral.” *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

**II. The Exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel Is Viewpoint Discrimination, Not Mere Content Discrimination**

The District’s rule prohibiting “religious purposes” is not viewpoint neutral. Lamb’s Chapel sought to present a film series on family values and child rearing — topics that the District permitted other groups to address. The only difference was that Lamb’s Chapel’s presentation offered a religious perspective. The District’s exclusion thus “discriminate[s] against speech on the basis of its viewpoint.” *Police Dept. of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 96 (1972). In *Carey v. Brown*, 447 U.S. 455, 462-63 (1980), this Court held that a statute prohibiting picketing “with the intent to encourage racial or religious discrimination” was impermissible content discrimination because it singled out certain messages. Similarly, the District here singled out religious speech for exclusion while allowing secular speech on identical subjects.

The Court of Appeals mistakenly concluded that the District had not opened the forum to religious groups. But the relevant inquiry is not whether the District previously admitted religious groups; it is whether the District excluded Lamb’s Chapel because of the religious viewpoint of its speech. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 (1981) (“religious worship and discussion” are forms of speech protected by the First Amendment). The District allowed a choral group to perform music with religious overtones and a lecturer to make incidental references to religion. Those uses demonstrate that the District did not exclude all religious content — only religious *viewpoints* when they were the primary purpose of the speech. That is viewpoint discrimination.

**III. The District’s Justification Does Not Survive First Amendment Scrutiny**

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation cannot justify viewpoint discrimination. In *Widmar*, this Court held that a state university’s open forum policy allowing religious groups to meet on campus did not violate the Establishment Clause because the policy had a secular purpose and did not have the primary effect of advancing religion. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 272-75. The same reasoning applies here: allowing Lamb’s Chapel to use school facilities on the same terms as other community groups would not constitute an endorsement of religion. *See also Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 248-49 (1990) (plurality opinion) (allowing religious student clubs under the Equal Access Act does not violate the Establishment Clause). The District’s fear of entanglement is unfounded.

**IV. The District’s Policy Is Not Reasonable Even in a Nonpublic Forum**

Even if the facilities were a nonpublic forum, the exclusion would still be unconstitutional because it is not “reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum.” *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. The District’s purpose in opening its facilities was to promote community use for educational and cultural activities. Excluding religious speech on family values — a topic of undeniable community interest — does not serve that purpose. Moreover, the restriction is not viewpoint neutral, as required even in nonpublic forums. *Id.* at 806 (the government may not “suppress expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker’s view”).

**V. The District’s Reliance on Prior Exclusion of Religious Groups Is Misplaced**

The Court of Appeals emphasized that the District had never allowed religious groups to use its facilities. But the First Amendment does not permit the government to “deny access to a forum for the purpose of suppressing a particular viewpoint.” *Heffron v. International Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647-48 (1981). The District’s consistent exclusion of religious speech only confirms that its policy is a deliberate effort to suppress religious viewpoints. That is precisely what the First Amendment forbids.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
