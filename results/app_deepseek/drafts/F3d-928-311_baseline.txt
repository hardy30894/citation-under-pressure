**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Designating Mr. Furlow as an Armed Career Criminal and Career Offender Because His Prior Convictions for Distribution of Crack Cocaine and First-Degree Arson Do Not Qualify as Predicate Offenses.**

A sentencing enhancement under either the Armed Career Criminal Act (“ACCA”) or the Guidelines’ career offender provision requires that the government prove the defendant has the requisite number of qualifying predicate convictions. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1); USSG § 4B1.1(a). Here, the district court erroneously counted Mr. Furlow’s South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA and as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines. It also erroneously counted his Georgia conviction for first-degree arson as a “violent felony” under the ACCA and as a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines. Without these predicates, Mr. Furlow lacks the three prior convictions necessary for the ACCA enhancement and the two prior convictions necessary for the career offender designation.

**A. Mr. Furlow’s South Carolina Conviction for Distribution of Crack Cocaine Is Not a Predicate “Serious Drug Offense” or “Controlled Substance Offense.”**

Under the ACCA, a “serious drug offense” includes “an offense under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance . . . for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). Similarly, the Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” as an offense “punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” that prohibits, *inter alia*, the distribution of a controlled substance. USSG § 4B1.2(b). Both definitions require that the predicate offense involve a federally controlled substance. *See United States v. Hope*, 906 F.3d 289, 293 (4th Cir. 2018) (holding that a state drug offense qualifies as a predicate only if it “necessarily involves a substance now listed on the federal controlled substances schedules”).

Here, Mr. Furlow’s South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not satisfy this requirement. The government failed to present any evidence that the substance involved in his state conviction is listed on the federal schedules as a controlled substance. Under the categorical approach, courts must look to the elements of the state offense, not the facts of the underlying conduct. *See Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). South Carolina’s drug statute criminalizes the distribution of substances that may not be federally controlled. *See, e.g., State v. Brown*, 575 S.E.2d 847, 850 (S.C. Ct. App. 2003) (holding that the state includes “any substance” deemed controlled “under the laws of this State”). Because the state definition is broader than the federal definition, Mr. Furlow’s conviction cannot serve as a predicate. *See United States v. Walker*, 934 F.3d 375, 380 (4th Cir. 2019) (reversing ACCA enhancement where state drug offense encompassed substances not federally controlled).

**B. Mr. Furlow’s Georgia Conviction for First-Degree Arson Is Not a Predicate “Violent Felony” or “Crime of Violence.”**

Both the ACCA and the Guidelines list “arson” as a qualifying predicate. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). However, the generic definition of arson under federal law requires the intentional “burning of property.” *See United States v. Vann*, 660 F.3d 771, 774 (4th Cir. 2011) (en banc) (defining generic arson as “the intentional and malicious burning of property”). Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, by contrast, encompasses conduct that is broader than generic arson. *See* Ga. Code Ann. § 16-7-60(a) (criminalizing damage by “fire or explosive” with the intent to “destroy” or “damage” a dwelling). Damage by explosive does not constitute “burning” in the generic sense. *See United States v. Hathaway*, 841 F.3d 320, 322 (4th Cir. 2016) (holding that a statute covering “explosive” damage is broader than generic arson). Because Georgia’s statute is overbroad, Mr. Furlow’s conviction cannot qualify as a predicate. *See Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 261 (2013) (courts must apply the categorical approach to determine if a state conviction matches the generic offense).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Furlow respectfully requests that this Court vacate his sentence and remand for resentencing without the ACCA or career offender enhancements.
