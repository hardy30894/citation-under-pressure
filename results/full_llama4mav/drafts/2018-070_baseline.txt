The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law to disputes arising on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) whenever state law pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the OCS "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "applicable" in this context, but the Fifth Circuit has developed a test that requires a "significant void or gap" in federal law before state law can be applied. See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

The Ninth Circuit rejected this test, holding that state law can be applied as long as it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. However, this interpretation ignores the purpose of the OCSLA, which was to provide a comprehensive body of law for the OCS. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). The OCSLA was enacted to address the jurisdictional dispute between the federal government and adjacent states over the OCS, and Congress intended to create a uniform body of law governing the OCS.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour disputes, leaving no room for state law to fill gaps. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), expressly allows for more protective state laws, but this does not mean that state law can be applied in addition to the FLSA. Rather, it means that state law can be applied only when there is a gap in the FLSA's coverage.

In this case, Newton's claims arise under California wage and hour laws, which are not necessary to fill a gap in the FLSA. The FLSA provides a comprehensive framework for addressing wage and hour disputes, and California law is not needed to supplement it. Therefore, the OCSLA does not permit the application of California law to Newton's claims.

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated. The OCSLA's choice of law provision requires a gap in federal law before state law can be applied, and the FLSA's comprehensive scheme leaves no room for California law to govern wage and hour disputes on the OCS.
