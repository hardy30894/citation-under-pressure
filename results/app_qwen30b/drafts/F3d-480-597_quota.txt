**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying Appellant’s Rule 29 motion for judgment of acquittal on the machine-gun conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), because the Government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Appellant possessed the requisite *mens rea*—specifically, that she knew the firearm in question was a machine gun. This failure violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment and the holding of *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793, 128 L.Ed.2d 608 (1994), which requires the Government to prove that the defendant knew the nature of the prohibited weapon. The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to satisfy this standard, and the conviction must be reversed.

In *Staples*, the Supreme Court held that the Government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun, because the statute criminalizes *knowing* possession of such a weapon. Id. at 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793. The Court emphasized that “the Government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun,” and that “a defendant who is unaware that his weapon is a machine gun cannot be convicted under § 922(o).” Id. at 608, 114 S.Ct. 1793. This principle is rooted in the fundamental requirement of *mens rea* in criminal law: “no person should be punished for conduct that he did not know was wrong.” *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72, 115 S.Ct. 469, 130 L.Ed.2d 372 (1994) (quoting *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 436, 98 S.Ct. 2864, 57 L.Ed.2d 854 (1978)).

Here, the Government presented no evidence that Appellant knew the AK-47 was a machine gun. The only evidence regarding her knowledge was her statement to an agent that she had “once opened the bag, looked in, and observed that there was a rifle in it, and that she knew it was an AK-47.” This statement, while indicating awareness of the weapon’s general type, does not establish knowledge of its classification as a machine gun. The AK-47 is a semi-automatic rifle, not a machine gun, under the statutory definition in 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(33)(A), which defines a machine gun as “any firearm which can fire automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger.” The Government did not introduce any evidence—expert testimony, documentation, or even a photograph showing the weapon’s mechanism—that would allow a reasonable jury to infer that Appellant knew the weapon could fire automatically more than once with a single trigger pull.

The absence of such evidence is fatal to the conviction. In *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510, 115 S.Ct. 2310, 132 L.Ed.2d 444 (1995), the Court held that “the Government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt,” including *mens rea* when required. The Court reiterated that “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 510, 115 S.Ct. 2310. The Government’s burden is not satisfied by mere speculation or inference based on a defendant’s general awareness of a weapon’s identity. *See also United States v. Baez*, 472 F.3d 1, 10 (1st Cir. 2006) (holding that “knowledge of a firearm’s general type does not equate to knowledge of its specific classification under the statute”).

Moreover, the Government’s failure to prove *mens rea* is not cured by the fact that Appellant admitted to possessing the weapon. In *United States v. Pena*, 433 F.3d 102, 107 (1st Cir. 2005), the court held that “a defendant’s admission to possession does not eliminate the need to prove the requisite mental state.” Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 458 F.3d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 2006), the court emphasized that “knowledge of the weapon’s identity is not synonymous with knowledge of its legal classification.” The Government must prove that the defendant knew the weapon was a machine gun—not just that it was a rifle or an AK-47.

The Government’s reliance on the fact that Appellant stored the weapon in a golf bag at the request of Alexis, the purported owner, does not establish *mens rea*. The mere act of storing a firearm for another does not imply knowledge of its classification. *See United States v. Baez*, 472 F.3d at 10 (noting that “a defendant’s role as a mere courier or storage agent does not establish knowledge of the weapon’s legal status”). Furthermore, Appellant’s statement that she “knew it was an AK-47” does not equate to knowledge that it was a machine gun. The distinction between a semi-automatic rifle and a machine gun is not self-evident to the average person, and the Government failed to present any evidence that Appellant was aware of this legal distinction.

Finally, the district court’s instruction to the jury did not cure the evidentiary deficiency. The court instructed the jury that “possession means having the power and the right to control the firearm,” but did not define “machine gun” or require the Government to prove knowledge of that classification. *See United States v. Mendoza*, 458 F.3d at 7 (holding that “a jury instruction that fails to require proof of *mens rea* for a statutory element is reversible error”). Without a proper instruction on *mens rea*, the jury was not properly guided in its deliberations.
