**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate right to present expert mental-state evidence to negate criminal intent when state law confines such evidence to the insanity defense. Arizona’s procedural framework comports with due process, as the State may allocate burdens and limit evidence without infringing fundamental fairness.

**I. States May Define the Insanity Defense and Restrict Evidence to That Defense**

This Court has long recognized that “[t]he definition of the elements of a criminal offense is a matter of state law, not of federal constitutional law.” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–02 (1977) (citing *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952)). In *Leland*, the Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting a due process challenge. *Id.* at 798–99. That holding affirmed that States may “place the burden of proving insanity upon the defendant,” *id.* at 799, and control how mental-state evidence is channeled. See also *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 486 (1895) (recognizing state discretion over insanity presumptions).

Arizona chose to channel all mental-state evidence into its insanity defense, a permissible allocation. When “a State provides an affirmative defense, it is generally free to determine the burden of proof and the procedures for its presentation.” *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 210 (citing *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 797). Clark’s claim that he had a freestanding right to use expert testimony to rebut *mens rea* collapses the distinction between intent and insanity. That distinction is constitutional. *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946) (States may “fix the line between mental states sufficient to constitute crime and those insufficient therefor”); *Tatum v. United States*, 88 U.S. App. D.C. 59, 62 (1951) (same).

**II. Due Process Does Not Require a Defendant to Use All Evidence for Both Guilt and Insanity**

A defendant’s right to present a defense is not absolute. This Court held in *Stein v. New York*, 346 U.S. 156, 196 (1953), that “[t]he accused does not have an unlimited right to introduce evidence which is incompetent, irrelevant, or otherwise inadmissible under established rules of evidence.” Arizona’s rule is a neutral evidentiary limitation, not a denial of process. Cf. *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 107 (1934) (due process is “a rule of fairness, not of procedure” that permits state experimentation).

Clark’s expert evidence remained available; it was simply confined to the insanity defense, where he bore the burden. This does not offend “those fundamental conceptions of justice which lie at the base of our civil and political institutions.” *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935). Indeed, in *Leland*, the Court rejected a nearly identical argument that Oregon’s burden allocation violated due process because it forced the defendant to prove insanity to avoid conviction for a crime requiring intent. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798–800.

**III. The Constitution Permits States to Presume Sanity and Intent**

Arizona may presume sanity and intent, as long as the prosecution retains the ultimate burden on *mens rea*. This Court in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. at 483, noted that the presumption of sanity is “a presumption of law . . . which may be rebutted.” But the State may define rebuttal methods. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 799 (upholding Oregon’s requirement that defendant rebut presumption by proving insanity beyond a reasonable doubt). And the presumption of intent itself is valid if “no more than a restatement of the common law inference that a person intends the natural consequences of his acts.” *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274 (1952) (citing *Commonwealth v. Webster*, 59 Mass. 295, 304 (1850)).

Arizona’s scheme does not shift the burden of proof on *mens rea*. The State still must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt. Clark’s failure to present evidence of his mental state at the guilt phase was a consequence of his own strategic choice—or Arizona’s permissible evidentiary boundary. That boundary does not violate “the essential demands of fairness,” *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942), overruled on other grounds by *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963).

**IV. History Confirms State Discretion Over Mental-State Evidence**

From the common law, “the respective rights of the State and the defendant” have varied. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 793. As early as *Regina v. M’Naghten*, 10 Cl. & F. 200 (1843), the insanity defense was a distinct plea, not a negation of intent. American courts followed. *Hopps v. People*, 31 Ill. 385, 392 (1863) (insanity is a separate question from specific intent). States have never been required to allow the same evidence to serve dual purposes. *Commonwealth v. Strickland*, 157 Mass. 284, 285 (1892) (evidence of insanity may be excluded from guilt phase if confined to plea). Arizona’s rule is consistent with this tradition.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
