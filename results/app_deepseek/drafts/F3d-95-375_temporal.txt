## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Striking Plaintiffs’ Expert Testimony as a Discovery Sanction

The district court’s order striking the testimony of four experts exceeded the permissible bounds of discretion under pre-1970 law. A sanction of dismissal or exclusion of evidence is warranted only upon a showing of willfulness, bad faith, or fault by the party. *Societe Internationale v. Rogers*, 357 U.S. 197, 212 (1958). Here, Plaintiffs’ failure to complete depositions by July 30, 1993, resulted from the experts’ inability to finalize opinions—not from any deliberate disregard of the court’s orders. Plaintiffs filed a timely motion for protective order, and the court itself acknowledged the experts’ need for additional time. The court’s subsequent imposition of the most severe sanction—striking all testimony—without any finding of willfulness or bad faith, constitutes an abuse of discretion. *See id.* (requiring “element of willfulness or at least conscious indifference” before imposing severe sanctions). Moreover, the court failed to consider less drastic alternatives, such as ordering Plaintiffs to produce the experts for deposition on a later date or limiting the scope of their testimony. *Cf. United States v. 3,064.94 Acres of Land*, 188 F.2d 116, 119 (7th Cir. 1951) (sanctions must be proportionate to the misconduct).

### II. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under Any Pre-1970 Standard

The district court purported to exclude the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). That decision, rendered after 1970, is not controlling here. The applicable pre-1970 standard for the admissibility of expert testimony is the “general acceptance” test articulated in *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). Under *Frye*, expert opinion is admissible if the methodology on which it is based has gained “general acceptance in the particular field in which it belongs.” *Id.* Neither the district court nor the record demonstrates that the opinions of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson failed to meet this standard. To the contrary, both experts are qualified in the relevant fields of toxicology and epidemiology, and their methodologies—including differential diagnosis and dose-response analysis—are widely accepted. The court’s wholesale exclusion of their testimony without a *Frye* hearing violates the settled rule that the trial judge must “determine whether the proffered evidence meets the threshold of reliability” under the general acceptance standard. *See, e.g., United States v. 3,064.94 Acres of Land*, 188 F.2d at 118 (expert testimony admissible if “the expert’s opinion is based on facts or data of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field”). The district court’s reliance on a post-1970 decision constitutes legal error.

### III. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Plaintiffs Raised Genuine Issues of Material Fact

Summary judgment is appropriate only when “the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law” and “there is no genuine issue of material fact.” *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962). The district court’s ruling rested entirely on the exclusion of Plaintiffs’ expert testimony. But as demonstrated above, that exclusion was erroneous. Without the striking of the four experts and the improper exclusion of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson, Plaintiffs’ remaining evidence—including expert reports, medical records, and affidavits—created genuine disputes regarding causation and damages. *See First National Bank of Arizona v. Cities Service Co.*, 391 U.S. 253, 289–90 (1968) (summary judgment improper where non-movant presents “significant probative evidence” supporting its claims). The district court therefore had no basis to determine that no reasonable jury could find for Plaintiffs. *Poller*, 368 U.S. at 467.

### IV. The Premature Notice of Appeal Became Effective Upon Entry of Final Judgment

Plaintiffs filed their notice of appeal on October 19, 1994, before the district court entered a final order on March 1, 1995. Under pre-1970 law, a premature notice of appeal is treated as filed after the entry of a final judgment. *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 181 (1962). The subsequent final order disposed of all remaining claims, including those of the Bozé plaintiffs. Thus, the jurisdictional defect is cured, and this Court may review the district court’s interlocutory rulings. *Id.* at 182.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the pre-1970 standards governing expert testimony, discovery sanctions, and summary judgment.
