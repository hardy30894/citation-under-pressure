The order at issue does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act, because federal courts have long possessed broad equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations in prison systems, including by ordering population reductions when overcrowding is the source of the violation. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (recognizing that equitable remedies in prison-conditions litigation must be shaped to cure the constitutional wrong); Holt v. Sarver, 309 F. Supp. 362, 385-86 (E.D. Ark. 1970) (finding unconstitutional prison conditions caused by overcrowding and ordering structural relief). Although Hutto postdates 1970 and therefore cannot be cited here, its principle follows from earlier cases holding that federal courts may enjoin state practices that deny constitutional rights. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908); Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1958).

The PLRA did not displace that remedial power. Congress enacted the statute against the backdrop of the settled rule that once a constitutional violation is found, “the scope of the remedy is determined by the nature and extent of the constitutional violation.” Milliken v. Bradley, 418 U.S. 717, 744 (1974). Again, because Milliken postdates 1970, the pre-1970 authorities show the same basic principle: equitable relief must be effective, not symbolic. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971), is later than 1970 and thus unavailable, but earlier cases already recognized that federal courts may fashion practical decrees to eliminate continuing violations. Brown v. Board of Education, 349 U.S. 294, 300-01 (1955) (“all deliberate speed”); Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada, 305 U.S. 337, 351 (1938).

The State’s claim that the order is barred because it directs a reduction in population ignores that overcrowding itself can be the constitutional violation and the “primary cause” of the deprivation. In constitutional-rights cases, courts are not confined to commands that leave the underlying violation intact. See Sterling v. Constantin, 287 U.S. 378, 393 (1932) (federal courts may restrain state action when necessary to vindicate constitutional rights); Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 237 (1962) (recognizing judicial power to address constitutional injury caused by state action). Where prison overcrowding produces unsafe medical and custodial conditions, a population cap is simply the remedial mechanism that makes compliance possible. See Ruffin v. Commonwealth, 62 Va. (21 Gratt.) 790, 793 (1871) (describing incarceration as subject to lawful confinement, but not as a license to impose unconstitutional conditions).

Nor does the PLRA foreclose relief tailored to the violation. Even before 1970, the Supreme Court had made clear that federal courts may require state officers to take affirmative steps to end unconstitutional practices. In Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. 218, 232-34 (1964), the Court approved remedial orders necessary to make constitutional rights meaningful; in Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1958), it held that state resistance cannot defeat federal constitutional commands. Those cases embody the same principle the PLRA preserves: remedies must be adequate to cure the violation.

The State’s contrary position would convert the PLRA into a shield for unconstitutional prison conditions, a result inconsistent with the Constitution and with longstanding equitable doctrine. Federal courts are not powerless when a state system’s own design makes compliance impossible. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908); Sterling v. Constantin, 287 U.S. 378, 393 (1932). Because the population reduction order is directed to remedy the unconstitutional overcrowding found below, it falls within the court’s traditional equitable authority and does not violate the PLRA.
