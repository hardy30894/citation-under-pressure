**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Equal Footing Doctrine by Evaluating Navigability at Statehood**

The equal footing doctrine provides that when a state enters the Union, it takes title to the beds and banks of rivers that are navigable for title purposes at the time of its admission. *United States v. Utah*, 284 U.S. 538, 545 (1932); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 14 (1894). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the navigability inquiry is tied to the moment of statehood. *Bonelli Cattle Co. v. Arizona*, 414 U.S. 313, 329 (1973) ("The crucial time for determining navigability is the time the State entered the Union."); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 28 (1935) ("The test of navigability is whether the stream was navigable in fact at the time the State was admitted into the Union.").

The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied this principle. It recognized that the equal footing doctrine vests title in the state only if the river was navigable *at statehood* (1889). *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 2012 MT 16, ¶ 60 (citing *United States v. 50.14 Acres of Land*, 294 F.3d 1148, 1153 (9th Cir. 2002)). The court did not err by considering historical evidence of navigability as it existed in 1889. *Id.* at ¶¶ 60–62. This approach is consistent with this Court’s instruction that courts may rely on historical records to determine navigability at the time of statehood. *Bonelli*, 414 U.S. at 329; *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 28.

PPL’s contention that the trial court should have considered post-statehood navigability is foreclosed by precedent. This Court has rejected the argument that later commercial use can retroactively establish navigability for title purposes. *Utah*, 284 U.S. at 545 ("The fact that a river is not at a particular time navigable does not prevent its becoming so by reason of subsequent changes in its condition."); *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 28 ("The test is not what the river is now, but what it was when Oregon was admitted into the Union."). The Montana Supreme Court properly adhered to this rule.

**II. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Admitting Historical Evidence of Navigability**

PPL challenges the admission of historical documents and expert testimony regarding the navigability of Montana’s rivers in 1889. This argument fails. Courts routinely rely on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood. *Bonelli*, 414 U.S. at 329; *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 28. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that such evidence is admissible under the Federal Rules of Evidence, including the ancient documents rule (Rule 901(8)) and the public records exception (Rule 803(8)). *PPL Montana*, 2012 MT 16, ¶¶ 92–94.

PPL’s hearsay and personal knowledge objections are unavailing. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the State’s exhibits, which were either self-authenticating under Rule 902 or admissible under Rule 803(8). *Id.* The court also properly considered expert testimony interpreting historical records, which is permissible under Rule 703. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993) (expert testimony may be based on otherwise inadmissible evidence if it is of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the field).

**III. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt Montana’s Claims**

PPL argues that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s ability to seek compensation for use of state-owned riverbeds. This contention is meritless. The FPA does not expressly preempt state property law, and this Court has recognized that the FPA establishes a "dual system" of federal and state authority. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 167–68 (1946). The FPA preserves state authority over "beds and banks" and state property rights. *Id.* at 161 n.6; *see also* 16 U.S.C. § 802.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the FPA does not preempt Montana’s claims under the Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA). *PPL Montana*, 2012 MT 16, ¶¶ 14–15. Federal courts have consistently held that the FPA does not bar state law claims for compensation for use of state-owned lands. *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983) ("A FERC license neither transfers nor diminishes any right of possession or enjoyment possessed by" a landowner.); *Nez Perce Tribe v. Idaho Power Co.*, 847 F. Supp. 791, 812 (D. Idaho 1993) (FPA does not preempt state property-based claims).

PPL’s reliance on *Federal Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), is misplaced. In *Niagara*, the Court held that the FPA did not authorize the Federal Power Commission to regulate nonnavigable tributaries. *Id.* at 249–51. That case did not address preemption of state property rights, which the FPA explicitly preserves. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 167–68.

**IV. The State’s Title Under the Equal Footing Doctrine Extends to the Riverbeds at Issue**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at statehood and that the State acquired title to the riverbeds under the equal footing doctrine. *PPL Montana*, 2012 MT 16, ¶¶ 60–62. This conclusion is supported by substantial evidence, including historical records and expert testimony. The trial court did not err in crediting this evidence.

PPL’s arguments to the contrary—such as its claim that the rivers were not navigable in 1889—are contradicted by the record and this Court’s precedent. The Montana Supreme Court’s decision is consistent with the equal footing doctrine and the navigability test established by this Court.

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine by evaluating navigability at statehood and admitting historical evidence to support its findings. The Federal Power Act does not preempt Montana’s claims, and the State’s title to the riverbeds is secure under longstanding precedent. The judgment below should be affirmed.
