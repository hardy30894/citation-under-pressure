The Sixth Amendment does not permit the Government to convert an uncounseled tribal-court conviction into an element of a federal felony.

Section 117(a) makes the existence of two prior domestic-violence convictions an element of the offense, not a sentencing factor. As the Supreme Court has long held, when the Government seeks to establish guilt, it must prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). That demand for reliability is at its apex when the predicate conviction is used to prove the defendant’s criminal status and secure a new felony conviction. Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948) (“to liberty in a government dedicated to justice under law”). A federal felony may not rest on a record that the Constitution would treat as infirm in a state or federal prosecution.

This Court’s recidivist cases confirm the point. In Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), the Court held that a conviction obtained in violation of the right to counsel could not be used “to support guilt or enhance punishment” because doing so would erode the right to counsel itself. The Court explained that “[t]o permit a conviction obtained in violation of Gideon v. Wainwright to be used against a person either to support guilt or enhance punishment” would be impermissible. Id. at 115. Likewise, in Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 452 (1962), the Court recognized that habitual-offender proceedings allow challenges to prior convictions that are “not constitutionally valid.” And in Chandler v. Fretag, 348 U.S. 3, 9 (1954), the Court held that a defendant must have a meaningful opportunity to obtain counsel when the State seeks habitual-criminal punishment.

The Government relies on Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), but Nichols is a sentencing case and does not control here. Nichols allowed consideration of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction in calculating criminal-history points under the Sentencing Guidelines, where the conviction “did not violate the Sixth Amendment.” Id. at 746-47. That holding rested on the distinction between sentencing and guilt. Here, by contrast, the prior convictions are not merely background facts affecting punishment; they are elements the jury must find to convict. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. at 364. Nichols did not authorize the Government to prove an element of a felony with a conviction obtained without counsel.

That distinction is critical under the Court’s recidivist cases. In Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554, 560-61 (1967), the Court upheld a state procedure that submitted prior convictions to the jury because recidivism statutes “penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” See also Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948) (recidivist sentence “is a stiffened penalty for the latest crime, which is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one”). But those cases did not involve using an uncounseled conviction as an element of a new offense, nor did they allow a conviction that would be constitutionally defective in ordinary federal or state court to be treated as reliable proof of guilt. Burgett, 389 U.S. at 115.

The fact that Bryant’s prior convictions were entered in tribal court makes the Government’s position worse, not better. It is true that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). But tribal-court judgments are respected in federal court as a matter of comity, not because they are exempt from all reliability review when the federal Government seeks to use them to establish a felony element. See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987); Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810-11 (9th Cir. 1997). Congress may define federal crimes, but it may not dispense with the Sixth Amendment’s core protection that criminal guilt be proven with reliable evidence. Winship, 397 U.S. at 364.

Nor does the use of the tribal convictions become valid simply because the Sixth Amendment did not bind the tribal court itself. Section 117(a) asks whether Bryant “has a final conviction” on prior occasions. A conviction valid under tribal law is not thereby transformed into constitutionally reliable proof for a later federal felony prosecution when the absence of counsel would have barred its use in state or federal court. Burgett, 389 U.S. at 115. The Constitution does not permit the Government to do indirectly what it may not do directly: obtain a felony conviction by relying on uncounseled misdemeanor convictions as essential elements of guilt. Cole, 333 U.S. at 201; Burgett, 389 U.S. at 115.

The judgment of the court of appeals should therefore be reversed.
