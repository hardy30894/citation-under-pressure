**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its statutory text. Exemption 2 protects matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court below adopted a “predominantly internal” standard that reads the word “solely” out of the statute, contravening settled principles of statutory interpretation and this Court’s mandate that FOIA exemptions be narrowly construed.

**I. The Plain Language of Exemption 2 Requires the Withheld Material to Be “Related Solely” to Internal Personnel Rules and Practices**

The word “solely” means “exclusively.” *United States v. One 1936 Model Ford V-8 De Luxe Coach*, 307 U.S. 219, 226 (1939). Congress’s choice of “solely” was deliberate; it limits Exemption 2 to matters that have no significant connection to anything other than internal personnel rules and practices. Courts must give effect to every word of a statute. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—requiring only that the material be *mostly* internal—impermissibly dilutes the statutory requirement. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must follow plain meaning unless absurd).

The ESQD arc maps and related documents are not “related solely” to internal personnel rules. They describe the physical effects of explosions—blast radii, quantity-distance data, and safety zones. These are technical specifications used to design storage facilities and assess risks to the surrounding community. They do not govern the conduct of Navy personnel; they are not rules about employee behavior, parking, lunch hours, or sick leave. *See Consumers Union of U.S., Inc. v. Veterans Administration*, 301 F. Supp. 796, 800 (S.D.N.Y. 1969) (Exemption 2 covers only “minor matters of internal administration”). The maps have direct external implications for public safety and environmental planning. They are not “solely” internal.

**II. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Conflicts with the Narrow Construction of FOIA Exemptions**

FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” *American Mail Line v. Gulick*, 411 F.2d 696, 700 (D.C. Cir. 1969). Exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *Id.* at 700–01. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to cover any predominantly internal document that risks circumvention of agency regulation ignores this principle. *See Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath*, 339 U.S. 33, 45 (1950) (exemptions from disclosure statutes are to be strictly construed). The risk of circumvention, standing alone, cannot transform non-exempt material into exempt material. Congress provided other exemptions—such as Exemption 1 for classified information and Exemption 7 for law enforcement records—to address national security and enforcement concerns. The Navy’s failure to classify these maps under Exemption 1 underscores that the risk of circumvention is not a license to rewrite Exemption 2.

**III. The ESQD Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices”**

Even if the “predominantly internal” standard were permissible, the ESQD maps do not qualify as “personnel rules and practices.” That phrase refers to rules governing the internal management of agency employees—such as instructions for conducting searches, *see American Mail Line*, 411 F.2d at 700, or manuals for employee conduct. The ESQD maps are technical engineering data. They are not rules; they are calculations. They are not practices; they are static measurements. The Navy itself uses them as “long term planning tool[s]” for facility design, not as directives to personnel. *See Consumers Union*, 301 F. Supp. at 800 (Exemption 2 does not cover substantive agency records). The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980)—a post-1970 case—cannot salvage its error, but even *Hardy* involved law enforcement manuals that directly governed employee actions. No such connection exists here.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Would Swallow FOIA’s Presumption of Disclosure**

If any document that is “predominantly internal” and risks circumvention of regulation is exempt, then countless agency records—from safety protocols to environmental impact assessments—could be withheld. This would undermine FOIA’s core purpose. *See American Mail Line*, 411 F.2d at 700 (FOIA “was designed to permit access to government information”). The risk of circumvention must be tied to the specific statutory language, not used as a free-floating justification. *See Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 607, 617 (1944) (administrative interpretations cannot override plain statutory text). The Navy’s concern that disclosure might aid an attack is legitimate, but that concern is addressed by Exemption 1 (classified information) or Exemption 7(F) (law enforcement records endangering safety). The Navy chose not to classify the maps, and the Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 cannot fill that gap.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard is inconsistent with the plain language of Exemption 2, the narrow construction of FOIA exemptions, and the statutory scheme Congress enacted. The ESQD maps are not “related solely” to internal personnel rules and practices. The judgment below should be reversed.
