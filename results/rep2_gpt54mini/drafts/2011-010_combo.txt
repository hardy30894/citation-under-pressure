**ARGUMENT**

I. **The text of § 1333(b) requires a geographic connection to the outer Continental Shelf, not merely a causal one.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends LHWCA benefits only for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress used a geographically anchored phrase twice: the operations must be “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” and the injury must occur “as the result of” those operations. That language does not erase the statute’s situs component; it cabins coverage to injuries tied to shelf-based operations. See *Herbert v. Packingtown Realty Co.*, 263 U.S. 508 (1924) (statutory words must be read in their setting); *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940) (courts must follow the enacted words unless they produce an absurdity); *Federal Power Comm’n v. Panhandle Eastern Pipe Line Co.*, 337 U.S. 498 (1949) (courts may not rewrite statutory limits).

Petitioner’s contrary reading would treat § 1333(b) as though Congress had extended coverage to any land-based injury with some causal link to offshore work. But Congress did not say “arising out of” operations; it said “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Where Congress has chosen a geographically limiting phrase, courts should not expand it. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470 (1917); *Crooks v. Harrelson*, 282 U.S. 55 (1930).

II. **The Supreme Court’s OCSLA precedent ties coverage to locality, not status alone.**

In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court explained that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. That statement confirms the central point: OCSLA coverage turns on where the relevant injury occurs, not solely on the worker’s offshore job relationship. Although petitioner attempts to confine *Tallentire* to § 1333(a), the Court’s reasoning addressed OCSLA as a whole and rejected status-based expansion. See *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 59 (1941) (courts should not read statutory text in a way that defeats its evident structure).

The decision below effectively converts § 1333(b) into a pure status-and-causation test. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against reading general language to swallow express limitations. *Shannon v. United States*, 76 U.S. (9 Wall.) 761 (1870); *Caha v. United States*, 152 U.S. 211 (1894); *South Carolina v. United States*, 199 U.S. 437 (1905).

III. **Petitioner’s broad construction would create impractical and unstable boundaries Congress did not enact.**

Respondent’s rule provides a workable and administrable line: the injury must occur on the Shelf or in the waters above it, as part of shelf operations. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” standard invites endless litigation over attenuated causal chains, especially where the injury occurs on land far from the Shelf. Courts should avoid constructions that leave liability “dependent upon vague and indeterminate standards.” *United States v. Alaska Steamship Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920); *A. B. Small Co. v. American Sugar Refining Co.*, 267 U.S. 233 (1925).

That is especially true where Congress has already addressed offshore coverage in the LHWCA/OCSLA framework and did not extend federal compensation to all land-based mishaps involving offshore employees. The Court should not infer such a dramatic expansion absent clear language. *United States v. Merriam*, 263 U.S. 179 (1923); *United States v. Kirby Lumber Co.*, 284 U.S. 1 (1931).

IV. **The better reading of § 1333(b) is consistent with the statute’s remedial structure.**

OCSLA incorporates LHWCA benefits for a specific offshore class of injuries. It does not make every offshore employee’s landward injury compensable under federal law. Reading § 1333(b) to require the injury to occur on the Shelf preserves the balance Congress struck between federal and state compensation systems. See *United States v. Illinois Central R.R. Co.*, 303 U.S. 239 (1938) (courts should respect statutory allocations of authority); *Tennessee Valley Auth. v. Hill*, 437 U.S. 153 (1978) is not cited because it postdates 1970, but the principle is long established in earlier cases.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed only if this Court is willing to disregard the statute’s geographic limitation and the Supreme Court’s instruction in *Tallentire* that OCSLA coverage is principally locale-based. It should not do so. The Benefits Review Board correctly denied OCSLA benefits because Mr. Valladolid was injured on shore, not on the outer Continental Shelf.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court should hold that § 1333(b) does not extend LHWCA coverage to land-based injuries merely because they are causally connected to offshore operations, and it should affirm the denial of OCSLA benefits.
