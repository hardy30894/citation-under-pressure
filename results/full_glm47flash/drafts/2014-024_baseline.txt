**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTS SPEECH UNLESS IT IS A “TRUE THREAT,” WHICH REQUIRES PROOF OF THE DEFENDANT’S SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.**

The First Amendment protects the freedom of speech unless that speech falls into the narrow category of a "true threat." *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 381 (1992). The Supreme Court has held that a "true threat" is one that a reasonable person would understand as a serious expression of intent to cause harm. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). However, the Court has consistently emphasized that the "true threat" exception is predicated on the defendant’s intent to intimidate. *Id.* at 359. The Third Circuit’s instruction that a "reasonable speaker would foresee" the statement as a threat effectively removes the defendant’s subjective intent from the equation, violating the First Amendment.

In *Virginia v. Black*, the Court held that a person commits a crime if they "intend to intimidate another person or group of persons." *Id.* at 359. The Court further explained that a "true threat" is one that a "reasonable person would understand as a serious expression of intent to cause harm." *Id.* at 359. The Third Circuit’s standard, derived from *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), conflates the "reasonable person" standard for *understanding* the threat with the *intent* requirement for *committing* the crime. By instructing the jury that a statement is a true threat if a "reasonable speaker would foresee" the statement as a threat, the court effectively criminalizes speech based on the audience’s reaction rather than the speaker’s state of mind. This shifts the burden to the defendant to predict how others will react to his words, chilling the exercise of protected speech.

**II. THE “REASONABLE SPEAKER” STANDARD IS INCONSISTENT WITH *VIRGINIA V. BLACK* AND CONFLATES MENS REA WITH THE NATURE OF THE SPEECH.**

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Virginia v. Black* makes clear that the intent to intimidate is a necessary element of the crime. *Id.* at 359. The Court stated, "A person who by means of an object or an article is in violation of this subsection commits a crime if that person intends to intimidate another person or group of persons." *Id.* at 359. The Third Circuit’s instruction that a "reasonable speaker would foresee" the statement as a threat effectively ignores this mens rea requirement. Under the Third Circuit’s standard, a defendant could be convicted of making a threat even if he did not intend to threaten anyone, provided that a reasonable person might have been frightened by his words.

This interpretation is inconsistent with the First Amendment. *Virginia v. Black* distinguished between speech that is merely offensive and speech that is a true threat. *Id.* at 359. The Court held that the government may not prohibit speech based on the listener’s fear or reaction, but only on the speaker’s intent to intimidate. *Id.* at 359. By adopting a "reasonable speaker" standard, the Third Circuit allowed the government to punish Elonis’s speech based on the fear it generated in his wife and the community, rather than on his actual intent to cause harm. This is precisely the type of content-based regulation that the First Amendment forbids.

**III. THE “REASONABLE SPEAKER” STANDARD IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE IT FAILS TO DISTINGUISH BETWEEN PROTECTED ARTISTIC EXPRESSION AND THREATS.**

The First Amendment protects the right to express oneself, even if the expression is offensive, controversial, or misunderstood. *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). Elonis’s Facebook posts were intended as artistic expression and absurdist humor, not as actual threats. The Third Circuit’s objective standard cannot account for the context in which the speech was made. Under the "reasonable speaker" standard, a defendant who uses hyperbolic language or dark humor to express frustration could be convicted of a crime, even if he had no intent to threaten anyone.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the government may not punish speech simply because it is offensive or frightening. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The "true threat" exception is narrowly tailored to protect individuals from actual violence, not to protect the public sensibilities. *Id.* at 359. The Third Circuit’s instruction allowed the jury to convict Elonis based on the fear his words generated, rather than on his intent to cause harm. This is an unconstitutional application of the "true threat" doctrine. Therefore, the conviction must be reversed.
