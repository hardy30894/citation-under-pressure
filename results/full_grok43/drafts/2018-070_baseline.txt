ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States as surrogate federal law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The text, structure, and purpose of the Act establish that state law applies solely to fill gaps in federal coverage. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law governs whenever it “pertains to the subject matter” and is not expressly preempted—contradicts controlling precedent and the Act’s design.

Congress enacted OCSLA to create a comprehensive body of federal law for the outer Continental Shelf. “The purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). To that end, § 1333(a)(1) extends the Constitution and federal laws to the Shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” State law enters only as a supplemental source when federal law is silent. See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988) (noting Congress’s recognition that federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself”).

The Supreme Court has consistently applied this gap-filling principle. In Rodrigue, state wrongful-death remedies were adopted because “the [federal] admiralty action under the [High Seas] Act no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave.” 395 U.S. at 366. The Court emphasized that state law filled the void left by the inapplicability of maritime remedies. Id. at 360. Similarly, in Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court held that Louisiana’s statute of limitations applied only because “federal law does not provide a limitations period.” Id. at 101. These decisions confirm that incorporation occurs only where federal law leaves “a significant void or gap.” Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive federal scheme governing wages and hours on the Shelf. Its savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws in areas of concurrent state-federal jurisdiction, but does not alter OCSLA’s distinct choice-of-law rule. Because the FLSA addresses the precise subject matter of Newton’s claims, no gap exists for California law to fill. The Ninth Circuit’s holding that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law would render the phrase “to the extent that they are applicable” superfluous and convert OCSLA into a general incorporation statute, contrary to Rodrigue and Huson.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
