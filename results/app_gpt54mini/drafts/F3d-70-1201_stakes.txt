**ARGUMENT**

Rule 407 does not authorize a party to create a misleading impression about post-accident safety changes and then invoke the Rule as a shield against corrective impeachment. The district court therefore abused its discretion when it allowed Morbark to suggest, in opening statement and on cross-examination, that the chipper remained unchanged, but then admonished the jury to disregard Infinger’s testimony that the infeed chute had been lengthened. Rule 407 bars admission of subsequent remedial measures only when offered to prove negligence or culpable conduct; it does not bar impeachment evidence when the opposing party has opened the door by creating a false or misleading impression. See Fed. R. Evid. 407; **Muzyka v. Remington Arms Co., 774 F.2d 1309, 1313-14 (5th Cir. 1985)**; **Gauthier v. AMF, Inc., 805 F.2d 337, 340-41 (9th Cir. 1986)**; **Kelly v. Crown Equipment Co., 970 F.2d 1273, 1277-78 (3d Cir. 1992)**.

This Court had not yet decided whether Rule 407 applies in strict products liability actions, but the better-reasoned authorities hold that even where Rule 407 is available in such cases, it does not exclude subsequent remedial measures when offered for impeachment. In **Muzyka v. Remington Arms Co., 774 F.2d 1309 (5th Cir. 1985)**, the Fifth Circuit held that the district court properly admitted evidence of design changes for impeachment where the defendant had argued the product was safe as designed. Likewise, in **Gauthier v. AMF, Inc., 805 F.2d 337 (9th Cir. 1986)**, the Ninth Circuit held that Rule 407 did not require exclusion when the evidence was admitted to impeach testimony that the product was as safe as it could be. And in **Kelly v. Crown Equipment Co., 970 F.2d 1273 (3d Cir. 1992)**, the Third Circuit recognized that impeachment is a proper, limited purpose under Rule 407, while cautioning against using the exception as a backdoor to prove defect. Those cases confirm the governing principle: a litigant may not take inconsistent factual positions before the jury and then demand the protection of Rule 407 to prevent rebuttal.

That is precisely what happened here. Morbark first told the jury in opening that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered “thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case,” R4-141-22, plainly implying the post-accident chipper remained the same. Morbark then reinforced that impression through cross-examination, asking whether the city still had “that Morbark chipper machine,” and eliciting affirmative answers that it was “the same machine.” R4-141-44. Only after Wood elicited the truth—that “another chute” had been added, making it “that much longer,” R4-141-51—did the court instruct the jury to disregard the evidence. That ruling was error because the evidence was not introduced to show negligence in the first instance; it was introduced to correct a misleading factual narrative created by Morbark itself.

The law does not permit a party to “open the door” and then close it when the opposing party walks through. The Eleventh Circuit has recognized that otherwise inadmissible evidence may be admitted to rebut a misleading impression created by an adversary. See **United States v. Rubio, 87 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 1996)**. And the Supreme Court has long held that evidentiary rules are to be applied in light of their purposes, not as talismans to shield distortion. See **United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45, 50-52 (1984)**. Here, the rebuttal testimony was necessary to prevent the jury from being left with the false belief that the allegedly dangerous machine had never been altered. Once Morbark made the machine’s post-accident condition part of its defense, fairness required that Wood be allowed to correct the record.

The district court’s contrary instruction was prejudicial. The central issue at trial was whether the seventeen-inch infeed chute rendered the chipper defective and unreasonably dangerous. Evidence that Morbark later lengthened the chute directly undermined its claim that the original design was safe and immutable. Excluding that testimony after Morbark itself had used the machine’s continued use as proof of safety likely affected the verdict. A new trial is warranted where an evidentiary error affects substantial rights and the jury may have been misled on a material issue. See **McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood, 464 U.S. 548, 553-54 (1984)**; **United States v. Stephens, 365 F.3d 967, 974 (11th Cir. 2004)**.

At minimum, this Court should hold that Rule 407 does not bar evidence of subsequent remedial measures offered to impeach a misleading defense theory in a strict products liability case. The district court’s admonition to disregard Infinger’s testimony deprived Wood of the fair impeachment to which she was entitled and requires reversal.
