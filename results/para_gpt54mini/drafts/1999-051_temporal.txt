Respondent’s position is compelled by the settled principle that federal habeas review is not available to rescue a claim whose own constitutional predicate has been forfeited in state court. In **Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72 (1977)**, the Court held that a state procedural default bars federal habeas review absent a showing of cause and prejudice. That rule reflects “the societal costs of reversing a conviction when the defendant has had a full and fair trial,” **id. at 90**, and it applies with equal force here.

Petitioner asks for a further step: to use an allegedly ineffective-assistance claim, itself procedurally defaulted, as “cause” to excuse the default of a separate underlying claim. That request cannot be squared with the Court’s causation cases. Cause must be something external to the defense; a defaulted claim cannot bootstrap itself into cause. The Court’s decisions in **Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986)** and **Murray v. Carrier, 498 U.S. 2 (1990)** confirm that attorney error may constitute cause only when the ineffectiveness claim has itself been properly presented to the state courts. A procedurally barred ineffectiveness claim is not an available basis to evade the independent state ground doctrine.

Earlier cases point the same way. In **Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963)**, the Court recognized that federal habeas is an extraordinary remedy, but it also stressed that a prisoner who deliberately bypasses state procedures forfeits the normal avenue of federal relief. **Id. at 438-39.** And in **Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953)**, the Court emphasized that habeas does not function as a substitute for orderly litigation in the state courts. Those decisions would be emptied of meaning if a prisoner could revive a forfeited claim by repackaging it as counsel ineffectiveness, notwithstanding a separate default of that ineffectiveness theory itself.

The requirement that the “cause” asserted be legally available is also consistent with the Court’s treatment of exhaustion. In **Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114 (1944)**, the Court held that federal courts should not intervene until state remedies have been pursued in the ordinary course. Likewise, in **Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200 (1950)**, the Court reiterated that a state prisoner must first present his federal claims to the state courts before seeking federal habeas relief. A defaulted ineffectiveness claim is not “presented” in the relevant sense, and it therefore cannot serve as the necessary gateway to federal review of another defaulted claim.

Petitioner’s rule would also undermine the finality and comity values at the heart of the procedural-default doctrine. The Court has repeatedly explained that state courts must be given the first opportunity to correct constitutional errors. **Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309 (1915)**; **Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86 (1923)**. If a prisoner may always invoke a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse any other default, then the state’s procedural rules cease to matter, and the federal court becomes a forum of first, not last, resort. That is not the law.

Nor does any pre-1970 authority support petitioner’s attempt to layer one default upon another. To the contrary, the older cases recognize that federal collateral review is limited where the prisoner has failed to comply with state procedure. In **Sunal v. Large, 332 U.S. 174 (1947)**, the Court refused to permit habeas to do the work of an omitted appeal, explaining that collateral review does not serve as a substitute for established review channels. **Id. at 178.** And in **United States ex rel. Kennedy v. Tyler, 269 U.S. 13 (1925)**, the Court refused to ignore procedural regularity in the state courts simply because the prisoner recast his grievance in federal constitutional terms.

The proper rule is straightforward: a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance cannot excuse the procedural default of another claim unless the ineffectiveness claim itself has been preserved in state court and is independently available as cause. Anything less would permit a prisoner to evade state procedure by means of a claim that the state courts never had a fair chance to decide. That is precisely what the Court’s procedural-default and exhaustion decisions forbid. **Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963); Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443 (1953); Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114 (1944).**

For those reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
