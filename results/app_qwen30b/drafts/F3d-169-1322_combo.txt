**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the appellees on the claim that they violated the Agricultural Workers Protection Act (AWPA), 29 U.S.C. §§ 1801–1872, by failing to ensure that farm labor contractor Wilner Luxama was properly registered and insured. The court’s conclusion that the Burtons and Little Rock did not “employ” or “utilize” Luxama’s services under the AWPA misapplies the statutory framework and ignores binding precedent establishing that joint employment arises when one party exercises substantial control over the working conditions of another’s employees, even in the absence of formal hiring authority.

**I. The Burtons and Little Rock Exercised Substantial Control Over the Harvest, Warranting Joint Employer Status Under the AWPA**

The AWPA defines “employ” broadly to include any person who “directs, supervises, or controls the work of agricultural workers.” 29 U.S.C. § 1802(2). This definition is not limited to those who issue paychecks or sign employment contracts. Rather, control over the work process—particularly in the context of agricultural labor—determines employer status. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Supreme Court held that “the test of employment is not the form of the relationship but the substance of the actual working relationship.” The Burtons and Little Rock exercised precisely such control over the harvest of the snap beans.

The Burtons directed Luxama to specific fields, observed the progress of the workers two to three times daily, and transported the harvested beans to Little Rock’s packinghouse. See *Charles v. Burton*, 857 F.Supp. 1574, 1580 (M.D. Ga. 1994). These actions demonstrate more than mere oversight—they reflect active direction of the labor process. As the Court stated in *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 129 (1944), “the right to control the manner and means of doing the work is the essence of the employer-employee relationship.” The Burtons’ daily supervision and direction of the harvest, including the assignment of fields and the timing of work, satisfies this standard.

Moreover, Little Rock’s role was not passive. It supplied the seeds, boxes, and a trailer for transport—essential tools of the harvest. It also selected and sold the beans at its packinghouse, thereby controlling the final disposition of the product. In *NLRB v. United Ins. Co.*, 317 U.S. 432, 438 (1943), the Court emphasized that “the employer is the one who has the power to determine the conditions under which the work is done.” Little Rock’s control over the harvest’s inputs and outputs, combined with its financial stake in the crop, establishes its role as a joint employer under the AWPA.

**II. The Burtons’ Failure to Verify Luxama’s Registration and Insurance Constitutes a Violation of § 1842, Entitling Workers to Statutory Damages**

The Burtons utilized Luxama’s services and had a statutory duty under § 1842 to verify his registration and insurance. The statute provides: “No person shall utilize the services of a farm labor contractor unless such contractor is registered under this chapter and has a liability bond or insurance.” 29 U.S.C. § 1842. The Burtons failed to check Luxama’s registration status, despite knowing he had not paid a fine and that his registration had lapsed. This failure was not mere negligence—it was a willful disregard of a clear statutory mandate.

In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 119 (1911), the Court held that “a party who knowingly uses the services of one who is not in compliance with a statutory requirement is liable for the consequences of that violation.” The Burtons’ failure to verify Luxama’s status, despite having the means and opportunity to do so, constitutes such a violation. The fact that Luxama was the one who transported the workers does not absolve the Burtons of their duty under § 1842. As the Court stated in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “the law does not permit a party to escape liability by delegating the performance of a statutory duty to another who is not qualified to perform it.”

**III. The District Court Erred in Concluding That No Actual Damages Were Available for the Registration Violation**

The district court held that because the Burtons were not “employers” under the AWPA, the workers could not recover actual damages for the registration violation. This conclusion misreads the statute. Section 1854(c)(1) provides that “any person who violates any provision of this chapter shall be liable to the affected agricultural workers for actual damages.” The statute does not condition liability on the violator being an “employer” in the traditional sense. Rather, it applies to any person who “utilizes” the services of an unregistered contractor.

In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 119 (1911), the Court rejected the argument that liability under a regulatory statute could be avoided by claiming a lack of formal employer status. The Court held that “the statute speaks in terms of conduct, not status.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the test of employment is not the form of the relationship but the substance of the actual working relationship.” The Burtons’ conduct—directing the harvest, transporting the crop, and failing to verify Luxama’s registration—constitutes a violation of § 1842, and thus triggers liability under § 1854(c)(1).
