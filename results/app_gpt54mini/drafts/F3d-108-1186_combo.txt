### Argument

The district court’s handling of the verdict cannot stand because the jury returned internally inconsistent damage findings, and Unit timely asked the court to resolve the ambiguity before the jury was discharged. Where a verdict is uncertain, the trial judge has both the power and the duty to seek clarification while the jurors remain available. See *St. Louis S.W. Ry. Co. v. Simpson*, 286 U.S. 346, 352–53 (1932); *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 67 F.2d 397, 402 (4th Cir. 1933), aff’d in part, rev’d in part on other grounds, 333 U.S. 364 (1948). The refusal to do so here deprived Unit of the jury’s actual resolution of damages and permitted the court to substitute its own reconstruction of what the jury might have meant.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 51 requires a timely objection to erroneous jury instructions, but the rule is satisfied when the party alerts the court to the defect and requests correction before the verdict becomes final. *Tinker v. Texas & Pac. Ry. Co.*, 254 U.S. 629, 635 (1921); *Delaware, Lackawanna & W. R.R. Co. v. Converse*, 139 U.S. 469, 472–73 (1891). Unit did exactly that. It objected that the verdict forms were “extremely difficult to understand” and asked the court to question the jury before discharge. That preserved the issue the only way it could be preserved once the ambiguity appeared. See *New York Cent. & H.R.R. Co. v. Fraloff*, 100 U.S. 24, 31 (1879) (trial court should correct a verdict before the jury is discharged when the defect is apparent).

The court’s later interpretation of the forms was also error. A trial judge may not rewrite a verdict where the jury’s meaning can be ascertained, and any ambiguity must be resolved by the jury itself if possible. *United States v. 196.10 Acres of Land*, 115 F.2d 878, 880 (2d Cir. 1940); *McDonald v. Pless*, 238 U.S. 264, 267–68 (1915). Here, the jury found for Unit on breach and awarded $70,768.02 on Form 1, then separately awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3. Those findings are readily understood in the way Unit proposed: the jury first fixed Unit’s recoverable invoice damages at $157,262.36 less the repair cost it attributed to Enron’s set-off, and then reflected Enron’s independent recovery on the set-off form. The court instead subtracted the Form 3 number from the Form 1 number and entered zero damages, a construction that assumes the very issue the jury was never asked to make explicit.

That was not harmless. A judgment must reflect the jury’s actual verdict, not a judicial guess at arithmetic. *Fillippon v. Albion Vein Slate Co.*, 250 U.S. 76, 83–84 (1919); *Wabash Ry. Co. v. De Tar*, 141 U.S. 304, 310–11 (1891). When a verdict is susceptible to more than one reasonable interpretation, the proper course is to send the jury back for clarification before discharge. *Simpson*, 286 U.S. at 352–53; *Fraloff*, 100 U.S. at 31. The district court’s refusal to do so was especially prejudicial because the instructions themselves invited the confusion: the jury was told both to determine Unit’s damages and to consider Enron’s set-off, without clear instruction that the same repair costs could not be double-counted. A verdict form that permits duplicative treatment of the same loss is legally defective. See *Baltimore & Carolina Line, Inc. v. Redman*, 295 U.S. 654, 660–61 (1935); *United States v. Lewis*, 340 U.S. 590, 594 (1951) (courts must effectuate, not distort, the jury’s actual determination).

At minimum, the ambiguity required a new trial on damages. Federal courts have long recognized that when a verdict is so uncertain that the court cannot determine the jury’s intention with confidence, the appropriate remedy is a new trial rather than judicial speculation. *McDonald*, 238 U.S. at 267–68; *Converse*, 139 U.S. at 472–73. Unit preserved that remedy by moving for a new trial after the court refused clarification. The denial of that motion was an abuse of discretion because the court’s own interpretation created the inconsistency it then used to wipe out Unit’s recovery.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions either to enter judgment consistent with the jury’s actual intent or, if that cannot be reliably determined, to grant a new trial on damages.
