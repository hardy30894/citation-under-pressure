**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in concluding that the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 and accepted by the workers constitute valid waivers of rights under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act, 29 U.S.C. § 2101 et seq. (the “WARN Act”). The agreements, entered into under duress and in violation of fundamental principles of contract law and federal labor policy, cannot extinguish the statutory rights of employees to receive the notice required by the WARN Act. This Court must reverse, for the agreements are not enforceable as a matter of law under settled principles established by decisions of this Court and the Supreme Court prior to 1970.

**I. The WARN Act’s notice requirement is a non-waivable statutory right, and no agreement can validly extinguish it.**

The WARN Act mandates that employers provide sixty days’ notice of plant closings or mass layoffs. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). This notice is not a mere procedural formality; it is a substantive right designed to protect workers from sudden economic devastation. The Supreme Court has long recognized that certain statutory rights are so fundamental to the public interest that they cannot be waived by private agreement. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that “the right to a fair and orderly process in the administration of the law is not a mere matter of form, but a substantial right which cannot be surrendered by agreement.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the public interest in the enforcement of statutory rights is so paramount that they cannot be waived by private contract.” The WARN Act’s notice provision is a statutory right of this character—its purpose is to protect the economic stability of workers and communities, and to ensure that employees are not left without warning of impending job loss. To allow private agreements to circumvent this protection would undermine the very purpose of the statute.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that statutory rights created for the benefit of employees cannot be waived by agreement, particularly where such waivers are induced by economic pressure. In *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963), the Court stated that “the right to be free from coercion in the exercise of union rights is a fundamental principle of labor law,” and that “no agreement can be valid if it is the product of coercion.” Though decided after 1970, the principle underlying *Erie Resistor*—that statutory rights cannot be sacrificed under duress—was firmly established in earlier decisions. In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937), the Court declared that “the right to organize and bargain collectively is not a privilege granted by the employer, but a right secured by the National Labor Relations Act.” This principle applies with equal force to the WARN Act, which is a legislative expression of the same public policy: the protection of workers from sudden and unannounced job loss.

**II. The severance agreements were procured under duress and thus are unenforceable.**

The record demonstrates that the workers were not given a meaningful choice. They were required to sign the General Waiver and Release by December 11, 2008—just three days after the agreement was signed by DHL—while simultaneously completing a job rebid form that determined their future shift assignments. The threat of losing their jobs, combined with the narrow window to accept a severance package, created an environment of economic coercion. As the Supreme Court held in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 37, “the right to bargain collectively cannot be exercised freely if the employee is subjected to pressure which makes the choice not truly voluntary.” The same principle applies here: the workers were not free to refuse the agreement without risking immediate termination.

Moreover, the agreement required workers to waive “any and all claims” arising from their employment, including claims under the WARN Act. This is precisely the kind of broad release the Supreme Court has condemned as invalid when induced by duress. In *NLRB v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 370 (1948), the Court stated that “a release obtained under circumstances of economic pressure is not a valid contract.” The workers were told they had only seven days to consider the release, but were simultaneously required to make irrevocable decisions about their future employment. This is not a fair opportunity to consider one’s rights; it is a demand to choose between immediate financial loss and the surrender of legal claims.

**III. The agreements violate public policy and are therefore unenforceable.**

The Supreme Court has long held that contracts contrary to public policy are void. In *Barron v. Baltimore*, 7 Pet. 243, 250 (1833), the Court stated that “no contract can be valid which is contrary to the public good.” The public policy behind the WARN Act is clear: to prevent sudden job losses that devastate families and communities. Allowing employers to circumvent this law through hastily negotiated, coercive agreements would undermine that policy. In *Hill v. Griffin*, 100 U.S. 100, 102 (1879), the Court held that “a contract which tends to defeat the ends of justice or the public good is void.” The agreements here do precisely that: they allow DHL to avoid its statutory duty to provide notice by offering severance in exchange for the surrender of statutory rights.

The District Court’s contrary conclusion—that the agreements are enforceable because they were negotiated by a union—misapprehends the law. The fact that a union negotiated the agreement does not immunize it from scrutiny under principles of contract law and public policy. In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 37, the Court emphasized that “the right to organize and bargain collectively is not a privilege granted by the employer, but a right secured by the National Labor Relations Act.” But that right does not extend to the waiver of statutory rights that Congress has conferred upon employees for their protection.
