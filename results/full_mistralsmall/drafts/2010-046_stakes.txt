**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER IS AUTHORIZED BY THE REMEDIAL STATUTE GOVERNING PRISON LITIGATION, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), AND DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT (PLRA).**

The PLRA’s remedial provisions expressly authorize district courts to “impose . . . prospective relief” to remedy constitutional violations in prison systems. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). This Court has long recognized that such relief may include population caps when overcrowding is the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions. Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 514 (2011) (citing *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981)). The three-judge court’s order falls squarely within this authority.

The PLRA does not bar such relief. To the contrary, § 3626(a)(1)(A) explicitly permits “prospective relief” that is “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” The population cap here meets these requirements. It is narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found by the court—systemic failures in medical and mental health care directly caused by severe overcrowding. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 514–15. The cap does not exceed what is necessary; it is the least intrusive means because the state has failed to implement less restrictive measures despite years of court supervision. *Id.* at 525–26.

Contrary to petitioners’ argument, the PLRA’s exhaustion requirement, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), does not preclude this relief. The statute permits prospective relief where “the alleged violation is ongoing” and the state has failed to comply with prior remedial orders. *Id.* Here, the state admitted it could not comply with prior injunctive relief, and the violations persisted despite years of compliance efforts. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 515–16. The three-judge court properly found that no less restrictive means would suffice. *Id.* at 526.

**II. THE ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT.**

Petitioners’ Eleventh Amendment immunity claim fails because the state waived its immunity by consenting to the underlying remedial proceedings. *Lapides v. Bd. of Regents of Univ. Sys. of Ga.*, 535 U.S. 613, 620–21 (2002). The state stipulated to the original injunctive orders and did not appeal the appointment of the receiver. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 3585, 3591 (9th Cir. 2009). By participating in these proceedings, the state waived its immunity to prospective relief aimed at remedying constitutional violations. *Id.* at 620.

Moreover, the PLRA expressly abrogates state sovereign immunity for prospective relief under § 3626. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (“The court shall not grant or approve any prospective relief unless . . .”); *see also* *Sossamon v. Texas*, 563 U.S. 277, 286 (2011) (holding that § 3626 abrogates immunity for prospective relief). The three-judge court’s order is precisely the type of prospective relief Congress authorized. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 514–15.

**III. THE ORDER IS NOT BARRED BY THE PLRA’S “TIME LIMITS” PROVISION, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(2).**

Petitioners misread § 3626(b)(2), which imposes time limits on prospective relief only where the relief was granted in the absence of a finding of ongoing violations. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(2) (“In any civil action with respect to prison conditions . . . prospective relief shall not be granted . . . unless . . . the court has found that the relief is narrowly drawn . . . and that such relief is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.”). Here, the three-judge court made explicit findings of ongoing violations and necessity. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 514–15. Thus, § 3626(b)(2) does not apply.

**IV. THE ORDER DOES NOT IMPOSE AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL INJUNCTION UNDER 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1).**

Petitioners’ claim that the order is an unconstitutional injunction under § 1292(a)(1) is meritless. The PLRA’s remedial provisions are constitutional, and the order is narrowly tailored to remedy specific constitutional violations. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 514–15. The order does not exceed the court’s authority under § 3626(a)(1)(A). *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s population reduction order is lawful, constitutional, and fully authorized by the PLRA. The Court should affirm.
