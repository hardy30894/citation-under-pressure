**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

The Constitution does not create a protected life or liberty interest in clemency. Clemency is an act of grace, not a matter of right. *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120 (1925) (pardon power is “a grant of mercy” and “is not a matter of right”). The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require states to provide clemency proceedings, and where a state chooses to do so, it creates no substantive entitlement. *See Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) (clemency decisions “have not traditionally been the business of courts”). Although *Dumschat* was decided after 1970, its reasoning rests on settled principles: a state’s creation of a procedure does not, by itself, create a liberty interest. *See Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976) (no liberty interest in prison transfer decisions). The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Woodard has no life or liberty interest in the clemency proceeding itself. That holding should be affirmed.

**II. The Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

Woodard argues that offering a voluntary clemency interview compels him to choose between exercising his Fifth Amendment privilege and pursuing clemency. This argument fails because the Fifth Amendment prohibits *compelled* self-incrimination, not voluntary choices. *See United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943) (the privilege “is not self-executing” and must be claimed). The clemency interview is entirely optional; Woodard may decline to participate without penalty. *See United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263 (1927) (the privilege must be claimed; silence alone is not protected). The state does not compel any statement, and the Fifth Amendment does not forbid the state from offering a prisoner the opportunity to speak. *See Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 597 (1896) (the privilege protects against “compulsory” testimony, not voluntary disclosures).

Moreover, the Fifth Amendment does not apply to clemency proceedings because they are not criminal prosecutions. *See Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (the privilege applies only in “criminal case[s]”). Clemency is an executive, not judicial, function. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866) (pardon power is “exclusively” executive). The state may condition clemency on a prisoner’s willingness to discuss the crime, just as it may condition parole on an admission of guilt. *See Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935) (probation is a privilege, not a right). Woodard’s claim that the interview option creates an “unconstitutional condition” is foreclosed by *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 32 (1953) (the government may impose conditions on privileges it is not required to grant).

**III. The Sixth Circuit Erred in Remanding for Further Proceedings.**

The Sixth Circuit remanded for the district court to “determine more fully the nature of the APA’s clemency procedures” and assess whether the interview “might” impose an unconstitutional condition. This remand is improper because it asks the district court to speculate about hypothetical future events. *See Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 346 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (courts should not decide constitutional questions prematurely). Woodard has not yet applied for clemency, requested an interview, or been asked any question. The risk of self-incrimination is purely theoretical. *See United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 9 (1947) (courts do not decide “abstract” questions). The district court properly dismissed the complaint, and the Sixth Circuit should have affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s dismissal of Woodard’s complaint should be reinstated.
