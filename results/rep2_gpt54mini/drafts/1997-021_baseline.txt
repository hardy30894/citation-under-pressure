**Argument**

The Board’s polling standard is not rationally related to the National Labor Relations Act and should not be sustained. The Act itself contains no express rule governing employer polls of employees’ union support. As this Court has explained, polling is a Board-created practice, and the Board’s power is therefore one of accommodation, not statutory command. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990); *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978). In this setting, the Board must make a reasoned choice that fits the Act’s central policies of employee free choice and stable bargaining relationships. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 38-41 (1987).

The Board’s current rule does not. Under *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989), the Board permits polling only when the employer already has “reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status—the same showing the Board requires for withdrawal of recognition. That standard makes polling largely redundant. If an employer already has sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal, the poll adds little; if it lacks that evidence, it may not poll. The Board justified this restriction by saying polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling.” *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62. But the Board cannot simultaneously acknowledge that polling may serve the legitimate function of testing employee sentiment and then so restrict it that the practice becomes practically unavailable. The result is arbitrary line-drawing, not reasoned administration of the Act. See *Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB*, 522 U.S. 359, 373-77 (1998).

Courts of appeals have correctly recognized that polling must be available on a lesser showing than withdrawal. The Fifth Circuit held in *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), that an employer may poll employees if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is not enough to justify withdrawal. The Sixth Circuit adopted the same standard in *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867-68 (6th Cir. 1982), and the Ninth Circuit did likewise in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). Those decisions recognize the obvious: a poll is a tool for testing uncertainty, not a reward for certainty already established. The Board’s contrary standard makes polling “marginally useful” at best. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 369.

The Board’s rule is also inconsistent with the Act’s structure. A Board-supervised RM election under § 9(c)(1)(B), 29 U.S.C. § 159(c)(1)(B), exists to resolve doubt through a fair and formal process. See *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. at 778. Yet the Board’s rule makes it easier for an employer to invoke an unsupervised poll than to seek a Board election, because both require the same antecedent showing. That inversion cannot be squared with the Act’s preference for regulated, secret-ballot determinations of majority status. *Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 38-41; *Beth Israel Hosp.*, 437 U.S. at 500-01. If the Board believes employer polling is disfavored, it may regulate the practice, but it may not do so in a way that deprives employers of any meaningful opportunity to confirm employee sentiment. See *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 373-77.

Even if the standard were valid, the Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked an objective reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. See *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The record showed that several employees made antiunion statements; some asked whether the new company would remain unionized, others expressed dissatisfaction with the union, and still others reported that groups of employees opposed representation. The Board discounted much of this evidence by parsing statements too finely and by refusing to credit employee reports of coworkers’ views. But the substantial-evidence standard does not permit the Board to ignore the practical force of unrebutted, contemporaneous evidence bearing on majority support. *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868; *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 377-78.

The Board also erred in treating the statements of employees during hiring interviews as inherently unreliable when the employer had indicated the new operation would be nonunion. In such circumstances, the Board has recognized that expressions of antiunion sentiment may be probative. See *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (5th Cir. 1978). Likewise, employee dissatisfaction with union dues or representation may evidence lack of support for the union itself. See *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989); *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). By disregarding that evidence, the Board substituted speculation for record-based judgment.

At bottom, this is the same case described by the court below in *Thomas Industries*: substantial objective evidence of lost support, a carefully conducted poll, and no substantial evidence that the employer acted without doubt. 687 F.2d at 868. The Board’s contrary conclusion is neither rational nor supported by substantial evidence. The order should be denied enforcement and set aside.
