ARGUMENT

I. The Ten-Year Extension in Section 6502(a) Runs Only to the Taxpayer Against Whom an Assessment Was Actually Made

The Internal Revenue Code permits the government to collect an assessed tax "by levy or by a proceeding in court" if begun "within 10 years after the assessment of the tax." 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But that extended period attaches to "the assessment," and an assessment is defined as "the liability of the taxpayer" recorded by the Secretary. 26 U.S.C. § 6203. Both the Partnership and each individual Debtor are separately defined "person[s]" and "taxpayer[s]" under 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14), each bearing a distinct taxpayer identification number. Nothing in the text collapses these separate taxpayers into one for purposes of § 6502(a).

This Court's decision in United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), confirms that the limitations period governing collection from one person does not automatically extend to a different person merely because the underlying debt is the same. In Updike, the government sought to collect a corporation's unpaid tax from the transferees of the corporation's assets. The Court held that the transferee's own liability, and the period for enforcing it, ran independently of any steps taken against the transferor corporation; the government could not rely on proceedings against the corporation to extend the time within which it could proceed against the transferee. Updike, 281 U.S. 489. The relationship between a partnership and its general partners is closely analogous: the partnership's assessment fixes the partnership's liability and the ten-year period applicable to it, but it does not, without more, fix or extend the period applicable to a legally distinct taxpayer—the individual partner—who was never assessed.

This Court's decision in Phillips v. Commissioner, 283 U.S. 589 (1931), likewise recognizes that summary collection remedies ordinarily available against an assessed taxpayer may be extended to a third party's assets only where Congress has specifically so provided and the third party has been given the statutorily prescribed process directed at that party individually. No comparable provision authorizes the IRS to treat an assessment against a partnership as an assessment against each general partner.

II. Extensions of the Limitations Period Must Be Clear and Are Not to Be Supplied by Implication

This Court has long held that provisions altering the ordinary limitations period for the collection of taxes are to be strictly construed and not expanded beyond their terms. In Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282 (1929), the Court held that a waiver extending the assessment period must be measured by its own terms and cannot be stretched by inference to cover matters the waiver does not address. The same principle governs here: the "10-year" extension in § 6502(a) is triggered by "assessment," and where no assessment was made against Debtors, nothing in the statute supplies the missing step by implication.

The government's own settled position that limitations provisions affecting the sovereign's collection rights receive strict construction, see Bowers v. New York & Albany Lighterage Co., 273 U.S. 346, 349 (1927) ("Statutes of limitation sought to be applied to bar rights of the Government, must receive a strict construction in favor of the Government"), cuts against, not for, the IRS here. If strict construction controls when the government's own collection rights are at stake, the same discipline must apply when the government seeks to enlarge, by inference, the class of persons against whom its favorable extension operates. This Court reaffirmed the general reluctance to expand limitations exceptions beyond their evident purpose in Rothensies v. Electric Storage Battery Co., 329 U.S. 296 (1946), explaining that limitations rules, however arbitrary, exist to bring finality and should not be enlarged by courts sympathetic to one side's convenience. The IRS's proposed rule—that an assessment against an entity silently extends the limitations period against every individual member of that entity—asks this Court to do exactly what Rothensies forbids.

III. The Formal-Assessment Requirement Reflects the Substantive Role Assessment Plays in Enabling Summary Collection

Assessment is not a mere bookkeeping formality. As this Court explained in Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247 (1935), an assessment operates much like a judgment, giving the government the power to proceed directly against the assessed taxpayer's property without further litigation. Because assessment carries such significant consequences—including the extended ten-year collection period—due process and the statutory scheme require that the assessment identify, and thereby give fair warning to, the particular taxpayer against whom those consequences will run. Debtors here were never assessed; they never received the notice that triggers the extended period as to them individually.

IV. State Law Supplies No Substitute for the Missing Assessment or Judgment

Even accepting that California law renders general partners liable for partnership debts, that liability does not translate into an enforceable collection claim absent a judgment against the partner. See Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960) (partner's substantive liability under state law did not establish that the IRS could collect the tax without assessment, judgment, or payment); United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961) (invalidating a lien against a partner's individual property because the assessment identified only the partnership and unnamed, unidentified taxpayers). Because the IRS neither assessed Debtors nor obtained a judgment against them within the applicable period, its claims in bankruptcy are, under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), unenforceable "under applicable law" and were properly disallowed.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
