**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by admonishing the jury to disregard testimony concerning subsequent remedial measures that Morbark itself had invited. Evidence of post-accident design changes to the wood chipper’s infeed chute was properly admitted to impeach Morbark’s misleading implication that the machine remained unchanged and safe. The court’s error in excluding this evidence denied Wood a fair trial, warranting a new trial.

**I. The District Court Erred by Excluding Evidence of Subsequent Remedial Measures Admitted for Impeachment Under Rule 407**

Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence excludes evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered “to prove negligence or culpable conduct,” but it expressly permits such evidence when offered for other purposes, including impeachment. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 407 (enacted 1975); *see also Boeing Airplane Co. v. Brown*, 291 F.2d 476, 478 (9th Cir. 1961) (subsequent remedial measure evidence admissible to rebut a party’s claim that a design was safe). Here, Morbark’s counsel, in opening statement and cross-examination, suggested that the wood chipper had not been altered since the accident—implying it was safe and unchanged. When Wood’s counsel fairly rebutted that implication by eliciting testimony about the lengthened chute, the district court initially permitted it, stating that Morbark “opened the door.” R4-141-56. Yet the court later admonished the jury to disregard the testimony, effectively nullifying Wood’s impeachment. This was reversible error.

Evidence of subsequent repairs is admissible to contradict a witness’s testimony that a product was unchanged or safe. *Christopher v. United States*, 237 F.2d 696, 701 (9th Cir. 1956) (repairs admissible to impeach testimony that a road was safe). Morbark’s implication that the chipper “is exactly the same machine” and still in use by the Army Corps of Engineers directly invited the conclusion that no safety defects existed. *See also Louisville & N.R. Co. v. Williams*, 112 F. 685, 689 (5th Cir. 1901) (subsequent changes admissible to rebut assertion of safety). The district court’s ruling to the contrary contravened this settled principle.

**II. The District Court’s Error Prejudiced Wood’s Case**

The exclusion of the impeachment evidence was not harmless. The central dispute at trial was whether the seventeen-inch infeed chute was unreasonably dangerous. Morbark’s strategy rested on the false premise that the design was unchanged and widely used. *See* Opening Statement: “the evidence will indicate that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered thirty machines *just like the one* that is involved in this case.” R4-141-22 (emphasis added). The later evidence that the chute was lengthened directly undercut that narrative. Without this impeachment, the jury was left with Morbark’s unchallenged implication of safety—a prejudice that cannot be cured by a simple admonition. *See Missouri, K. & T. Ry. Co. v. Elliott*, 184 F. 80, 84 (8th Cir. 1911) (error to exclude evidence that would have rebutted misleading testimony). Wood is entitled to a new trial.

**III. Rule 407 Does Not Apply to Strict Products Liability Claims**

Finally, Rule 407’s limitation to “negligence or culpable conduct” does not extend to strict products liability. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 407; *cf. Hays v. City of Seattle*, 9 Wash. 2d 530, 534 (1941) (subsequent remedial measures admissible in strict liability to show defect). This Court should adopt that view, but the district court’s error is independently reversible because the evidence was admissible for impeachment.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
