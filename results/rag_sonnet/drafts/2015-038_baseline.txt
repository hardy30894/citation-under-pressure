# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE NAVIGABLE WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS

Petitioner's argument rests on a fundamental misreading of ANILCA § 103(c), which provides that "[o]nly those lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are public lands . . . shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This provision addresses which *lands* are deemed part of a conservation system unit for certain purposes; it does not purport to divest the National Park Service of its independent regulatory authority over activities—including navigation—that occur within park boundaries, regardless of who holds title to the submerged lands beneath navigable waters.

This Court has long recognized that the federal government retains substantial regulatory authority over activities occurring on or affecting navigable waters, even where the underlying submerged lands belong to a State. As Justice Scalia observed in the analogous context of Glacier Bay, "the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters . . . by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75, [Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part]. This principle applies with full force here. The transfer of title to submerged lands under the Statehood Act's equal-footing doctrine does not, by itself, extinguish the federal government's authority to regulate navigation and other activities on those waters for purposes consistent with the management of adjacent federal conservation lands.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized this distinction, holding that "NPS's hovercraft ban is not a regulation that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska." The regulation is not a land-use regulation directed at ownership of the riverbed; it is a regulation of *conduct*—the operation of hovercraft—that Congress specifically authorized NPS to promulgate to protect the values of conservation system units as a whole, including the waters that flow through them.

## II. ANILCA'S STRUCTURE AND PURPOSE CONFIRM THAT CONGRESS DID NOT INTEND TO CARVE OUT STATE-OWNED WATERS FROM NPS'S REGULATORY REACH

This Court's decision in *Amoco Production Co. v. Village of Gambell*, 480 U.S. 531 (1987), confirms that ANILCA's "primary purpose was to complete the allocation of federal lands in the State of Alaska," providing "for additions to the National Park System, National Wildlife Refuge System" and other conservation designations "within the State of Alaska." *Id.* at 531. Nothing in this purpose suggests that Congress intended to create regulatory checkerboards within park boundaries, where NPS could regulate hunting, camping, and other activities on adjacent federal lands but would be powerless to regulate the very rivers that hunters like Petitioner use to access those lands.

Such a reading would "thwart the obvious purpose of the statute." *Amoco Production Co.*, 480 U.S. at 531 (quoting *Griffin v. Oceanic Contractors, Inc.*, 458 U.S. 564, 571 (1982)). Congress created conservation system units as integrated ecological and recreational areas; it did not intend for the Park Service's authority to evaporate every time a river happened to cross state-owned submerged lands within otherwise federally managed preserve boundaries.

This Court's decision in *Organized Village of Kake v. Egan*, 369 U.S. 60 (1962), further supports the conclusion that federal statutory schemes affecting Alaska need not be read to divest federal regulatory authority absent clear congressional intent to do so. There, the Court recognized that "'absolute' federal jurisdiction is not invariably exclusive jurisdiction," and that federal reservations of authority "ha[ve] nothing to do with" state regulatory displacement absent specific statutory text so providing. *Id.* at 60. Section 103(c) contains no such clear statement displacing NPS's traditional authority to regulate conduct affecting park resources merely because the underlying waters are state-owned.

## III. NPS RETAINS INDEPENDENT STATUTORY AUTHORITY TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES WITHIN PARK BOUNDARIES UNDER ITS ORGANIC ACT AND ANILCA'S SAVINGS CLAUSE

Even apart from § 103(c), ANILCA's savings clause preserves NPS's pre-existing regulatory authority. Section 1319 provides that "[n]othing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions." 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The hovercraft regulation at issue, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), was promulgated under NPS's longstanding Organic Act authority to regulate all activities within park units for the protection of park resources and values.

This Court's decision in *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228 (1925), recognized the breadth of NPS's regulatory authority even as against state sovereignty concerns, addressing federal regulations governing use of park roads and facilities notwithstanding state objections to federal control. That NPS regulations may incidentally affect activities on state-owned submerged lands within park boundaries does not exceed the scope of authority this Court has long recognized the Service possesses.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals affirming summary judgment for Respondent should be affirmed.
