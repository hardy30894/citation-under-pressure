The school district's exclusion of the Good News Club from its limited public forum did not abridge free speech. A state may restrict the use of its property to the purposes for which it was dedicated without violating the First Amendment. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford opened its facilities only for social, civic, and recreational meetings, and the record establishes that the Club's proposed activities constituted religious instruction and prayer rather than discussion of secular subjects from a religious viewpoint.

This distinction is one of subject matter, not viewpoint. The First Amendment does not require government to subsidize or facilitate religious exercises on public property. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947); *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948). When a school permits access for moral instruction by secular groups such as the Boy Scouts, it need not also open its doors to sectarian worship or proselytizing. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952).

The district's policy advances the Establishment Clause command that public schools remain neutral in matters of religion. *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 431-32 (1962); *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963). Allowing the Club's meetings would have placed the imprimatur of the state on religious indoctrination of young children during school hours and on school grounds. *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 106 (1968).

Nor does the exclusion constitute impermissible content discrimination. Speech rights are not absolute; they may be regulated when the regulation is narrowly drawn to serve a compelling interest in avoiding an establishment of religion. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 638 (1943). The district's Community Use Policy is viewpoint neutral on its face and as applied, barring all religious worship while permitting secular moral instruction.

Finally, the Club's activities fall outside the protection afforded pure speech in a nonpublic forum. *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 513 (1969). Because Milford's concern for Establishment Clause compliance supplied an adequate justification independent of any speech restriction, the judgment below should be affirmed.
