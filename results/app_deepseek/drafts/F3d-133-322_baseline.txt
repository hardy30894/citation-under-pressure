**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Indictment Was Deficient Because Mrs. Parker Lacked the Authority to Grant or Deny Benefits, and Thus Her Conduct Did Not Constitute an “Official Act” Under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).**

The indictment charged Mrs. Parker with public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), which prohibits inducing a public official to commit an act in violation of her official duty. However, Mrs. Parker’s duties as a clerk to Administrative Law Judge Aronson were strictly ministerial—assisting during hearings, recording notes, and typing and mailing decisions. She had no authority to grant or deny Supplemental Security Income benefits, nor was she authorized to sign Judge Aronson’s name. Because she lacked such authority, her actions in forging letters and approving benefits fell entirely outside the scope of any “official act” as defined by statute.

The term “official act” is defined broadly under 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3) as “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity.” But this definition presupposes that the act is one the official is empowered to perform. Congress intended the term to “include any decision or action taken by a public official in his capacity as such.” S. Rep. No. 87-2213, at 3856 (1962). An act taken outside that capacity—such as a clerk forging a judge’s signature—is not an “official act” because it is not performed in the official’s role.

This Court has recognized that “official acts” are not limited to those within an official’s specific authority. *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983). But *Gjieli* involved a public official who had *some* authority over the matter at issue. Here, Mrs. Parker had *no* authority to grant or deny benefits. Her conduct was not a misuse of delegated power but a wholly unauthorized act. The Supreme Court has held that duties not completely defined by written rules may be established by “settled practice,” *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914), but no settled practice authorized Mrs. Parker to approve benefits. Her actions were criminal forgeries, not bribery.

Because the indictment failed to allege an “official act,” it was deficient as a matter of law. This Court should reverse Mrs. Parker’s conviction.

**II. Mr. Parker’s Conviction Must Be Reversed Because the Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove He Knowingly Participated in a Conspiracy to Commit Public Bribery.**

Mr. Parker was convicted of conspiracy to commit public bribery and three counts of public bribery. The government’s evidence showed only that Mr. Parker accompanied his wife to Simmons’ home and accepted $500. There was no evidence that Mr. Parker knew the money was a bribe for Mrs. Parker’s unauthorized acts. Mere presence at the scene of a crime, without more, is insufficient to prove knowing participation in a conspiracy. *United States v. Gonzalez-Maldonado*, 115 F.3d 9, 15 (1st Cir. 1997). The government failed to introduce any recorded conversations, statements, or conduct showing Mr. Parker’s intent to further the alleged bribery scheme. His conviction must be reversed.
