**ARGUMENT**

The District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel’s religious film series from school facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. By opening its property to a broad array of community groups, the District created a public forum from which it may not exclude speech based on its religious viewpoint.

**I. The District Created a Public Forum, and Once It Did So, It Could Not Exclude Speech Based on Religious Content.**

The District’s policy and practice allowed a wide range of secular organizations—including the Southern Harmonizers, a lecture series on psychology and the unknown, and other civic and cultural groups—to use school premises for expressive activities. This affirmative opening of the forum to the public for discussion of diverse topics transformed the property into a public forum. When the government opens its property for expressive activities, it may not exclude speech based on its content or viewpoint. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-304 (1940) (holding that the Free Exercise Clause “embraces two concepts—freedom to believe and freedom to act”). The District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s application solely because the films were “church related” is a classic content-based restriction, forbidden under the First Amendment.

**II. Religious Speech Is Fully Protected Within a Public Forum.**

The First Amendment’s protection of free speech extends to religious expression. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-304 (1940) (holding that the Free Exercise Clause protects both belief and action). In *Martin v. City of Struthers*, 319 U.S. 141, 143 (1943), this Court recognized that the “vital freedom” of speech includes the right to distribute religious literature. The District cannot treat religious speech as a disfavored subject. *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 293-294 (1951) (striking down an ordinance that “gives an administrative official arbitrary power to grant or refuse permits” to speak on religious topics). Because the District permitted other non-religious organizations to discuss family values and child rearing, excluding Lamb’s Chapel solely because of the religious perspective of its films is constitutionally impermissible.

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral; It Discriminates Against Religion.**

The District’s rule barring any group from using facilities for religious purposes does not merely exclude speech that endorses a particular religion; it excludes all religious expression. This constitutes viewpoint discrimination. *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (invalidating a permit system that was administered to deny religious groups the use of a public park while secular groups were permitted). The District’s absolute ban on religious use is not a reasonable time, place, or manner regulation; it is a content-based exclusion that cannot be justified by a mere desire to avoid controversy. *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88 (1949) (plurality opinion) (upholding a content-neutral noise regulation but recognizing that speech may not be suppressed because of its message). The District cannot claim that its policy is viewpoint-neutral when it has permitted secular discussion of family issues but denied the same discussion from a religious perspective.

**IV. The District’s Justification—Avoiding Establishment Clause Violations—Does Not Justify Censorship.**

The District’s argument that permitting religious use would violate the Establishment Clause is unavailing. The government may not suppress religious speech in a public forum on the ground that it might be perceived as endorsing religion. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953) (holding that a city could not prohibit religious meetings in a public park while permitting secular meetings, as such discrimination is not the kind of regulation that the First Amendment permits). *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506 (1946) (explaining that when an owner opens property for use by the public, the owner’s rights become circumscribed by the constitutional rights of those who use it). Here, the District opened its facilities to the public, and the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech in a limited public forum.

**V. Conclusion**

This Court has long held that the right of free speech may not be abridged on the ground that the speech is religious. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-304 (1940). The District’s policy—denying access to a public forum solely because of the religious viewpoint of the speech—violates the First Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
