**Argument**

The Patent Act does not permit awarding an infringer’s entire profits when the design patent covers only a component of a larger product. By its terms, the statute authorizes recovery of “the total profit” only on “the article of manufacture to which the patented design or colorable imitation has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. That language limits disgorgement to the relevant “article of manufacture,” not to downstream profits from a multifaceted product that merely incorporates the patented design. See **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444-45 (1885)** (rejecting recovery measured by the entire profit from the finished carpet where the patented design was only one part of the article, and requiring apportionment to the patented contribution); **Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10, 17-18 (1886)** (same, holding that damages must reflect the value added by the patented design, not the whole product’s gains).

This Court has long recognized that damages may not be based on value unrelated to the patented feature. In **Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884)**, the Court held that a patentee “must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion” the profits attributable to the patented feature from those attributable to unpatented features, unless the entire market value of the product is shown to rest on the patent. The rule is grounded in fairness and causation: a patentee “must give evidence” of the patented feature’s contribution because a defendant is not liable for gains caused by other components or by the product as a whole. Id.; see also **Sessions v. Romadka, 145 U.S. 29, 50-51 (1892)** (requiring proof connecting the award to the patented improvement rather than the defendant’s total profits from the whole article).

Samsung’s position follows directly from those cases. A design patent covering a phone component does not automatically entitle the patentee to the infringer’s profits from the entire smartphone. The profits award must be limited to the article embodying the patented design, or at least to the portion of profits attributable to that design. **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. at 444-45**; **Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121**. Otherwise, the award would convert a limited design monopoly into a windfall on unrelated technological, functional, and marketing value. The Court has rejected such overbroad recoveries since the nineteenth century. See **Crosby Steam Gage & Valve Co. v. Consolidated Safety-Valve Co., 141 U.S. 441, 444-46 (1891)** (damages may not rest on conjecture and must be tied to the patented improvement’s contribution).

Apple may respond that the statute speaks of “total profit,” but those words do not erase the statutory qualifier “to which the patented design ... has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. The profit must be total only for the relevant article of manufacture; it is not total for every finished product sold downstream. That reading harmonizes the text with the Court’s long-standing apportionment rule. **Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121**; **Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. at 17-18**. If Congress had intended to award profits on an entire multicomponent product whenever any patented design appeared somewhere within it, it would not have conditioned recovery on the particular “article of manufacture” bearing the design.

Nor is there any basis to assume that the patentee may elect the most profitable level of abstraction. The Court has repeatedly refused to award profits from unpatented matter simply because it is sold in combination with a patented feature. **Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121**; **Sessions, 145 U.S. at 50-51**. The same principle applies here. A phone is not one indivisible article for purposes of profit disgorgement merely because it is sold as a single retail unit. If the patented design is only one component, the law requires separation of the value attributable to that component from the value attributable to the remainder. **Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. at 444-45**.

The contrary rule would produce arbitrary and excessive awards untethered to the patented design’s actual contribution. That is precisely what the Court condemned in **Garretson**, where the patentee could not recover on “the entire value of the article” absent proof that the whole value was due to the patent. 111 U.S. at 121. The same limitation should govern here. Because Samsung’s accused smartphones included many unpatented features, Apple was required to show what portion of profits was attributable to the patented design component. It could not simply seize the entire profit from the finished device. **Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. at 17-18**; **Crosby Steam Gage, 141 U.S. at 444-46**.

The judgment should therefore be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to limit any design-patent profits award to the profits attributable to the patented component, not the infringer’s entire profits from the finished product.
