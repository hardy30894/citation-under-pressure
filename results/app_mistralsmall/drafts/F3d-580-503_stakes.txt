**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Appellant’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and Motion to Continue**

The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of a continuance for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Williams*, 814 F.3d 473, 480 (7th Cir. 2016). A continuance is warranted where a defendant demonstrates “(1) due diligence in preparing the case for trial, (2) the need for the continuance, (3) the harm that would result from denial, and (4) the likelihood that the continuance would alleviate the harm.” *Id.* (quoting *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.3d 657, 661 (7th Cir. 2007)). The same standard applies to a request for expert assistance. *United States v. Gatto*, 995 F.3d 623, 630 (7th Cir. 2021).

Here, the district court erred in denying Appellant’s motion for a fingerprint expert and motion to continue. Appellant demonstrated due diligence by filing his motion six days after the pretrial conference, well in advance of trial. The need for the continuance was clear: Appellant sought expert testimony to rebut the government’s fingerprint evidence, which was central to its case. The harm from denial was substantial—Appellant was deprived of the opportunity to challenge the government’s fingerprint analysis, a critical piece of evidence linking him to the recovered narcotics and firearm. Finally, a continuance would have alleviated this harm by allowing Appellant to retain and consult with a fingerprint expert.

The district court’s cursory denial—“woefully late”—was an abuse of discretion. The Seventh Circuit has held that late requests for expert assistance are not per se untimely if the defendant acted with due diligence and the request is not made in bad faith. *Gatto*, 995 F.3d at 631. Appellant’s motion was timely under these standards, and the district court’s denial deprived him of his constitutional right to a fair trial. *See United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 655-56 (1984) (holding that denial of meaningful opportunity to present a defense violates the Sixth Amendment).

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Appellant’s Conviction for Possession of a Firearm in Furtherance of a Drug Trafficking Crime**

The Seventh Circuit reviews sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenges de novo, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government and drawing all reasonable inferences in its favor. *United States v. Johnson*, 515 F.3d 731, 735 (7th Cir. 2008). To sustain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c), the government must prove that the defendant “actively employed” the firearm in relation to the drug trafficking crime. *Smith v. United States*, 508 U.S. 223, 238 (1993); *United States v. Taylor*, 569 F.3d 790, 795 (7th Cir. 2009).

Here, the evidence was insufficient to support Appellant’s conviction under § 924(c). The government’s theory—that Appellant possessed the firearm in furtherance of drug trafficking—rested on the mere proximity of the firearm to the narcotics. However, the Seventh Circuit has repeatedly held that proximity alone is insufficient to establish “use” or “possession in furtherance” under § 924(c). *United States v. Carter*, 970 F.3d 675, 682 (7th Cir. 2020) (citing *Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137, 143-44 (1995)). The government presented no evidence that Appellant actively employed the firearm in relation to the drug trafficking offense. The firearm was recovered separately from the narcotics, and there was no evidence that Appellant brandished or referenced the firearm during the drug transaction. Accordingly, the conviction under § 924(c) must be reversed.

**III. The District Court Erred in Applying a Two-Level Enhancement for Possession of a Firearm in Connection with Another Felony Offense**

The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court’s sentencing decisions for abuse of discretion, giving due deference to the district court’s factual findings. *United States v. Figueroa*, 622 F.3d 734, 738 (7th Cir. 2010). The district court may impose a two-level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) if the defendant “used or possessed any firearm ... in connection with another felony offense.” The government bears the burden of proving this enhancement by a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.*

Here, the district court erred in applying the enhancement. The government did not prove that Appellant “used” the firearm in connection with another felony offense. As discussed above, the evidence showed only that Appellant possessed the firearm in proximity to narcotics. The Seventh Circuit has held that mere possession of a firearm in the same location as narcotics is insufficient to trigger the enhancement. *United States v. Hargrove*, 41 F.3d 59, 62 (7th Cir. 1994). The district court’s application of the enhancement was therefore an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**
