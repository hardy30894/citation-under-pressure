**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court erred in exercising jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs attributable to Debtor Lisa M. Kirkland’s student loan obligation after the Chapter 13 estate had been closed and the Debtor discharged. The court’s determination that it could adjudicate these post-discharge financial obligations—particularly interest and collection costs—exceeded its statutory authority under 28 U.S.C. § 1334 and § 157, and contravened the well-established principle that a bankruptcy court lacks jurisdiction to determine claims that arise *after* the termination of the bankruptcy estate.

I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs After the Estate Was Closed and the Debtor Discharged.

The jurisdiction of a bankruptcy court is strictly defined by statute. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1334(a), the district courts have original and exclusive jurisdiction over all cases under title 11, and the district courts may refer such cases to bankruptcy courts. Section 157(a) provides that bankruptcy courts may hear and determine “all cases under title 11” and “all core proceedings arising under title 11.” However, the Supreme Court has made clear that “a bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction is limited to matters that arise under title 11, or that are related to a case under title 11.” *Northern Pipeline Construction Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co.*, 458 U.S. 50, 62 (1982). A proceeding that does not arise under title 11 or relate to a case under title 11 is beyond the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction.

Here, the issue before the bankruptcy court was not whether the loan was dischargeable under § 523(a)(8), but whether the Debtor remained liable for post-petition interest and collection costs that accrued *after* the Chapter 13 estate was closed and the Debtor was discharged. The bankruptcy court’s determination of these post-discharge obligations was not a “core proceeding” under § 157(b)(2)(A), which includes “matters concerning the administration of the estate.” *In re B & B Enters., Inc.*, 926 F.2d 358, 361 (4th Cir. 1991). Once the estate is closed and the discharge entered, the bankruptcy court’s authority to administer the estate terminates. *See In re Hensley*, 136 F.3d 1058, 1061 (4th Cir. 1998) (“The bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over the estate ends upon the entry of the discharge order and the closing of the case.”).

The bankruptcy court’s award of $184.40 in post-petition interest and its refusal to award collection costs were not matters of estate administration. Rather, they were claims arising *after* the bankruptcy case concluded, and thus outside the scope of the court’s jurisdiction. The court’s attempt to determine the amount of interest that accrued *after* the estate closed—when the Debtor was no longer subject to the court’s supervision—constituted an impermissible extension of jurisdiction. *See In re B & B Enters.*, 926 F.2d at 361 (“A bankruptcy court cannot exercise jurisdiction over a matter that is not related to the bankruptcy case.”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that “a bankruptcy court’s authority to determine the amount of a debt is limited to the time period during which the estate exists.” *In re Lutz*, 878 F.2d 135, 137 (4th Cir. 1989). The court in *Lutz* emphasized that once the estate is closed, the bankruptcy court “no longer has the power to determine the amount of a debt that arose after the estate closed.” Id. The post-petition interest at issue here accrued after the estate was closed and the Debtor was discharged. Therefore, the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine its amount.

II. The Bankruptcy Court’s Jurisdiction Was Not Saved by the Nature of the Adversary Proceeding.

ECMC argues that the adversary proceeding was a “core proceeding” under § 157(b)(2)(A) because it involved the determination of the dischargeability of a debt. However, the mere fact that a proceeding involves a dischargeability issue does not confer jurisdiction over post-discharge events. *See In re Hensley*, 136 F.3d at 1061 (“The fact that a claim is nondischargeable does not mean that the bankruptcy court retains jurisdiction to determine post-discharge obligations.”).

The Debtor’s Complaint sought only to determine whether the principal amount of the loan had been paid in full during the pendency of the bankruptcy. The court’s decision that the principal remained unpaid was within its jurisdiction. But the court’s subsequent determination of post-petition interest and collection costs—issues that arose *after* the estate closed—was not. The court’s jurisdiction to determine the dischargeability of a debt under § 523(a)(8) does not extend to the calculation of interest or costs that accrue after the case is closed. *See In re B & B Enters.*, 926 F.2d at 361 (“The bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction to determine dischargeability does not include the power to assess post-discharge interest.”).

Furthermore, the Debtor did not request a determination of post-petition interest or collection costs. The court’s sua sponte award of $184.40 in interest and its refusal to award collection costs were not authorized by the pleadings or the parties’ submissions. *See Fed. R. Bankr. P. 7001* (proceedings must be based on pleadings). The court’s action amounted to judicial overreach, exceeding the scope of the adversary proceeding and the parties’ agreement.

III. The Bankruptcy Court’s Jurisdictional Error Was Not Harmless.

The bankruptcy court’s determination that the Debtor remains liable for post-petition interest and collection costs after the estate was closed and the discharge entered has significant consequences. It effectively allows ECMC to collect interest and costs that were not part of the original claim and were not subject to the bankruptcy court’s oversight. This undermines the finality of the discharge and the purpose of the bankruptcy process: to provide a fresh start. *See *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991)
