The National Labor Relations Board's requirement that an employer must possess objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition before it may poll employees is inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. In Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 104 (1954), the Court recognized that the Act balances stability of bargaining relationships against employee freedom of choice. Equating the evidentiary threshold for a poll with that for outright withdrawal renders polling a nullity and frustrates the statutory policy favoring accurate ascertainment of employee sentiment.

The Board's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951), requires that a reviewing court examine the entire record, including the body of evidence opposed to the Board's view. Multiple employees expressed to management that they no longer desired union representation, that they received no value from their dues, or that the union would lose any vote. These statements, together with the steward's assessment that the employees did not want the union, constitute objective indications that the union had lost majority support.

In NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 600 (1969), the Court acknowledged an employer's legitimate interest in testing employee sentiment when it has not created a coercive atmosphere. The record contains no suggestion of coercion or other unfair labor practices by Allentown Mack. The Board's refusal to credit the statements as raising a reasonable doubt therefore rests on an impermissible substitution of its own judgment for the uncontradicted evidence of employee disaffection.

Because the poll was conducted under procedural safeguards and revealed that a majority of employees opposed the union, Allentown Mack was entitled to rely on the results and to refuse further recognition. The Board's contrary order should be set aside.
