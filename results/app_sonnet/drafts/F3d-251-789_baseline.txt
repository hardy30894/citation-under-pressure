# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court's factual findings under the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, incorporated into United States law as the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601-11610, for clear error, but reviews de novo whether those facts satisfy the legal standard for the consent defense. See Mozes v. Mozes, 239 F.3d 1067, 1073 (9th Cir. 2001) (reviewing mixed questions of law and fact arising under the Convention with deference to subsidiary factual findings but independent scrutiny of the ultimate legal conclusion). Because the district court's ultimate finding of "consent" rests on a misapplication of the narrow legal standard that governs that defense, reversal is warranted even though many of the subsidiary facts are undisputed.

## II. The District Court Erred in Finding That Gonzalez-Caballero Consented to Danelsy's Permanent Removal and Retention in the United States

### A. The Convention's exceptions to return are narrowly construed, and Mena bore the burden of proving consent.

The Hague Convention's core purpose is to secure the prompt return of children wrongfully removed or retained, restoring the status quo that existed before the wrongful act and deterring parents from crossing international borders in search of a more favorable forum for custody litigation. Friedrich v. Friedrich, 78 F.3d 1060, 1064 (6th Cir. 1996). Exceptions to the return remedy—including consent under Article 13(a)—are to be construed narrowly, lest the exception swallow the rule. Id. at 1067. Congress placed the burden of proving such an exception squarely on the party opposing return: the respondent must establish consent by a preponderance of the evidence. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B). Mena, not Gonzalez-Caballero, bore that burden below.

### B. Consent must reflect the petitioner's actual, subjective agreement to the specific removal or retention that occurred—not a vague or conditional arrangement.

The consent inquiry is not satisfied by any general willingness on a parent's part to allow a child to travel; it requires the fact-finder to determine what the petitioning parent "actually contemplated and agreed to" with respect to the child's relocation. Mozes, 239 F.3d at 1075 (framing the analogous habitual-residence inquiry around the parties' actual, shared intent rather than one party's unilateral characterization of events). A parent's ambiguous or conditional statements cannot support a finding of the unequivocal consent necessary to defeat the Convention's return remedy. In re Prevot, 59 F.3d 556, 562 (6th Cir. 1995) (examining whether the petitioning parent's conduct was truly inconsistent with a continuing claim to custody before treating it as consent or acquiescence).

### C. The record establishes, at most, a conditional consent that Mena did not fulfill—not the unconditional consent to permanent retention that the district court found.

Even accepting Mena's version of events, Gonzalez-Caballero's agreement was expressly conditioned on his assistance in securing her own lawful immigration to the United States so that she could reassume custody of Danelsy. Mena himself testified that he told Gonzalez-Caballero he "would try to help her legally immigrate to the United States" if she made preliminary arrangements through her sister. That is not the language of unconditional relinquishment; it is the language of a bargain—one in which Gonzalez-Caballero's continued separation from her daughter was expressly tied to Mena's reciprocal performance. Where consent is conditional, it does not ripen into the kind of unequivocal agreement to a foreign, permanent retention that Article 13(a) demands. See Friedrich, 78 F.3d at 1070 (requiring that the acts relied upon to show consent or acquiescence be unambiguous and inconsistent with a continuing assertion of custodial rights).

The district court's finding ignored this conditional structure entirely, treating Gonzalez-Caballero's agreement to a temporary, purpose-bound arrangement as if it were an open-ended surrender of custody. That was clear error. A parent's consent to a specific, bounded arrangement—here, temporary retention pending an immigration process that Mena controlled and never completed—cannot be transmuted into consent to indefinite retention simply because the other parent later declined to hold up his end of the bargain.

### D. Gonzalez-Caballero's own conduct immediately after Danelsy's departure is flatly inconsistent with the unconditional consent the district court found.

Consent analysis must account for the petitioning parent's conduct following the child's departure. Prevot, 59 F.3d at 562. Here, Gonzalez-Caballero's conduct was the opposite of acquiescence. Within a day of Mena and Danelsy's departure, when Mena failed to call as promised, she went to Panamanian child-custody authorities and the police. Days later she again sought help from local police, the Judge Advocate General Corps liaison, and the Institute for Children. By mid-November she had escalated her efforts to the office of the Panamanian President, Panamanian radio stations, and the Children's Commission—ultimately securing government assistance and an airline ticket to travel to the United States herself to recover her daughter. This sustained, multi-agency campaign to recover Danelsy—commenced almost immediately and continuing for months—is precisely the sort of conduct that negates any inference of unconditional consent to permanent retention. See Friedrich, 78 F.3d at 1070.

## III. Conclusion

Because the district court misapplied the narrow legal standard governing the consent defense and because the undisputed record shows, at most, a conditional consent that Mena failed to satisfy, this Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to order Danelsy's return to Panama.
