# ARGUMENT

## I. Because Lamb's Chapel Sought to Use School Property for Constitutionally Protected Expression, the District's Exclusion Based on the Religious Content of That Expression Violates the First Amendment.

Government property need not be a street or park to trigger First Amendment scrutiny of content-based exclusions. Once a governmental body opens its property to expressive use by outside groups, it may not selectively bar categories of speech because of the ideas they convey. This principle traces to this Court's recognition that public places entrusted for communicative use—whether the traditional forum of the street or a facility a government chooses to make available—must remain open on equal terms. See *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions"). The Center Moriches School District, by regularly permitting outside organizations—civic groups, garden clubs, and cultural performers—to use its auditorium after hours, created precisely such a forum for community expression. Having done so, it could not then draw the line at religious speech alone.

That the expression here took the form of a film series does not diminish its constitutional protection. This Court held decades ago that motion pictures are entitled to the same First Amendment protection as the printed or spoken word, recognizing their importance as a medium through which ideas are conveyed to the public. *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). The District cannot avoid its constitutional obligations by treating a film about family values differently than it would treat a lecture or a live presentation on the identical subject, merely because the film happened to be produced from a religious perspective. See *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (recognizing that the liberties secured by the First Amendment protect religious expression from discriminatory suppression by the State). Suppressing speech simply because the ideas it contains are unpopular or unorthodox with those in authority is antithetical to the constitutional design; government may not single out religious expression for disfavored treatment while tolerating comparable secular expression. See *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 108–09 (1943) (holding that a State may not impose burdens on the dissemination of religious ideas that it does not impose on other forms of expression). The District's effort to silence Lamb's Chapel because its message on child-rearing was religiously informed strikes at the very core of that principle.

## II. Excluding Religious Speech While Permitting Comparable Secular Speech Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly condemned government action denying access to public facilities specifically because the excluded speaker's message was religious, while similar secular uses were tolerated. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court struck down the denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses where other groups, religious and secular alike, had been permitted to use the same park, and the denial rested on disagreement with the group's religious views rather than any neutral, generally applicable standard. The Court reached the same result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), invalidating the discriminatory application of a park ordinance against a Jehovah's Witness religious meeting where other groups conducting similar gatherings were left undisturbed. This Court has likewise made clear that a licensing scheme vesting officials with unguided discretion to approve or deny access to public facilities, exercised in a manner that discriminates against disfavored speakers, cannot stand. See *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 560–62 (1948) (invalidating an ordinance conferring unregulated discretion on local officials to permit or forbid the use of sound amplification equipment).

The record confirms the same infirmity here. The District permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform, allowed a library-sponsored lecture series touching on topics the lecturer himself described as spanning parapsychology and metaphysics, and opened its facilities to numerous civic organizations—yet drew the line categorically at anything the District's own clerk labeled religious. Indeed, the correspondence in the record shows that the District refused Lamb's Chapel's application for the film series with the explanation that it "does appear to be church related and therefore your request must be refused." That admitted rationale is a plain instance of the viewpoint discrimination condemned by *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Saia*.

## III. Public School Property Is Not an Enclave Where Officials May Suppress Disfavored Viewpoints.

This Court has long insisted that publicly funded educational institutions remain subject to the constraints of the First Amendment when they regulate expression on their premises. This Court has explained that "it can hardly be argued that either students or teachers shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate," and that "State-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism." *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). If those who study and teach within the schoolhouse retain their expressive rights while on school property, the District cannot claim unfettered license to exclude outside speakers from a forum it has otherwise opened to the public, simply because their message is religious. Nor does the associational and expressive character of the group seeking access lose protection simply because it is a religious congregation rather than a civic club; the right to advocate one's views collectively, and to associate for that purpose, is itself protected activity. See *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 460 (1958) (recognizing that "effective advocacy of both public and private points of view, particularly controversial ones, is undeniably enhanced by group association").

## CONCLUSION

The District's blanket exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Hague*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Saia*, *Burstyn*, *Murdock*, or *Tinker*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
