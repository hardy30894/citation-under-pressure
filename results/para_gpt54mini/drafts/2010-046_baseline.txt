### Argument

This Court should affirm because the district court’s remedial population cap is authorized by the Prison Litigation Reform Act and is consistent with longstanding equitable power to remedy constitutional violations in prison systems. The PLRA permits prospective relief that is “narrowly drawn,” extends “no further than necessary to correct the violation,” and is “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). Where prison conditions violate the Eighth Amendment, federal courts may order population reduction if “crowding is the primary cause of the violation” and “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). The district court’s order fits that standard.

This Court and the Supreme Court have repeatedly recognized that overcrowding can produce unconstitutional prison conditions requiring structural relief. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (“the length of time prisoners must spend in overcrowded conditions is a relevant factor in determining whether they are being subjected to cruel and unusual punishment”); Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347-50 (1981) (explaining that double-celling is not per se unconstitutional, but overcrowding may violate the Eighth Amendment when it causes deprivation of basic human needs); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12 (2011) (affirming population reduction order where overcrowding was the “primary cause” of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations). The district court made the necessary findings after extensive proceedings: California’s overcrowding was the primary cause of inadequate medical and mental health care, and no effective remedy short of a reduction in population would cure the violations. That finding is entitled to deference. See Brown, 563 U.S. at 526-27.

California’s principal contention—that the order is barred because it requires release of prisoners—is foreclosed by the PLRA’s text and by Brown. The statute expressly contemplates prisoner-release orders when the court has first entered less intrusive relief and convened a three-judge court. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A); see Brown, 563 U.S. at 501-03. The statute does not prohibit release orders; it regulates them. Brown held that the three-judge court may order population reduction when overcrowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violation and no other relief will remedy it. 563 U.S. at 511-12. That is precisely what occurred here.

Nor does the order exceed the PLRA because it is “too broad.” The Supreme Court has instructed that “courts must be mindful of the principles of comity” but may not “deny relief simply because prison administrators have too many prisoners.” Brown, 563 U.S. at 511, 531. A court need not wait until a system has completely collapsed before acting. Helling v. McKinney, 509 U.S. 25, 33 (1993); Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. at 531-32. The district court’s carefully calibrated cap at 137.5 percent of design capacity was the product of a record showing persistent Eighth Amendment violations and repeated failure of narrower remedies.

California’s reliance on federalism principles also fails. The PLRA embodies Congress’s balancing of federalism and constitutional enforcement, and it does not insulate a state from effective relief when it is unable or unwilling to cure unconstitutional conditions. See Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347-48 (2000) (recognizing the PLRA’s limits on relief while confirming Congress preserved courts’ authority to issue prospective remedies meeting the statute’s standards). Once the statutory findings are made, a population order is lawful even though it has serious practical consequences. Brown, 563 U.S. at 511-12, 541-42.

Finally, California’s suggestion that the order intrudes on the state’s criminal justice authority ignores the settled principle that “federal courts may not ignore constitutional violations in prison because the remedy is difficult.” Brown, 563 U.S. at 511. The Constitution controls when imprisonment conditions inflict cruel and unusual punishment. The district court found such a violation on a massive scale, and its order was an appropriate and lawful remedy under the PLRA. See Brown, 563 U.S. at 527-31; Hutto, 437 U.S. at 687-88.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
