**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court's Population Order Satisfies the PLRA Because No Remedy Short of a Population Reduction Could Cure the Ongoing Eighth Amendment Violation**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act requires that prospective relief in prison-conditions litigation be "narrowly drawn," extend "no further than necessary to correct the violation," and be "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). Where a population limit is sought, the PLRA further requires a finding that "crowding is the primary cause of the violation" and that "no other relief will remedy the violation." § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court made precisely these findings after presiding over this litigation for nearly a decade, and its findings are entitled to deference.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy sustained constitutional violations in institutional settings when less intrusive measures have failed. In *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), this Court upheld a district court's order limiting the duration of confinement in punitive isolation, explaining that "the ongoing history of litigation" justified a remedy "more stringent" than might otherwise be necessary, because "a court has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past." *Id.* at 687 (internal quotation marks omitted). The record here reflects an even longer history of failed remedial efforts. As the Ninth Circuit's opinion below recounts, the district court entered stipulated remedial orders in 2002 and 2004, found after "six days of evidentiary hearings in 2005" that California had failed to comply, and in 2006 was forced to appoint a Receiver after California itself "admitted that it was unable to comply with the injunctive relief to which it had stipulated." *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, No. 08-17412 (9th Cir. Mar. 25, 2009). Even receivership—an extraordinary intrusion into state sovereignty—proved insufficient, as California thereafter refused to fund the Receiver's court-approved construction plans, prompting contempt proceedings. This history of noncompliance is the paradigmatic circumstance in which "no other relief will remedy the violation," § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii), because the State has demonstrated, through years of stipulated orders, receivership, and contempt litigation, that it cannot or will not deliver constitutionally adequate care while operating at the population levels the three-judge court found unconstitutional.

This Court's decision in *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), does not counsel otherwise. *Rhodes* held that double-celling alone does not violate the Eighth Amendment where it does not lead to deprivations of "essential food, medical care, or sanitation." *Id.* at 348. But *Rhodes* expressly reserved the question presented here: whether overcrowding that *does* produce such deprivations is unconstitutional. The three-judge court found, based on extensive evidentiary hearings, that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of ongoing violations of the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishment." That finding tracks this Court's holding in *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 (1976), that "deliberate indifference to serious medical needs of prisoners" violates the Eighth Amendment, *id.* at 104, and *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), which confirmed that prison officials violate the Eighth Amendment when they "know of and disregard an excessive risk to inmate health or safety." *Id.* at 837. Where the very structure of the institution—its population relative to capacity—creates that excessive risk, a structural remedy addressing population is not merely appropriate but constitutionally compelled.

**II. The Order Reflects the "Substantial Weight" Owed to Public Safety and Was the Product of Extensive Consideration of Alternatives**

The PLRA requires courts to "give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety." § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court did so, considering extensive expert testimony on the relationship between population levels, recidivism, and public safety, and structuring the remedy to permit the State substantial discretion in how to achieve the reduction—through parole reform, sentencing adjustments, or construction—rather than mandating specific releases. This approach honors the principle recognized in *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343 (1996), that courts must accord "adequate deference to the requirements of prison administration" and "leave with the prison officials the primary responsibility for devising a remedy." *Id.* at 362–63 (internal quotation marks omitted). The order here does exactly that: it sets a population ceiling as the outer boundary while leaving to California the choice of means.

Nor does deference to state sovereignty require otherwise. This Court has recognized that even where compliance with a remedial order requires substantial state expenditures or structural change, federal courts retain the equitable power to enforce constitutional guarantees against recalcitrant state defendants. *Missouri v. Jenkins*, 495 U.S. 33 (1990), upheld a federal court's authority to order a tax increase to fund a school desegregation remedy, recognizing that federal courts may impose significant burdens on state governance when necessary to vindicate constitutional rights that state officials have failed, over years, to secure. The population order here is considerably less intrusive than the remedy sanctioned in *Jenkins*, and it follows the same course of escalating remedial efforts—stipulated orders, receivership, contempt proceedings—that California's own conduct necessitated.

The judgment of the three-judge court should be affirmed.
