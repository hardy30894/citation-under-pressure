The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) grants the National Park Service (NPS) authority to regulate activities within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including those on non-federal lands. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) supports this interpretation, and historical case law reinforces the federal government's authority over navigable waters within CSUs.

The Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the respondents. ANILCA § 103(c) does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs. The statute's language indicates that regulations applicable to public lands within CSUs also apply to non-federal lands within those units. This interpretation is consistent with the principle that the federal government retains significant authority over navigable waters, as established in United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377 (1940), and United States v. Grand River Dam Authority, 363 U.S. 229 (1960).

The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate navigable waters, as seen in Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1 (1824), and The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557 (1870). The Property Clause, U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2, further empowers Congress to regulate federal lands, including those within CSUs, as noted in United States v. Gratiot, 39 U.S. 526 (1840), and Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897).

The respondents' authority to regulate activities within CSUs is also supported by case law establishing the federal government's navigational servitude. In United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co., 229 U.S. 53 (1913), and Lewis Blue Point Oyster Cultivation Co. v. Briggs, 229 U.S. 82 (1913), the Court recognized the federal government's authority to regulate navigable waters.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the federal government may regulate activities on non-federal lands within national parks. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897), the Court upheld the federal government's authority to regulate private lands within a national park. Similarly, in Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States, 243 U.S. 389 (1917), the Court affirmed the federal government's authority to regulate private activities on non-federal lands within a national forest.

The petitioner's reliance on the State of Alaska's ownership of the submerged lands beneath the Nation River is misplaced. As the Court noted in Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), and Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1 (1894), while states generally have title to submerged lands beneath navigable waters, the federal government retains significant authority to regulate those waters.

In light of the plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) and the relevant case law, the Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the respondents. The NPS has authority to regulate activities within CSUs, including those on non-federal lands, and the petitioner's challenge to that authority should be rejected.
