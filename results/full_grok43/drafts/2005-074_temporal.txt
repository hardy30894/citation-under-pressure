ARGUMENT

The reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not reach an alien whose re-entry occurred before its effective date. "Words of a statute ought not to have a retrospective operation, unless they are so clear, strong, and imperative, that no other meaning can be annexed to them." United States v. Heth, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806). Nothing in the text supplies the requisite clarity. The statute speaks only of "an alien who has reentered the United States illegally," without specifying the timing of that re-entry relative to enactment. Absent explicit direction, the provision must be read to operate only upon future conduct.

Even if Congress intended broader reach, the application would be impermissibly retroactive. Legislation "must be considered as addressed to the future, not to the past." Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964). A statute that attaches new disabilities to a completed re-entry and bars all forms of relief destroys rights that had vested under prior law. Such an alteration "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws." Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). The Constitution does not permit Congress to impose that consequence without unmistakable language.

The Tenth Circuit's contrary conclusion rests on the absence of a "protectable expectation." That reasoning inverts the inquiry. The relevant question is whether the new statute "changes the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date." Greene, 376 U.S. at 160. Because the reinstatement statute does precisely that with respect to pre-enactment re-entries, it cannot be applied here.
