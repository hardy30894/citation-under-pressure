**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TRIBAL COURT DENIED SELAM HIS CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS.**

The Sixth Amendment’s compulsory process clause applies to tribal courts through the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). *See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 65 n.15 (1978) (recognizing that ICRA incorporates many constitutional protections). Compulsory process guarantees a defendant the right “to compel the attendance of witnesses in his favor.” *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 22 (1967). This right is violated when a defendant is prevented from presenting witnesses material to his defense. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973).

Here, Selam’s appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, attempted to locate five potential witnesses but could only reach three, two of whom refused to testify. The tribal judge and counsel advised Selam of his right to subpoena witnesses, yet Selam never requested one. The Ninth Circuit has held that a defendant’s failure to request a subpoena does not waive his compulsory process right where counsel’s efforts were inadequate. *United States v. Brackeen*, 965 F.2d 1122, 1126 (9th Cir. 1992). Selam’s counsel’s efforts were insufficient, and the tribal court erred by not ensuring his right to compulsory process was vindicated.

**II. SELAM DID NOT WAIVE HIS CONFRONTATION CLAIM UNDER ICRA.**

The district court held that Selam waived his confrontation claim by failing to raise it in tribal appellate proceedings. However, ICRA’s confrontation clause, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), is not subject to state procedural default rules. *See National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe*, 471 U.S. 845, 855 (1985) (holding that tribal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction and federal habeas review is not barred by failure to exhaust tribal remedies where tribal law does not require exhaustion). Moreover, the Supreme Court has cautioned against applying federal procedural default rules to tribal proceedings. *See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 16 (1987).

Selam’s confrontation claim is not barred. The tribal court of appeals did not address the merits of his ICRA claim, and federal habeas review remains available. *See Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (holding that a federal habeas petitioner need not raise a claim in state court if state law does not require it).

**CONCLUSION**

The district court erred in denying Selam’s habeas petition. The tribal court violated his right to compulsory process, and his confrontation claim was not waived. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
