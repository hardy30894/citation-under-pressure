**ARGUMENT**

The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover cleanup costs from the United States. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) constituted a judicially approved settlement resolving Guam’s liability for response actions at the Ordot Dump, thereby triggering CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing Guam’s right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The plain language of the statute, the settled principles of statutory construction, and binding precedent from this Court and the lower courts compel this conclusion.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs). The key inquiry is whether the settlement “resolved” liability for a response action. The term “resolved” is not limited to admissions of fault or judicial findings of liability. Rather, it refers to the *practical effect* of the agreement: whether the party has assumed obligations that discharge its responsibility for the cleanup. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 440 (1978) (“The purpose of a settlement is to end litigation and to provide finality.”).

Here, the 2004 Consent Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system to prevent further contamination. These obligations were not merely procedural; they were *substantive* and *final*. The decree mandated that Guam undertake the full burden of remediation, including the construction of a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate—precisely the type of action Congress intended to be covered under CERCLA’s definition of “remedial action.” *See 42 U.S.C. § 101(24)*. The fact that the decree was entered without a finding of liability does not negate its effect. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, a settlement may resolve liability even in the absence of an admission of fault. *Id.* at 440. The parties’ intent to settle, as evidenced by the performance of obligations, is dispositive.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that settlements that impose binding obligations on a party are sufficient to trigger statutory rights and limitations. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that a consent decree “operates as a final adjudication of the rights of the parties” and “binds the parties and those in privity with them.” The Consent Decree here was judicially approved and enforceable in the District Court of Guam. It was not a mere agreement to negotiate; it was a final, binding resolution of the Clean Water Act violations. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. at 440. Thus, the decree resolved Guam’s liability for a response action within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B).

**II. Section 107(a) Is Inapplicable Because Section 113(f)(3)(B) Is the Exclusive Remedy for Settlement-Related Claims**

Guam’s claim under § 107(a) is foreclosed because § 113(f)(3)(B) provides the exclusive remedy for a party that has settled with the government. The legislative history of CERCLA confirms that Congress intended to create a clear hierarchy of remedies: a party that settles with the government may not later invoke § 107(a) to recover costs. *See H.R. Rep. No. 99-448, at 10 (1986)* (explaining that § 113(f)(3)(B) was added to “clarify the right of contribution” and “to prevent duplicative claims”).

This principle is rooted in the settled doctrine that a party cannot simultaneously benefit from a settlement and then seek recovery under a different statutory provision. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, the Court emphasized that “a consent decree is not a mere agreement to litigate; it is a final judgment.” *438 U.S. at 440*. Once a party has settled its liability, it cannot later claim that it is entitled to cost recovery under a different provision. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543. The same logic applies here: Guam cannot claim that it “incurred” costs under § 107(a) when it had already resolved its liability through a judicially approved settlement.

The Court has long held that a party cannot circumvent statutory limitations by recharacterizing its claim. In *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911), the Court rejected the argument that a party could avoid the statute of limitations by reframing its claim. “The form of the action is not controlling,” the Court stated, “but the substance of the claim.” *Id.* Similarly, Guam’s attempt to recharacterize its settlement obligations as “costs incurred” under § 107(a) is a mere formality. The substance of the claim is that Guam paid for cleanup under a settlement. That is precisely the scenario Congress intended to address under § 113(f)(3)(B).
