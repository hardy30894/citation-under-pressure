**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy constitutes a valid limited public forum that may exclude religious speech.**

Milford Central School’s policy authorizing the use of its facilities for "social, civic and recreational meetings" creates a limited public forum. *Healy v. James, 408 U.S. 169 (1972)*. A limited public forum is a place where the government has designated for certain types of speech, and the government may impose reasonable restrictions on the subject matter of speech permitted therein. *Rosenberger v. Rector, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995)*. The policy explicitly states that "School premises shall not be used by any individual or organization for religious purposes." By defining the permissible subject matter as "social, civic and recreational" and explicitly excluding religious purposes, Milford has lawfully established the boundaries of its forum.

**II. The Good News Club’s activities constitute "religious instruction" and "worship," which fall outside the permissible subject matter of the forum.**

The First Amendment does not require a public school to open its limited public forum to religious instruction. *Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981)*. The activities of the Good News Club—prayer, singing hymns, memorizing scripture, and the "challenge and invitation" to accept Jesus Christ as one's Savior—constitute religious instruction and worship. *Widmar, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6*. The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club's subject matter is "quintessentially religious" and that its activities "fall outside the bounds of pure 'moral and character development.'" *Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 202 F.3d 502, 511 (2d Cir. 2000)*. Because the Club’s purpose is to provide religious instruction rather than secular moral development, its exclusion from the forum is a valid exercise of subject-matter regulation.

**III. The exclusion is a valid exercise of subject-matter regulation, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.**

Milford’s policy does not discriminate against the viewpoint that morality should be based on religious principles. Rather, it excludes the subject matter of religion itself. *Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 829*. The policy allows the use of facilities for secular moral development, as evidenced by the school’s allowance of the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts. *Good News Club, 202 F.3d at 511*. The distinction drawn by the Second Circuit is between the *subject matter* of the speech—religion versus secular morality—and the *viewpoint* regarding that subject matter. *Id.* at 510. Therefore, Milford’s refusal to permit the Good News Club’s meetings does not violate the First Amendment.
