**ARGUMENT**

**I.  The Supreme Court’s Decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis* Effectively Overrules the D.C. Circuit’s Precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*.**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit is erroneous because it relies on a distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals that the Supreme Court in *Kloeckner v. Solis* effectively eliminated. In *Kloeckner*, the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case on a procedural ground, judicial review resides in district court. 568 U.S. 41, 604 (2012). The D.C. Circuit attempts to distinguish *Kloeckner* because it involved untimeliness, whereas *Powell* involved a jurisdictional bar. However, this distinction is artificial. The Supreme Court’s analysis in *Kloeckner* focused on the statutory definition of a "mixed case" and the need for district court review when the Board does not reach the merits of the discrimination claim. The result in *Kloeckner*—that procedural dismissals go to district court—applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals when the Board never reaches the merits.

**II.  The Statutory Definition of a "Mixed Case" Supports District Court Review.**

A "mixed case" is defined by the employee being "affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). In *Kloeckner*, the Court noted that "no one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB and that she alleged discrimination." 568 U.S. at 604. This language applies equally to the present case. Perry was affected by a retirement and suspension that were appealable to the Board if involuntary. The Board dismissed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction, not because the action was unappealable, but because it found the action voluntary. The D.C. Circuit’s argument that jurisdictional dismissals are different because the action was *never* appealable is flawed. The action (the retirement) was appealable; the *claim* that it was involuntary was the jurisdictional issue. The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on the Board’s ability to cure procedural defects is irrelevant to the statutory definition of a mixed case.

**III.  The "Uniformity" Argument in *Powell* is Weakened by *Kloeckner*.**

*Powell v. Department of Defense* held that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit to ensure "uniform interpretation" of the Board’s jurisdiction. 158 F.3d 597, 600 (D.C. Cir. 1998). However, *Kloeckner* resolved the split regarding procedural dismissals in favor of district court review. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner* effectively eviscerated the *Powell* distinction. The D.C. Circuit cannot cling to *Powell* when the Supreme Court has already resolved the split regarding procedural dismissals in favor of district court review. The Supreme Court’s holding that procedural dismissals go to district court undermines the *Powell* rationale that uniformity requires a single forum for jurisdictional issues. The Supreme Court has now established that the "mixed case" exception applies broadly to any dismissal that prevents the Board from reaching the merits, regardless of whether the dismissal is labeled procedural or jurisdictional. Therefore, the D.C. Circuit erred in applying *Powell* and should have transferred the case to district court.
