ARGUMENT

I. The Mootness of the Declaratory and Injunctive Claims Renders the Entire Case Nonjusticiable

Article III of the Constitution limits federal courts to deciding actual “Cases” and “Controversies.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 90 (2013). A case becomes moot when “the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Here, after Georgia Gwinnett College revised its Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct—and after Uzuegbunam graduated—no live controversy remains regarding the prior policies. The revised policies now permit expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged conduct-code provision has been removed. There is no reasonable expectation that the college will reenact the prior policies. *See Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1256–57 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Appellants do not contest this conclusion. *See* Appellants’ Br. at 8 (conceding that claims for injunctive and declaratory relief are moot). Because the only concrete, ongoing injury alleged has been eliminated, the case is moot.

II. A Claim for Nominal Damages Alone Cannot Revive an Otherwise Moot Constitutional Challenge

Appellants’ remaining request for nominal damages does not salvage their moot claims. This Court has long recognized that “a prayer for nominal damages” generally “will not save an otherwise moot case.” *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1258. The rationale is straightforward: nominal damages are a “technical” form of relief that recognizes a violation but do not remedy a continuing injury. *See Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 267 (1978) (noting that nominal damages are “a symbol of the violation” rather than compensation for harm). When a plaintiff seeks only to vindicate a past wrong without any ongoing or prospective harm, the case is moot because a federal court cannot “give an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101 (1998) (citation omitted).

Appellants mistakenly rely on the rule that a claim for damages survives mootness when the plaintiff suffers a “continuing, live injury.” *See, e.g., Memphis Light, Gas & Water Div. v. Craft*, 436 U.S. 1, 9 (1978). But here, Appellants alleged no actual injury from the prior policies. Their complaint mentions “injury” only twice, and never specifies any concrete harm such as monetary loss, physical pain, or emotional distress. *See Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230–31 (11th Cir. 2000) (requiring “actual injury” for compensatory damages under § 1983). Because Appellants sought only nominal damages—a remedy for the “abstract value” of the rights violated—their claims are indistinguishable from those in *Flanigan’s*, where this Court held that nominal damages “cannot breathe life into an otherwise moot controversy.” *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1258.

The fact that Appellants’ challenge includes both facial and as-applied components does not alter the analysis. The mootness inquiry focuses on whether the plaintiff’s personal stake in the outcome persists. *See Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 90–91. Once the challenged policy is repealed and the plaintiff no longer faces a threat of enforcement, the case is moot regardless of the type of challenge. *See, e.g., University of Texas v. Camenisch*, 451 U.S. 390, 393–94 (1981) (mootness where policy changed). Appellants’ attempt to resurrect their claims by recharacterizing their damages request on appeal cannot overcome the absence of a live controversy.

III. The District Court Properly Denied Leave to Amend

Appellants’ belated request to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages was procedurally improper and substantively futile. This Court has held that “a request for leave to amend ‘simply imbedded within an opposition memorandum’ does not properly raise the issue.” *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (citation omitted). Appellants included their request only in a response to a motion to dismiss, without filing a separate motion or specifying the additional factual allegations they would add. See *Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018) (requiring substance of proposed amendment). Moreover, even if Appellants had properly sought leave, they cannot cure the mootness by adding a compensatory-damages claim that lacks any factual support. *See Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962) (leave to amend may be denied for futility). Appellants’ failure to allege any actual injury rendered the proposed amendment futile.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
