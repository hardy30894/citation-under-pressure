The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 171 (1976). A punishment is unusual if it is contrary to the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Those standards are measured “by objective factors to the maximum possible extent.” Roper v. Simmons, 543 U.S. 551, 563 (2005) (quoting Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957, 1000 (1991) (Kennedy, J., concurring in part and concurring in judgment)).

The clearest objective indicium is legislative enactments. Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 331 (1989). When this case was decided below, only two States barred execution of the mentally retarded, while thirty-eight authorized capital punishment. That pattern mirrored the national consensus sustained in Penry, which held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit execution of mentally retarded offenders. Id. at 335.

Sentencing practices confirm the same conclusion. Juries in multiple jurisdictions have imposed death sentences on defendants with sub-average intellectual functioning. See, e.g., McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279, 311 (1987) (noting that “discretion is essential” and that “the power to be lenient” is the “heart of individualized sentencing”). No national consensus against such sentences had emerged.

The Court’s precedents likewise reject a per se rule. In Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), the Eighth Amendment barred execution only of the insane, a class defined by total incapacity. Mental retardation, by contrast, encompasses a range of functioning; many such defendants possess sufficient understanding to warrant punishment. Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 370-372 (1989), similarly declined to impose a categorical exemption for seventeen-year-olds, emphasizing that “the work of legislatures” supplies the controlling evidence.

Proportionality review does not alter the result. A sentence is excessive only if it makes no measurable contribution to accepted goals of punishment. Gregg, 428 U.S. at 183. Retribution and deterrence remain legitimate objectives even when applied to mentally retarded offenders whose crimes reflect deliberation and moral culpability. See Tison v. Arizona, 481 U.S. 137, 157 (1987).

Because no objective indicium demonstrates that execution of the mentally retarded offends contemporary standards, and because precedent squarely sustains the practice, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
