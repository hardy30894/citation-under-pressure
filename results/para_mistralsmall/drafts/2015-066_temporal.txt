**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts, and an uncounseled conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to . . . have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right is fundamental and applies to every criminal prosecution, regardless of the forum. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 60 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal courts but extend to state prosecutions as well. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963) (overruling *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), and holding that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to state courts via the Fourteenth Amendment). The same principle must apply to tribal courts, for the Sixth Amendment’s command is universal: “[I]n all criminal prosecutions,” the accused enjoys the right to counsel. U.S. Const. amend. VI.

The government’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* permitted the use of an uncounseled conviction for sentencing enhancement purposes, but it did not address whether such a conviction could be used as an *element* of a new offense. The distinction is critical. Sentencing enhancements are not themselves offenses; they do not alter the legal consequences of past conduct but merely adjust the punishment for the current offense. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747 (“The sentence imposed in each case was determined by the preponderance of the evidence, not by the reasonable-doubt standard used at trial.”). By contrast, § 117(a) makes prior convictions an *essential element* of the new offense. The government must prove those prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (“The Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.”). An uncounseled conviction, obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment, cannot satisfy this burden.

**II. Tribal court convictions are not exempt from the Sixth Amendment’s reach when used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions.**

The government argues that tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment, but this contention misapprehends the nature of the constitutional right at issue. The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel is not a limitation on the *court* that renders the conviction; it is a right of the *defendant*. *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 60 (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). When the government seeks to use a prior conviction as an element of a new federal offense, it must ensure that the conviction was obtained in a manner consistent with the Constitution. *United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 446 (1971) (“[A] court is bound to notice that a prior conviction used to enhance a sentence was obtained in violation of the Constitution.”).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is unavailing. *Ant* held that uncounseled tribal court convictions could not be used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions, and this Court should affirm that principle. The Sixth Amendment’s protections are not diminished by the forum in which the prior conviction was obtained. *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 60. If a conviction is uncounseled, it is invalid for Sixth Amendment purposes, and the government may not bootstrap that invalidity into a new federal offense.

**III. The use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses undermines the reliability of the factfinding process and violates due process.**

The Due Process Clause requires that every element of a federal offense be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. An uncounseled conviction is inherently unreliable because it was obtained without the assistance of counsel, a right the Court has described as “necessary to insure fundamental human rights of life and liberty.” *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 63. The government cannot rely on such a conviction to establish an essential element of a new offense without violating due process.

The Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is essential to ensuring the reliability of criminal proceedings. *Powell*, 287 U.S. at 60 (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). When the government seeks to use a prior conviction as an element of a new offense, it must ensure that the conviction was obtained in a constitutionally valid manner. *Tucker*, 404 U.S. at 446. An uncounseled conviction cannot satisfy this requirement.

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. An uncounseled conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because it violates the Sixth Amendment and undermines the reliability of the factfinding process. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
