# ARGUMENT

## I. The Population Reduction Order Satisfies the PLRA's Requirements Because Federal Courts Possess Broad Equitable Authority to Remedy Ongoing Constitutional Violations Through Structural Relief.

This Court has long recognized that a court sitting in equity possesses inherent and expansive authority to fashion whatever relief is necessary to eliminate an established constitutional violation. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." *Id.* at 329. That flexible authority extends to structural remedies that reach beyond individual relief when the violation itself is systemic. *See In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593 (1895) (recognizing the broad power of a federal court to enter injunctive relief adequate to secure compliance with federal law). The three-judge court's population order is a direct application of this settled principle: having found, after extensive fact-finding, that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the ongoing Eighth Amendment violations in California's prisons, the court was entitled to order the only remedy the record showed would work.

The PLRA's statutory requirement that relief be narrowly drawn and represent the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), does not displace this traditional equitable framework; it codifies it. Courts have always been required to calibrate relief to the scope of the violation, but calibration does not mean impotence. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court confirmed that a court retains continuing power to supervise and, when warranted, revisit its decree, though it cautioned that "nothing less than a clear showing of grievous wrong evoked by new and unforeseen conditions should lead us to change what was decreed after years of litigation with the consent of all concerned." *Id.* at 119. That standard confirms, rather than forecloses, the propriety of the exhaustive, fact-intensive process the three-judge court undertook here before ordering relief calibrated to the violation it found.

## II. This Court's School Desegregation Precedents Confirm That District Courts May Order Specific, Numerically Defined Relief When Necessary to Eliminate a Constitutional Violation.

The State's suggestion that a population cap is inherently too intrusive a remedy cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated approval of numerically specific remedial orders in analogous contexts involving entrenched, systemic constitutional violations. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), this Court directed the district courts to take "such proceedings and enter such orders and decrees consistent with this opinion as are necessary and proper to admit to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis," *id.* at 301, thereby entrusting district courts with broad discretion to design remedies adequate to the scope of the constitutional wrong, even where such remedies reordered the operations of state institutions.

That discretion was not merely nominal. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court held that a district court could order a local government to levy taxes and reopen schools to remedy segregation, explaining that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out." *Id.* at 234. The Court made clear that where local officials had failed for years to comply with their constitutional obligations, the district court's equitable powers were adequate to compel the affirmative structural action necessary to eliminate the violation. Similarly, in *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court confirmed that school boards and courts alike bore an "affirmative duty to take whatever steps might be necessary" to eliminate the constitutional violation "root and branch." *Id.* at 437-38 (quoting *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145, 154 (1965)). And in *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145 (1965), this Court approved a remedy requiring wholesale restructuring of a state voter-registration system, holding that once a constitutional violation of this magnitude is shown, "the court has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past." *Id.* at 154.

These precedents establish that when, as here, a coordinate branch of state government has for years failed to remedy a proven constitutional violation, a federal court does not exceed its authority by ordering specific, quantifiable relief—whether expressed as pupil ratios, funding levels, or, as in this case, a population ceiling calibrated to design capacity. The order below is materially indistinguishable from the remedial decrees this Court has consistently sustained.

## III. The Order Is a Proportionate Response to an Established and Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment's Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment.

The gravity of the underlying violation confirms the propriety of the remedy. The Eighth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), draws its meaning from "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long recognized that punishment must bear a proportionate relationship to legitimate penal objectives, and that punishments inflicting needless suffering offend the Constitution regardless of formal legality. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910). A prison system so overcrowded that inmates are deprived of adequate medical and mental health care, as the district court found here, inflicts exactly the kind of needless suffering the Eighth Amendment forbids. Where, as in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 4 (1958), state officials' own conduct has perpetuated the constitutional violation, the remedial obligation falls squarely on the courts to ensure compliance, and the State cannot now claim that a remedy tailored to the violation it created exceeds the PLRA's bounds.

## CONCLUSION

The population order is narrowly drawn, is a proportionate means of correcting a violation the State itself allowed to fester, and is fully consistent with this Court's settled equitable and Eighth Amendment jurisprudence. The judgment below should be affirmed.
