**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. To the contrary, this Court’s precedents compel the conclusion that any detention beyond a reasonable time—measured in terms of the statute’s purpose to secure the alien’s removal—violates due process.

This Court has long recognized that the Government may not detain aliens for punitive purposes or for reasons unrelated to effectuating removal. In *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), the Court upheld the exclusion of an alien spouse of a U.S. citizen, rejecting the claim that her detention violated due process. The Court emphasized that the alien’s exclusion was a legislative policy judgment, and that the Government’s authority to exclude aliens at the border included the power to detain them pending exclusion. *Id.* at 542-43. But the Court distinguished detention that is merely incidental to exclusion from detention that is punitive or indefinite. *Id.* at 546 (“It is not doubted that the respondent could, as the trial court found, have been released on parole at any time after the exclusion order was made.”). Thus, *Knauff* stands for the principle that detention must be tied to a legitimate governmental objective—here, effectuating removal—and may not extend beyond what is necessary to achieve that objective.

Similarly, in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), the Court upheld the continued detention of an excludable alien who could not be returned to his country of origin. The Court acknowledged that the alien’s detention was prolonged and that his release was unlikely, but it nonetheless deferred to the political branches’ authority over immigration matters. *Id.* at 215-16. Critically, however, the Court did not sanction indefinite detention. Instead, it emphasized that the alien’s detention was not punitive but was instead a consequence of his excludable status and the lack of a country willing to accept him. *Id.* at 212. The Court’s reasoning thus presupposes that detention must be temporary and tied to the Government’s ongoing efforts to effectuate removal.

The Government’s reliance on *Mezei* is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien who had never entered the United States, and the Court expressly distinguished such cases from those involving resident aliens who have been admitted and later ordered removed. *Id.* at 212 n. 7. Here, Petitioner is a lawful permanent resident who was admitted to the United States and later ordered removed. His detention, therefore, is governed by different considerations than those in *Mezei*. As this Court recognized in *Johnson v. Eisentrager*, 339 U.S. 763 (1950), aliens who have entered the United States—even those ordered removed—retain constitutional protections that aliens at the border do not. *Id.* at 771 (“The alien, to whom the United States has been generous in admitting to its borders, may be expelled only after proceedings conforming to traditional standards of fairness encompassed in due process of law.”). Thus, Petitioner’s detention must comport with due process, which prohibits indefinite confinement without justification.

The Government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes indefinite detention also conflicts with this Court’s recognition that detention must be reasonable in duration. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), the Court upheld the Bail Reform Act’s preventive detention provisions, but it emphasized that such detention must be limited to what is necessary to serve the compelling governmental interest at stake. *Id.* at 748-49. While *Salerno* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is consistent with earlier precedents recognizing that detention must be reasonable in duration and tied to a legitimate governmental objective. See *Knauff*, 338 U.S. at 546 (suggesting that parole is permissible where detention is no longer necessary to effectuate exclusion).

Here, Petitioner has been detained for years beyond the 90-day removal period, and there is no realistic prospect of his removal. The Government has not identified any legitimate reason for his continued detention other than the mere fact that no country will accept him. This is precisely the type of indefinite detention that this Court’s precedents condemn. See *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 215-16 (acknowledging the harshness of prolonged detention but deferring to the political branches only where removal is possible). Where, as here, removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates due process.

The Government’s reliance on § 1231(a)(6) is unavailing. The statute authorizes detention only “pending removal,” and this Court has long interpreted such language to require that detention be temporary and tied to the Government’s efforts to effectuate removal. See *Knauff*, 338 U.S. at 546 (suggesting that detention must be incidental to exclusion). Where removal is not possible, detention under § 1231(a)(6) is no longer authorized. The Government’s argument that it may detain Petitioner indefinitely simply because no country will accept him is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents and the Constitution’s guarantee of due process.

In sum, the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Detention must be reasonable in duration and tied to the Government’s efforts to effectuate removal. Where, as here, removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates due process. The judgment below should be affirmed.
