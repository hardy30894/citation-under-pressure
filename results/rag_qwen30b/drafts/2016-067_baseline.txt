**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt for its own account, acted as a creditor, not a debt collector, when it sought to collect on the debts it acquired. The FDCPA’s text, structure, and precedent compel this conclusion.

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as including any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” This definition is not triggered by the mere fact that a debt was in default at the time of purchase. Rather, the critical inquiry is whether the entity collects debts *on behalf of another*—a distinction rooted in the Act’s purpose to regulate third-party collection practices, not the conduct of creditors collecting their own debts.

The Fourth Circuit’s interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291 (1995), which affirmed that the FDCPA applies to lawyers who “regularly attempt to obtain payment of consumer debts through legal proceedings,” because such conduct constitutes “attempt[ing] to collect” debts owed to another. Id. at 293. The Court emphasized that the Act’s definition of “debt collector” includes those who “regularly collect[] or attempt[] to collect, directly or indirectly, [consumer] debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” Id. (quoting § 1692a(6)). This language makes clear that the focus is on the *relationship* between the collector and the debt’s original obligee—not the default status of the debt at the time of acquisition.

Here, Santander did not collect on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. The complaint itself alleges that on December 1, 2011, CitiFinancial Auto sold the defaulted loans to Santander “for a few cents on the dollar.” After that date, Santander became the owner of the debt and sought to collect it for its own account. The complaint does not allege that Santander collected on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto or any other third party. Indeed, the very fact that Santander purchased the debt—rather than merely servicing it—demonstrates that it assumed the role of creditor, not agent.

The plaintiffs’ argument that Santander must be a “debt collector” because it acquired the debt after default is foreclosed by the Act’s structure. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” is not contingent on default status. Instead, the Act distinguishes between creditors and debt collectors based on *who owns the debt* and *for whom the collection is conducted*. As the Fourth Circuit correctly observed, “the overall structure of § 1692a(6) makes clear that when assessing whether a person qualifies as a ‘debt collector,’ we must first determine whether the person satisfies one of the statutory definitions given in the main text of § 1692a(6) before considering whether that person falls into one of the exclusions.” The plaintiffs’ argument fails at the threshold: Santander does not satisfy the definition of a debt collector because it did not regularly collect debts owed to another. It collected debts it had purchased—its own debts.

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ interpretation would render the FDCPA’s exclusions meaningless. The Act explicitly excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt “which was not in default at the time it was obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). If default status alone determined whether a purchaser was a debt collector, this exclusion would be superfluous. Congress could not have intended to exclude non-defaulted debt collectors while simultaneously including defaulted debt collectors—especially when the Act’s purpose is to regulate *third-party* collection, not the conduct of creditors collecting their own debts.

The plaintiffs’ attempt to equate Santander’s prior role as a servicer with its status as a debt collector after purchase is equally unpersuasive. The fact that Santander may have collected on the same loans *before* purchasing them does not establish that it was acting as a debt collector *after* the purchase. As the Fourth Circuit noted, “a company such as Santander — which, as a consumer finance company, lends money, services loans, collects debt for itself, collects debt for others, and otherwise engages in borrowing and investing its capital — would be subject to the FDCPA for all of its collection activities simply because one of its several activities involves the collection of debts for others.” This would eviscerate the distinction between creditor and debt collector. Congress did not intend such a result.

Finally, the plaintiffs’ argument that Santander “remained a debt collector” after purchase ignores the fundamental shift in status that occurs upon acquisition of a debt. Once Santander purchased the loans, it became the obligee. It no longer collected on behalf of another; it collected on its own behalf. The FDCPA does not regulate creditors collecting their own debts. See *Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, L.P.A.*, 559 U.S. 573, 580 (2010) (noting that the FDCPA “regulates interactions between consumer debtors and ‘debt collector[s]’” and does not apply to creditors collecting their own debts).

For these reasons, the Fourth Circuit correctly concluded that the complaint failed to allege facts showing that Santander was a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6). The judgment must be affirmed.
