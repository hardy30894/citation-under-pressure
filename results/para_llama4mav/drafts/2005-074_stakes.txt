The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if it did, its application to such aliens would be impermissibly retroactive.

First, the plain language of the statute and the relevant legislative history do not clearly indicate that Congress intended the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Supreme Court established a two-step test for determining whether a statute applies retroactively. The first step is to determine whether Congress has clearly indicated that the statute should be applied retroactively. Id. at 280. If not, the second step is to assess whether the statute has an impermissible retroactive effect. Id. Here, the statute's language and legislative history are silent on the issue of retroactivity, failing to meet the first step's requirement for clear congressional intent.

Furthermore, applying the reinstatement statute to aliens who re-entered before its enactment would have an impermissible retroactive effect. As the Court noted in INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), a statute has a retroactive effect when it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." Id. at 321 (quoting Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270). By reinstating prior deportation orders against aliens who re-entered before the statute's effective date, Section 241(a)(5) would impose new legal consequences on past actions, thereby having a retroactive effect.

The government's argument that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is unpersuasive. The Court has recognized that an alien's reliance on prior law is a relevant factor in determining retroactivity. See St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 323-24. Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States and remained here for many years, forming connections and potentially seeking relief under the law as it existed at the time. The reinstatement statute's denial of relief to such individuals would unfairly disrupt their settled expectations.

The Tenth Circuit's decision should be reversed, as the reinstatement statute does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas or, alternatively, its application to him is impermissibly retroactive. The judgment of the Circuit Court should be vacated, and the case remanded for further proceedings.

In Fernandez-Vargas v. Ashcroft, the Tenth Circuit relied on its prior decision in Avila-Macias v. Ashcroft, 328 F.3d 108 (3d Cir. 2003), which is not binding on this Court. Moreover, other circuits have taken a different view, holding that the reinstatement statute does not apply retroactively. See, e.g., Faiz-Mohammad v. Ashcroft, 395 F.3d 799 (7th Cir. 2005). This Court should follow the reasoning in St. Cyr and Landgraf to hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas or that its application to him is impermissibly retroactive.
