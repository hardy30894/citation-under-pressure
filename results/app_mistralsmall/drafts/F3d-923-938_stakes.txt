**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TAX COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MERUELO FAILED TO ESTABLISH A "BONA FIDE INDEBTEDNESS" RUNNING DIRECTLY TO HIM.**

The Internal Revenue Code permits an S corporation shareholder to deduct losses to the extent of his basis in the corporation’s stock and any indebtedness of the corporation to him. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). Treasury Regulation § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i) clarifies that such indebtedness must be "bona fide indebtedness" that "runs directly" to the shareholder. The Tax Court concluded that Meruelo failed to satisfy this requirement because the alleged indebtedness ran from Merco to other entities, not directly to him. *Meruelo v. Comm’r*, T.C. Memo. 2018-11 (citing *Prashker v. Comm’r*, 59 T.C. 172 (1972)). This conclusion misapplies the law.

A shareholder may establish basis in corporate indebtedness even where the funds are routed through intermediaries if the transactions reflect a genuine debtor-creditor relationship. *See Estate of Mixon v. United States*, 464 F.3d 1163, 1170 (10th Cir. 2006) ("The substance of the transaction, not its form, governs for tax purposes."). Here, Meruelo’s transfers through affiliates were not mere bookkeeping entries but reflected actual economic outlays made on Merco’s behalf. The Tax Court improperly disregarded the economic reality of the transactions by focusing solely on the formal path of the funds.

Moreover, the Tax Court’s reliance on *Prashker* is misplaced. In *Prashker*, the Tax Court denied basis where the shareholder’s alleged loans were merely bookkeeping entries with no evidence of a genuine obligation. *Prashker*, 59 T.C. at 176. Here, by contrast, Meruelo’s CPA memorialized the indebtedness in a promissory note and annually charged interest, demonstrating a bona fide debtor-creditor relationship. *See Gilday v. Comm’r*, 103 T.C. 176, 180 (1994) (holding that a shareholder’s advances, documented by promissory notes and interest charges, established bona fide indebtedness).

II. **THE TAX COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MERUELO FAILED TO ESTABLISH AN "ACTUAL ECONOMIC OUTLAY."**

To claim basis in corporate indebtedness, a shareholder must demonstrate an "actual economic outlay." *See Putnam v. Comm’r*, 352 U.S. 82, 85, 77 S.Ct. 175, 1 L.Ed.2d 144 (1956). The Tax Court concluded that Meruelo did not make an actual economic outlay because the funds were routed through affiliates. This conclusion ignores the economic substance of the transactions.

A shareholder need not directly advance funds to the corporation to establish basis if the funds are advanced on his behalf and he remains ultimately liable for the debt. *See Estate of Leavitt v. Comm’r*, 875 F.3d 1138, 1145 (10th Cir. 2017) ("A shareholder’s economic outlay is measured by the net effect of the transactions, not the formal path of the funds."). Here, Meruelo obtained a personal loan and directed the proceeds to affiliates that, in turn, advanced the funds to Merco. The Tax Court erred in treating this as a mere pass-through of funds rather than an actual economic outlay by Meruelo.

The Tax Court’s holding also conflicts with *Oren v. Comm’r*, 92 T.C. 763 (1989), where the Tax Court permitted a shareholder to claim basis in corporate indebtedness where the funds were advanced by a third party but the shareholder remained liable for the debt. *Oren*, 92 T.C. at 768. Here, Meruelo’s personal liability on the loan and his direction of the funds to Merco’s benefit establish an actual economic outlay.

III. **THE TAX COURT ERRED IN DISREGARDING THE PROMISSORY NOTE AND ANNUAL INTEREST CHARGES AS EVIDENCE OF A GENUINE INDEBTEDNESS.**

The Tax Court discounted the promissory note and annual interest charges as "pro forma" and not reflective of a genuine indebtedness. This conclusion is unsupported by the record and misapplies the law.

A shareholder’s execution of a promissory note and payment of interest are strong indicia of a bona fide indebtedness. *See Gilday*, 103 T.C. at 180. Here, Meruelo’s CPA drafted a promissory note memorializing a $10 million line of credit, and Merco’s tax returns reflected annual interest charges on the indebtedness. The Tax Court erred in dismissing these documents as lacking economic substance.

The Tax Court’s reliance on *Koch v. Comm’r*, 71 T.C. 54 (1978), is misplaced. In *Koch*, the Tax Court denied basis where the shareholder’s advances were not documented and bore no interest. *Koch*, 71 T.C. at 60. Here, by contrast, the indebtedness was documented by a promissory note and bore interest, demonstrating a genuine debtor-creditor relationship.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Tax Court erred in denying Meruelo’s claimed deduction. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
