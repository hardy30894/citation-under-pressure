# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

The First Circuit correctly held that Massachusetts' thirty-five-foot buffer zone is a valid time-place-manner regulation. Under *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989), a content-neutral restriction on speech in a public forum is permissible if it is "narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest" and "leave[s] open ample alternative channels for communication." The Act satisfies each requirement.

**A. The Act Is Content-Neutral.**

The Act "is not concerned with regulating speech, but rather with the effects of clinic access." *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000). Its text draws no distinction based on subject matter or viewpoint; it restricts all persons—pro-life counselors, pro-choice supporters, and passersby alike—from standing within the buffer zone, regardless of what they intend to say. This Court's own precedent confirms that regulations of this character are content-neutral. In *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado statute creating a one-hundred-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, explaining that the statute was "not directed to the content of the speech" but merely regulated "the places where some speech may occur." 530 U.S. at 719, 730. The same reasoning controls here.

Petitioners' argument that the statute's four exemptions render it content-based fails for the same reasons this Court rejected an analogous challenge in *Hill*. The exemptions—for clinic employees, law enforcement, and persons merely passing through—are justified "without reference to the content of the speech," *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791, but rather by the individual's function relative to the facility. Distinguishing patients and employees from protesters is not viewpoint discrimination; it is a permissible, functional classification necessary to the exemption scheme, just as the "consent" requirement in *Hill* did not transform Colorado's law into a content-based regulation. 530 U.S. at 721.

**B. Massachusetts's Interests Are Significant.**

The Commonwealth's interests in "protecting the health and safety of patients and clinic staff," ensuring "unimpeded access to health-care facilities," and preserving "public safety" are precisely the interests this Court found sufficient in *Hill*. 530 U.S. at 715. As the *Hill* Court recognized, "[t]he First Amendment does not demand that patients at a medical facility undertake Herculean efforts to escape the cacophony of political protests." *Id.* at 729. Massachusetts's legislative record—including years of documented congestion, harassment, and safety concerns outside clinics predating the 2007 amendment—amply supports these interests. *See McGuire v. Reilly*, 260 F.3d 36, 40–42 (1st Cir. 2001) (documenting history of clinic-front confrontations justifying the predecessor statute).

**C. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored.**

Narrow tailoring under intermediate scrutiny does not require that the government employ the least restrictive means available; it requires only that the regulation "not burden substantially more speech than is necessary." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. Massachusetts's thirty-five-foot zone is narrower than the buffer this Court approved in *Hill*, and it leaves petitioners free to engage in "sidewalk counseling," display signs, distribute literature, sing hymns, pray aloud—with or without amplification—and speak to patients from just outside the zone. The First Circuit found, based on an extensive trial record, that petitioners in fact continued to counsel patients, hand out literature, and, by their own account, persuaded women to forgo abortions even after the buffer zones took effect. Where speakers retain "many other means of communication," *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 729, the tailoring requirement is satisfied.

**D. Ample Alternative Channels Remain.**

The trial record—canvassing the Boston, Worcester, and Springfield clinics—demonstrates that petitioners retained effective means to reach their intended audience. That the buffer zone makes petitioners' preferred method of communication (extremely close personal contact) marginally less convenient does not offend the First Amendment. "A regulation of the time, place, or manner of protected speech must be narrowly tailored to serve the government's legitimate, content-neutral interests but... need not be the least restrictive or least intrusive means of doing so." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798.

## II. HILL v. COLORADO WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* disregards the stringent standard this Court applies to stare decisis in constitutional cases. Overruling precedent requires a "special justification," *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000), beyond a mere belief that the case was wrongly decided. No such justification exists here.

*Hill* remains sound because it correctly applied *Ward*'s content-neutrality framework to a materially identical statute. The buffer zone at issue in *Hill* was, if anything, broader than Massachusetts's thirty-five-foot zone, yet the Court sustained it because the statute "regulates the location of protected speech" and does so "without reference to content." 530 U.S. at 719–20. The reasoning of *Hill* has not been undermined by subsequent decisions. Petitioners' reliance on *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced, as the First Circuit recognized: those cases articulate "no more than conventional First Amendment principles" applied to "factual scenarios far different" from clinic-access buffer zones. None addressed, let alone disturbed, the specific analytical framework *Hill* applied to location-based restrictions outside healthcare facilities.

Moreover, *Hill* itself built upon settled doctrine recognizing that sidewalks near healthcare facilities, while "quintessential public forums," remain subject to reasonable time-place-manner regulation when patients' safety and access are at stake. 530 U.S. at 715. Overruling *Hill* would destabilize this settled framework and cast doubt on numerous state and municipal buffer-zone laws enacted in reliance upon it.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
