**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act), 29 U.S.C. § 651 et seq., to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations occurring aboard the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels—whether inspected or uninspected—preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1). That holding contradicts controlling precedent, misreads the scope of federal preemption, and fails to account for Congress’s intent to preserve OSHA’s role in protecting workers in non-transportation industries, including offshore oil and gas operations.

I. THE COAST GUARD DOES NOT HAVE EXCLUSIVE JURISDICTION OVER WORKING CONDITIONS ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard has exclusive authority over “working conditions of seamen” on all vessels in navigation—including uninspected barges—rests on an overbroad interpretation of *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). Those cases did not establish a categorical exclusion of OSHA from regulating any vessel, inspected or not. Rather, they held only that OSHA regulations do not apply to *seamen* when their work is part of the operation of a vessel in navigation—i.e., when the vessel is engaged in transportation or commercial maritime activity. See *Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1122 (“OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.”); *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826–27.

Here, the *MR. BELDON* was not engaged in navigation. It was a fixed, non-self-propelled drilling barge moored in place while conducting oil and gas exploration—a function distinct from maritime transportation. As this Court has recognized, “a vessel may be subject to different regulatory regimes depending on its use.” *United States v. Spector*, 476 U.S. 229, 235 (1986). The *MR. BELDON*’s primary purpose was industrial: it served as a platform for drilling operations, not as a means of transporting goods or persons across waterways. Thus, the workers aboard were not seamen performing duties related to vessel operation; they were employees engaged in a high-hazard industrial occupation, akin to those on land-based oil rigs.

This distinction is critical. In *Clary*, the plaintiff was injured while working on a drilling barge, but the injury occurred during routine maintenance—specifically, tripping over a steel plate welded to the deck. The court held that OSHA standards could not be applied because the injury arose from a condition affecting the vessel’s operation. But the court did not hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over *all* working conditions on *all* drilling barges. Indeed, the court explicitly noted that its holding applied only where the worker’s duties were “in connection with the operation of the vessel.” *Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1121. Here, the explosion resulted from failure to properly manage well pressure during drilling—a core industrial safety issue unrelated to vessel navigation. The *MR. BELDON* was not “in navigation” at the time of the incident; it was stationary, functioning as a workplace for industrial labor.

Moreover, the Coast Guard’s own regulations confirm that it does not regulate the full spectrum of occupational safety in such settings. While the Coast Guard has authority to prescribe certain safety equipment standards for uninspected vessels—such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems—those rules are limited in scope and do not address process safety management, hazard communication, or emergency response protocols essential to preventing blowouts. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. The absence of comprehensive process safety standards demonstrates that the Coast Guard does not exercise exclusive control over the *workplace* environment in which these workers labored.

II. SECTION 4(B)(1) OF THE OSH ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA WHERE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED STATUTORY AUTHORITY TO PRESERVE SAFETY AND HEALTH STANDARDS

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that OSHA shall not apply “with respect to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision reflects a policy of avoiding regulatory overlap—not a rule of absolute preemption.

The Fifth Circuit incorrectly assumed that the mere existence of Coast Guard authority over some aspects of vessel safety triggers preemption. But Congress intended § 4(b)(1) to apply only when another agency has *statutory authority to prescribe or enforce occupational safety and health standards*. See *Barnes v. United States*, 453 U.S. 135, 140 (1981) (“Congress did not intend to create a blanket exemption for every federally regulated industry.”). The Coast Guard’s authority over vessels is primarily focused on navigational safety, pollution prevention, and vessel seaworthiness—not on the day-to-day safety and health of workers performing industrial tasks.

In *Hoffman Plastic Compounds, Inc. v. National Labor Relations Board*, 530 U.S. 120 (2000), the Supreme Court emphasized that preemption must be clear and express. “Where Congress intends to preempt state law, it must do so clearly.” Id. at 130. The same principle applies to federal preemption. There is no such clear congressional directive here. The OSH Act itself contemplates concurrent regulation: it includes a provision allowing OSHA to issue standards even where other agencies have jurisdiction, so long as there is no conflict. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(2).
