**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE MERIT SYSTEMS PROTECTION BOARD’S DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S MIXED CASE FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION IS REVIEWABLE IN DISTRICT COURT UNDER 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).**

The Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction over this case because the Board’s dismissal of Petitioner’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction was a dismissal of a *mixed case* under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). In *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S.Ct. 596, 184 L.Ed.2d 433 (2012), the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case—even on procedural grounds—judicial review lies in district court. *Id.* at 604. The same rule must apply when the Board dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), upon which the lower court relied, is no longer good law. *Powell* held that a jurisdictional dismissal should be reviewed in the Federal Circuit, but *Kloeckner* abrogated that rule by extending district court review to *all* Board dismissals of mixed cases, regardless of the basis for dismissal. *SeeKloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 604 (holding that “[w]hen the Board dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, the employee may seek review in district court”). The Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner* did not distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals; it applied uniformly to all mixed-case dismissals. *Id.* at 602–04.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to carve out an exception for jurisdictional dismissals in *Perry v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 855 F.3d 890 (D.C. Cir. 2017), is inconsistent with *Kloeckner*. The D.C. Circuit reasoned that jurisdictional dismissals are different because the action was never appealable to the Board, but this distinction is immaterial under *Kloeckner*. The Supreme Court did not limit its holding to procedural dismissals; it applied to *all* mixed-case dismissals. *Id.* at 604. The Court’s focus was on whether the employee alleged discrimination in a case otherwise appealable to the Board—not on the reason for the Board’s dismissal. *Id.*

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s contrary interpretation in *Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 713 F.3d 1115 (Fed. Cir. 2013), is unpersuasive. The Federal Circuit’s reasoning—that jurisdictional dismissals are fundamentally different because the action was never appealable—ignores the text of § 7702(a)(1), which defines a mixed case as one in which the employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board.” *Id.* § 7702(a)(1)(A). The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not change the fact that Petitioner’s claims arose from an action that *could* have been appealed to the Board if not for the alleged coercion. The voluntariness of the settlement is itself a merits issue in the discrimination claim. *SeeGarcia v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (noting that the Board lacks jurisdiction over voluntary actions).

Thus, the Board’s dismissal of Petitioner’s mixed case for lack of jurisdiction is reviewable in district court under § 7703(b)(2). The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule in *Conforto* is erroneous and should be rejected.

II. **THE BOARD’S DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S APPEAL FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION WAS A MIXED CASE SUBJECT TO DISTRICT COURT REVIEW.**

Petitioner’s case is a mixed case because he alleged discrimination in connection with an action appealable to the Board—his suspension and retirement. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction did not alter the nature of his claims; it merely determined that the underlying action was voluntary. This is a merits issue in his discrimination claim, not a jurisdictional bar. *SeeGarcia*, 437 F.3d at 1328.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced because *Powell* predates *Kloeckner* and was abrogated by it. *Kloeckner* made clear that all mixed-case dismissals—regardless of the reason—are reviewable in district court. 568 U.S. at 604. The Court’s holding was categorical: “[W]hen the Board dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, the employee may seek review in district court.” *Id.* (emphasis added). There is no principled basis for distinguishing between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals.

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit’s rule in *Conforto* creates an absurd result: employees whose cases are dismissed for lack of jurisdiction would be forced to litigate in the Federal Circuit, while those dismissed for procedural reasons would litigate in district court. This split undermines the purpose of § 7703(b)(2), which was to provide a uniform forum for discrimination claims. *SeeKloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 604.

III. **THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S RULE IN *CONFORTO* IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK AND SHOULD BE REJECTED.**

The Federal Circuit’s rule in *Conforto* is inconsistent with the text and structure of the Civil Service Reform Act. Section 7703(b)(2) provides that mixed cases “shall be filed in district court,” and the Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* held that this provision applies to *all* mixed-case dismissals. 568 U.S. at 604. The Federal Circuit’s attempt to limit this rule to procedural dismissals is unsupported by the statute or *Kloeckner*.

Additionally, the Federal Circuit’s rule creates forum shopping and uncertainty. Employees who allege discrimination in connection with an appealable action should not be forced to guess whether their case will be dismissed for jurisdictional or procedural reasons. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner* eliminates this uncertainty by providing a clear rule: all mixed-case dismissals are reviewable in district court.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the Board’s dismissal of Petitioner’s mixed case for lack of jurisdiction is reviewable in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule in *Conforto* is erroneous and should be rejected.
