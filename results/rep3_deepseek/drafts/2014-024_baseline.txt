**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONVICTION UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 875(C) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENTS TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS THREATS.**

The First Amendment shields speech from governmental suppression unless the speech falls within a narrowly defined category of unprotected expression. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). This Court has recognized that “true threats” constitute one such category. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). A true threat is a statement “where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The critical inquiry is the speaker’s *mens rea*—whether the speaker subjectively intended his words to be taken as a threat.

The text of 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of “any communication containing any threat.” This Court has long held that criminal statutes containing no explicit *mens rea* requirement nonetheless require proof of a culpable mental state. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Indeed, “the existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence.” *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 436 (1978). Section 875(c) is no exception. The word “threat” itself imports a mental state—the speaker must intend to communicate a threat, not merely utter words that a reasonable listener might find alarming.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard—asking whether a “reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat”—impermissibly dilutes the *mens rea* required for criminal liability. *See United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991). That standard criminalizes speech based on negligence, punishing speakers who did not intend to threaten but whose words were objectively threatening. This Court has consistently rejected negligence as a sufficient *mens rea* for statutes that criminalize otherwise protected speech. *See, e.g., New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 279-80 (1964) (requiring actual malice for defamation of public officials); *Garrison v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 64, 74 (1964) (same). The same principle applies here: the First Amendment demands that the government prove the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be threatening.

This Court’s decision in *Black* confirms that subjective intent is required. In *Black*, the Court defined true threats as statements “where the speaker *means to communicate* a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” 538 U.S. at 359 (emphasis added). The phrase “means to communicate” denotes purposeful conduct, not mere objective foreseeability. The Court further explained that the true-threats exception protects individuals from “the fear of violence” and “the disruption that fear engenders.” *Id.* at 360. But that rationale does not justify punishing a speaker who did not intend to instill fear. The fear of a reasonable listener, standing alone, cannot transform protected speech into a crime.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Black* is misplaced. The court below acknowledged *Black*’s definition but concluded that it did not overrule *Kosma*’s objective standard. That conclusion is untenable. *Black*’s language—“means to communicate”—unambiguously requires subjective intent. The Third Circuit’s contrary interpretation effectively reads *Black* out of existence.

Moreover, the objective standard chills protected speech. A speaker who engages in hyperbole, satire, or artistic expression—such as the rap lyrics at issue here—may be convicted if a reasonable listener misinterprets his words. This Court has long recognized that “the threat must be distinguished from what is constitutionally protected speech.” *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707. The objective standard blurs that distinction, punishing speakers for the reactions of their audience rather than their own culpable intent.

The government’s interest in protecting individuals from fear of violence is legitimate, but that interest does not justify eliminating *mens rea*. A subjective intent standard adequately protects that interest: speakers who intend to threaten can be prosecuted, while those who do not—such as Mr. Elonis, who testified he posted rap lyrics as a therapeutic outlet—remain free from criminal liability. The First Amendment requires no less.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that Mr. Elonis subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.
