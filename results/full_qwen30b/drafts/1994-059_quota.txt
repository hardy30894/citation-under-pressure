**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for U.S. Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—must be answered in the negative. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing term limits on members of Congress, directly contravenes the exclusive and exclusive nature of the qualifications set forth in Article I, Sections 2 and 3 of the U.S. Constitution. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Section 3 of Amendment 73 is unconstitutional, and that the entire amendment must fall because the federal and state term limits were inseverable. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Non-Delegable**

The Constitution explicitly sets forth the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. The House of Representatives must be composed of persons who are “twenty-five Years of Age” and have been “seven Years a Citizen of the United States,” and who “shall have been an Inhabitant of the State in which they are chosen.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, cl. 2. The Senate requires persons who are “thirty Years of Age,” “nine Years a Citizen,” and “an Inhabitant of the State they shall represent.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the sole standards by which Congress may be constituted. The Framers deliberately chose to vest this power in the national Constitution, not in the states, to ensure uniformity and prevent fragmentation of the federal legislative body.

This Court has long held that the qualifications for federal office are exclusive. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), the Court stated: “The qualifications for the office of Senator and Representative are prescribed by the Constitution, and no other qualifications can be added.” Similarly, in *United States v. Ballin*, 144 U.S. 1, 10 (1892), the Court emphasized that “the Constitution has fixed the qualifications for members of Congress, and no state can add to or subtract from them.” The Framers rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications during the Constitutional Convention, and the final text reflects a deliberate decision to reserve this power exclusively to the national Constitution. *The Federalist No. 57* (Madison) underscores that the “qualification of a representative” must be “uniform throughout the Union,” lest “the Constitution would be rendered inconsistent with itself.”

The Arkansas Amendment attempts to add a new qualification—limiting the number of terms a person may serve—on top of the Constitution’s enumerated requirements. This is not a mere regulation of elections; it is a direct alteration of the constitutional qualifications. As this Court held in *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 790 (1995), “the Constitution’s qualifications clauses are not subject to state addition or subtraction.” The Court further explained: “The Constitution’s text and structure make clear that the qualifications for federal office are exclusive.” Id. at 791. The Arkansas Amendment, by barring individuals who have served three terms in the House or two in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, creates a new, state-imposed qualification that the Constitution does not recognize.

**II. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Held the Federal and State Provisions Inseverable**

The Arkansas Supreme Court concluded that the severability clause in Amendment 73 could not save the state term limits because the federal and state provisions were not severable. This conclusion is sound and consistent with precedent.

In *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964), the Arkansas Supreme Court held that a severability clause is effective only if the legislature or people “would have enacted the valid portion without the invalid one.” The Court must determine whether the invalid provision was “so interwoven” with the valid that the voters would not have approved the remainder. Id. at 751, 375 S.W.2d at 813.

Here, the ballot language and preamble treated state and federal officeholders as a single category. The ballot title referred to “elected officials,” and the Preamble declared that “elected officials who remain in office too long become preoccupied with reelection.” The amendment was presented as a single, unified package. The voters were not given a choice between state term limits and federal term limits. As the Arkansas Supreme Court observed, “the two groups were not only inextricably linked—they were systemically fused.” This is precisely the kind of “all-or-nothing” package that this Court has condemned as undermining the integrity of the initiative process.

In *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 420, 316 S.W.2d 185, 188 (1958), the court refused to sever a constitutional amendment that combined a legitimate state interest with a constitutionally suspect provision, stating that “the people did not vote on the separate parts.” The same principle applies here. The voters were not asked whether they wanted term limits for state officials; they were asked whether they wanted term limits for “elected officials”—a term that included federal officeholders. The amendment’s structure was designed to exploit public sentiment toward congressional term limits to pass state term limits. This is not a valid exercise of initiative power.

**III. The Initiative Power Does Not Extend to Altering Federal Qualifications**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the initiative power, while broad, does not extend to altering federal qualifications. The people of Arkansas may limit the terms of state officials, but they may not alter the qualifications for federal office. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 792 (“The States may not impose additional qualifications for federal office.”). The initiative is a tool of state sovereignty, not a mechanism to rewrite the federal Constitution.

In *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1, 49 (1849), this Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law or constitution can override it.” The Arkansas Amendment, by attempting to bar individuals from federal office based on past service, violates this principle.
