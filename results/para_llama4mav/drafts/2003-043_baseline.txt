The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is contrary to the Internal Revenue Code and relevant case law. The Code requires that a tax be assessed against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return, 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), and defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14). Both a partnership and its individual partners are considered separate "taxpayers" under the Code.

The government's assessment against the partnership in this case did not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, "the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified 'taxpayer,'" and the assessment against the partnership "left unaltered the limitations period applicable to" the individual partners. United States v. Galletti, 298 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir. 2002).

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because he was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D.Cal.1959). However, the court also acknowledged that the government would have had to either assess the tax against the partner individually or bring a court action to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. United States v. Galletti, 298 F.3d at 1114.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson supports the respondents' position. In Coson, the court held that an assessment against a partnership was insufficient to create a lien against the property of an individual partner. 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir.1961). Although the court ultimately affirmed on different grounds, the district court's holding that an assessment against the partnership did not create a lien against the partner's property because the partner had not been assessed individually is instructive. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Under California law, a creditor may not collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The government has not obtained a judgment against the respondents, and the time for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Therefore, the government's claim against the respondents is barred by the statute of limitations.

In conclusion, the government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is contrary to the Internal Revenue Code and relevant case law. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners, and the government's claim against the respondents is therefore barred.
