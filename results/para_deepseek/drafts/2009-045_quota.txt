**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes, but only in the rare and exceptional case where specific evidence in the record demonstrates that the lodestar does not adequately compensate counsel for superior performance and exceptional results.

**I. Supreme Court Precedent Permits Enhancements in Rare and Exceptional Cases**

The lodestar method—multiplying reasonable hours by a reasonable hourly rate—creates a presumptively reasonable fee. *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (*Delaware Valley I*). However, the Court has consistently recognized that upward adjustments remain permissible in “certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* (quoting *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 898-901 (1984)).

In *Blum*, the Court expressly rejected the argument that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” 465 U.S. at 901. The Court explained that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)). The *Blum* Court denied enhancement only because the applicants offered “no evidence” of exceptional results or superior performance. *Id.* at 899. The Court explicitly left open the possibility that “specific evidence” of superior quality and exceptional success could justify an enhancement. *Id.*

*Delaware Valley I* reaffirmed this framework. The Court stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible” in “rare” and “exceptional” cases. 478 U.S. at 565. Although the Court noted that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” it added the critical qualifier “ordinarily,” leaving the door open for extraordinary circumstances. *Id.* at 566. The Court did not overrule *Blum*; it cited *Blum* with approval throughout. *Id.* at 564, 568.

**II. The District Court Properly Applied the Governing Standard**

The district court found that “the superb quality of counsel’s representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court based this finding on “its own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Id.* at 1290. The court also noted its “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge.” *Id.*

This is precisely the type of “specific evidence” that *Blum* requires. 465 U.S. at 899. The district judge, who observed counsel’s performance firsthand, was uniquely positioned to evaluate whether the representation was exceptional. As the Eleventh Circuit recognized, “the district judge has an unparalleled opportunity to observe the attorney’s performance in a given case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1246 (11th Cir. 2008) (Wilson, J., concurring in denial of rehearing en banc). Deference to that judgment is appropriate.

**III. Every Circuit to Consider the Issue Agrees That Enhancements Are Permissible**

There is no circuit split on this question. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all hold that a district court may enhance the lodestar for exceptional performance and results in rare cases. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). This uniform consensus reflects a correct understanding of *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*.

**IV. Petitioners’ Arguments Misread Supreme Court Precedent**

Petitioners argue that *Delaware Valley I* categorically bars enhancements for superior performance. But the Court in that case expressly declined to overrule *Blum* and instead reaffirmed that enhancements remain permissible in “rare” and “exceptional” cases. 478 U.S. at 565. The Court’s statement that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors” does not foreclose enhancements; it merely establishes that the lodestar is the starting point, not the endpoint. *Id.* at 566.

Moreover, the dicta in *Hensley* and *Blum*—that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified”—carries persuasive weight. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 435; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901. This Court should not lightly disregard language that has guided lower courts for decades.

**V. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Not an Abuse of Discretion**

The district court’s enhancement was supported by detailed findings and specific evidence. The court did not rely on “unidentified and unreviewable personal experience,” as petitioners contend. Rather, the court explained that counsel’s performance was exceptional based on its own observation of the litigation, the complexity of the case, and the comprehensive injunction obtained. *Kenny A.*, 454 F. Supp. 2d at 1286, 1290. This is precisely the type of record evidence that *Blum* requires. 465 U.S. at 899.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion. *Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1242. The court of appeals properly deferred to the district court’s superior vantage point, recognizing that “only the district judge can evaluate the attorney’s performance from the day he or she files the complaint to the day the judge enters the order.” *Id.* at 1246 (Wilson, J., concurring).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The district court acted within its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on specific evidence of exceptional performance and results, consistent with *Blum*, *Delaware Valley I*, and the uniform consensus of the federal circuits.
