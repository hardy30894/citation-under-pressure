**ARGUMENT**

The judgment should be reversed because the district court abused its discretion in imposing the most drastic discovery sanction—excluding four expert witnesses—and erred in excluding two additional experts under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), on this record. Those rulings left Appellants without the expert proof necessary to oppose summary judgment, and the resulting judgment cannot stand. See *Little v. Liquid Air Corp.*, 37 F.3d 1069, 1075-76 (5th Cir. 1994) (en banc).

I. **The district court abused its discretion by striking four experts for discovery noncompliance.**

A district court has discretion to manage discovery, but exclusion of evidence as a sanction must be tempered by the principle that cases should be decided on the merits whenever possible. *Cox v. City of Freeman*, 321 F.2d 887, 891 (8th Cir. 1963); *Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs., Inc.*, 765 F.2d 511, 514 (5th Cir. 1985). In this Circuit, the court should consider the availability of lesser sanctions and the degree of prejudice before imposing a sanction that effectively dismisses a claim. *Batson*, 765 F.2d at 514; *United States v. $49,000 Currency*, 330 F.3d 371, 379 (5th Cir. 2003).

Here, the district court entered an order directing expert depositions by July 30, 1993, after Appellants sought a protective order because their experts had not yet finalized opinions. When Appellants did not complete depositions by that date, the court struck four experts. That sanction was disproportionate. The record shows that the dispute centered on timing and expert preparedness, not bad faith concealment of evidence or willful defiance of a fully developed discovery schedule. Where a party’s noncompliance is attributable at least in part to ambiguity or incompleteness in the expert process, exclusion is too severe absent a showing that lesser measures would be ineffective. See *Batson*, 765 F.2d at 514; *Texas A&M Research Found. v. Magna Transp., Inc.*, 338 F.3d 394, 402-03 (5th Cir. 2003).

The court also failed to cabin the sanction to the prejudice actually caused. Discovery sanctions should be proportionate to the harm and should not extend beyond what is necessary to remedy the violation. *In re Terra Int’l, Inc.*, 134 F.3d 302, 306 (5th Cir. 1998). Striking four experts entirely, rather than compelling depositions, reopening deadlines, or imposing costs, extinguished substantial portions of Appellants’ proof and effectively determined the merits without trial. That was an abuse of discretion. *Batson*, 765 F.2d at 514.

II. **The district court misapplied *Daubert* in excluding Drs. Schroeder and Watson.**

Under *Daubert*, the trial court acts as a gatekeeper, but its role is to assess reliability, not to replace the adversary process or weigh competing scientific conclusions. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 589-95; *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 152 (1999); *Moore v. Ashland Chem. Inc.*, 151 F.3d 269, 276 (5th Cir. 1998) (en banc). Expert testimony may be admitted if it is grounded in sufficient facts or data and is the product of reliable principles and methods reliably applied. Fed. R. Evid. 702; *Pipitone v. Biomatrix, Inc.*, 288 F.3d 239, 244-45 (5th Cir. 2002).

The record indicates that Drs. Schroeder and Watson were excluded after the court concluded, under *Daubert*, that their testimony was inadmissible. But nothing in the record suggests that the court conducted the searching reliability analysis required by *Daubert* and its progeny. Reliable expert testimony need not be “scientifically certain” or universally accepted; it need only be sufficiently grounded to assist the trier of fact. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 590; *Huss v. Gayden*, 571 F.3d 442, 452-53 (5th Cir. 2009). Here, Appellants’ experts were offered to prove causation in a toxic-exposure case, precisely the sort of matter where specialized medical and scientific testimony is routinely necessary and where conflicting opinions are ordinarily for the jury to resolve. *Pipitone*, 288 F.3d at 245; *Johnson v. Arkema, Inc.*, 685 F.3d 452, 464-65 (5th Cir. 2012).

III. **Because the expert rulings removed Appellants’ proof, summary judgment was improper.**

Summary judgment is appropriate only if, after adequate discovery, there is no genuine issue of material fact. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56; *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986). Where a party is deprived of essential evidence by erroneous evidentiary rulings, summary judgment should not be affirmed on that basis. *Little*, 37 F.3d at 1075-76. The district court’s orders eliminated six experts who were central to Appellants’ case. Without that testimony, Appellants could not prove exposure, causation, or damages. Because those exclusions were erroneous, the derivative summary judgment must be vacated. *Johnson v. Arkema, Inc.*, 685 F.3d at 464-65; *Pipitone*, 288 F.3d at 244-45.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
