# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Application of a Unitary "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard to Polling Is Not Rationally Related to the Purposes of the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not speak to employer polling; the practice is a creature of Board policy. This Court has long recognized that an employer confronted with objective evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support faces a genuine dilemma: it must choose between continuing to bargain with a union that may no longer represent its employees and risking liability for withdrawing recognition improvidently. See *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969) (discussing the tension between an employer's duty to bargain and its right to test a union's continuing majority status). Polling, no less than an RM election, serves the Act's core purpose of ensuring that bargaining representatives actually represent the employees for whom they purport to speak. *Cf. Medo Photo Supply Corp. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 678, 683–84 (1944) (an employer's dealings with its employees must respect the integrity of the union's status as exclusive bargaining representative).

The Board's decision to impose upon polling the identical evidentiary threshold required for outright withdrawal of recognition—rather than a lesser showing calibrated to the far less drastic character of a poll—cannot be reconciled with the Act's design. An administrative agency's rule is entitled to deference only if "there must be a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962). Here, the Board's own stated rationale—that RM elections are the "preferred" method of testing employee sentiment because they carry procedural safeguards absent from polling—points toward a *lower*, not identical, threshold for the less consequential act of polling. An agency's reasoning must be internally coherent; "the orderly functioning of the process of review requires that the grounds upon which the administrative agency acted be clearly disclosed and adequately sustained." *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943). Collapsing three distinct employer options—withdrawal, RM election, and polling—into one undifferentiated standard, without accounting for their obviously different risks to employees and to the collective-bargaining relationship, is the sort of unreasoned equivalence that *Chenery* forbids.

This Court has likewise cautioned that the Board's freedom to fashion policy is not boundless where it collides with employers' legitimate interests recognized elsewhere in the Act's structure. See *Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945) (Board must accommodate competing statutory interests rather than mechanically apply a single rule). The Board's remedial and procedural discretion must likewise be exercised consistently with the Act's underlying purposes rather than for administrative convenience alone. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941). The Board's insistence on a single "reasonable doubt" threshold for polling, withdrawal, and RM elections alike—while simultaneously declaring elections preferable—reflects exactly the kind of unreasoned, one-size-fits-all policy that these decisions do not sanction.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt about the Union's majority status cannot stand. Review of Board factfinding is not toothless. "Substantial evidence is more than a scintilla, and must do more than create a suspicion of the existence of the fact to be established. It means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). A reviewing court may not treat the Board's resolution of a factual question as conclusive merely because the Board has selected among competing inferences; the inference chosen must itself be reasonable in light of the record as a whole, and the Board may not simply credit the evidence favoring one party while disregarding unrebutted evidence favoring the other. *NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 407–08 (1962) (per curiam).

The Board's treatment of the record here reflects precisely this kind of one-sided factfinding. The ALJ and the Board discounted, one by one, every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown—discrediting Ridgick's and Marsh's statements as equivocal, refusing to credit Bloch's and Mohr's reports of coworker sentiment because the affected employees did not personally testify, and excluding employees no longer on the payroll from the calculus entirely—while crediting nothing that corroborated the Union's continuing support. This selective winnowing of the evidence is the kind of asymmetrical factfinding that this Court's precedents do not permit. Just as an employer may not rest a defense on bare assertion without objective substantiation, *NLRB v. Truitt Mfg. Co.*, 351 U.S. 149, 152–53 (1956) (a party's claims must be tested against the record rather than accepted or rejected on say-so alone), the Board likewise may not reject an employer's proffered evidence of doubt through categorical disbelief untethered to record-based reasoning.

Nor can the Board's approach be justified as ordinary credibility assessment entitled to deference. Where, as here, the Board applies a categorical rule—discounting entire categories of evidence, such as hearsay reports of coworkers' views and statements made during hiring interviews, without individualized assessment—it has not performed the evidentiary weighing that *Consolidated Edison* and *Walton* demand; it has instead erected an evidentiary standard so exacting that no employer's evidence could ever satisfy it. The cumulative, uncontradicted testimony of numerous employees—far more than the Board's crabbed count of seven—demonstrates that Allentown's doubt was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
