**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Opinion Testimony from ADAs and a Police Lieutenant on Credibility, Probable Cause, and the Meaning of the Evidence.**

A trial court abuses its discretion when it admits testimony that invades the province of the jury, vouches for another witness, or supplies legal conclusions instead of facts. *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 142-44 (2d Cir. 1988); *United States v. Garcia*, 413 F.3d 201, 210-11 (2d Cir. 2005); *Andrew v. The Town of Stonington*, 2010 WL 143740, at *5 (2d Cir. Jan. 15, 2010) (summary order). That happened here. The jury heard from two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant—officials clothed with governmental authority—who opined on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, on whether probable cause existed, and on whether the evidence helped or hurt Appellants’ case. Those opinions should have been excluded.

Federal Rule of Evidence 701 permits lay opinion only if it is rationally based on the witness’s perception and helpful to the jury. Fed. R. Evid. 701; *Bank of China, N.Y. Branch v. NBM LLC*, 359 F.3d 171, 180-81 (2d Cir. 2004). It does not permit a witness to tell the jury whom to believe. *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d at 141-44. Nor may a witness announce the legal bottom line. *Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 363-64 (2d Cir. 1992). Nor may one witness bolster another by expressing an opinion that the other is truthful. *United States v. Cruz*, 981 F.2d 659, 664-65 (2d Cir. 1992).

The testimony admitted below crossed all three lines. First, the ADAs and lieutenant vouched for Ramos’s and Rivera’s credibility. This was classic improper bolstering. The Second Circuit has repeatedly held that witness credibility is for the jury, not for other witnesses to certify. *Scop*, 846 F.2d at 142-44; *Cruz*, 981 F.2d at 664-65. Second, the witnesses testified in the form of legal conclusions when they addressed probable cause. That too is impermissible. In *Hygh*, this Court held that expert testimony characterizing conduct as “justified” or “appropriate” invaded the court’s function and the jury’s role. 961 F.2d at 363-64. A fortiori, testimony from law-enforcement and prosecutorial officials that probable cause existed was exactly the sort of legal conclusion the jury should have been left to decide. See also *United States v. Offill*, 666 F.3d 168, 174-75 (4th Cir. 2011) (legal conclusions inadmissible); *United States v. Duncan*, 42 F.3d 97, 101-02 (2d Cir. 1994) (witness may not define legal standards for jury).

Third, the witnesses interpreted the evidence for the jury. But jurors were fully capable of evaluating the photographs, testimony, and sequence of events on their own. Lay witnesses may not “draw conclusions” from evidence that the jury can assess directly. *Bank of China*, 359 F.3d at 181; *United States v. Garcia*, 413 F.3d at 211. The testimony therefore had little or no legitimate evidentiary purpose and great potential for prejudice.

The error was not harmless. A nonconstitutional evidentiary error requires reversal when there is a “fair assurance” it did not influence the verdict. *Tesser v. Board of Educ. of City Sch. Dist. of City of New York*, 370 F.3d 314, 319 (2d Cir. 2004). Here, the improper testimony went directly to the central disputed issues: whether Ramos and Rivera were credible and whether they had probable cause to arrest Cameron and Higgenbottom. That testimony came from prosecutors and a police supervisor, enhancing its prejudicial force. Because the jury’s verdict turned on who to believe, there is no fair assurance the verdict was unaffected. *Scop*, 846 F.2d at 147; *Tesser*, 370 F.3d at 319.

**II. The Court Should Hold That Higgenbottom Was Entitled to an Instruction That “Obstructing Governmental Administration” Requires Lawful Official Action.**

Under New York law, a person commits obstructing governmental administration only when she intentionally obstructs “administration of law or other governmental function” by means of physical interference, and the officer must be engaged in a lawful function. N.Y. Penal Law § 195.05; *People v. Case*, 42 N.Y.2d 98, 101-03, 396 N.Y.S.2d 841, 365 N.E.2d 872 (1977); *People v. Offen*, 96 N.Y.2d 149, 152-53, 726 N.Y.S.2d 100, 750 N.E.2d 499 (2001). Where the lawfulness of the underlying police conduct is disputed, the jury must be instructed accordingly. *People v. Kisoon*, 8 N.Y.3d 129, 134-35, 831 N.Y.S.2d 880, 863 N.E.2d 1010 (2007). Because Higgenbottom’s arrest theory depended on whether Ramos and Rivera were lawfully arresting Cameron, the requested instruction was necessary.

**III. The Court Should Also Hold That a Punitive Damages Instruction Was Warranted.**

Punitive damages are available under § 1983 when a defendant acts with “evil motive or intent” or with “reckless or callous indifference” to federally protected rights. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983); *King v. Macri*, 993 F.2d 294, 297-98 (2d Cir. 1993). Under New York law, punitive damages similarly require conduct showing malice, fraud, or wanton dishonesty. *Ross v. Louise Wise Servs., Inc.*, 8 N.Y.3d 478, 489, 836 N.Y.S.2d 509, 868 N.E.2d 189 (2007). Appellants’ evidence—that Ramos and Rivera knowingly fabricated the basis for the arrests and prosecution—would permit a reasonable jury to infer malice. The district court therefore should have instructed on punitive damages, and the issue should be preserved for retrial.
