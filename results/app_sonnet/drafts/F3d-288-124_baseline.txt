# ARGUMENT

## I. BRYANT HAS STANDING TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD.

The district court erred in holding that Bryant lacked standing to seek enforcement of the arbitration award he obtained against Bell Atlantic. Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 185(a), confers a federal cause of action for violation of contracts between an employer and a labor organization, and the Supreme Court has long recognized that an individual employee, not merely the union, may invoke that remedy. In *Smith v. Evening News Ass'n*, 371 U.S. 195 (1962), the Court held that an employee may maintain a § 301 suit against his employer for breach of a collective bargaining agreement notwithstanding the union's own right to sue, because "the existence of a company grievance settlement does not deprive an employee of the right to seek judicial enforcement." *Id.* at 200. The Court reaffirmed this principle in *General Drivers, Warehousemen & Helpers Local Union No. 89 v. Riss & Co.*, 372 U.S. 517 (1963), holding that a party to a labor arbitration award may bring an independent action under § 301 to compel compliance with that award.

Bryant prevailed in arbitration on his grievance under the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA. Bell Atlantic's failure to comply with that award constitutes an actionable breach of the contract, and Bryant, as the aggrieved employee and beneficiary of the award, is a proper party to enforce it. The district court's conclusion that only the CWA possessed enforcement standing conflicts with *Smith* and *Riss* and should be reversed.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT FAILED TO APPLY THE SUPREME COURT'S CLARIFICATION OF THE McDONNELL DOUGLAS BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK.

Under the familiar burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), a Title VII or Section 1981 plaintiff who establishes a prima facie case of discrimination shifts the burden to the employer to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for the challenged action; the plaintiff must then be afforded the opportunity to show that the employer's stated reason was pretextual. *Id.* at 802–04. The district court, however, appears to have required Bryant to independently prove discriminatory intent even after he produced evidence sufficient to discredit Bell Atlantic's proffered justification—precisely the additional burden that the Supreme Court rejected in *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000).

In *Reeves*, the Court squarely held that "a plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated." *Id.* at 148. The Court explained that once the employer's explanation has been shown to be unworthy of credence, "it is permissible for the trier of fact to infer the ultimate fact of discrimination from the falsity of the employer's explanation." *Id.* at 147 (quoting *St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993)). No separate, independent evidence of discriminatory animus is categorically required.

Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic's stated rationale for disciplining and terminating him—rigid, inflexible enforcement of the Red Letter Day policy—was pretextual: white and male employees with comparable scheduling conflicts were afforded accommodations that Bryant, an African-American single father, was denied or given only nominally. The district court's failure to recognize that this pretext evidence alone could sustain Bryant's claim, without additional independent proof of animus, contravenes *Reeves* and requires reversal.

## III. GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT PRECLUDE SUMMARY JUDGMENT.

Summary judgment is appropriate only where there is no genuine issue of material fact, and the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, would not permit a reasonable jury to find in his favor. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322–23 (1986). A genuine issue exists whenever "the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party." *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 248. The court must not weigh the evidence or resolve credibility disputes at summary judgment. *Id.* at 249.

The record here reveals precisely such disputed facts. Bryant offered evidence that Bell Atlantic refused the CWA president's request to allow co-workers to cover Bryant's Red Letter Day assignments, while similarly situated non-minority or male employees received more flexible accommodations for scheduling conflicts. Bryant also presented evidence that his difficulty meeting overtime obligations stemmed from documented child care responsibilities that Bell Atlantic's "accommodation" options failed realistically to address. Whether Bell Atlantic's disciplinary actions and ultimate termination were motivated by legitimate business necessity or by discriminatory and retaliatory animus toward Bryant turns on credibility determinations and inferences that are for a jury, not the court, to make. *See Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255 ("Credibility determinations, the weighing of the evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge.").

Because genuine disputes of material fact remain as to pretext, disparate treatment, and retaliatory motive, the district court's grant of summary judgment was improper.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for further proceedings consistent with *Smith*, *Reeves*, and *Anderson*.
