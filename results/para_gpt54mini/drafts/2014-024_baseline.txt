## Argument

### I. Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten.

Section 875(c) makes it a crime to transmit “any communication containing any threat” to injure another person. 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). The statute says nothing about negligence or an objective “reasonable person” standard. When Congress omits a mens rea term, courts presume at least a culpable mental state is required. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–63 (1952); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605–19 (1994); *Carter v. United States*, 530 U.S. 255, 267–69 (2000). That presumption is especially strong where, as here, the statute carries substantial criminal punishment and reaches speech. *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723, 734–35 (2015).

This Court has already recognized that a “true threat” is a statement “expressing an intent to inflict injury in the present or future.” *United States v. Stock*, 728 F.3d 287, 293 (3d Cir. 2013). But the question is not merely whether words can be read as threatening by a listener; it is whether Congress intended to punish only those who themselves meant to threaten. Under the ordinary rule that criminal statutes require “guilty mind,” *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 251, § 875(c) cannot be read to impose felony liability for careless, accidental, or misunderstood speech. See also *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 737–38.

The Third Circuit’s rule—liability whenever a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat—dispenses with subjective culpability. That approach conflicts with the Supreme Court’s repeated insistence that criminal statutes should not be construed to “dispense with a conventional mens rea element.” *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 605; *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 70–72 (1994). The Court in *Elonis* rejected the proposition that negligence is enough under § 875(c), holding that “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal.” *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 734 (quoting *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 252). The same reasoning requires proof that the defendant intended his communication as a threat, or at least knew it would be so understood. *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 737–38.

### II. Virginia v. Black confirms that “true threats” are excluded from First Amendment protection because the speaker means to threaten.

In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Court explained that “true threats” encompass statements where the speaker “means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Id.* at 359. The Court further held that the First Amendment permits punishment of such threats because they place the victim in fear and “protect[ ] individuals from the fear of violence.” *Id.* at 360. That rationale turns on the speaker’s purpose in communicating a threat, not merely on how a reasonable listener might react.

The Third Circuit read *Black* too narrowly. Although *Black* invalidated a Virginia statute’s cross-burning presumption, it did not approve a purely objective test for all threat prosecutions. To the contrary, the Court’s description of true threats repeatedly centers on what the speaker “means.” *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The Court later reiterated that protected speech ends where the speaker deliberately communicates a threat. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707–08 (1969) (per curiam). An objective-only test risks punishing hyperbole, irony, political ranting, artistic expression, and other protected speech—precisely the danger present here, where the government relied on Facebook posts, rap-style lyrics, and obvious self-conscious references to “true threat jurisprudence.”

### III. The First Amendment and due process both forbid conviction without proof of subjective intent.

A criminal law that punishes speech must give clear notice of what conduct is forbidden and avoid chilling protected expression. *FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc.*, 567 U.S. 239, 253–54 (2012); *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108–09 (1972). An objective standard under § 875(c) fails that basic requirement because speakers cannot reliably know whether a jury will later deem their words threatening from the standpoint of a hypothetical reasonable person. The Constitution does not permit felony punishment to turn on such after-the-fact guesswork.

This Court should hold that conviction under § 875(c) requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant intended his communication as a threat, or at minimum knew that it would be understood that way. That rule best accords with the statute’s text, the presumption of mens rea, and the First Amendment limits recognized in *Black* and *Watts*. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
