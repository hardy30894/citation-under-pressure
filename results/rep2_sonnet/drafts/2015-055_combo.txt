**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Rule Announced in Johnson Applies Retroactively Because It Is a Substantive Rule That Narrows the Government's Power to Punish.**

Since its earliest exercises of the habeas power, this Court has recognized that the writ reaches convictions and sentences imposed under laws the government never had authority to enforce. In *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193 (1830), Chief Justice Marshall held that habeas corpus tests whether the judgment under which a prisoner is held was one the court had jurisdiction to render. That principle was reaffirmed in *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), where the Court held that a prisoner could challenge his conviction on habeas because an unconstitutional statute "is not a law" and confers no power to punish. The same logic controls here: because the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague, it never validly authorized the fifteen-year mandatory minimum imposed on Mr. Welch, and habeas is the proper vehicle to test that want of authority.

This Court's later habeas jurisprudence confirms that the writ's central office is to police the outer boundary of the State's power to convict and sentence. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court explained that "the writ of habeas corpus has always been a fundamental instrument for safeguarding individual freedom against arbitrary and lawless state action," *id.* at 401-02, and that its function is not merely procedural correction but the vindication of the prisoner's right not to be held under an invalid judgment. Johnson's holding—that the residual clause's methodology is "unconstitutionally vague," *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. ___ (2015)—goes precisely to that question: whether the ACCA ever validly authorized Mr. Welch's enhanced sentence at all.

**II. Under This Court's Traditional Retroactivity Analysis, Rules That Go to the Integrity of the Underlying Judgment—Rather Than Merely Regulating Trial Procedure—Must Be Applied Retroactively.**

Before crafting any categorical bar to retroactivity, this Court asked whether a new constitutional rule was the kind that affected "the very integrity of the fact-finding process," or the government's basic authority to impose punishment, as opposed to a rule of purely prophylactic or procedural character. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), first announced that "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect," *id.* at 629, and directed courts to weigh the purpose of the new rule, reliance on the old rule, and the effect on the administration of justice. Applying that framework, *Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406 (1966), and *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966), gave retroactive effect to rules bearing on the reliability and legitimacy of the conviction itself, while withholding retroactivity from rules that were merely designed to deter future police misconduct. *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), and *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969), applied the same distinction.

Johnson's rule falls squarely on the retroactive side of that line. It does not create a new trial procedure or a new method of gathering evidence; it holds that an entire category of sentence enhancement was void from the moment of its enactment because it failed to give ordinary people fair notice of what conduct would trigger a mandatory fifteen-year minimum. That defect concerns the very legitimacy of the punishment, not merely the process by which guilt was found—precisely the kind of rule *Linkletter*, *Tehan*, and *Johnson v. New Jersey* recognized must be given retroactive effect.

**III. Johnson's Vagueness Holding Is Analogous to Due Process Vagueness Decisions That This Court Has Applied to Persons Already Convicted or Sentenced.**

This Court's void-for-vagueness precedents confirm that a decision holding a penal provision unconstitutionally indefinite operates upon the underlying authority to punish, not merely upon future proceedings. In *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225 (1957), the Court held that due process forbids punishing conduct under a standard that fails to give fair warning, vacating the very conviction before it. In *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347 (1964), the Court likewise applied its vagueness holding to the parties in the case then before it, recognizing that a statute so indefinite that it fails to inform persons of ordinary intelligence what is prohibited cannot be the basis for punishment, retroactively or otherwise. Johnson's residual-clause holding rests on the identical due process defect: the clause required courts to imagine "the hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version" of an offense, a standard incapable of principled application. Because that defect infects the very authority to impose the ACCA enhancement, it cannot be confined to defendants who happened to raise the claim before final judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, and consistent with this Court's long-settled understanding of the writ's function in testing the government's authority to punish, the rule of *Johnson v. United States* should be held to apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, including Mr. Welch's.
