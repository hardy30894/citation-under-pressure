ARGUMENT

**I. The Tribal Court Denied Selam His Right to Compulsory Process Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.**

The right to compulsory process is a fundamental element of due process. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) (holding that the right to compel witnesses is “in plain terms the right to present a defense”). The tribal court failed to ensure that Selam could exercise this right. Although the judge and counsel advised Selam of his right to subpoena witnesses, they did not take adequate steps to secure his ability to do so. Selam provided a list of five potential character witnesses, but his appointed lay spokesperson, Patricia Leno-Baker, could not locate them without his assistance. Selam made excuses, but the court did not intervene to compel attendance or to ensure that Selam understood the consequences of inaction. A waiver of a fundamental right must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). Selam’s failure to request subpoenas, without a clear showing that he knowingly and intelligently waived his right, cannot constitute a valid waiver. *See Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (holding that the duty to protect a defendant’s rights rests on the court, not solely on the defendant). Because no defense witnesses were presented, Selam was effectively denied the opportunity to present a defense, violating the compulsory process guarantee of the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) (due process requires “an opportunity to be heard” and to present evidence).

**II. The Tribal Court Denied Selam His Right to Confrontation Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.**

The Confrontation Clause of the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), guarantees the right “to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” This right is fundamental and applies to tribal proceedings. *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 403 (1965) (holding that the right of confrontation is a fundamental right). In the second trial, the five-year-old victim did not testify. Instead, the prosecution introduced her out-of-court statements through four witnesses: Dr. Beamer, the victim’s parents, and Investigator Clements. Selam had no opportunity to cross-examine the child. The Confrontation Clause requires that hearsay testimony be admitted only if the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. *Barber v. Page*, 390 U.S. 719, 722 (1968). Here, the prosecution made no showing that the child was unavailable, and Selam had no prior opportunity to cross-examine her. The admission of these statements violated Selam’s confrontation right. *Douglas v. Alabama*, 380 U.S. 415, 418 (1965) (holding that the use of a witness’s out-of-court statement without opportunity for cross-examination violates the Confrontation Clause).

**III. Selam Did Not Waive His Confrontation Claim by Failing to Raise It in the Tribal Court of Appeals.**

The district court held that Selam waived his confrontation claim by not exhausting it in the tribal appellate court. However, exhaustion of tribal remedies is not required when those remedies are inadequate or when the petitioner did not deliberately bypass them. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 117 (1944) (exhaustion excused if state remedy is “plainly inadequate”). Selam’s compulsory process argument in the tribal appeal—that he was denied the right to present witnesses—substantially overlapped with his confrontation claim. Both concern the right to challenge the prosecution’s evidence. The tribal court of appeals dismissed Selam’s appeal without a hearing, indicating that it did not provide a full and fair opportunity to litigate any issue. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963) (habeas relief not barred by failure to raise claim in state court unless the petitioner “deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts”). Selam did not deliberately bypass tribal remedies; he raised a related claim and received no meaningful review. Moreover, waiver of a constitutional right must be knowing and intelligent. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464. Selam, represented by a lay spokesperson, cannot be deemed to have knowingly waived a separate confrontation claim when the tribal court itself did not distinguish between the two rights. The district court erred in finding waiver.

**IV. Conclusion**

For these reasons, Selam’s petition for habeas corpus should be granted. The tribal court violated his rights to compulsory process and confrontation under the Indian Civil Rights Act. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
