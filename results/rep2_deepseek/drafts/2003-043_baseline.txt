**ARGUMENT**

The Internal Revenue Code mandates that the government must assess each individual partner separately to invoke the 10-year statute of limitations for collection. The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the disallowance of the IRS’s claims because the IRS assessed only the partnership, not the individual partners.

**I. The Statutory Framework Requires Separate Assessment of Each Taxpayer**

Sections 6203 and 6502 of the Internal Revenue Code require that an assessment be made against a specific “taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203 (“assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer”). The Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). An individual partner is a separate taxpayer from the partnership. Each has its own taxpayer identification number. By its plain terms, § 6502(a) extends the statute of limitations to ten years “where the assessment of any tax . . . has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto.” That extension applies only to the taxpayer against whom the assessment was made—here, the partnership. The partners were never assessed. Therefore, the three-year limitations period under § 6501(a) governs claims against them, and it expired long before the IRS filed its proofs of claim.

The implementing regulation confirms that a valid assessment must “provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The IRS identified only the partnership. It did not identify the individual partners. The IRS’s failure to assess the partners within the three-year period bars collection from them. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien cannot exist against an individual’s property unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against that individual under § 6203).

**II. The IRS’s Reliance on Case Law Is Misplaced**

The IRS points to *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), but that case does not support its position. In *Young*, the partner had already paid the tax and sought a refund. The district court held that the partner could not recover a refund because “the government would have been entitled to collect the same tax . . . had the partner refused to pay it.” *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959). The court expressly noted that “to collect the tax for which the partner was liable, the IRS would have had to either resort to court action or individually assess the taxes against the partner.” *Id.* at 76,050. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, stating only that the partner was “personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership, including its tax liability.” *Young*, 283 F.2d at 910. That statement does not hold that the IRS could collect without an individual assessment or judgment. Indeed, *Coson* later clarified that an assessment against the partnership alone does not create a lien against a partner’s property. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is equally unavailing. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the statute of limitations for the partners was tolled during the partnership’s bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. The Seventh Circuit had no occasion to decide whether an assessment against the partnership alone extends the limitations period for partners. *Id.* at 563. Here, no assessment was made against the partners, so *Wright* is inapposite.

**III. California Law Does Not Provide an Independent Basis for Collection**

The IRS argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, so it may collect directly from the partners without an individual assessment or judgment. But California law explicitly requires a judgment against the partner before a creditor may satisfy a partnership debt from the partner’s personal assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) (“A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”). The IRS has obtained no judgment against the partners. The time to do so has expired because the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a) has run. The IRS’s assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period only for the partnership, not for the partners. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The bankruptcy court properly disallowed the IRS’s claims as unenforceable under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

**CONCLUSION**

The government cannot collect a partnership’s unpaid taxes from individual partners without first assessing the partners individually or obtaining a judgment against them. The IRS failed to do either within the three-year limitations period. The decision of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
