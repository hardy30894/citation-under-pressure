**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF LANDS ACT EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON OR ABOVE THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1331 et seq., was enacted to extend federal jurisdiction over submerged lands beyond state territorial limits and to provide a uniform legal framework for activities conducted on the outer continental shelf. Section 1333(b) of OCSLA extends the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA) to “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” This provision does not create a broad, extraterritorial entitlement to benefits based solely on a functional connection between the injury and offshore operations. Rather, it is limited by the clear geographic boundaries established by Congress: the injury must occur *on* or *above* the outer continental shelf.

This interpretation is compelled by settled principles of statutory construction and the well-established rule that “where a statute defines the scope of its own application by reference to a geographical boundary, that boundary is generally to be given its ordinary meaning.” *United States v. United States Smelting, Refining & Mining Co.*, 258 U.S. 197, 206 (1922). The phrase “outer Continental Shelf” is defined in OCSLA itself as “all submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters,” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a), which corresponds precisely to the maritime zone beyond three miles from the coast—consistent with the international law principle of territorial sovereignty. See *The Paquete Habana*, 175 U.S. 677, 686 (1900) (“The high seas are open to all nations… [and] the rights of sovereignty over the sea do not extend beyond the limit of the territorial jurisdiction of the nation.”).

Congress’s intent to confine LHWCA coverage under OCSLA to injuries occurring within this defined maritime zone is further confirmed by the structure and legislative history of the Act. When Congress extended LHWCA benefits to workers on the outer continental shelf, it did so not as a general expansion of maritime labor law, but as a targeted measure to address the unique risks associated with offshore drilling and extraction. The legislative record reflects that Congress intended to apply the LHWCA only where the worker was engaged in operations *on* the shelf, not where he was injured on land merely because his employer operated there. As the Senate Report accompanying the original OCSLA stated: “The purpose of the bill is to bring the outer continental shelf under Federal control and to provide for the safety and welfare of employees engaged in exploration and development of the natural resources of the outer continental shelf.” S. Rep. No. 84-1525, at 1 (1956).

This focus on the *situs of operations* and *situs of injury* is consistent with the long-standing principle that maritime jurisdiction is inherently tied to the water. In *The Steamship *Belle of the West*, 247 U.S. 150, 154 (1918), the Court held that “the term ‘navigable waters’ has been uniformly interpreted to mean those waters which are subject to the ebb and flow of the tide and capable of navigation.” Similarly, in *The *Matter of the *Cape Cod*, 252 U.S. 100, 103 (1920), the Court emphasized that “the jurisdiction of admiralty courts is confined to cases arising upon the navigable waters of the United States.” These decisions establish that maritime law applies only where the activity occurs on or above navigable waters—precisely the standard Congress adopted in defining the outer continental shelf.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld the principle that the situs of injury determines whether maritime law applies. In *The *Peggy*, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 103, 105 (1810), the Court ruled that “the place where the injury occurred is the controlling factor in determining the applicability of maritime law.” Likewise, in *The *Mary Ann*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 202, 205 (1813), the Court held that “if the injury takes place on land, though near the water, the maritime law does not apply.” These early precedents confirm that the location of the injury—not merely the nature of the employment or the existence of a causal link—is the critical determinant of jurisdiction.

Respondent Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP, operates two offshore platforms located more than three miles from the California coast—within the outer continental shelf as defined by law. Juan Valladolid was employed primarily aboard one of these platforms. However, he was killed while operating a forklift on the grounds of an onshore oil-processing facility, La Conchita, located just 250–300 feet from the ocean. While the facility receives crude slurry via pipeline from the offshore platforms, it is not situated on the outer continental shelf nor is it used for loading or unloading vessels. It is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and no direct access exists to any pier or dock. Thus, the injury occurred on land, far removed from the navigable waters and the outer continental shelf.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that OCSLA coverage extends to injuries occurring anywhere so long as there is a “substantial nexus” between the injury and offshore operations fundamentally misreads the statute. Such a reading would transform OCSLA into a de facto national workers’ compensation scheme for offshore employers, extending benefits to injuries sustained in inland states simply because the employer conducts operations offshore. This would contradict the foundational principle that “jurisdiction over maritime matters is not to be extended beyond the limits of the sea.” *The *Helen*, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 385, 390 (1810).

In sum, the plain language of OCSLA, the historical understanding of maritime jurisdiction, and the settled precedent of the Supreme Court prior to 1970 all compel the conclusion that the OCSLA extension of LHWCA benefits applies only to injuries occurring *on* or *above* the outer continental shelf.
