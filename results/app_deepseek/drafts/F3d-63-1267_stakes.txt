**ARGUMENT**

**I. Stelwagon Failed to Prove Actual Antitrust Damages, Precluding Any Recovery Under the Clayton Act.**

A plaintiff seeking treble damages under Section 4 of the Clayton Act must prove “actual injury” that is “attributable to” the alleged price discrimination. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). This requires a causal link between the discriminatory pricing and the claimed loss—not merely lost sales or profits that could have resulted from other market factors. *Id.* at 565. Stelwagon’s damages evidence was entirely speculative and failed to isolate the effect of Tarmac’s pricing from other variables, such as Stelwagon’s own decision to stop marketing non-Tarmac products or general market conditions. Its expert’s methodology—comparing Stelwagon’s actual profits to a hypothetical “but-for” world—did not account for these confounding factors and thus provided no reasonable basis for estimating antitrust damages. *See Big Apple BMW, Inc. v. BMW of North America, Inc.*, 974 F.2d 1358, 1363 (3d Cir. 1992) (damages must be based on “a just and reasonable estimate” and not “speculation or guesswork”). Because Stelwagon failed to present sufficient proof that the price discrimination caused its alleged losses, the district court erred in denying Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on damages. The trebled award must be vacated.

**II. Stelwagon Also Failed to Establish a Prima Facie Violation of the Robinson-Patman Act.**

Even if damages were proven, Stelwagon’s Robinson-Patman claim fails because it did not show that Tarmac’s pricing had a “probable effect” on competition. *Falls City Industries, Inc. v. Vanco Beverage, Inc.*, 460 U.S. 428, 435 (1983). Section 2(a) requires proof of competitive injury—either primary-line (harm to competitors) or secondary-line (harm to disfavored buyers). Stelwagon offered only evidence that it lost some sales to competitors who received lower prices. But lost sales alone do not demonstrate a substantial lessening of competition; the plaintiff must show that the discrimination “may substantially lessen competition” or “tend to create a monopoly.” *Brooke Group Ltd. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.*, 509 U.S. 209, 225 (1993). Stelwagon presented no evidence that Tarmac’s pricing threatened competition in the Philadelphia MAPs market, that Stelwagon was forced to exit the market, or that consumers suffered. The district court therefore erred in denying Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on liability.

**III. The Breach of Contract Claim Is Unenforceable and the Damages Are Speculative.**

The alleged oral exclusive distributorship agreement is unenforceable under the Uniform Commercial Code’s statute of frauds, which requires a writing for contracts for the sale of goods for $500 or more. *See* 13 Pa. Cons. Stat. § 2201(a). The agreement involved the sale of Tarmac’s MAPs—goods—and the value of the exclusive distributorship far exceeded $500. No writing exists. Moreover, the agreement lacked definiteness: the parties never agreed on essential terms such as duration, pricing, or volume. *See Channel Home Centers v. Grossman*, 795 F.2d 291, 298 (3d Cir. 1986) (enforcing oral exclusive dealing agreement only if terms are sufficiently definite). Even if enforceable, the district court’s remitted damages of $74,242 are unsupported. Stelwagon’s claimed lost profits were based on the same speculative methodology rejected for the antitrust claim, and the court’s reduction to a “reasonable” figure was arbitrary. The contract damages should be reversed.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed, and judgment entered for Tarmac on all claims. Alternatively, the treble damages award should be vacated and the contract damages reversed.
