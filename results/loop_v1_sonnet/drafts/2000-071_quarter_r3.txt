# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building, unlike the streets and parks that have traditionally served as venues for public assembly and debate, see *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), is a facility of limited and specific dedication. During the school day it is dedicated to education; after hours, Milford's Community Use Policy opens it only to social, civic, and recreational uses benefiting the community, as reflected in the Policy's own terms in the record. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains substantial authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. This Court has recognized that a government entity may control the use of property under its charge for the purposes to which that property is lawfully dedicated, and need not throw the property open to every use a citizen might wish to make of it simply because access has been granted for some limited purposes. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966). Just as a State may require a permit for the use of public parks and streets, and may deny that permit where the proposed use falls outside the terms on which access is granted, so long as the requirement is applied evenhandedly and is not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas, *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), Milford may likewise exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it has opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. Milford's Community Use Policy operates evenhandedly: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. This Court held in *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), that government may not compose or sanction a prayer for schoolchildren to recite as part of a program carried on by the state. Nor may the State sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting; the state must remain neutral, favoring neither religion nor its absence. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. This Court struck down the use of public school buildings for religious instruction conducted by outside religious teachers even where attendance at the program was not formally compelled, reasoning that the use of the tax-supported school system to facilitate religious instruction on school premises offended the Establishment Clause. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948).

To be sure, the Establishment Clause does not require government to be actively hostile toward religion, and this Court has upheld regulation whose incidental effect happens to coincide with the tenets of some religions where the government's purpose and practice remain secular and neutral. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961). Even so, neutrality cuts against Milford's opening its own building, during the hours the school itself controls, to a program of prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion aimed at the very children enrolled at that school. This Court has likewise recognized that the State may extend indirect benefits to religious and secular activities alike without running afoul of the Establishment Clause only where the benefit is genuinely neutral in its design and application. *Board of Educ. v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities—prayer, scripture memorization, and an invitation to personal salvation directed at grade-school children on school premises immediately after the school day—Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
