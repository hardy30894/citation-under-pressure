**ARGUMENT**

I. State courts possess subject-matter jurisdiction over this legal-malpractice action because it does not “aris[e] under” federal patent law within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).

Section 1338(a) grants federal courts exclusive jurisdiction over civil actions “arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents.” The phrase “arising under” carries the same meaning as in 28 U.S.C. § 1331. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988). A state-law claim therefore falls within § 1338(a) only when “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Id.* at 809.

Minton’s malpractice claim is created by Texas common law. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Jurisdiction therefore turns on whether the claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

The experimental-use exception to the on-sale bar, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), is necessary to Minton’s “case within a case” and is actually disputed. But it is not substantial in the *Grable* sense. The issue is “fact-bound and situation-specific,” turning on the particular lease between Minton and Stark and the evidence available to counsel at the time of the underlying litigation. *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006). Its resolution “will not control numerous other cases” and will not affect the validity or scope of any patent. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313.

Moreover, exercising federal jurisdiction here would upset the congressionally approved balance. Legal-malpractice claims have traditionally been the province of state courts. *See, e.g., Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 334, 339 (5th Cir. 2008). Allowing every malpractice suit that touches patent litigation to be removed would “herald a potentially enormous shift of traditionally state cases into federal courts.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319. The Federal Circuit’s contrary decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), failed to perform this federalism analysis and therefore provide no persuasive guidance.

Because the patent issue embedded in Minton’s malpractice claim is not substantial and federal adjudication would disturb the federal-state balance, the claim does not arise under § 1338(a). The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
