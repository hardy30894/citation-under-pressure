# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING AMERICAN STEAMSHIP'S EXPERT OPINION CONCERNING THE CAUSE OF THE LINE'S PARTING

The district court abused its discretion in admitting the testimony of American Steamship's expert, Thomas R. Anderson, regarding the cause of the manila line's parting. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, expert testimony is admissible only if it "will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue." *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993). The Supreme Court has emphasized that the trial court must serve as a "gatekeeper" to ensure that expert testimony is both relevant and reliable. *Id.* at 597.

Here, Anderson's opinion that the line parted due to pre-existing damage was not grounded in any testing, inspection of the line, or established methodology. Rather, Anderson admitted he had never examined the line and could not identify any specific defect. His opinion was pure speculation, which this Court has consistently rejected. *See Turpin v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 959 F.2d 1349, 1360 (6th Cir. 1992) (expert testimony must be based on more than subjective belief or unsupported speculation).

Moreover, Anderson's opinion addressed the ultimate issue of causation, yet he lacked the factual foundation necessary to render such an opinion. In *Smithers v. United States*, 53 F.3d 726 (6th Cir. 1995), this Court held that an expert's testimony must be excluded when it is "unsupported by sufficient facts or data." Here, Anderson's opinion was based on nothing more than his assumption that the line must have been damaged because it parted—a classic exercise in circular reasoning.

The admission of this testimony was highly prejudicial. The jury was presented with an "expert" whose conclusions were unsupported by evidence, lending an unwarranted appearance of scientific reliability to the defendant's theory of the case. This error alone requires reversal.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK'S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON UNSEAWORTHINESS

The district court should have granted Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. The doctrine of unseaworthiness imposes an absolute duty on the shipowner to provide a vessel and its appurtenances that are reasonably fit for their intended use. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). A vessel is unseaworthy if any part of its equipment is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 102 (1944).

The undisputed evidence demonstrates that the boom's bearings contained summer grease that had frozen in the sub-zero temperatures, preventing the boom from pivoting. This required Cook to de-ice the boom while simultaneously being responsible for his own safety. The failure to use appropriate winter grease made the vessel's equipment unfit for its intended purpose. *See United States v. N.S. & Co., Inc.*, 1992 A.M.C. 1119 (6th Cir. 1992) (frozen equipment constitutes unseaworthiness).

Furthermore, the ship's master ordered the crew to delay rigging until the vessel had passed the harbor breakers, leaving only fifteen to twenty minutes to complete the operation. This created a foreseeable risk that crew members would be forced to work under compressed time constraints with equipment that had not been properly maintained for winter conditions.

The evidence was overwhelming that the vessel was unseaworthy, and Cook was entitled to judgment as a matter of law on this issue. The district court's denial of the motion was error.

## III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK'S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE

The district court also erred in submitting the issue of comparative negligence to the jury. Under the Jones Act, the burden of proving contributory negligence rests with the defendant. *See Johnson v. Offshore Exploration Co.*, 645 F.2d 5 (6th Cir. 1981). The defendant must present "substantial evidence" that the plaintiff's negligence was a cause of his injury. *See Joia v. Jo-Ja Service Corp.*, 817 F.2d 908, 916 (1st Cir. 1987).

Here, American Steamship presented no evidence that Cook acted negligently. Cook was assigned the task of de-icing the boom and rigging the bos'n's chair under conditions dictated by the master's orders. He was working with equipment that had been improperly maintained for winter conditions. There was no evidence that Cook used the torch improperly, failed to inspect the line, or otherwise departed from reasonable care.

The defendant cannot establish comparative negligence merely because Cook was injured while performing his duties. To hold otherwise would effectively nullify the protections of the Jones Act and the doctrine of unseaworthiness.

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK'S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS

Because American Steamship presented no evidence connecting Cook's alcohol-related ailments to the cause of his fall, the admission of this evidence was arguably irrelevant and prejudicial. However, even assuming the district court properly admitted this evidence, the other errors above independently require reversal.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial. On remand, the district court should be instructed to grant Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness, and to exclude the speculative expert testimony regarding the cause of the line's parting.
