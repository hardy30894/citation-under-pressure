**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not invalidate an uncounseled tribal court conviction used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Constitution’s right to counsel does not apply to tribal court proceedings, and the use of a prior conviction—valid under the law that governed it—to establish an element of a recidivist offense violates no constitutional right.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Constrain Tribal Courts**

Indian tribes are “distinct, independent political communities” retaining sovereign powers predating the Constitution. *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515, 559 (1832). They are not “States of the Union” and are not subject to the Bill of Rights. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896) (Fifth Amendment grand jury requirement inapplicable to tribal courts). The Sixth Amendment, like the Fifth, “does not apply to Indian tribes.” *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 381 (1886) (tribes as “domestic dependent nations”); *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 571 (1883) (tribal sovereignty not constrained by federal constitutional guarantees absent congressional action). Congress, through the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, chose to provide a limited right to counsel in tribal court—but that Act does not mirror the Sixth Amendment, and it expressly permits uncounseled misdemeanor adjudications. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)(6) (1970 ed.). The tribal court convictions here complied with federal law, and the Sixth Amendment is irrelevant to their validity.

**II. Using a Valid Prior Conviction as a Predicate Offense Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment**

Recidivist statutes do not punish the prior offense; they “penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895). The prior conviction is a historical fact, not a new determination of guilt. *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616, 623-24 (1912) (habitual criminal law not ex post facto because it “does not punish the offender for the prior offense, but for the last one”). The government may prove that fact by introducing a certified record of the conviction. *See, e.g., Carlesi v. New York*, 233 U.S. 51, 57 (1914) (prior federal conviction admissible in state recidivist proceeding). The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches to the *current* prosecution, ensuring a fair trial for the offense charged. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963). It does not require relitigation of the constitutional adequacy of a prior adjudication that was valid under the law that governed it. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938) (counsel required in federal court, but not retroactively for convictions already final). Because the tribal court convictions were valid under the ICRA and the tribe’s sovereign authority, they are reliable enough to establish a fact—the defendant’s status as a recidivist—without violating the Sixth Amendment.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Guilt-Phase Distinction Lacks Precedent**

The Ninth Circuit held that an uncounseled tribal conviction can be used for sentencing but not to prove an element of a felony. *See Concurrence in Denial of Rehearing En Banc* at 6-7. No pre-1970 decision of this Court supports that distinction. The Court has consistently treated the use of a prior conviction as a factual predicate, whether at sentencing or as an element of a recidivist offense, without requiring counsel in the prior proceeding. *Moore*, 159 U.S. at 677; *Graham*, 224 U.S. at 624. The reliability of a prior conviction is determined by the law that authorized it, not by the function it serves in a later prosecution. *See Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942) (prior to *Gideon*, right to counsel not universal; convictions valid if fundamentally fair). *Gideon* did not retroactively invalidate all prior uncounseled convictions; it only required counsel for future trials. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342. The tribal court convictions here were valid when entered, and Congress has expressly authorized their use as predicates in § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit’s rule imposes a Sixth Amendment requirement on tribal courts that this Court has never recognized.

**IV. The Decision Undermines Tribal Sovereignty and Congressional Intent**

Congress, in enacting § 117(a), relied on the settled rule that tribal court convictions are entitled to comity and respect. *See Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997) (applying comity principles). This Court has long held that federal courts must “be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence.” *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (citing *Talton*, 163 U.S. at 384). The Ninth Circuit’s decision disregards that principle, requiring tribal courts to adopt procedures identical to federal courts. *See Concurrence in Denial of Rehearing En Banc* at 14 (noting the court “imposes judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts”). This Court has rejected such paternalism, emphasizing that “Indian tribes are distinct, independent political communities” and that “federal courts must be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence.” *Wilson*, 127 F.3d at 810 (citing *Worcester*, 31 U.S. at 559). The decision also frustrates Congress’s clear intent to combat domestic violence in Indian country by allowing tribes to use their own convictions to establish federal felony liability. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) (explicitly listing “Indian tribal court” as a source of predicate convictions). The result is a circuit split that leaves tribal victims in the Ninth Circuit less protected than those in the Eighth and Tenth Circuits. *Compare United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011), *and United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595 (8th Cir. 2011), *with* the decision below.

**CONCLUSION**

The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings, and a tribal court conviction valid under federal law may be used as a predicate offense under § 117(a). The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
