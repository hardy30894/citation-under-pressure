**ARGUMENT**

I. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA because it is a creditor collecting its own debts.

The FDCPA regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (citing 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)). A creditor is “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Santander became the owner of the plaintiffs’ debts when it purchased them from CitiFinancial Auto. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (defining “creditor” to include one to whom a debt is owed). As the owner of the debts, Santander was collecting its own debts, not debts owed to another. *Cf. United States v. Philadelphia Nat’l Bank*, 374 U.S. 321, 350 (1963) (holding that a creditor collecting its own debt is not acting as a debt collector).

The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” expressly excludes creditors collecting their own debts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (excluding from the definition of “debt collector” a creditor collecting its own debts). This exclusion reflects Congress’s intent to regulate only third-party collectors, not creditors acting on their own behalf. *See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698* (explaining that the FDCPA was intended to cover “all third persons who regularly collect debts for others”).

II. The default status of the debt at the time of purchase does not transform Santander into a “debt collector.”

The plaintiffs argue that because the debts were in default when Santander acquired them, Santander must be a “debt collector.” This argument misreads the statute. The FDCPA does not condition its coverage on whether a debt was in default when acquired. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (defining “debt collector” without reference to the default status of the debt). The Fourth Circuit correctly rejected this argument, holding that the default status of the debt is irrelevant to whether an entity is a “debt collector.” *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc.*, 814 F.3d 131, 136 (4th Cir. 2016).

The plaintiffs rely on the exclusion in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the definition of “debt collector” a person collecting a debt that was not in default when obtained. This exclusion, however, applies only to persons collecting debts on behalf of others. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) (excluding from the definition of “debt collector” a person collecting a debt not in default “owed or due another”). Santander, however, was collecting its own debts, not debts owed to another. *Cf. United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Ass’n*, 322 U.S. 533, 552 (1944) (holding that a person acting on its own behalf is not acting as an agent for another).

III. The FDCPA does not regulate creditors merely because they purchase defaulted debts.

The plaintiffs contend that Santander’s purchase of defaulted debts transformed it into a “debt collector.” This argument conflates the status of a creditor with that of a debt collector. The FDCPA regulates the latter, not the former. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293. A creditor remains a creditor even if it purchases a defaulted debt. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (defining “creditor” to include one to whom a debt is owed). The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander was a creditor collecting its own debts, not a “debt collector” subject to the FDCPA. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 136.

The plaintiffs’ argument would render the FDCPA’s exclusion for creditors meaningless. If purchasing a defaulted debt automatically transformed an entity into a “debt collector,” the exclusion for creditors would be superfluous. *Cf. United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955) (holding that statutory exclusions must be given effect).

IV. The FDCPA’s legislative history supports the conclusion that creditors are not subject to the Act.

The Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA explains that the Act was intended to regulate “debt collectors,” not creditors. *S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698*. The Report further explains that the Act was not intended to cover “the activities of ... mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” *Id.* at 4. This history confirms that the FDCPA does not regulate creditors collecting their own debts, regardless of whether the debts were in default when acquired.

**CONCLUSION**

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA because it is a creditor collecting its own debts. The judgment below should be affirmed.
