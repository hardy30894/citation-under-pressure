The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana. The Due Process Clause permits specific jurisdiction when a defendant has “purposefully availed” itself of the forum and the litigation is sufficiently connected to those forum contacts. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-20 (1945); McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957); Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). Ford’s extensive Montana business—advertising, selling, and servicing vehicles and parts in the State—easily satisfies purposeful availment. See International Shoe, 326 U.S. at 316-19; Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia, 339 U.S. 643, 647-49 (1950).

Ford’s narrower argument—that no jurisdiction exists because its Montana contacts did not cause the accident—finds no support in the Court’s pre-1970 precedents. Specific jurisdiction does not require strict causation in the tort sense. The constitutional inquiry is whether, in light of the defendant’s forum activities, maintaining the suit “does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” International Shoe, 326 U.S. at 316. Where a defendant has deliberately created continuing ties with the forum, the forum may adjudicate claims arising from that relationship even if the precise injury-producing instrumentality did not first enter the forum through the defendant. McGee, 355 U.S. at 223-24; Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437, 445-48 (1952). Ford did precisely that here: it cultivated an ongoing market in Montana for the very product line that injured the plaintiff.

World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), is outside the permissible citation window, but the Court’s earlier decisions already show why Ford loses. In International Shoe, a corporation was subject to suit in Washington because of “continuous and systematic” activities in the State, 326 U.S. at 320, and the Court emphasized that jurisdiction turns on the “quality and nature” of the defendant’s forum activities. Id. at 319. Here, Ford’s in-state marketing, dealer network, and parts distribution are not random or isolated; they are the kind of deliberate, repeated contacts that make it fair to answer in Montana for defects in the very products Ford places into the stream of commerce for Montana consumers. See McGee, 355 U.S. at 223; Travelers Health, 339 U.S. at 647-49.

Nor does due process demand that the plaintiff’s particular injury be physically caused by the defendant’s forum conduct. The Court has upheld jurisdiction when the suit bears a real and substantial relationship to the defendant’s forum-directed activity, even if the exact transaction occurred elsewhere. McGee, 355 U.S. at 223-24; Perkins, 342 U.S. at 445-48. Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction is especially fair because Ford knowingly serves the Montana market for Explorer vehicles and related components, and the accident occurred in Montana, injured a Montana resident, and implicates the safety of a product Ford extensively markets there. See International Shoe, 326 U.S. at 317-19.

Ford’s reliance on a causation-only rule would revive formalism rejected long ago. The Court has recognized that jurisdiction depends on “fairness and reasonableness,” not mechanical territoriality. Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 356-57 (1927); Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457, 463-64 (1940). Those cases reflect the modern rule: when a defendant purposefully engages the forum and the suit is connected to that engagement, jurisdiction is proper. Ford’s contrary position would immunize a national manufacturer from suit in a State where it deliberately profits from selling and servicing the very product at issue, so long as the final sale to the plaintiff was made by a third party. That is not the Due Process Clause.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
