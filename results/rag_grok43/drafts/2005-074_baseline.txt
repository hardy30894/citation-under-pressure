**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Whose Reentry Preceded Its Effective Date.**

The reinstatement provision, enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 and effective April 1, 1997, authorizes reinstatement of a prior deportation order against an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Petitioner reentered in 1982. The statute therefore reaches only post-enactment conduct.

This Court has long held that immigration statutes do not apply retroactively absent clear congressional direction. In *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685 (1957), the Court construed § 241(d) of the 1952 Act, which made certain deportation grounds applicable “notwithstanding * * * that the facts * * * occurred prior to the date of enactment.” *Id.* at 689. Because that explicit language was absent from the savings clause of § 405(a), the Court refused to apply the new grounds to pre-Act convictions. *Id.* at 690. The 1996 reinstatement provision contains no comparable language directing application to pre-1997 reentries. Accordingly, the statute reaches only those who reenter after its effective date.

The same principle controlled in *Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692 (1957). There the Court held that an alien’s pre-Act narcotics conviction did not render him deportable under the 1952 Act because the savings clause preserved his “status” of nondeportability. *Id.* at 694. Petitioner’s 1982 reentry likewise fixed his immigration status; nothing in the 1996 Act expressly disturbs it.

**II. Application of Section 241(a)(5) to Pre-1997 Reentries Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Even if the statute were ambiguous, its application here would attach new legal consequences to Petitioner’s completed 1982 reentry. In *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560 (1964), the Court refused to treat a 1942 departure as interrupting continuous residence under § 249 because the alien had remained in the United States thereafter; the Court looked to the alien’s settled status at the time of the later application. *Id.* at 564-65. Petitioner’s continuous presence since 1982 created a settled expectation that his 1981 deportation order would not be reinstated without the opportunity for discretionary relief. The Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding—that Petitioner lacked any “protectable expectation”—contradicts this Court’s recognition that settled immigration status carries legal weight.

The statute’s denial of all forms of relief further distinguishes it from the discretionary provisions upheld in *Foti v. INS*, 375 U.S. 217 (1963), and *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72 (1957). Those cases involved applications for suspension or voluntary departure that remained available; here the reinstatement bar eliminates every avenue of relief. Because the 1996 Act supplies no clear statement that Congress intended this consequence for pre-1997 reentries, the statute cannot be applied to Petitioner.
