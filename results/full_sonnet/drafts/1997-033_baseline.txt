# ARGUMENT

## SECTION 1407(a) MEANS WHAT IT SAYS: A TRANSFEREE COURT CANNOT USE SECTION 1404(a) TO CIRCUMVENT THE PANEL'S MANDATORY REMAND OBLIGATION

### A. The Plain Text of Section 1407(a) Forecloses Self-Transfer

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This Court has repeatedly held that the word "shall" generally indicates a mandatory duty. *See Lopez v. Davis*, 531 U.S. 230, 241 (2001) ("shall" typically connotes a mandatory command). The statute's remand instruction is not phrased as a suggestion or discretionary option—it is a directive, and Congress reinforced its mandatory character by specifying that remand must occur "at or before the conclusion" of pretrial proceedings, foreclosing any interpretation permitting the transferee court to retain the case for trial.

The Ninth Circuit majority did not dispute this plain language. Instead, it acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate," but nonetheless upheld the transfer because Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) "explicitly authorizes a transferee court to transfer the action to itself for trial pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a)." Pet. App. 12. A Panel rule, however, cannot override an unambiguous statutory command. As Judge Kozinski's dissent below recognized, courts confronting this issue have "simply disregarded the statutory mandate," starting with *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971), which "rejected as absurd a result that would delay section 1404(a) motions until after remand"—a policy judgment, not a textual one. Pet. App. 17. Even the district court in *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971), candidly admitted "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial." Pet. App. 17.

### B. Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) Are Reconcilable Without Judicial Rewriting

The court of appeals erred in assuming an irreconcilable conflict between §§ 1404(a) and 1407(a) that could be resolved only by judicial fiat. No such conflict exists. As the dissent correctly explained, "[p]retrial proceedings under 1407 are obviously limited to those raising issues common to other cases, primarily discovery; that's the entire point of the multidistrict process." Pet. App. 18. Section 1404(a) motions filed *during* the pretrial period fall within the transferee court's authority precisely because they are themselves pretrial matters—but that authority does not extend to the transferee court's assignment of the case to *itself* for trial, an act that by definition marks the "conclusion" of pretrial proceedings and triggers the mandatory remand.

This reading gives full effect to both statutes. The transferee court retains complete authority over pretrial matters, including ruling on venue motions directed to *other* districts. What it cannot do is use § 1404(a) as a vehicle to retain the case permanently for itself, thereby nullifying § 1407(a)'s remand command. As the dissent observed, "[t]his precludes use of the general transfer section by district judges to subvert the panel's responsibility." Pet. App. 18.

### C. Section 1407(a) Embodies a Deliberate Policy Choice That Courts Are Not Free to Override

Congress made a considered choice in enacting § 1407(a): cases may be transferred to distant districts for the limited, defined purpose of coordinated discovery and pretrial management, but the case must return to the transferor court for trial. This structure preserves what the dissent called plaintiffs' "ancient right to select the forum" for trial and dispositive proceedings. Pet. App. 19. Had Congress intended to permit the Panel—or transferee courts acting unilaterally—to reassign cases permanently for trial, "it would have drafted the statute much differently." Pet. App. 19.

The practical consequences of the Ninth Circuit's rule illustrate why fidelity to the text matters. Here, petitioners "were dragged hundreds of miles from their home district in Illinois to appear before a judge in Arizona who had no familiarity with the case," and who, moreover, presided over a case in which petitioners "participated in no common proceedings" and "benefitted from no coordinated discovery." Pet. App. 20. The transferee court then leveraged the very inconvenience §1407(a) was designed to limit—the sunk costs of extended, forced litigation in a remote forum—as its rationale for retaining the case, reasoning that transfer "simply leaves the parties where they are." Pet. App. 10. This bootstrapping inverts the statute: the remedy for improperly prolonged pretrial proceedings in a distant district cannot be to make that transfer permanent.

### D. The "Clearly Erroneous" Standard Applied Below Cannot Justify Departure from Unambiguous Text

Even under the deferential mandamus standard, the panel majority conceded that "upon review on direct appeal, we might well reach the opposite result." Pet. App. 12. That concession underscores that the transferee court's action rested not on any genuine ambiguity in the statute, but on decades of accumulated, uncorrected error. As the dissent noted, courts across the circuits have "simply taken their place at the head of an ever-growing string cite," Pet. App. 17, without confronting the statutory text. This Court should not permit a quarter-century of unexamined practice, however widespread, to override the statute Congress actually enacted. *See Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (courts must presume Congress "says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says").

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse and hold that § 1407(a) required remand of petitioners' case to the Northern District of Illinois for trial.
