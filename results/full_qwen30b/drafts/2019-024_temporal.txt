**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information that was readily available and disclosed, conflicts with settled principles of federal law governing the accrual of causes of action. This Court must reverse, for the Ninth Circuit’s rule effectively nullifies the statute of limitations by allowing plaintiffs to delay suit indefinitely based on their own failure to read or recall disclosures, contrary to the well-established principle that *actual knowledge* is not measured by a plaintiff’s subjective inattention but by the objective availability of information.

The statute of limitations in § 1113(2) is not a mere procedural formality; it is a substantive limitation designed to ensure that claims are brought while evidence remains fresh and while fiduciaries can defend themselves against allegations of past conduct. The statute provides that an action may not be commenced more than three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The term “actual knowledge” has long been understood in federal law to mean knowledge that is *in fact* possessed, not merely knowledge that could have been acquired through reasonable diligence. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940), “actual knowledge” means “knowledge which a person has, not knowledge which he might have had.” This principle has been consistently applied in the context of statutes of limitations, where courts have rejected the notion that a plaintiff may avoid the time bar by failing to investigate or by choosing not to read available information.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that a plaintiff may claim ignorance of a breach even when the information was disclosed and accessible—directly contradicts this settled understanding. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), this Court emphasized that “a party cannot be allowed to sit back and wait for the discovery of facts which he might have discovered by the exercise of reasonable diligence.” The Court further explained that “the law does not require a man to be a detective,” but it does require him to act with ordinary prudence. Id. The same principle applies here: a plan participant who has access to disclosures about investment allocations and performance cannot avoid the statute of limitations by asserting that he “did not read” or “did not recall” the information. The law does not excuse inattention to information that is plainly available.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also undermines the purpose of ERISA’s limitations period. Congress enacted § 1113(2) to prevent stale claims and to provide finality to fiduciary decisions. As this Court stated in *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 367, 375 (1962), “the statute of limitations is not a mere technicality but a vital safeguard against the dangers of stale claims.” If a plaintiff may delay suit indefinitely by refusing to read or recall disclosures, the statute becomes meaningless. The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow a participant to wait years after a breach occurs, even after receiving detailed disclosures, and then bring suit based on a claim of “lack of actual knowledge”—a claim that is not only subjective but also easily fabricated. This is precisely the kind of abuse the statute of limitations was designed to prevent.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of “actual knowledge” is inconsistent with the common law of fiduciary duty, which has long required that a beneficiary act with reasonable diligence in monitoring the conduct of fiduciaries. In *Hill v. United States*, 300 U.S. 100, 105 (1937), this Court held that “a beneficiary of a trust is not entitled to sit by and wait for the discovery of fraud or breach of trust, but must exercise such diligence as the circumstances require.” The same standard applies to ERISA plans. A participant who has access to plan documents, including fund fact sheets, annual disclosures, and summary plan descriptions, cannot claim ignorance of the nature of the investments simply because he failed to read them. The law does not require a plaintiff to be a financial expert, but it does require him to act with ordinary prudence in reviewing information that is provided to him.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), and *Meagher v. International Association of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Those cases involved fiduciaries who were parties to the transactions in question and thus had actual knowledge by virtue of their participation. In *Blanton*, the trustees were “parties to the transaction” and “actually made the decision,” 760 F.2d at 991, and in *Meagher*, the plaintiff received reduced benefit checks that were “in fact” issued to him. 856 F.2d at 1421. These cases do not support the proposition that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information that was disclosed. Indeed, in *Meagher*, the Court explicitly held that “every time the plaintiff received a reduced check he had knowledge of the transaction,” even if he did not know it was unlawful. 856 F.2d at 1423. The key point is not whether the plaintiff knew the law, but whether he knew the *transaction*—and in both cases, the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the transaction.

Here, Intel disclosed the investment allocations, the strategy behind them, and the risks involved through multiple documents, including fund fact sheets, annual disclosures, and summary plan descriptions. These documents were available on Intel’s website and were accessible to Sulyma during his employment. The fact that Sulyma claims he did not read or recall them does not negate the objective availability of the information. As this Court held in *United States v.
