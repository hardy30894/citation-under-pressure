## ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court’s Supplemental Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial

The district court erred by giving the jury a definition of “shielding” that was impermissibly vague and overbroad, thereby misstating the law. This Court reviews jury instructions de novo, reversing if the instruction misstates the law or misleads the jury. *United States v. Swanquist*, 161 F.3d 1064, 1068 (7th Cir. 1998). Even if the instruction is not plainly erroneous, reversal is required if it “fails to adequately inform the jury of the applicable law.” *United States v. Gonzalez*, 933 F.2d 417, 424 (7th Cir. 1991).

The term “shielding” in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) requires proof of affirmative conduct intended to prevent the government from detecting illegal aliens. Every circuit to address the issue has held that “shielding” demands more than mere employment or passive inaction; it requires an “affirmative act to prevent detection.” *United States v. Ozuna-Cabrera*, 663 F.3d 496, 502 (1st Cir. 2011); *United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567, 574 (2d Cir. 1999) (shielding “requires some affirmative conduct, such as hiding or secreting the aliens”); *United States v. Vargas*, 552 F.3d 479, 482 (6th Cir. 2009) (shielding means “to protect from discovery”); *United States v. Tipton*, 518 F.3d 591, 595 (8th Cir. 2008) (shielding “requires some affirmative act to prevent detection”). The district court’s instruction—which defined “shielding” without requiring any affirmative conduct—was therefore erroneous.

Ye objected to any definition, but the court overruled him and gave a definition that omitted the critical element of affirmative action. This error was not harmless. The jury’s note specifically requested a definition of “shielding,” indicating confusion. The erroneous instruction allowed the jury to convict Ye based on conduct—such as hiring illegal aliens and paying them in cash—that does not constitute “shielding” under the statute. *See United States v. Zheng*, 306 F.3d 1080, 1088 (11th Cir. 2002) (harboring requires “conduct that tends to substantially facilitate an alien’s remaining in the United States illegally and to prevent detection”). Because the instruction misled the jury, Ye’s conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed.

### B. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to Prevent Detection

Even if the instruction were correct, the evidence was insufficient to support a finding that Ye acted with the specific intent to shield his employees from detection. *See Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (evidence must allow a rational trier of fact to find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt). The government’s evidence showed only that Ye hired illegal aliens, paid them in cash, and provided housing—conduct consistent with employment, not with an intent to evade detection. *United States v. You*, 382 F.3d 958, 966 (9th Cir. 2004) (mere employment of illegal aliens, without more, does not constitute harboring or shielding). Ye did not hide the workers; they were observed openly working in the restaurant. He did not destroy records; he simply failed to maintain them. He even told the Hispanic workers they could purchase fake documents, which he would accept—an act that demonstrates a willingness to comply with paperwork requirements, not an intent to conceal.

The government’s evidence that Ye submitted wage reports listing only Chinese workers does not show an intent to shield Hispanic workers from detection; it shows only that Ye failed to report wages for workers he knew were illegal. Such omissions are not affirmative acts of shielding. *United States v. Sanchez*, 440 F.3d 1243, 1249 (10th Cir. 2006) (shielding requires “evidence of an intent to evade detection”). Moreover, Ye’s provision of housing and transportation is typical of an employer who hires migrant workers, not of one who seeks to hide them. *United States v. Aguilar*, 883 F.2d 662, 671 (9th Cir. 1989) (providing housing and transportation, without more, does not establish intent to shield). Because no rational jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that Ye intended to prevent the government from detecting his employees, the conviction must be reversed.
