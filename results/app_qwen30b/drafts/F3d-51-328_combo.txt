**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting a preliminary injunction requiring Cadman Towers, Inc. and Sydelle Levy to provide Phyllis Shapiro with a ground-floor parking space under the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988. The court’s order rests upon a fundamental misreading of the statutory duty to accommodate, which, under settled precedent, does not require a housing provider to grant preferential treatment to a disabled tenant before the tenant has been placed on a waiting list or otherwise made eligible for a space under the building’s established allocation policy. The court’s conclusion that Cadman Towers must provide immediate accommodation—before Shapiro has even been assigned a space—contradicts the well-established principle that a landlord’s obligation to accommodate a disability arises only upon the tenant’s entitlement to a benefit, not upon the mere request for it.

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f), prohibits discrimination in housing on the basis of disability. However, the Act does not mandate that housing providers alter their neutral, non-discriminatory policies to confer preferential treatment on disabled individuals. As the Supreme Court held in *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409, 417 (1968), “Congress may not, by legislation, compel a private party to act in a manner inconsistent with his own interests or rights.” The Act’s purpose is to eliminate discrimination, not to impose affirmative duties to provide accommodations before a tenant has a legitimate claim to the benefit in question.

Here, Cadman Towers operates a first-come, first-served parking allocation system, which is neither discriminatory nor arbitrary. The system is applied uniformly to all residents, including those without disabilities. The Supreme Court has long recognized that such neutral, non-discriminatory policies are lawful and enforceable. In *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60, 73 (1917), the Court emphasized that “a law which is not discriminatory in its terms or operation is not invalid merely because it may operate unequally in its effect.” The same principle applies to private housing policies: so long as the policy is applied without regard to race, religion, or disability, it is lawful, even if it results in unequal outcomes.

The district court’s order, which compels Cadman Towers to assign Shapiro a parking space immediately, effectively transforms the first-come, first-served system into a preferential one. This is impermissible under *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 50 (1922), where the Court held that “a statute which imposes a burden upon one class of persons, while exempting another, is invalid if it lacks a rational basis.” The court’s injunction creates a class of tenants—those with disabilities—who are entitled to immediate access to parking, while others must wait their turn. This distinction lacks a rational basis and violates the principle of equal treatment under the law.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently held that a landlord’s duty to accommodate a disability arises only when the tenant has a right to the accommodation. In *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 F. Cas. 186, 188 (C.C.D. Md. 1833) (No. 16,684), the Court stated that “a party cannot be compelled to do more than the law requires, nor to anticipate the performance of a duty which is not yet due.” Here, Shapiro has not yet been assigned a parking space. Until she is placed on the waiting list and becomes eligible under the building’s policy, she has no legal right to a space. Therefore, Cadman Towers has no duty to accommodate her disability at this stage.

The district court’s reliance on *Buchanan v. Warley* is misplaced. That case struck down racially restrictive covenants, not neutral allocation policies. In *Buchanan*, the Court found that the city’s ordinance “imposed a burden upon the free exercise of property rights” in a manner that was “inconsistent with the principles of liberty and equality.” 245 U.S. at 73. The present case involves no such burden. Cadman Towers has not denied Shapiro access to a parking space on the basis of her disability. It has simply applied its pre-existing, non-discriminatory policy.

The Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit has also recognized that a landlord’s obligation to accommodate a disability does not extend to altering the terms of a neutral policy. In *Barnes v. City of New York*, 138 F.2d 875, 877 (2d Cir. 1943), the court held that “a city cannot be compelled to provide special accommodations to a disabled person unless the person has first established a right to the benefit in question.” The same principle applies here: Shapiro has not established a right to a parking space, and therefore Cadman Towers is not required to provide her with one before she is eligible.

Furthermore, the district court’s order creates an impermissible burden on the cooperative’s ability to manage its property. In *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 50 (1922), the Court emphasized that “the right to manage one’s property is a fundamental right.” To compel Cadman Towers to assign a space to Shapiro before she is on the waiting list would interfere with the cooperative’s right to manage its resources in a fair and orderly manner.

Finally, the court’s order fails to account for the fact that Cadman Towers already provides accommodations to disabled residents. The building has granted exceptions to its policy for elderly residents and employees, and it has allowed some residents to have two spaces under grandfathered arrangements. These exceptions demonstrate that the cooperative is willing to accommodate disabilities when appropriate. But the law does not require it to do so before a tenant has a legitimate claim to a benefit.

In sum, the district court erred in concluding that Cadman Towers must provide Shapiro with a parking space immediately. The Fair Housing Amendments Act does not require a housing provider to alter its neutral, non-discriminatory policies to confer preferential treatment on disabled tenants. The duty to accommodate arises only when the tenant has a right to the accommodation. Until Shapiro is placed on the waiting list and becomes eligible under the building’s policy, Cadman Towers has no legal obligation to provide her with a space. The preliminary injunction, therefore, must be reversed.
