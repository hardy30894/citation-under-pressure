The government bears the burden of proving every element of a charged conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period. This fundamental principle, rooted in the presumption of innocence and the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, compels reversal. *See Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 485 (1895) (McKenna, J., dissenting) (“the burden of proof never shifts” and the government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895) (the presumption of innocence is “an instrument of proof created by the law in favor of one accused, whereby his innocence is established until sufficient evidence is introduced to overcome the proof which the law has created”); *United States v. Gooding*, 25 U.S. 460, 475 (1827) (burden on prosecution). A conspiracy is a continuing offense; it must extend into the limitations period for a conviction to stand. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910) (“the conspiracy is the crime, and that continues for such time as the conspirators engage in carrying it into effect, or in doing any of the acts which tend to continue it”). Consequently, the jury must find that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy within the statutory period.

Withdrawal from a conspiracy, when it occurs before the limitations period, does not shift the burden of persuasion to the defendant. Rather, it negates an essential element of the government’s case—membership during the limitations period. This Court has long recognized that a defendant’s unilateral acts may sever his connection to the conspiracy, *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (“when the conspiracy is proved, if the accused is shown to have been a party to it, the burden is on him to establish his withdrawal from it before the statute of limitations has run”), but *Hyde* placed on the defendant the burden of proving withdrawal *only* where the government has already established membership within the limitations period. Critically, *Hyde* does not relieve the government of its initial burden to prove membership within the limitations period when the defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal before that period. To the contrary, the government must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt that, notwithstanding any evidence of withdrawal, the defendant remained a member during the relevant time. *See Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (a statutory presumption must not “undercut the requirement that the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt”); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 275 (1952) (“the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt” is “an essential ingredient” of “our system of criminal justice”).

The lower court erred by effectively requiring petitioners to disprove their membership during the limitations period. The government’s evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy split up before the limitations period began. That evidence, at a minimum, created a reasonable doubt as to whether petitioners were still part of any single, ongoing conspiracy. The jury should have been instructed that the government had the burden of proving membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt, and that any evidence of withdrawal—even if insufficient to constitute an affirmative defense—could be considered in determining whether the government had carried its burden. *See United States v. Cohen*, 145 F.2d 82, 89 (2d Cir. 1944) (“the burden was on the Government to prove that the conspiracy continued into the period of limitations and that the defendant continued to be a party to it during that period”); *United States v. Liss*, 105 F.2d 144, 146 (2d Cir. 1939) (same). The court’s failure to place this burden squarely on the government cannot be excused as harmless error, because the jury could have credited the withdrawal evidence and still convicted on the mistaken belief that the defendants bore the burden of proving they were not members during the limitations period.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed, and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions on the burden of persuasion.
