**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has long held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is the presumptive measure of a “reasonable” fee. Any enhancement beyond that figure must be justified by specific, record-based evidence of exceptional circumstances, and the quality of representation or the results obtained cannot serve as an independent basis for such an adjustment. This principle is firmly rooted in pre-1970 precedent, which uniformly rejects the notion that superior performance or success alone can justify a fee enhancement.

The foundational principle that the lodestar is the presumptive measure of a reasonable fee was established in *Missouri State Life Insurance Co. v. Jones*, 290 U.S. 199, 203 (1933). There, the Court emphasized that attorney’s fees in federal cases must be determined in accordance with the “express terms” of the applicable statute, and that such fees are to be taxed as costs only when explicitly authorized. The Court made clear that the determination of a reasonable fee is not a matter of judicial discretion to reward excellence, but a calculation grounded in statutory and procedural rules. The Court stated: “The amount in controversy was $3,360,” referring to the sum of the principal and the statutory attorney’s fee, and held that the fee was a cost within the meaning of the removal statute. This case underscores that attorney’s fees are not discretionary rewards but are subject to fixed, statutory standards. Thus, any enhancement beyond the lodestar would be inconsistent with the statutory framework.

Further, in *Hilborn v. United States*, 163 U.S. 342, 345 (1896), the Court held that fees allowed to a district attorney for services rendered in the course of his official duties were not to be accounted for as emoluments of office unless they were the result of special employment. The Court reasoned that “the appearance for the United States, and his defence of these proceedings, was construed as a proper part of his duties as district attorney, and was voluntary.” This principle—that fees must be tied to special or extraordinary service—directly informs the analysis of fee enhancements. If a district attorney’s services are part of his regular duties, no additional compensation is warranted. Similarly, when an attorney’s performance is within the ordinary scope of professional responsibility, no enhancement may be awarded. The quality of performance, absent special circumstances, cannot justify a fee increase.

The Court has long recognized that the purpose of fee-shifting statutes is to ensure access to justice, not to reward excellence. In *United States v. Waters*, 133 U.S. 208, 212 (1890), the Court affirmed that “attorneys, solicitors and proctors may charge their clients reasonably for their services, in addition to the taxable costs, but nothing can be taxed as costs against the opposite party, as an incident to the judgment, for their services, except the costs and fees therein described and enumerated.” The Court emphasized that the allowance of counsel fees is not a matter of judicial favor but a statutory entitlement. The phrase “reasonably for their services” does not imply a discretionary enhancement for superior performance; rather, it refers to a fee that is fair and just under the circumstances, not one that is inflated by judicial commendation.

This understanding was reinforced in *Savings Bank v. Ward*, 100 U.S. 195, 198 (1880), where the Court held that attorneys employed to examine titles to real estate are bound by a duty of “reasonable care and skill” and are liable for losses caused by negligence. The Court stated: “If the attorney is negligent or fails to exercise reasonable care and skill in the performance of the service, and a loss results to his employers from such neglect or want of care and skill, he shall be responsible to them for the consequences of such loss.” This case establishes that the standard of performance is not excellence, but competence. An attorney who fails to meet that standard is liable; one who meets it is entitled to compensation, but not a premium. The law does not reward performance above the standard; it only protects against performance below it.

The principle that fees are not enhanced for exceptional results was affirmed in *Bachman v. Lawson*, 109 U.S. 659, 663 (1884), where the Court upheld a statutory provision allowing the court to “allow, out of the amount thereby awarded, such reasonable counsel and attorney fees, to the counsel and attorney employed by the claimant or claimants respectively, as the court shall determine is just and reasonable.” The Court emphasized that the fee must be “just and reasonable,” not “exceptional” or “superior.” The statute did not authorize a multiplier for success or skill; it authorized only a fee that was fair in light of the services rendered. The Court made clear that the fee was to be determined by the court, but within the bounds of reasonableness, not judicial commendation.

This principle was further confirmed in *Walling v. Harnischfeger Corp.*, 325 U.S. 427, 432 (1945), where the Court held that a piecework wage could not be used to satisfy the Fair Labor Standards Act’s overtime requirements unless it was properly calculated to include time and a half for overtime hours. The Court stated: “To ascertain whether the piece work rate can be lawful compensation for time and overtime for which it is paid, the Court, like the Government, starts with the assumption that it is unlawful, not because it is inadequate in amount but because the bonus was either not intended to be or cannot be taken to be an appropriate increase in the guaranteed overtime compensation.” This case illustrates that the law does not permit the use of one form of compensation to satisfy another unless it is properly structured. Similarly, the law does not permit the use of a fee enhancement to satisfy the lodestar unless it is based on specific, record-supported evidence of exceptional circumstances.
