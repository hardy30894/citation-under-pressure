The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has construed this language to extend to cases in which a plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988).

To determine whether a state-based lawsuit with embedded federal issues arises under federal jurisdiction, the Supreme Court has set forth a four-part test: (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co., 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed.Cir.2005).

Second, the experimental use exception is actually disputed, as Minton and Gunn have differing views on its applicability. The Federal Circuit has recognized that the experimental use exception is a complex and nuanced issue, requiring careful consideration of the facts and circumstances surrounding the sale of the invention. See id.

Third, the experimental use exception is a substantial federal issue, as it directly impacts the validity of Minton's patent and the outcome of his infringement litigation. The Federal Circuit has emphasized the importance of uniformity in the application of patent law, and allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could potentially disrupt this uniformity. See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262, 1272-73 (Fed.Cir.2007).

Finally, exercising federal jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The federal courts have a strong interest in maintaining uniformity in the application of patent law, and adjudicating patent issues in the context of malpractice claims is consistent with this interest. See id. at 1273. In contrast, allowing state courts to adjudicate such claims could potentially create inconsistent and conflicting interpretations of patent law.

The Supreme Court of Texas erred in dismissing the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, and this Court should reverse that decision.
