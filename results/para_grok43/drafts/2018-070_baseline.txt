**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States as surrogate federal law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The text and history of the Act establish that state law applies solely to fill gaps in federal coverage. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law governs whenever it is “applicable” and not expressly inconsistent—contradicts controlling precedent and must be reversed.

Congress enacted OCSLA to create a comprehensive federal regime for the outer Continental Shelf. It rejected both maritime law and direct extension of state boundaries, instead declaring that federal law would govern “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law was borrowed only because “federal law, standing alone, would be inadequate.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 357 (1969) (quoting S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953)). The Supreme Court has therefore described OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision as one that “adopt[s] the laws of the adjacent States as surrogate federal law” precisely “where federal law, of its own force, does not apply.” Id. at 355, 358.

This Court reaffirmed the gap-filling character of the statute in Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19 (1988). There the Court explained that OCSLA “borrows” state law only to the extent necessary to supply rules “for the portion of the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf” that would otherwise lack governing federal provisions. Id. at 27. The 1975 amendment, which made prospective incorporation of state law explicit, did not alter this limitation; it merely ensured that subsequently enacted state statutes could serve the same gap-filling function. See id. at 26–27.

The Fifth Circuit, which has long administered the bulk of OCSLA litigation, has consistently applied the same rule. In Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is assimilated only when “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” exists. Id. at 1036. That test was later refined in Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), which requires, as its first element, “a gap in federal law.” Id. at 1047. The Fifth Circuit has never abandoned the gap requirement. See, e.g., Demette v. Falcon Drilling Co., 280 F.3d 492, 502 (5th Cir. 2002).

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive federal scheme governing wages and hours on the outer Continental Shelf. Its savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws only where those laws would otherwise apply; it does not create new rights on federal enclaves. Because the FLSA leaves no “significant void or gap” on the subject of minimum wage, overtime, and meal periods, California’s wage-and-hour statutes are not assimilated under § 1333(a)(2)(A). The district court correctly so held.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
