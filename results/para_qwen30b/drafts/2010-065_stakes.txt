**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant has standing to challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. The Third Circuit correctly held that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and this Court should affirm.

### I. A Criminal Defendant Lacks Standing to Challenge a Federal Statute on Tenth Amendment Grounds

Standing is not a mere procedural formality; it is a constitutional prerequisite to judicial review. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized, “a party seeking to invoke the power of a federal court must demonstrate a personal stake in the outcome.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94, 118 S.Ct. 1003, 140 L.Ed.2d 210 (1998). This principle applies with equal force to challenges based on the Tenth Amendment. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939), the Court explicitly rejected a private party’s attempt to assert a Tenth Amendment claim, holding that “the sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment” and that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. That holding remains binding precedent.

Bond’s argument—that she is entitled to challenge § 229 because it allegedly intrudes upon state sovereignty—misunderstands the nature of the Tenth Amendment. The Tenth Amendment does not confer rights upon individuals; rather, it reserves powers to the States. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559, 115 S.Ct. 1624, 131 L.Ed.2d 599 (1995). When Congress enacts legislation pursuant to a valid treaty, the Constitution permits such action even if it affects areas traditionally governed by the States. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920). But crucially, the right to enforce the Tenth Amendment belongs to the States—not to private litigants. *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, 306 U.S. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366.

This Court has consistently held that private parties cannot sue to vindicate the sovereign interests of a State. In *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005), the court affirmed that the standing issue in *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* was not dicta but an essential jurisdictional determination. The Court explained: “Because the standing question in Tenn. Elec. Power Co. concerned the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, its resolution predominates any discussion of the merits.” Id. (citing *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 94–95, 118 S.Ct. 1003). Thus, even if the underlying constitutional claim were meritorious, the absence of standing bars adjudication.

Bond’s assertion that *Tennessee Electric Power Co.* is “textbook dicta” is incorrect. The Court did not merely comment on the issue—it resolved it as a matter of jurisdiction. To hold otherwise would allow every individual prosecuted under a federal law to challenge its validity on federalism grounds, regardless of whether the State itself objects. Such a rule would collapse the distinction between federal and state authority and invite forum shopping. It would also undermine the federal system by allowing private actors to displace state interests through litigation. That is not the design of our Constitution.

### II. The Treaty Power Provides Independent Constitutional Authority for § 229

Even if Bond had standing, her constitutional challenge would fail on the merits. Section 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority under the Treaty Power. The United States ratified the Chemical Weapons Convention in 1993, which obligates signatories to prohibit the use, development, and stockpiling of chemical weapons. 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Under Article VI of the Constitution, treaties are “the supreme Law of the Land,” and Congress may enact laws necessary to fulfill those obligations. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382.

Congress’s enactment of § 229 was a direct implementation of the Convention. The statute prohibits the possession and use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes, including acts like Bond’s attempted poisoning. The definition of “chemical weapon” includes any toxic chemical used to cause harm, regardless of scale or location. 18 U.S.C. § 229F(1)(A). The statute does not require proof of interstate commerce or federal interest because it is grounded in a treaty obligation, not the Commerce Clause. *Lue v. United States*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998) (“[I]f a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute implementing it under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”).

The scope of the treaty power is broad. As the Court stated in *Holland*, “there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” 252 U.S. at 433, 40 S.Ct. 382. The threat posed by chemical weapons transcends state borders and threatens national security. Congress therefore had ample authority to act.
