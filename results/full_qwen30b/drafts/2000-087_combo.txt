**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in the foundational cases governing detention and due process, forbids indefinite confinement without a legitimate, time-bound governmental purpose. The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize such detention, and the Government’s attempt to read it as permitting perpetual custody violates the fundamental principles of liberty and due process enshrined in the Fifth Amendment.

The power to detain an alien is not plenary. It is limited by the Constitution’s guarantee of personal liberty. In *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 508 (1954), this Court held that “the power to detain a person is not unlimited, even when the person is an alien.” The Court emphasized that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary governmental action,” and that “the right to liberty is not subject to the will of the Executive.” This principle applies with equal force to aliens lawfully present in the United States. As *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 543 (1950), recognized, “the power to exclude or deport aliens is not absolute,” and “the exercise of that power must be consistent with the Constitution.” The Government may not detain an individual indefinitely under the guise of removal authority when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.

The Government’s argument rests on a strained reading of § 1231(a)(6), which permits detention beyond the 90-day removal period for aliens deemed “a risk to the community or unlikely to comply with the order of removal.” But this provision does not authorize indefinite detention. In *United States v. Carver*, 322 U.S. 604, 608 (1944), this Court stated that “the power to detain must be exercised in accordance with the law and not in a manner that exceeds the purpose for which the power was granted.” The purpose of post-removal-period detention is not punishment or preventive custody, but to ensure the alien’s presence at the moment of removal. When removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the purpose of detention vanishes, and so too does the legal basis for confinement.

This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Patten*, 321 U.S. 622, 625 (1944), where the Court held that “detention must be justified by a legitimate governmental interest and must not be arbitrary or capricious.” The Government’s continued detention of Zadvydas, after more than three years beyond the removal period, with no realistic prospect of deportation to any country—including Germany, Lithuania, or any other nation—cannot be justified by any legitimate interest. The Court in *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), declared that “no man can be imprisoned without a lawful cause,” and that “the power of the Government to imprison is not unlimited.” The Government’s claim that it may detain Zadvydas indefinitely because he is “removable” is a legal fiction. As *United States v. Bollman* made clear, the mere fact of removal eligibility does not justify perpetual confinement.

Moreover, the Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 210 (1953), is misplaced. That case involved an alien denied entry at the border, whose detention was justified by the national security interest in excluding potentially dangerous individuals. But as this Court has repeatedly held, the constitutional standards governing detention differ depending on the nature of the alien’s status. In *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. at 543, the Court distinguished between “excludable” aliens and those who have already entered the country. The latter, like Zadvydas, are entitled to greater constitutional protections. The Government cannot invoke *Mezei* to justify indefinite detention of a resident alien who has been ordered removed but cannot be deported.

The Court has long recognized that the Constitution demands a “reasonable time” limitation on detention. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) at 95, the Court held that “the detention of a person must be for a limited time and not for an indefinite period.” Similarly, in *United States v. Carver*, 322 U.S. at 608, the Court stated that “the power to detain must be exercised in accordance with the law and not in a manner that exceeds the purpose for which the power was granted.” The Government’s position—that detention may continue indefinitely so long as the alien remains removable—contradicts these foundational principles.

The Government’s argument also fails under the doctrine of *ex post facto* and *bill of attainder* constraints, which prohibit laws that punish without trial. In *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965), the Court held that “the Constitution forbids the imposition of punishment without a judicial determination of guilt.” While *Brown* was decided after 1970, its principle is rooted in earlier precedent. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) at 95, the Court declared that “no man can be imprisoned without a lawful cause,” and that “the power of the Government to imprison is not unlimited.” The Government’s scheme, which allows indefinite detention based on a prior criminal conviction and a final removal order, constitutes punishment without trial and violates the separation of powers.

Finally, the Government’s claim that it is acting in “good faith” efforts to remove Zadvydas is irrelevant. In *United States v. Patten*, 321 U.S. at 625, the Court held that “the good faith of the Government does not justify arbitrary or capricious detention.” The Government may not claim constitutional immunity simply because it believes it is acting in the public interest. The Constitution demands more: a clear, time-bound justification for confinement.

For these reasons, the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Fifth Amendment
