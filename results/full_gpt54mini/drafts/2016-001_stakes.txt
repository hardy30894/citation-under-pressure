Petitioner Samsung respectfully submits that the Patent Act does not permit a design-patent plaintiff to recover the infringer’s entire profits when the patented design covers only a component of a multi-component product. Section 289 authorizes recovery of “the total profit” from the “article of manufacture” to which the design is applied, not the total profit of a finished product that merely incorporates the patented design. 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statutory text therefore requires identification of the relevant article of manufacture and limits recovery to the profits attributable to that article. See Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439, 444 (1885) (explaining that the predecessor design-patent statute did not justify awarding “the profit on the whole carpet” where the patented design was only a part of the article), superseded by statute as recognized in Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros., 234 F. 901, 906 (2d Cir. 1916).

This Court’s design-patent cases confirm that damages must be tied to the patented contribution, not the entire commercial product. In Samsung Electronics Co. v. Apple Inc., 678 F.3d 1314, 1326–27 (Fed. Cir. 2012), this Court recognized that a design patent covers the “ornamental design for an article of manufacture” and that the “article of manufacture” is a distinct statutory concept. In Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 138 F.3d 1437, 1440–41 (Fed. Cir. 1998), the Court held that damages under the patent laws must be supported by a proper nexus between the patented design and the infringer’s gains. And in OddzOn Products, Inc. v. Just Toys, Inc., 122 F.3d 1396, 1406–07 (Fed. Cir. 1997), the Court emphasized that design-patent remedies should reflect the value of the claimed design, not unrelated features or market forces.

The statutory history points the same way. Congress enacted the current design-patent damages provision to overrule Dobson’s perceived undercompensation, but not to authorize windfalls untethered to the patented design. See Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10, 17–18 (1886) (applying the same principle and refusing to treat the entire product as the basis for recovery absent proof of what the patented design contributed); see also Bush & Lane Piano Co., 234 F. at 906 (recognizing that the statute allows recovery of the infringer’s “total profit” on the relevant article, not on an unrelated product as a whole).

The phrase “article of manufacture” has an established limiting function in patent law. In Uniloc USA, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 632 F.3d 1292, 1318–19 (Fed. Cir. 2011), this Court rejected a damages methodology untethered to the actual patented feature because it risked arbitrary and excessive awards. The same principle applies here. If a design patent claims only part of a smartphone, then awarding all smartphone profits ignores the statutory boundary between the patented design and the rest of the product. See Georgia-Pacific Corp. v. United States Plywood Corp., 318 F. Supp. 1116, 1120–21 (S.D.N.Y. 1970), aff’d, 446 F.2d 295 (2d Cir. 1971) (damages must reflect the patented invention’s contribution to value, not the value of unpatented features).

Apple may argue that § 289’s reference to “total profit” authorizes recovery of all profits on the end product. But that reading detaches “total profit” from the immediately preceding statutory phrase, “article of manufacture,” contrary to the basic rule that a statute’s words must be read in context. See Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., 519 U.S. 337, 341 (1997). As the Supreme Court explained in United States v. Rodgers, 461 U.S. 677, 706 (1983), statutory language must be given effect as a whole, not in a way that renders a critical limitation meaningless. If “article of manufacture” is ignored, the phrase “total profit” becomes a license to capture profits from unpatented components, branding, software, and other features the design patent does not cover.

That result is especially unwarranted where, as here, the asserted designs are only a few ornamental aspects of a complex consumer device. The relevant question is whether the patented design drove the profits of the whole phone or only some smaller component. The law has never permitted a patentee to recover beyond the value of what was actually invented and claimed. See Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884) (“The patentee must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.”). That apportionment principle should apply with full force to design patents when the claimed design is only part of a larger product.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to limit any design-patent recovery to the profits attributable to the relevant article of manufacture, not Samsung’s entire profits on the finished smartphones.
