**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Prescribes a Single Standard for Competency to Proceed, and *Dusky* States That Standard.**

In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he "has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. This standard implements the core guarantee of due process: a defendant may not be tried, or made to plead, unless he understands the nature of the proceedings and can assist in his defense. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966).

Nothing in this Court's precedents suggests that the constitutional measure of competency varies with the particular decision a defendant proposes to make. To the contrary, the Court has consistently used *Dusky*'s formulation to define the capacity due process requires whenever a defendant's mental condition is put in issue. Whether the defendant faces trial, offers a plea, or elects to represent himself, the question is the same: does he possess a rational understanding of the proceedings and the ability to consult with counsel? A defendant who meets that test is competent to proceed; a defendant who does not is not.

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard has no footing in this Court's decisions. The Court has never held that a defendant must meet a higher level of mental functioning to waive counsel or plead guilty than to stand trial. Instead, those decisions that address waivers of constitutional rights presuppose a competent defendant. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), the Court defined waiver as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." That definition assumes a defendant with the capacity to understand his rights and the consequences of relinquishing them—precisely the capacity *Dusky* measures.

**II. The Ninth Circuit Conflated Competency with the Separate Requirement of a Knowing and Intelligent Waiver.**

The court of appeals' error lies in merging two distinct inquiries. Competency asks whether the defendant has the basic mental capacity to understand the proceedings and assist counsel. The waiver inquiry asks whether, given that capacity, the defendant actually understood the specific rights he relinquished and the consequences of doing so. A defendant found competent under *Dusky* may nonetheless fail to make a knowing and intelligent waiver—for example, if he is misinformed about the charges or the range of penalties. Conversely, a competent defendant is free to make decisions that others might consider foolish, including pleading guilty and forgoing mitigating evidence. The Constitution requires process, not protection from unwise choices.

*Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), recognizes the right to waive counsel and proceed pro se, but it does not announce a new competency standard. It addresses whether the waiver was "knowingly and intelligently" made. *Id.* at 835. Likewise, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969), requiring that a guilty plea be an intelligent act "done with sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances and likely consequences," speaks to the adequacy of the waiver, not to a heightened threshold of mental capacity. And *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966), merely held that where the record raises a genuine doubt about a defendant's capacity to waive counsel, a hearing is required before the waiver may be accepted. *Westbrook* did not adopt a more demanding competency standard; it applied the settled rule that a hearing is necessary when good faith doubt exists.

The Ninth Circuit's heightened standard would require trial courts to make a predictive judgment about the *quality* of a defendant's decision-making—an amorphous inquiry for which the competency framework was never designed. The *Dusky* standard, by contrast, is workable, familiar, and fully protective: it ensures that no defendant proceeds unless he rationally understands the proceedings and can assist counsel. That is all the Due Process Clause requires.

**III. The State Courts Applied the Correct Standard, and Their Findings Are Entitled to Deference.**

The state post-conviction court found Moran competent based on the reports of two court-appointed psychiatrists. Dr. O'Gorman concluded that Moran was "knowledgeable of the charges being made against him," could "assist his attorney, in his own defense, if he so desires," and was "fully cognizant of the penalties if convicted." That language tracks *Dusky*'s own terms. The reports amply support the finding that Moran had a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and could consult with counsel.

The Ninth Circuit discounted these reports because the psychiatrists were appointed to evaluate competency to stand trial rather than competency to waive counsel. That distinction is immaterial once it is recognized that a single constitutional standard governs both inquiries. The doctors evaluated Moran's cognitive functioning; whether the evaluation was requested in anticipation of trial or a plea does not change what it measured. Nor did the medication Moran was taking separately require a new hearing. The state judge was aware that Moran was on medication, and the psychiatric evaluations had already accounted for his mental state. There was no good faith doubt that Moran satisfied *Dusky*.

The state court's factual findings are entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The Ninth Circuit substituted its own view of the evidence and imposed a legal rule that has never been the law of this Court. Because Moran was competent under the governing *Dusky* standard, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
