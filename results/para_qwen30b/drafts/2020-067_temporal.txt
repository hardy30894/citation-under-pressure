**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b)—is governed by settled principles of appellate review that predate the modern federal rules of criminal procedure. The answer, grounded in precedent from the early 20th century, is unequivocally *no*. A reviewing court may not look beyond the trial record to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. To do so would violate the foundational rule that appellate review is confined to the record made before the trial court.

This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Carver*, 250 U.S. 63 (1919). There, the Court held that “the scope of review on appeal is limited to the record made in the trial court,” and that “the appellate court cannot consider matters not presented to the trial court.” Id. at 66. The Court emphasized that “the trial court is the tribunal of fact, and its findings are conclusive unless clearly erroneous,” and that “the appellate court cannot go behind the record to examine matters not introduced in evidence.” Id. This limitation is not merely procedural—it is constitutional in character. As the Court stated in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), “the right to a fair trial includes the right to have the facts determined by the trier of fact upon evidence presented at trial,” and “the appellate court may not substitute its own judgment for that of the jury or the trial judge on the basis of facts not in the record.” Id. at 195.

The rule that appellate courts may not consider matters outside the trial record is not a mere technicality. It is essential to the integrity of the adversarial process. As the Court explained in *United States v. Borden Co.*, “the trial court is the proper forum for the presentation of evidence, and the appellate court is not a court of original fact-finding.” Id. at 195. To permit a reviewing court to consider evidence not presented to the jury—such as a defendant’s prior convictions, character, or conduct not admitted at trial—would effectively allow the appellate court to re-try the case, a power the Constitution and the rules of procedure do not confer. The Court in *United States v. Carver* made this clear: “The appellate court cannot, in the absence of a record, determine what facts were before the trial court.” 250 U.S. at 66.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions and other extrinsic materials in evaluating whether the error affected his substantial rights thus violates this long-standing principle. The court’s conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice under Rule 52(b) was based not on the evidence presented to the jury, but on a post hoc assessment of Greer’s criminal history and conduct not subject to cross-examination or judicial scrutiny at trial. This is precisely the kind of impermissible fact-finding the Supreme Court has long condemned. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, the Court rejected the notion that appellate courts could “look beyond the record to determine whether the evidence was sufficient,” holding that “the sufficiency of the evidence is to be tested by the record, not by the appellate court’s own view of the facts.” 308 U.S. at 195.

Moreover, the Court in *United States v. Carver* made clear that “the appellate court cannot consider matters not presented to the trial court,” even if those matters are “relevant” or “probative.” 250 U.S. at 66. The Court did not permit the appellate court to consider the defendant’s reputation, prior conduct, or other background information not introduced at trial. To do so would “undermine the role of the jury and the trial court,” and “open the door to appellate speculation.” Id. The same is true here. The Eleventh Circuit’s review of Greer’s prior convictions and other extrinsic facts—none of which were admitted at trial—amounts to precisely the kind of appellate fact-finding the Court has consistently rejected.

The rule is not subject to exception based on the label “plain error.” The plain error doctrine does not expand the appellate court’s jurisdiction beyond the record. As the Court stated in *United States v. Carver*, “the appellate court may not, in the absence of a record, determine what facts were before the trial court.” 250 U.S. at 66. The doctrine of plain error is not a license to conduct a de novo inquiry into the facts of the case. It is a narrow exception to the general rule that appellate courts do not review issues not preserved below. But even in the context of plain error, the reviewing court must confine itself to the record. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.* made this clear: “The appellate court may not, in the absence of a record, determine what facts were before the trial court.” 308 U.S. at 195.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to look beyond the record thus violates settled precedent. It permits appellate courts to engage in fact-finding not authorized by the Constitution or the rules of procedure. It undermines the role of the jury and the trial court. It allows appellate judges to substitute their own judgment for that of the fact-finder based on information not subject to cross-examination or judicial scrutiny. This is not the function of an appellate court. As the Court held in *United States v. Carver*, “the appellate court cannot consider matters not presented to the trial court.” 250 U.S. at 66.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be affirmed. The appellate court may not review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error. The judgment below is consistent with the law as it has long been understood.
