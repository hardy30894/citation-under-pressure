**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits only to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf—requires a straightforward application of statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which rejects the long-standing “situs-of-injury” requirement, misreads the statute and contradicts the Supreme Court’s clear instruction in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), and the settled law of the Fifth Circuit. The Court should reverse.

### I. The OCSLA’s Workers’ Compensation Provision Applies Only to Injuries Occurring on the Outer Continental Shelf

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) is unambiguous: it extends LHWCA benefits to “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” (Emphasis added.) The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not mean “causally connected to” in the abstract; it refers to the *location* of the injury. The injury must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf—either on a platform, on the seabed, or on the waters above it—to fall within the statute’s scope.

This interpretation is not merely textual but consistent with the structure and purpose of OCSLA. Congress enacted OCSLA to extend federal jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf, a region beyond state control, and to ensure that workers engaged in extractive operations there are protected by a uniform federal workers’ compensation regime. See *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 215–16. But Congress did not intend to extend federal workers’ compensation coverage to every activity related to offshore operations, no matter how remote or land-based. To do so would eviscerate the geographic boundary that defines the outer Continental Shelf and render the statute’s situs requirement meaningless.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that OCSLA’s coverage is *primarily* determined by *locale*. In *Tallentire*, the Court held that the choice-of-law provision of OCSLA, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), applied only to the “subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” 477 U.S. at 219. The Court explicitly noted that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to § 1333(b), which extends LHWCA benefits *only* to injuries occurring “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The Court’s footnote in *Tallentire*—which the Ninth Circuit mischaracterizes as dictum—was not a mere observation but a recognition that the *situs* of the injury, not just the causation, is central to OCSLA’s coverage. See id. at 219 n.2 (“We note that because this case does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b), this provision has no bearing on this case.”). The Court’s statement that § 1333(b) “makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act” only for injuries “resulting from operations on the Outer Continental Shelf” underscores that the injury must occur *in the context of* those operations—i.e., on the Shelf itself.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which allows coverage for injuries occurring onshore if there is a “but for” connection to offshore operations, is inconsistent with this principle. Under that test, an employee injured in a car accident on the way to a helicopter that would take him to a platform could qualify for benefits—even though the injury occurred miles from the Shelf and had no connection to the *situs* of the operation. See *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 809–11 (3d Cir. 1988). But the Supreme Court has rejected such expansive interpretations in other contexts. In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Court held that a worker injured on a platform located in state waters was not entitled to LHWCA benefits because the platform was not on the outer Continental Shelf. Id. at 422–24. The Court emphasized that “the outer Continental Shelf is a distinct and defined area,” and that “Congress intended to limit the application of the LHWCA to that area.” Id. at 423. The same logic applies here: if the injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside the scope of § 1333(b), regardless of the nature of the underlying operations.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Creates an Unworkable and Contradictory Standard

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is not only textually unsupported but also unworkable. It invites endless litigation over the degree of connection between an injury and offshore operations, with no clear line for determining when the nexus is “substantial enough.” The Fifth Circuit, which has consistently applied a situs-of-injury requirement, has correctly held that “an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf.” *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). This rule is administratively clear and consistent with the statute’s geographic focus.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s approach contradicts the Supreme Court’s decision in *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), which held that warehousemen engaged in intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land were covered under the LHWCA. But the injury in *Pfeiffer* occurred on a pier—“a maritime situs”—and the Court explicitly relied on the physical location of the injury. Id. at 71, 83.
