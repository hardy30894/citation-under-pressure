**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for “retirement funds” does not turn on a label alone. This Court has repeatedly required a statute’s words to be read in context and in light of their evident function. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543-44 (1940). Read that way, an inherited IRA is not money set aside for the debtor’s retirement; it is money required to be distributed, and therefore available for present consumption, long before retirement. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that such an account falls outside the exemption.

First, an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” as Congress used that phrase. The account here was inherited from Heidi Clark’s mother. Unlike a traditional IRA held for the owner’s own retirement, an inherited IRA forbids further contributions and requires annual distributions. That structure is fundamentally inconsistent with the ordinary understanding of retirement savings. Congress “does not, one might say, hide elephants in mouseholes.” *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 531 U.S. 457, 468 (2001). If Congress intended to exempt funds that are no longer locked away for retirement but instead must be paid out on an accelerated schedule, it would have said so expressly. See *Security Nat’l Bank v. Young*, 806 F.2d 1309, 1311-12 (5th Cir. 1987) (bankruptcy exemptions construed according to statutory language).

Second, the exemption protects funds that retain their retirement character, not funds whose character has changed. Under ordinary principles, a statutory term takes its meaning from the thing described, not from the thing’s distant origins. The inherited IRA here is a tax-deferral vehicle, not retirement savings for Heidi. It may postpone taxation, but it does not preserve money for use after her working life. A statutory exemption should not be expanded beyond its terms simply because the asset was once associated with retirement planning. As this Court has observed, “general words are not to be so construed as to include things not within the intention of the legislature.” *United States v. Merriam*, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923).

Third, the debtor’s reading would create an exemption detached from bankruptcy’s basic principle that the estate includes the debtor’s property unless a specific exemption applies. *Schwab v. Reilly*, 560 U.S. 770, 774-75 (2010). The Bankruptcy Act historically favored a fresh start, but only within defined limits. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244-45 (1934). Those limits matter because exemptions are exceptions to the general rule that creditors may reach a debtor’s assets. See *Harmon v. United States*, 116 U.S. 195, 198-99 (1886). Here, the debtor seeks to exempt a fund that the tax code itself treats as subject to mandatory distributions. That is not retirement wealth in any meaningful sense.

The contrary view relies on the fact that the original owner’s IRA was once retirement money. But the relevant inquiry is the nature of the asset in the debtor’s hands at bankruptcy. This Court has long treated exemption questions as turning on present legal status, not remote history. See *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310, 313-14 (1924). Once Heidi inherited the account, she no longer held a retirement fund for her own later years; she held a distribution stream taxable and usable now. That is why the Seventh Circuit correctly rejected the attempt to transform a mandatory-withdrawal account into exempt retirement property.

Finally, the debtor’s position would create an unwarranted windfall. Congress chose to protect retirement savings, not inheritances. A contrary rule would allow debtors to shelter assets simply because they once passed through an IRA. Courts should not infer so sweeping an exemption absent clear statutory text. *United States v. Ryan*, 284 U.S. 167, 175 (1931). Because an inherited IRA is not a fund maintained for the debtor’s retirement, it is not exempt under the Bankruptcy Code. The judgment should be affirmed.
