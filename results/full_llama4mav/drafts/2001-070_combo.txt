ARGUMENT

The execution of mentally retarded persons is not "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that he could not be sentenced to death because he is mentally retarded. Although the Court in Penry held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded persons, it noted that the sentencing jury should be able to consider and give effect to mitigating evidence of mental retardation. See also Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910) (noting that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual" in that they are "greatly disproportioned to the offense charged").

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" is rooted in the English Bill of Rights, which prohibited "cruel and unusual punishments" as a check on the power of the monarch. See Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 675 (1962) (Douglas, J., concurring) (citing 1 Journals of the Continental Congress 76-77 (1774)). The American colonies adopted this prohibition, and it was later incorporated into the Eighth Amendment. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 446-47 (1890) (noting that the Eighth Amendment was adopted to prevent the infliction of "cruel and unusual punishments" such as those prohibited by the English Bill of Rights).

Historically, the Eighth Amendment has been interpreted to prohibit punishments that are "barbarous" or "cruel" in the sense that they involve "torture or a lingering death." In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. at 447. See also Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878) (noting that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that involve "torture" or "unnecessary cruelty"). The execution of mentally retarded persons does not involve such "barbarous" or "cruel" treatment.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" is not a static concept, but rather one that evolves with societal norms and values. See Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (noting that the Eighth Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society"). However, as the Court noted in Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947), the fact that a punishment is "unusual" does not necessarily render it "cruel and unusual." See also Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. at 378 (noting that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "cruel" or "unusual" in the sense that they are "greatly disproportioned to the offense charged").

The fact that some states have enacted legislation prohibiting the execution of mentally retarded persons does not necessarily establish a national consensus against such executions. See Malloy v. South Carolina, 237 U.S. 180, 185-86 (1915) (noting that the Eighth Amendment is not violated by a punishment that is authorized by state law, even if it is considered "cruel and unusual" by some). As the Court noted in Howard v. Fleming, 191 U.S. 126, 136 (1903), the Eighth Amendment is not a limitation on the power of the states to impose punishments, but rather a check on the federal government's power to impose "cruel and unusual punishments."

In conclusion, the execution of mentally retarded persons is not "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh in rejecting Atkins' contention that he could not be sentenced to death because he is mentally retarded. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court.
