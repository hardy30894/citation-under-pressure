The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted expansion of the Equal Protection Clause.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954) (striking down segregation in public schools); McLaughlin v. Florida, 379 U.S. 184 (1964) (invalidating a statute that prohibited interracial cohabitation). The Clause is not a general antidiscrimination provision, but rather is aimed at protecting against certain types of invidious discrimination. See, e.g., Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256, 272 (1979) ("[T]he guarantee of equal protection ... is not a source of substantive rights or liberties, but rather a right to be free from invidious discrimination in statutory classifications and other governmental activity.").

The Seventh Circuit's "class of one" theory ignores this fundamental principle and would effectively constitutionalize many ordinary torts and contract disputes. See, e.g., Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) ("The unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination."). Respondent Olech's claim that the Village of Willowbrook demanded an additional 18-foot easement from her, while only requiring a 15-foot easement from other property owners, is more properly characterized as a dispute over the Village's exercise of its discretion, rather than a claim of invidious discrimination.

Furthermore, allowing "class of one" claims would create a significant risk of judicial second-guessing of the decisions of local governments and other state actors. See, e.g., FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993) ("In areas of social and economic policy, a statutory classification that neither proceeds along suspect lines nor infringes fundamental constitutional rights must be upheld against equal protection challenge if there is any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis for the classification.").

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's "class of one" theory is not supported by the text or history of the Equal Protection Clause. Respondent Olech's claim should be dismissed for failure to state a cognizable claim under the Equal Protection Clause.
