ARGUMENT

Harold Wasek’s evidence creates a triable issue that Paul Ottobre’s conduct constituted harassment because of sex. Title VII prohibits discrimination “because of . . . sex,” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and same-sex harassment is actionable when the plaintiff shows the conduct was motivated by sexual desire, Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 80 (1998). Ottobre’s repeated grabbing of Wasek’s buttocks, poking him with tools, and statements such as “you know you like it sweetheart” and “you’ve got a pretty mouth” supply direct evidence of sexual motivation. See id. at 80-81.

The conduct was also severe or pervasive. A reasonable person would find the combination of physical touching and explicit commentary both objectively and subjectively hostile. Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986); Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21-22 (1993). Wasek repeatedly objected and ultimately resigned, satisfying both prongs.

Arrow Energy is liable because it failed to remedy the harassment after actual notice. Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742, 759 (1998); Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 807 (1998). Wasek reported the conduct to Pat Tripp, who threatened retaliation, and to Dick Smillie and Bill Strong, who suggested fighting rather than investigating. This response was unreasonable as a matter of law. Williams v. Gen. Motors Corp., 187 F.3d 553, 561 (6th Cir. 1999).

Wasek’s resignation constituted a constructive discharge. The employer’s refusal to act left him no reasonable alternative but to quit. Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders, 542 U.S. 129, 147 (2004). The same facts support his claim under the Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act, which Michigan courts construe consistently with Title VII. Chambers v. Trettco, Inc., 463 Mich. 297, 313-14 (2000); Downey v. Charlevoix County Bd. of Comm’rs, 291 Mich. App. 415, 425 (2011).

Because genuine issues of material fact exist on each element, summary judgment was improper.
