**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (“OSH Act”) applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Rig 52 was a workplace: it was a fixed location where employees performed drilling operations in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Act’s purpose is “to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). Nothing in the Act excludes uninspected vessels from its coverage.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision preempts OSHA only when another agency *actually exercises* authority over the specific working conditions at issue. *See Southern Steamship Co. v. NLRB*, 316 U.S. 31, 38 (1942) (preemption requires “actual exercise of power”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal and does not extend to the hazards cited here.

The Coast Guard regulates uninspected vessels only as to specific equipment: fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and alerting equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. It has no authority to prescribe or enforce standards for drilling operations, blowout prevention, or general workplace safety—the very conditions that caused the explosion on Rig 52. The Coast Guard’s own investigation did not issue any citations or regulations addressing those conditions. Thus, the Coast Guard does not “exercise statutory authority” over the working conditions at issue, and § 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA.

The Fifth Circuit erred in relying on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). Both cases involved *inspected* vessels subject to comprehensive Coast Guard regulation. *Clary* did not address uninspected vessels; the court simply stated that OSHA regulations “do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” 609 F.2d at 1122. But that statement was dicta—the vessel in *Clary* was inspected, and the court did not consider the distinction. *Donovan* likewise involved an inspected vessel and reaffirmed *Clary* without analyzing uninspected vessels. 720 F.2d at 826. Neither case holds that the Coast Guard’s minimal authority over uninspected vessels preempts OSHA.

Moreover, the workers on Rig 52 are not “seamen” under the Jones Act or general maritime law. They are land-based drilling employees temporarily assigned to a barge. *See McDermott Int’l, Inc. v. Wilander*, 498 U.S. 337, 354 (1991) (seaman status requires “substantial connection to a vessel in navigation”). The Fifth Circuit’s own precedent recognizes that drilling barge workers may not be seamen. *See Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray*, 361 F.2d 432, 436 (5th Cir. 1966) (drilling barge worker not a seaman when vessel is not in navigation). Even if they were seamen, the Coast Guard’s limited regulations do not occupy the field. *See* *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 241 (2002) (OSHA may regulate where Coast Guard has not acted).

The Fifth Circuit’s holding creates an anomaly: uninspected vessels, which receive *less* Coast Guard oversight, would be *more* insulated from OSHA regulation than inspected vessels. Congress intended no such result. The OSH Act’s preemption provision is narrow, designed to avoid “overlapping regulation.” *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 827. Where the Coast Guard does not regulate, there is no overlap. OSHA’s citation here fills a regulatory gap, consistent with the Act’s purpose.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit and hold that OSHA has jurisdiction over uninspected vessels subject to minimal Coast Guard regulation.
