**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances not required to be determined by a unanimous jury—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has repeatedly held that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, nor does it require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation of death. *See Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000); *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296, 301 (2004); *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002). The Florida Supreme Court’s interpretation of *Ring*—that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find aggravating circumstances in a capital sentencing scheme where the legislature has designated them as sentencing factors rather than elements of the offense—remains sound and consistent with precedent.

In *Ring*, the Court held that Arizona’s capital sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because the aggravating circumstances were “elements” of the greater offense of first-degree murder, not mere sentencing factors. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 592. The Court emphasized that “the Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial applies to any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum.” *Id.* at 592. But the Court did not hold that every aggravating circumstance in every capital sentencing scheme must be found by a jury, nor did it require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation. Indeed, the Court explicitly acknowledged that “the Constitution does not require that a jury find every fact necessary to impose a sentence.” *Id.* at 609. The Court left open the question of whether a state may constitutionally assign the determination of aggravating circumstances to the judge, so long as the jury’s role is meaningful and the defendant has a right to a fair sentencing proceeding.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies this standard. Under Florida law, the jury’s role is to make a recommendation of death or life imprisonment based on the evidence presented. The trial court then considers the jury’s recommendation, the aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and the sentencing guidelines before imposing sentence. *See § 921.141, Fla. Stat.* The jury is not required to unanimously find aggravating circumstances, nor is it required to unanimously recommend death. But the jury is instructed on the statutory aggravating and mitigating factors, hears all relevant evidence, and deliberates in a structured, guided process. This process ensures that the jury’s recommendation is informed and meaningful, and that the defendant receives a fair and reliable sentencing determination.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find aggravating circumstances in capital cases. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 650 (1990) (upholding Arizona’s capital sentencing scheme in which the judge, not the jury, found aggravating circumstances); *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 431, 442 (2016) (per curiam) (affirming that *Ring* does not require a jury to find aggravating circumstances in Florida’s scheme). In *Hurst*, the Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme did not violate the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s recommendation of death was not a mere advisory opinion but a binding part of the sentencing process. *Id.* at 442. The Court emphasized that “the jury’s role in the capital sentencing process is not advisory but is instead a critical component of the sentencing determination.” *Id.* at 443. The fact that the jury need not be unanimous in its recommendation does not render the process unconstitutional. *See id.* at 444.

Nor does the lack of unanimity in the jury’s recommendation violate the Eighth Amendment. The Eighth Amendment requires that capital sentencing procedures be reliable and that the death penalty be imposed only in the most egregious cases. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 188 (1976). Florida’s scheme satisfies this standard. The jury is instructed on the statutory aggravating and mitigating factors, hears extensive evidence, and deliberates in a structured manner. The trial court then reviews the entire record and makes an independent determination of whether the aggravating circumstances outweigh the mitigating circumstances. *See § 921.141(7), Fla. Stat.* This bifurcated process ensures that the death penalty is not imposed arbitrarily and that the sentence is based on a reasoned, individualized determination.

The dissent’s concern about Florida’s “outlier status” is misplaced. The fact that Florida is the only state that does not require a unanimous jury recommendation of death does not render its scheme unconstitutional. The Constitution does not require states to conform to a national consensus on capital sentencing procedures. *See *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (the Eighth Amendment is not “a mere reflection of the prevailing moral standards of the community”). The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that a state’s capital sentencing scheme is unconstitutional simply because it differs from those of other states. *See *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991) (“The Eighth Amendment does not require that a State’s capital punishment scheme be identical to that of other States.”).

Moreover, the dissent’s reliance on *Woodward v. Alabama*, 134 S.Ct. 405 (2013), is inapposite. That case involved a denial of certiorari, not a merits decision, and the dissenting Justices expressed “deep concerns” about Alabama’s scheme, not a conclusion that it was unconstitutional. *Id.* at 405 (Sotomayor, J., dissenting). The Court has not held that a non-unanimous jury recommendation violates the Constitution.
