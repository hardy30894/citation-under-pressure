**ARGUMENT**

The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover cleanup costs from the United States. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA resolved Guam’s liability for response actions at the Ordot Dump, thereby triggering CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing Guam’s right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statutory framework, disregards binding precedent, and undermines the carefully calibrated balance Congress struck between encouraging settlements and preserving equitable recovery rights. This Court should reverse.

### I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs). The statute of limitations for such a claim is three years, see 42 U.S.C. § 113(g)(3), and the right to contribution is the exclusive remedy for costs incurred pursuant to a settlement. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). The key inquiry is whether the settlement “resolved” liability for a response action.

Here, the 2004 Consent Decree unambiguously resolved Guam’s liability. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system—precisely the remedial actions mandated by the Clean Water Act and later recognized as “response actions” under CERCLA. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 101(24) (defining “remedial action” as “actions consistent with permanent remedy” including “collection of leachate and runoff”). The Decree was “judicially approved,” *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, slip op. at 1 (D. Guam Jan. 24, 2008), and explicitly bound both parties. *Consent Decree* at 3, J.A. 92. The fact that the Decree disclaimed liability does not negate its effect: “parties often expressly refuse to admit liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1118, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). The Supreme Court has held that “a settlement agreement that imposes obligations to perform remedial actions constitutes a resolution of liability for purposes of CERCLA’s contribution provisions.” *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 64 (1998).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that the Decree did not “resolve” liability because it lacked a formal admission of fault—misapplies the law. *See* *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding that a consent decree resolved liability even though it contained a disclaimer of liability). The Court has long recognized that “liability” under CERCLA is not synonymous with fault or guilt, but rather with legal responsibility for response costs. *See* *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009). The Decree’s substantive obligations—paying penalties, closing the landfill, and installing a cover system—constitute a full performance of response actions, which is precisely what § 113(f)(3)(B) contemplates.

### II. Section 107(a) Is Inapplicable Once a Settlement Resolves Liability

Guam’s claim under § 107(a) is foreclosed by the statutory structure of CERCLA. Section 107(a) allows recovery of “all costs of removal or remedial action incurred by the United States Government or a State or an Indian tribe,” or “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person.” 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(A), (B). But § 113(f)(3)(B) explicitly provides that a party who has settled with the government “shall not be liable for claims for contribution regarding matters addressed in the settlement.” Id. § 113(f)(2). This creates a clear hierarchy: once a settlement resolves liability, the right to contribution under § 113(f) is triggered, and the right to cost recovery under § 107(a) is extinguished.

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that Guam may proceed under § 107(a) despite the settlement “undermines the statutory scheme.” *See* *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167. If a party could bypass the contribution regime by invoking § 107(a), Congress’s intent to incentivize settlements would be defeated. *See* *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. at 64 (“Congress intended to encourage settlements by providing immunity from contribution claims”). The Court has held that “a party that has settled with the government cannot later bring a cost-recovery action under § 107(a) for the same response costs.” *United States v. A.H. Robins Co.*, 788 F.2d 994, 1000 (4th Cir. 1986). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding creates a loophole that Congress did not intend.
