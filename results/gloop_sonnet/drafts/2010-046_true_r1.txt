**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court's Population Order Was a Permissible Exercise of the Judiciary's Longstanding Equitable Power to Remedy Constitutional Violations in State Prisons.**

This Court has never doubted that conditions of confinement may themselves constitute the constitutional violation to be remedied. In *Sweeney v. Woodall*, 344 U.S. 86 (1953), the prisoner asserted "that his past confinement had amounted to cruel and unusual punishment" and that continued confinement of that character would perpetuate the violation. The premise underlying that claim — that a State's own prison conditions can offend the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments — is precisely the premise on which the district court here acted after six days of evidentiary hearings and years of failed remedial efforts.

Once such a violation is found, the federal courts possess broad authority to compel the responsible sovereign to correct it. This authority does not evaporate merely because the defendant is a State exercising its penal function. In *Johnson v. Avery*, 393 U.S. 483 (1969), this Court struck down a prison regulation because it "conflicted with" the federal right at issue, notwithstanding the State's asserted interest "in preserving prison discipline." The Court's willingness to override prison administrators' own judgment about how to run their institutions when that judgment collides with a federal right applies with equal — indeed greater — force where, as here, the very structure of confinement (overcrowding) is the mechanism of the violation.

Nor is the duty to provide humane custodial conditions a novel judicial invention. Congress itself long ago recognized that those who hold prisoners in custody must "provide suitable quarters and provide for the safekeeping, care, and subsistence of all persons charged with or convicted of offenses against the United States." *United States v. Muniz*, 374 U.S. 150 (1963) (quoting 18 U.S.C. § 4042). Even a nineteenth-century territorial convict-labor statute, reviewed by this Court, conditioned any contract for prisoner labor on the inmates being "properly clothed and fed." *Nugent v. Arizona Improvement Co.*, 173 U.S. 338 (1899). If the minimal baseline of adequate subsistence was understood to be nonnegotiable even in the context of contracted convict labor a century ago, it cannot now be argued that a court is powerless to order the reduction of a population so large that the State itself cannot supply that baseline.

**II. The Order Was Narrowly Tailored, Following Years of Less Intrusive Remedies That Had Failed.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act's insistence that relief be no broader than necessary does not require courts to ignore a record establishing that less drastic remedies were tried and failed. This Court has long refused to force litigants through additional, futile procedural hoops before affording relief when the available alternative avenues have already proven inadequate. In *Houghton v. Shafer*, 392 U.S. 639 (1968), the Court reversed a dismissal premised on failure to exhaust administrative remedies, recognizing that requiring resort to an illusory process before affording relief serves no purpose. The same logic controls here: the district court did not leap to a population order. It first entered stipulated remedial orders in 2002 and 2004, then appointed a Receiver in 2006 after finding, on an extensive evidentiary record following six days of hearings, that California had a lengthy history of constitutional violations in its prison medical system and had failed to comply with the court's earlier remedial orders — findings California did not even appeal. Only after those measures failed did the court turn to population reduction. That sequence is itself the embodiment of the narrow-tailoring principle.

Moreover, a population-based remedy is not some unprecedented intrusion into prison administration; it is a species of classification of the kind this Court has repeatedly sustained when grounded in legitimate distinctions among categories of prisoners. In *Finley v. California*, 222 U.S. 28 (1911), the Court upheld a statute differentiating punishment for "life termers" from that imposed on prisoners serving lesser terms, explaining that "[t]he classification in question is not arbitrary but is based upon valid reasons and distinctions." A remedial order directing the release of prisoners identified according to appropriate, non-arbitrary criteria — the very approach contemplated by the three-judge court — reflects no greater intrusion on state penal authority than the classification schemes this Court has already approved.

**III. Federalism Concerns Do Not Bar the Relief Ordered.**

Petitioners' invocation of comity cannot immunize continuing constitutional violations. This Court has recognized that principles of comity between sovereigns yield when the enforcement of a judgment requires it; the sovereign holding custody may retain control "until the end of his term," but that control is always subject to the demands of law and cannot be wielded to defeat a court's remedial authority. *Ponzi v. Fessenden*, 258 U.S. 254 (1922). And this Court has not hesitated to adjudicate disputes bearing directly on the size and management of state prison populations. *Alabama v. Arizona*, 291 U.S. 286 (1934). At the same time, any remedy must remain tied to the violation actually found — a principle this Court applied in construing the scope of federal authority over prison officials' conduct toward inmates. *United States v. Jones*, 345 U.S. 377 (1953). The order below satisfies that principle: it is confined to the population level the three-judge court found necessary, and no more, to eliminate the Eighth Amendment violation.

The judgment should be affirmed.
