# ARGUMENT

## I. The Warrant Lacked Probable Cause Connecting the Alleged Crimes to Joubert's Parents' Home

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant issue only upon "probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation," and that support must consist of "facts or circumstances," not conclusions. *Nathanson v. United States*, 290 U.S. 41, 47 (1933). A "mere affirmance of belief or suspicion is not enough." *Id.* The affidavit here rested on precisely such conclusory assertions: the officer-affiant's generalized statement that molesters "often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents" supplied no particularized link between that generalization and the residence to be searched. As in *Giordenello v. United States*, 357 U.S. 480, 486 (1958), where the Supreme Court invalidated a warrant issued on a complaint that "did not indicate any sources for the complainant's conclusion" that the defendant committed the offense, the affidavit supporting the Joubert warrant supplied conclusions rather than the "underlying circumstances" necessary for an independent judicial determination. *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 114 (1964).

The affidavit's factual core—reports of molestation occurring years earlier at other addresses, and SJ's account of Joubert dismantling a computer—did not establish that evidence of the charged crimes would be found at the Manchester residence. This is exactly the failing the Fourth Amendment guards against: permitting police, rather than "a neutral and detached magistrate," to draw the inferential leap from general criminal propensity to a specific location. *Johnson v. United States*, 333 U.S. 10, 14 (1948). The warrant here substituted the officer's training and experience for the particularized nexus the Constitution demands, and the resulting search, and all evidence flowing from it including the VHS tape, must be suppressed.

## II. Admission of Uncharged Molestation Testimony Was an Abuse of Discretion

The district court permitted the government to introduce testimony regarding uncharged sexual misconduct spanning decades, unrelated to the charged offenses involving KC. Courts "following the common-law tradition almost unanimously have come to disallow resort by the prosecution to any kind of evidence of a defendant's evil character to establish a probability of his guilt." *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948). This rule exists because propensity evidence "overpersuades" the jury, inviting conviction based on who the defendant is rather than what he did. *Id.* at 476.

The Fourth Circuit's decision in *Lovely v. United States*, 169 F.2d 386 (4th Cir. 1948), is instructive. There, the court reversed a conviction where the prosecution introduced testimony of prior sexual misconduct with a different victim, holding that such evidence's "slight" probative value with respect to the charged offense was outweighed by its capacity to inflame the jury against the defendant as a person of bad character. *Id.* at 390. The uncharged testimony admitted against Joubert—involving SJ's decades-old allegations bearing no temporal or factual connection to KC—suffered the identical defect. Its only function was to portray Joubert as a person predisposed to molestation generally, precisely the inference *Michelson* forbids. The error was not harmless, given that the VHS tape, while damning, related only to KC, and the uncharged testimony substantially bolstered the government's broader narrative of predation.

## III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert's Conduct

Congress's Commerce Clause authority, even under its broadest formulations, requires that regulated activity "have such a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce" that regulation is appropriate to protect that commerce from burdens. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937). Even the expansive aggregation principle of *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), was grounded in activity's demonstrable effect, in the aggregate, on an interstate market. Joubert's production of a single videotape, filmed and retained wholly within New Hampshire and Maine, for his own private possession, never entered the stream of commerce contemplated by *Wickard* or by *Champion v. Ames*, 188 U.S. 321 (1903), which upheld federal regulation of materials actually transported across state lines. Absent proof that the videotape or photographs moved in or substantially affected interstate commerce, the statutes' application to this purely local, non-commercial conduct exceeds the Commerce Clause power recognized even under the most permissive pre-existing precedent.

## IV. The 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

While sentencing judges enjoy broad discretion, *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949), that discretion is not boundless; punishment must bear a rational relationship to the offense and the offender. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910) (punishment must be "graduated and proportioned" to the offense). A forty-year term, effectively a life sentence for a first-time federal offender convicted of offenses centered on a single course of conduct with one victim, is grossly disproportionate when measured against sentences imposed for comparable federal exploitation offenses. The district court's reliance on uncharged, unadjudicated allegations spanning decades to enhance the sentence compounded the disproportionality, effectively punishing Joubert for conduct never subjected to the safeguards of trial. The sentence should be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing untainted by consideration of unproven allegations.
