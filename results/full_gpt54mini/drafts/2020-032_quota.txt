## Argument

The judgment should be affirmed. A case becomes moot only when “it is impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever to the prevailing party.” *Knox v. Serv. Emps. Int’l Union, Local 1000*, 567 U.S. 298, 307 (2012). But that principle does not bar nominal-damages claims. The Constitution’s case-or-controversy requirement is satisfied where a plaintiff seeks “retrospective relief” for a completed constitutional injury. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978); *Memphis Cmty. Sch. Dist. v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 307–08 (1986). And this Court has long recognized that “the prevailing party in a § 1983 action may recover nominal damages” when actual injury is not proved. *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 112 (1992).

A. A completed First Amendment violation is not erased by later policy revision.

Respondents’ claims arose from a completed injury: campus police stopped Uzuegbunam twice under the challenged speech policy, and Bradford alleged a continuing deterrent effect from the same policy. A government defendant cannot moot such claims simply by changing the challenged rule midstream. Although voluntary cessation can sometimes moot a case, the defendant bears the “heavy burden” of showing that “the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to start up again.” *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000); *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982). That burden is especially demanding where the defendant retains discretion to reinstate the old policy. *Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia, Inc. v. Comer*, 582 U.S. 449, 457 n.1 (2017).  

Here, the policy revision did not extinguish the alleged past constitutional violations. As this Court has explained, “repeal of a challenged law” does not necessarily moot a claim for retrospective relief. *Coal. for the Abolition of Marijuana Prohibition v. City of Atlanta*, 219 F.3d 1301, 1328 (11th Cir. 2000). The relevant question is not whether respondents still need an injunction, but whether the court can still award relief for the injury already inflicted. Because nominal damages serve that function, the case remains live.

B. Nominal damages preserve Article III jurisdiction for completed constitutional violations.

A nominal-damages award is not advisory; it is a judgment that the plaintiff’s rights were violated. In *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978), the Court held that because the denial of procedural due process is “absolute,” a plaintiff who proves a violation but no actual injury is entitled to nominal damages. *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 112 (1992), repeated that nominal damages are proper where a plaintiff’s rights were invaded but no compensatory damages are shown. The same logic applies to First Amendment claims. *Stachura*, 477 U.S. at 308, rejected damages measured by the “abstract value” of constitutional rights, but it did not question nominal damages for completed violations.  

The Court’s recent mootness decisions confirm the point. In *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 592 U.S. 279, 293 (2021), the Court held that a request for nominal damages “can redress a past injury” and therefore keeps a case justiciable. The Court explained that “[a] request for nominal damages satisfies the redressability element of standing where a plaintiff’s claim is based on a completed violation of a legal right.” *Id.* at 287. That rule controls here. Respondents alleged a completed violation of their speech rights under a policy that was enforced against them; nominal damages therefore prevent mootness.

C. Respondents’ request for nominal damages is sufficient even though injunctive relief became moot.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach cannot be squared with Article III or with *Uzuegbunam*. This Court has repeatedly recognized that standing and mootness turn on whether the court can grant meaningful relief. *Church of Scientology of Cal. v. United States*, 506 U.S. 9, 12 (1992); *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). A claim for nominal damages supplies that relief when a constitutional violation has already occurred. *Uzuegbunam*, 592 U.S. at 293; *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266. The fact that the college changed its policy may defeat prospective relief, but it does not eliminate the justiciability of a claim seeking recognition and redress of past harms. *Camreta v. Greene*, 563 U.S. 692, 710 n.7 (2011).

D. At minimum, the complaint should not have been dismissed as moot before respondents could amend.

Even if the original pleading were unclear, dismissal with finality was improper. Leave to amend should be “freely give[n]” when justice so requires. *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962). A district court abuses its discretion when it denies leave without a substantial reason such as undue delay, prejudice, or futility. *Forman*, 371 U.S. at 182; *Santiago v. Wood*, 904 F.2d 673, 675 (11th Cir. 1990). Here, respondents promptly indicated they could clarify and supplement their damages allegations. That is not a basis for jurisdictional dismissal.  

Because nominal damages redress completed constitutional injury, the revised policy did not moot respondents’ claims. The judgment should be reversed.
