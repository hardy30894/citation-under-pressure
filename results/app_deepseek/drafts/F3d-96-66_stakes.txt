**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because the Vessel Breached Its Active Duty to Maintain Safe Working Conditions**

The vessel’s crew created a dangerous condition by leaving the snatch block in the down position, resting atop cargo, and failed to correct it despite actual knowledge of the risk. Under *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156, 167 (1981), a vessel owes a duty to longshoremen to “exercise reasonable care to turn over the ship and its equipment in such condition that an expert stevedore can carry on cargo operations with reasonable safety.” This duty extends to “active involvement” in the stevedore’s operations when the vessel knows of a dangerous condition and the stevedore has not taken adequate steps to address it. *Id.* at 175-76.

Here, the crew left the snatch block in the horizontal position, obstructing cargo removal. The block weighed approximately 100 pounds and was fixed to the hatch cover, with metal projections that could cause injury. The crew had exclusive responsibility for moving these blocks. By failing to secure the block in the vertical position before the longshoremen began work, the vessel actively created a hazard that the stevedore could not reasonably be expected to avoid. *See Bender v. A/S Les Grosse*, 835 F.2d 67, 72 (3d Cir. 1987) (vessel liable for leaving a dangerous condition that stevedore could not safely remedy).

Moreover, the vessel had actual knowledge of the condition. The crew operated the block-and-pulley system earlier that morning and was aware that the block remained down. The vessel cannot abdicate its duty by simply waiting for the stevedore to request correction. *See Hurst v. Triad Shipping Co.*, 554 F.2d 1237, 1248 (3d Cir. 1977) (vessel’s duty includes “taking reasonable steps to prevent harm to longshoremen where the condition is known to the vessel and is not obviously subject to correction by the stevedore”). The record shows that Serbin believed the block was unsafe because it interfered with the hookup man’s escape route. The condition was not open and obvious to the stevedore’s supervisors; indeed, the stevedore had not yet addressed it. The vessel’s failure to act makes it liable.

**II. Serbin’s Decision to Move the Block Was Reasonable and Does Not Excuse the Vessel’s Negligence**

The district court likely concluded that Serbin’s voluntary action in moving the block was a superseding cause. But the vessel’s duty is not extinguished simply because a longshoreman attempts to remedy a dangerous condition created by the vessel. Under *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167, the vessel must “warn of latent defects” and “take reasonable steps to prevent injury.” The block’s location was a patent defect, but the danger of moving it—given the weight and the slippery, uneven surface of crates—was not obvious to Serbin, who had successfully moved such blocks before. *See Carpenter v. Consolidated Rail Corp.*, 854 F.2d 621, 626 (3d Cir. 1988) (holding that a plaintiff’s awareness of a condition does not amount to assumption of risk when the danger is not fully appreciated).

Serbin’s motivation was to avoid delay in a competitive industry; he reasonably believed the crew would take too long to respond. The vessel, having created the hazard, cannot rely on the longshoreman’s pragmatic decision to work around it. *See Griffith v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 929 F.2d 941, 945 (3d Cir. 1991) (vessel’s duty to intervene arises when the stevedore’s failure to correct a dangerous condition is “so obvious that the vessel should have realized it posed an unreasonable risk to the longshoremen”). The crew’s inaction left Serbin with no safe alternative. The vessel’s negligence was a proximate cause of his injury.

**III. The Vessel Had a Duty to Intervene Upon Learning of the Dangerous Condition**

Even if the stevedore bore primary responsibility for cargo operations, the vessel’s duty to intervene is triggered when it has actual knowledge of a dangerous condition that the stevedore has not corrected. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 175-76. The crew knew the block was down; they had placed it there. The vessel’s failure to send a crew member to secure the block before the longshoremen resumed work was a breach of that duty. *See Bender*, 835 F.2d at 72 (vessel liable for failing to remove a plywood sheet left by crew that created a tripping hazard). The district court’s contrary conclusion must be reversed.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for trial.
