Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Eighth Amendment does not preclude the execution of mentally retarded offenders. Although the Court acknowledged that there was a growing national consensus against executing the mentally retarded, it concluded that the consensus was not sufficiently broad to render the practice unconstitutional.

However, this trend does not necessarily indicate a national consensus against the practice. As the Court noted in Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361 (1989), the fact that some states have abolished a particular punishment does not necessarily mean that it is unconstitutional. The Eighth Amendment is not a "progressive" provision that evolves with changing societal norms, but rather it is grounded in the original understanding of the provision at the time of its adoption. See Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957 (1991).
