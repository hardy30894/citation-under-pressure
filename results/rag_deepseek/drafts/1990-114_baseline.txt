**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Preempt Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Because Appointed State Judges Are “Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level” Excluded from the Act’s Definition of “Employee.”**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person chosen by [an elected official] to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall squarely within this exclusion.

The plain language of § 630(f) demonstrates Congress’s intent to exempt high-level state appointees who exercise significant discretion. Judging indisputably involves policymaking. As the First Circuit recognized, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Missouri judges, through their common-law decisions, establish binding rules that govern future conduct. *See, e.g., Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc) (adopting comparative negligence). Moreover, Missouri’s Supreme Court exercises supervisory and rulemaking authority over the entire state judiciary. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. These responsibilities place appointed judges squarely within the “policymaking level.”

Petitioners’ narrow reading—that the exception applies only to executive or legislative appointees—would produce an absurd result: elected judges are exempt, but appointed judges would receive *no* exemption under any of § 630(f)’s four categories. Congress could not have intended to exclude entirely the appointed judiciary from the ADEA’s exemptions while exempting elected judges. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460-61 (D. Mass. 1988). The legislative history, which references “persons with comparable responsibilities at the local level,” Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers, 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179-80, does not compel a contrary result; state courts are powerful “agencies” of state government.

Because the ADEA does not clearly and unequivocally preempt state authority over judicial tenure, this Court must respect Missouri’s sovereign choice. *See United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (requiring clear statement in “traditionally sensitive areas” affecting federal balance). The Eighth Circuit correctly held that appointed state judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” and thus excluded from ADEA coverage.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.**

The Fourteenth Amendment requires only that Missouri’s age-70 retirement provision for judges be rationally related to a legitimate state interest. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 312 (1976) (per curiam). Age classifications are not suspect, and judges do not hold a fundamental right to continued public employment. *Id.* at 313.

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision satisfies rational-basis review. The State may reasonably conclude that age 70 is an appropriate line to ensure judicial competence and public confidence. As this Court has recognized, “physical ability generally declines with age,” *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 315, and the work of judges “makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode,” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir.), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). Mandatory retirement also “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), *cert. denied*, 449 U.S. 955 (1980), and “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 629. These rationales are sufficient.

Petitioners may disagree with the wisdom of Missouri’s choice, but the Constitution does not permit courts to second-guess a state’s reasonable line-drawing. *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 111 (1979) (“[E]ven if the classification . . . is not made with mathematical nicety, the Fourteenth Amendment permits it.”). The Eighth Circuit correctly affirmed the dismissal of petitioners’ equal protection claim.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
