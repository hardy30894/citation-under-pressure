Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA when it collects debts it owns. The statute’s text, structure, and purpose distinguish between those who collect “debts owed or due another” and those who collect debts for themselves. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); 15 U.S.C. § 1692e. The Act “generally does not regulate creditors,” only debt collectors. **Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 294 (1995)**; **Nagy v. Fox, 417 F.3d 134, 138 (1st Cir. 2005)**. Because Santander purchased the receivables and thereafter sought to collect on its own account, it acted as a creditor, not a debt collector. **Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 135-36 (4th Cir. 2016)**, aff’d, **137 S. Ct. 1718 (2017)**.

The starting point is the statutory definition. A “debt collector” is a person whose “principal purpose” is the collection of debts, or who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). A “creditor,” by contrast, is a person to whom the debt is owed. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). These definitions are mutually exclusive in the ordinary case. **Maguire v. Citicorp Retail Servs., Inc., 147 F.3d 232, 235 (2d Cir. 1998)**; **Pettit v. Retrieval Masters Creditors Bureau, Inc., 211 F.3d 1057, 1059-60 (7th Cir. 2000)**. Santander purchased the loans before the challenged collection activity, so the debts were owed to Santander, not “another.” **Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 137 S. Ct. 1718, 1721-23 (2017)**.

Petitioners’ theory collapses the creditor/debt-collector distinction by reading the phrase “owed or due another” to mean “originally owed to another.” But the Supreme Court rejected that reading. In **Henson**, the Court held that a company collecting debts it owns is not a debt collector under § 1692a(6)’s “regularly collects” prong, even if it acquired the debts after default. **137 S. Ct. at 1721-22, 1724**. The Court explained that the statute “focuses on third party collection agents working for a debt owner, not on a debt owner seeking to collect debts for itself.” **Id. at 1724**. That is dispositive here.

Nor does default status change the analysis. The FDCPA’s exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) addresses persons collecting nondefaulted debts “owed or due another,” but it does not transform every purchaser of defaulted debt into a debt collector. **Henson, 137 S. Ct. at 1722-24**; **Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314-16 (11th Cir. 2015)**. As the Fourth Circuit correctly recognized, Congress used default as a marker for certain servicers, not as a freestanding rule that every post-default purchaser is regulated as a debt collector. **Henson, 817 F.3d at 135-37**. The statute’s “principal purpose” prong likewise does not help petitioners unless they plausibly allege that Santander’s principal business is debt collection. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); **Barbato v. Greystone All., LLC, 916 F.3d 260, 267-68 (3d Cir. 2019)**. Petitioners did not.

This reading also accords with the FDCPA’s purpose. Congress enacted the statute “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” not to regulate all creditors collecting their own accounts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e); **Zimmerman v. HBO Affiliate Group, 834 F.2d 1163, 1167 (3d Cir. 1987)**. Extending the Act to every entity that purchases receivables after default would erase the distinction Congress drew and would subject ordinary creditors to a regime designed for third-party collection agents. **Henson, 137 S. Ct. at 1724**; **Bridge v. Ocwen Federal Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012)**.

Petitioners rely on lower-court decisions suggesting that defaulted debt purchasers are always debt collectors. Those cases cannot survive **Henson** to the extent they rest on default status alone. See **Bridge, 681 F.3d at 359**; **FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159, 171-73 (3d Cir. 2007)**; **Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1208-10 (9th Cir. 2013)**. The governing rule is the one the Supreme Court announced: a company that owns the debt it collects is not a debt collector under § 1692a(6)’s “regularly collects” definition merely because it bought the debt after default. **Henson, 137 S. Ct. at 1721-24**.

Because petitioners’ complaint alleges only that Santander purchased defaulted receivables and then attempted to collect them for its own account, it fails to plead that Santander is a debt collector under the FDCPA. The judgment should be affirmed. **Henson, 817 F.3d at 136-38; Henson, 137 S. Ct. at 1724**.
