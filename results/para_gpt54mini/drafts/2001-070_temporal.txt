## Argument

The Eighth Amendment does not forbid a State from imposing the death penalty on a defendant who is mentally retarded. The Amendment’s meaning is fixed by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), but this Court has always applied that principle through the democratic and penological judgments of the States. See *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 378 (1910). Nothing in the historical understanding of “cruel and unusual punishments” establishes a categorical exemption for mentally retarded offenders. To the contrary, the Constitution leaves substantial room for states to define punishments for murder, subject only to the outer limits of civilized justice. *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 99-101.

This Court has never held that diminished mental capacity absolutely bars capital punishment. In *Ex parte Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), the Court explained that the Eighth Amendment proscribes punishments involving “torture” or “a lingering death,” not all punishments that may seem severe by modern standards. Likewise, in *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135-36 (1878), the Court upheld the death penalty and made clear that the Amendment does not constitutionalize a broad judicial power to invalidate accepted punishments simply because they are harsh. Those decisions reflect a restrained understanding of the Amendment: the question is whether the punishment is of the kind the Amendment forbids, not whether the offender is among the class of persons a court might think less culpable.

Nor does this Court’s precedent create a categorical bar based on mental condition. In *Baatz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 8 (1967) (mem.), the Court left in place a death sentence for a defendant whose mental condition was at issue, reflecting that the Constitution does not automatically disable the capital sentencing authority where mental impairment is present. More broadly, the Court has consistently entrusted juries and sentencing authorities to consider mitigating evidence, including mental impairment, rather than treating such evidence as a constitutional prohibition on punishment. See *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246-50 (1949); *Andrews v. State of Texas*, 313 U.S. 236, 248-49 (1941). A rule of categorical exemption would go well beyond those cases.

At most, mental retardation is one circumstance that may lessen blameworthiness and should be weighed in sentencing. The Court in *Smith v. State of Texas*, 311 U.S. 128, 132-33 (1940), recognized that the criminal law may take account of mental abnormality in assessing punishment, but it did not suggest that such evidence constitutionally removes a murderer from the reach of capital punishment. And in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236-37 (1941), the Court rejected the notion that due process or the Fourteenth Amendment requires judicial substitution of a federal rule for state sentencing judgments unless fundamental fairness is denied. Virginia gave Atkins a full jury sentencing proceeding, in which he could present evidence of intellectual disability and argue for mercy. The Constitution requires no more. See *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 246-50.

The lower court’s reliance on the fact that Atkins presented mitigating evidence is consistent with this Court’s precedents. The jury heard that evidence and nonetheless concluded that death was appropriate for a brutal murder. The Eighth Amendment does not permit this Court to overturn that determination absent a settled constitutional rule. But no such rule existed before 1970. The Court’s capital cases instead show that the States retain authority to impose death for murder, even where individual characteristics of the offender are urged in mitigation. See *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. at 447; *Wilkerson*, 99 U.S. at 135-36; *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 99-101.

Finally, petitioner’s theory would require the Court to announce a substantive categorical exemption unsupported by text, history, or precedent. The Eighth Amendment’s core concern is with barbarity in the method of punishment, not a judicial reclassification of entire groups of offenders absent constitutional command. *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 378; *Wilkerson*, 99 U.S. at 135-36. Because mentally retarded offenders have never been held constitutionally immune from capital punishment, the judgment below should be affirmed.
