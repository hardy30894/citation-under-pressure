# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA'S TEXT, STRUCTURE, AND PURPOSE FORECLOSE A SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

Section 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Petitioners ask this Court to insert a geographic limitation into this text that Congress did not write. The statute conditions coverage on a causal relationship between the injury and shelf operations—not on the situs of the injury itself. This Court should reject Petitioners' invitation to rewrite the statute.

### A. The Statutory Text Imposes a Causation Requirement, Not a Situs Requirement

The phrase "resulting from" is causal language, not geographic language. Congress required that the injury occur "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf"—it is the *operations* that must be situated on the shelf, not the injury. Had Congress intended a situs-of-injury requirement, it knew how to draft one. Indeed, elsewhere in the OCSLA, Congress used explicit situs language, extending federal law "to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Section 1333(b) contains no comparable geographic limitation.

This textual distinction was not lost on this Court in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986). There, the Court expressly distinguished § 1333(b) from § 1333(a)(2)(A), observing that "[o]nly one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement"—namely, § 1333(b). *Id.* at 219 n.2. That observation confirms what the text already shows: § 1333(b) operates differently from the choice-of-law provision, which is "determined principally by locale." *Id.* at 219. Because the *Tallentire* Court itself noted that § 1333(b) "has no bearing on this case," *id.*, its passing dictum cannot be read to impose a situs-of-injury requirement that would render meaningless the causal language Congress actually chose.

### B. The Third Circuit's "But-For" Test Properly Implements the Statute

The Third Circuit correctly held in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), that § 1333(b) requires only that the claimant satisfy a causal nexus test—whether the injury would not have occurred "but for" operations on the shelf. *Id.* at 809–11. Under this approach, an employee injured while traveling to a shelf platform remains covered, because the operations causing the journey originated on the shelf. *Id.* at 806, 811.

The Fifth Circuit's contrary rule in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), cannot be squared with the statutory text. *Mills* grafted a situs-of-injury requirement onto § 1333(b) based on this Court's decision in *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985). But *Herb's Welding* addressed an entirely different question—whether a worker on a platform in *state* waters qualified as engaged in "maritime employment" under the Longshore Act itself, not whether § 1333(b)'s extension provision contains a situs-of-injury requirement. *Id.* at 415–16. This Court's observation there that "the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation" refers to the geographic limitation on *operations*—shelf versus state waters—not to the situs of the resulting injury.

### C. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Would Undermine the Remedial Purpose of the OCSLA

The OCSLA is remedial legislation designed to ensure that workers engaged in the extraction of the Nation's offshore natural resources receive compensation for work-related injuries, regardless of the fortuity of where an accident occurs. A situs-of-injury requirement would produce arbitrary and irrational results: a worker struck by equipment while performing shelf-related duties on a platform would recover, while an identically situated worker performing the same shelf-related duties—transporting scrap metal, waiting for transport, receiving equipment—recovers nothing merely because the injury occurred moments after reaching shore.

This case illustrates the point. Juan Valladolid spent 98% of his working time on the Hogan platform. His death arose directly from operations integral to shelf extraction: consolidating scrap metal generated by platform operations for removal by third-party vendors. The forklift accident that killed him was not some random, unrelated mishap; it occurred in the course of maintaining and supporting the very shelf operations that § 1333(b) was designed to cover. A statute enacted to ensure compensation for shelf-related injuries should not be construed to deny compensation based solely on the happenstance that the fatal accident occurred onshore rather than on the platform itself.

### D. The "Substantial Nexus" Test Properly Cabins Coverage

Respondent does not contend that § 1333(b) has no limits. The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test—requiring "a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf"—faithfully implements the statutory text while avoiding the extremes of either an unbounded "but-for" causation standard or an artificial situs limitation. This standard ensures that coverage extends to injuries genuinely connected to shelf operations, without extending liability to injuries with only attenuated connections to shelf activity.

Applying this standard, the courts below should determine on remand whether Valladolid's death—arising from his consolidation of scrap metal generated by platform operations, work he performed as an integral part of his shelf-related duties—satisfies the substantial nexus requirement. The statutory text, this Court's decision in *Tallentire*, and the remedial purposes of the OCSLA all support remand for that determination, rather than the categorical geographic bar that Petitioners urge this Court to adopt.
