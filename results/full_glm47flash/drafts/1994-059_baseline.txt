**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF ARTICLE I, SECTION 5 OF THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION ARE EXCLUSIVE TO THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT AND PRECLUDE STATES FROM IMPOSING ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS ON MEMBERS OF CONGRESS.**

The Respondents argue that the Qualifications Clauses of Article I, Section 5 of the United States Constitution are exclusive to the federal government and that states lack the authority to impose additional qualifications on members of Congress. The Arkansas Constitution attempts to add a term limit qualification to the offices of U.S. Representative and Senator, but this violates the Supremacy Clause and the express terms of the federal Constitution.

The Constitution fixes the qualifications of the members of the two Houses. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 510 (1969). The House of Representatives is to consist of "Persons" who are "Citizens of the United States," "twenty-five Years of Age," and "seven Years a Citizen of the United States," and who are "Inhabitants of the State in which they shall be chosen." The Senate is to consist of "Senators" who are "Citizens of the United States," "thirty Years of Age," and "nine Years a Citizen of the United States," and who are "Inhabitants of the State in which they shall be chosen." *Id.* at 510. The Supreme Court has held that these qualifications are exclusive and that Congress cannot exclude a member for reasons not enumerated in the Constitution. *Id.* at 510–11. By extension, the States cannot add to these qualifications.

This principle was further articulated in *Hicklin v. Orbeck*, 434 U.S. 610 (1978). There, the Court held that the Constitution's grant of power to the States to regulate the times, places and manner of holding elections for Congress is limited to the mechanics of the election process, not to the qualifications of the candidates. *Id.* at 619. The Court reasoned that the Constitution's grant of power to the States to regulate the times, places and manner of holding elections for Congress is limited to the mechanics of the election process, not to the qualifications of the candidates. *Id.* at 619. The Arkansas Constitution attempts to regulate the "manner" of election by barring specific individuals from the ballot based on their service history. However, this is a regulation of qualifications, not a regulation of the mechanics of the election. The Arkansas Constitution cannot add a term limit qualification to the offices of U.S. Representative and Senator, as this would violate the exclusive qualifications set forth in the federal Constitution.

**II. THE SEVERABILITY CLAUSE CANNOT SAVE THE MEASURE BECAUSE THE STATE AND FEDERAL LIMITS WERE INEXTRICABLY LINKED IN A SINGLE PACKAGE.**

Even if the severability clause in Section 4 of Amendment 73 were given effect, the measure cannot stand because the state and federal limits were presented to the voters as a single, indivisible package. The amendment was a single initiative petition. The voters were not given a choice to approve term limits for state officials while rejecting term limits for federal officials. The amendment was presented as an "all or nothing" package. The preamble refers to "elected officials" in the singular, and Section 6 states that the provisions of the Amendment shall be applicable to "the offices specified in this Amendment." The offices specified are state and federal officeholders.

The Arkansas Supreme Court has held that the test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). There is no way to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders if Section 3 had not been in the picture. The sponsors of Amendment 73 created this uncertainty by lumping the two groups together. The two groups were not only inextricably linked — they were systemically fused in such a manner that each ceased to have a separate existence for voting purposes. Because the voters were not given a choice, the severability clause cannot save the measure.
