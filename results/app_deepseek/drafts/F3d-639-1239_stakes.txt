ARGUMENT

The district court erred by dismissing this class action as moot after the defendant served an unaccepted Rule 68 offer for the full amount of the named plaintiff’s individual claim. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a class action does not become moot simply because the named plaintiff’s personal stake is satisfied if the plaintiff has diligently pursued class certification. *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 402 n.11 (1975) (holding that mootness of named plaintiff’s claim does not moot class action when “the class was properly certified” and the controversy remains alive as to class members); *U.S. Parole Comm’n v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388, 404 (1980) (holding that a named plaintiff “retains a personal stake in the class certification issue” even after individual claim becomes moot); *Deposit Guar. Nat’l Bank v. Roper*, 445 U.S. 326, 336 (1980) (permitting appeal of class certification denial after tender of individual claim because plaintiff “remains a proper representative for the class”).

**A. The district court applied an overly rigid rule that conflicts with Supreme Court precedent.**

The district court relied on *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir. 2009), for the proposition that “a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified.” But *Clark* did not involve a situation where the defendant made a pre-certification offer of judgment while the plaintiff was diligently pursuing class certification under a court-approved schedule. In *Clark*, the named plaintiffs had already been denied class certification, and no further certification motion was pending. Here, by contrast, Lucero moved for class certification within the deadline set by the district court—a deadline that the parties had jointly proposed and the court had adopted. *Lucero v. Bureau of Collection Recovery, Inc.*, 716 F. Supp. 2d 1085, 1088–89 (D.N.M. 2010). The district court then vacated the certification hearing and dismissed the case, never ruling on the merits of the certification motion. This premature dismissal effectively denied Lucero any opportunity to invoke the “relation back” doctrine recognized in *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402 n.11, which allows a class certification motion to relate back to the filing of the complaint when the plaintiff has not been dilatory.

**B. The class certification motion was pending and should have been decided before any mootness determination.**

The Supreme Court in *Geraghty* emphasized that the “case or controversy” requirement is satisfied as long as the named plaintiff “continues to possess a ‘personal stake’ in the outcome of the controversy,” including the “right to have the class certified.” 445 U.S. at 404. Lucero’s personal stake in the certification issue remained alive because the certification motion was fully briefed and awaiting decision. The defendant’s unaccepted offer of judgment did not extinguish that stake. *See Weiss v. Regal Collections*, 385 F.3d 337, 340–41 (3d Cir. 2004) (holding that a Rule 209 offer does not moot a class action when the plaintiff has had a reasonable opportunity to move for class certification). The Third Circuit explained that a contrary rule would allow defendants to “pick off” class representatives before they can certify the class, undermining the purposes of Rule 23. *Id.* at 340. This court should adopt the same reasoning.

**C. The district court should have applied the “reasonable opportunity” test.**

The proper inquiry is whether the named plaintiff had a reasonable opportunity to file a motion for class certification before the defendant’s offer of judgment. *Weiss*, 385 F.3d at 340–41. Here, Lucero filed his motion for class certification within the court-ordered deadline—approximately eight months after the complaint was filed and within the time frame that the parties themselves had agreed would be devoted to class discovery. The defendant’s offer was served a mere six weeks after the answer was filed, before any discovery had begun. To hold that such an early offer instantly moots the class action would allow any defendant to avoid class certification by simply paying off the named plaintiff before the certification motion is due. That result is inconsistent with the “inherently transitory” exception recognized in *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402 n.11, and the policy of preserving the class action device.

**D. The district court’s dismissal prevented Lucero from appealing the certification issue.**

Under *Deposit Guaranty*, a plaintiff who has been offered full individual relief may still appeal the denial of class certification. 445 U.S. at 336. Here, the district court never ruled on the certification motion; it dismissed the entire case for lack of jurisdiction. This deprives Lucero of any opportunity to seek appellate review of the certification issue, which is a separate and important question. The court should have either ruled on the certification motion or, at minimum, allowed Lucero to proceed with his certification motion before dismissing the case.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s order dismissing the case as moot should be reversed, and the case remanded for a decision on the class certification motion.
