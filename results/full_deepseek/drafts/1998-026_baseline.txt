## ARGUMENT

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not warrant habeas relief when the defendant was already aware of that right and elected not to appeal. Such an omission is harmless error because it neither undermines the purpose of the rule nor prejudices the defendant.

### I. Rule 32(a)(2) Violations Are Subject to Harmless-Error Review

This Court has long recognized that not every deviation from the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires reversal. In *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537 (1982), the Court held that a violation of Rule 32(a)(1)—which requires the court to address the defendant personally and ask if he wishes to speak—is subject to harmless-error analysis. The Court explained that “the failure to comply with Rule 32(a)(1) does not automatically require reversal.” *Id.* at 549. Instead, the reviewing court must determine whether the error “affected substantial rights.” *Id.* at 548 (citing Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a)). The same principle applies to Rule 32(a)(2), which requires the court to advise the defendant of his right to appeal. Both provisions serve procedural purposes, and neither demands automatic vacatur when the defendant’s substantive rights remain unimpaired.

The harmless-error standard is particularly appropriate in the habeas context. In *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), the Court held that a violation of Rule 11 (governing plea colloquies) does not provide a basis for collateral relief unless the defendant can show “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice.” *Id.* at 783 (internal quotation marks omitted). A mere failure to recite a required warning, when the defendant already possesses the knowledge the warning was designed to convey, does not rise to that level.

### II. The Error Was Harmless Because Peguero Knew of His Right to Appeal

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero “knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred.” That factual finding is not clearly erroneous and is entitled to deference. Because Peguero already knew of his appeal right, the omission of the Rule 32(a)(2) advisory did not affect his ability to exercise that right. He made a knowing and voluntary decision not to appeal. The purpose of the rule—to ensure that defendants are informed of their appeal rights—was fully served.

This Court has consistently held that harmless-error review applies when a defendant’s actual knowledge renders a procedural omission inconsequential. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), the Court applied harmless-error analysis to a Rule 11 violation, noting that “the defendant’s own prior knowledge” of the information required to be conveyed can render the error harmless. *Id.* at 73. Although *Vonn* postdates the argument in this case, the principle it applied is well established: a procedural error that does not deprive the defendant of information he already possesses is harmless.

Similarly, in *United States v. Petty*, 80 F.3d 1381 (9th Cir. 1996), the court held that a district court’s failure to advise the defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless because the defendant “was aware of his right to appeal and knowingly waived it.” *Id.* at 1384. The Third Circuit’s decision below is consistent with this reasoning.

### III. Habeas Relief Requires Prejudice, Which Is Absent Here

A habeas petitioner must demonstrate that the alleged error “had a substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the outcome.” *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993). Peguero cannot make that showing. He knew of his right to appeal and chose not to pursue it. The district court’s failure to repeat that information caused him no harm. He does not claim that he would have appealed had the court advised him; indeed, the record shows he was aware and declined. Because the error did not affect his decision, habeas relief is unwarranted.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
