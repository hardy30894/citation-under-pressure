# ARGUMENT

## I. A Violation of Rule 32(a)(2) Is Not Automatically Cognizable on Collateral Attack

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) instructs that "[a]ny error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded." This Court has long held that procedural missteps in criminal proceedings are measured against their actual effect on the defendant, not evaluated as automatically reversible. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), this Court explained that the harmless-error inquiry asks whether "the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error"; only then can a court "conclude that substantial rights were not affected." *Id.* at 764–65. That principle applies with full force to the failure to advise a defendant of appellate rights under Rule 32(a)(2): the question is not whether the district court complied with the letter of the Rule, but whether that noncompliance had any effect on Peguero's ability to exercise his right to appeal.

More fundamentally, this Court has restricted collateral relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 to defects of constitutional or jurisdictional magnitude, or to errors that constitute "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). In *Hill*, the district court failed to comply with the parallel command of Rule 32(a) that a defendant be personally addressed and asked if he wished to speak before sentencing. This Court held that such a procedural omission, standing alone, did not warrant collateral relief because it was "not of itself an error of the character or magnitude cognizable under a writ of habeas corpus." *Id.* Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement, adopted from the same procedural framework, should be treated no differently. Peguero's claim—that the mere failure to recite an advisement automatically entitles him to relief regardless of his actual knowledge—cannot be reconciled with *Hill*.

This limitation on collateral review reflects the settled understanding, articulated in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), that habeas corpus was never intended to serve as a substitute for direct appeal or as a vehicle to correct every procedural irregularity that could have been, but was not, raised through ordinary appellate channels. *Id.* at 178–79. Likewise, in *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205 (1952), this Court described § 2255 as a mechanism to test the validity of a sentence in circumstances analogous to habeas corpus—not as an all-purpose remedy for technical noncompliance with procedural rules. *Id.* at 219.

## II. Peguero's Actual Knowledge of His Right to Appeal Fully Served the Purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal so that he may knowingly decide whether to exercise it. Where, as here, the district court found as a matter of fact that Peguero already possessed that knowledge, the Rule's purpose was fully realized notwithstanding the court's failure to recite the advisement. This Court's waiver jurisprudence confirms that what matters is the defendant's actual awareness of his rights, not the ritual by which that awareness is confirmed. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), this Court held that the relevant inquiry into a defendant's forfeiture of a right turns on "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right," *id.* at 464—precisely the circumstance the district court found here, since Peguero knew of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue it.

This Court's decision in *Michel v. Louisiana*, 350 U.S. 91 (1955), likewise underscores that the right to appeal is protected because of its practical importance to the defendant, not because of any freestanding entitlement to a particular script of judicial words. There, the Court examined whether the defendant's failure to pursue appellate remedies reflected a knowing choice, emphasizing that "a mere error in trial procedure" does not entitle a defendant to disregard settled procedural requirements where his own conduct demonstrates awareness of his rights. *Id.* at 93. So too here: Peguero's conceded awareness of his appellate rights forecloses any claim that the court's omission caused him to forfeit an appeal he did not know he had.

Finally, the scope of the writ itself counsels against relief. In *Holiday v. Johnston*, 313 U.S. 342 (1941), this Court reaffirmed that habeas corpus reaches only those errors that render a conviction or sentence void, not every departure from prescribed procedure. *Id.* at 349–50. And in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), this Court confirmed that federal collateral review exists to vindicate the substance of constitutional and fundamental rights, not to police technical compliance with rules whose protective purpose has already been fulfilled. *Id.* at 401–02.

## Conclusion

Because Peguero knew of his right to appeal notwithstanding the district court's procedural omission, the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied, no fundamental defect occurred, and collateral relief is unwarranted. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
