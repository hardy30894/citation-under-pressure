I. The Federal Circuit Erred in Affirming the District Court's Damages Award Based on Samsung's Entire Profits from its Smartphones

The Federal Circuit's decision to affirm the district court's damages award based on Samsung's entire profits from its smartphones contravenes the plain language of 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute provides that a design patent infringer is liable for "the total profit, but not less than $250, recovered by the defendant from the sale of the article in the case of an infringement of a patent for a design." 35 U.S.C. § 289. However, when the infringing design is only a component of a larger product, the statute requires that damages be limited to the profit attributable to the infringing component.

The Supreme Court's decision in Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd. v. Apple Inc., 137 S. Ct. 429 (2016), underscores the need for a nuanced approach to damages in design patent cases. While the Court held that § 289 requires that damages be awarded based on the "article of manufacture" to which the infringing design has been applied, it did not define the term. Id. at 435. The Federal Circuit's decision, however, effectively reads the "article of manufacture" requirement out of the statute by allowing damages to be awarded based on the entire product, rather than the component to which the infringing design was applied.

As the dissenting judges below noted, this approach is inconsistent with the statutory text and the Supreme Court's decision in Samsung. Chief Judge Prost's dissent, joined by Circuit Judges Dyk and Reyna, correctly observed that the majority's decision "effectively eliminates the 'article of manufacture' requirement" and "allows for the disgorgement of profits from the sale of an entire product, even when the infringing design is only a component of that product." Apple Inc. v. Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd., 838 F.3d 1254, 1261 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (Prost, C.J., dissenting).

II. The Federal Circuit's Decision is Inconsistent with Precedent and the Principles of Patent Damages

The Federal Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with precedent and the principles of patent damages. In cases involving utility patents, damages are typically awarded based on the value of the infringing feature, not the entire product. See, e.g., Lucent Techs., Inc. v. Gateway, Inc., 580 F.3d 1301, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2009) (damages should be based on the "value that the infringing technology adds to the accused product"). There is no principled reason why design patent damages should be treated differently.

Moreover, awarding damages based on the entire product can lead to absurd results, as it did in this case, where Samsung was liable for over $400 million in damages for infringing Apple's design patents, even though the infringing designs were only a small component of Samsung's smartphones. This outcome is inconsistent with the principles of patent damages, which aim to compensate the patent holder for the value of the infringing invention, not to punish the infringer. See, e.g., Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 648-49 (1915) (patent damages should be "proportioned to the value of the infringing invention").

The Federal Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the case remanded for a recalculation of damages based on the profit attributable to the infringing design components.
