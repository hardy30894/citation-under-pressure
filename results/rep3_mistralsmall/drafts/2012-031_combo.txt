**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it rests on an unsupported assumption that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This predominance requirement cannot be satisfied where the plaintiffs’ damages model is “speculative” or “arbitrary.” *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931). The Supreme Court has long held that antitrust plaintiffs must prove damages with reasonable certainty. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). A damages model that fails to account for individual differences in harm or relies on assumptions unsupported by the record cannot satisfy this standard.

Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class based on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which the court itself described as “speculative” and “unreliable.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 190 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Dr. McClave’s model assumed that every class member paid supracompetitive prices for non-basic cable programming due to Comcast’s clustering strategy. However, the model did not account for whether individual class members actually subscribed to the affected programming or whether they benefited from discounts or promotions. The Supreme Court has rejected damages models that ignore such individual variations. *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 123-24 (1969). Without a reliable methodology to measure damages on a class-wide basis, certification under Rule 23(b)(3) was improper.

II. The Third Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedent requiring rigorous analysis of damages models at the certification stage.

This Court has emphasized that district courts must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of all Rule 23 requirements, including predominance. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974). The Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), which the District Court relied upon, is inconsistent with this standard. The Third Circuit held that a damages model need only be “plausible” to satisfy predominance, but this Court has rejected such a low threshold. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (requiring “reasonable certainty” in damages proof).

The Third Circuit’s approach also conflicts with *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 448 (2d Cir. 1945) (L. Hand, J.), which held that antitrust damages must be proven with “some reasonable measure of exactness.” The District Court’s certification order ignored this requirement by accepting Dr. McClave’s model despite its speculative assumptions.

III. The District Court’s certification order violates the Seventh Amendment by resolving factual disputes about damages at the class certification stage.

This Court has cautioned against resolving merits issues at the certification stage. *Eisen*, 417 U.S. at 177. The District Court’s order did precisely that by accepting Dr. McClave’s damages model without resolving whether it accurately measured harm to each class member. The Supreme Court has long held that such factual disputes must be resolved at trial, not certification. *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local No. 391 v. Terry*, 494 U.S. 558, 572 (1990) (citing *Ross v. Bernhard*, 396 U.S. 531, 538 (1970)).

IV. The Third Circuit’s decision expands class certification beyond the limits set by this Court and risks undermining the due process rights of absent class members.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that class actions must provide due process protections to absent class members. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42 (1940). The Third Circuit’s decision, however, allows certification based on a damages model that does not account for individual variations in harm. This risks binding absent class members to a judgment that does not reflect their actual damages. *See, e.g., Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) (requiring notice that is “reasonably calculated” to apprise interested parties of the action).

**Conclusion**

The District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because it rests on a speculative damages model that fails to account for individual variations in harm. The Third Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedent requiring rigorous analysis of damages at the certification stage and risks undermining the due process rights of absent class members. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
