ARGUMENT

I. Clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest.

In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that a state’s clemency procedures confer no constitutionally protected liberty interest. The Court explained that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464. Because the power to grant or withhold clemency “is rarely, if ever, appropriate” for judicial oversight, *id.*, the Due Process Clause does not attach to the procedures by which a State exercises that power.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Ohio’s clemency process is “an integral part of the . . . system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant”—rests on *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). That decision, however, addressed only the first appeal as of right and expressly distinguished later stages of review. *See id.* at 394 (citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). *Dumschat* remains controlling on the distinct question of executive clemency and forecloses any claim that clemency itself creates a protected interest.

II. Voluntary participation in a clemency interview does not compel self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment prohibits only compelled self-incrimination. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316–17 (1976). Ohio’s regulation gives the inmate the option—not the obligation—to request an interview. The inmate “remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes.” Nothing in the regulation requires answers to any question or conditions clemency consideration on waiver of the privilege.

Because the interview is wholly voluntary, any statement given is not the product of state compulsion. *See id.* at 324 (holding that adverse inferences from silence in a prison disciplinary proceeding did not violate the Fifth Amendment absent compulsion). Speculation that a statement might later be used in some hypothetical future proceeding is “premature” and provides no basis for declaring the procedure unconstitutional. *Id.*

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
