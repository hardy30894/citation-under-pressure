**Argument**

Bond lacks standing to press her Tenth Amendment challenge. The Supreme Court has already held that private parties, standing alone, may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to invalidate federal action where the States themselves do not complain. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), the Court rejected private utilities’ federalism challenge, explaining that “absent the states or their officers, [they] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That rule follows from the basic structure of federal judicial power: federal courts decide cases and controversies brought by parties with their own legally cognizable interests, not generalized objections to the allocation of governmental authority. *Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co.*, 206 U.S. 230, 237–38 (1907); *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488–89 (1923).

Bond is not a State, a state officer, or a sovereign whose own powers are allegedly invaded. She is a criminal defendant seeking to defeat a valid conviction by asserting the supposed institutional rights of Pennsylvania. That is precisely the sort of claim the Court has rejected when advanced by private litigants. In *Doremus v. Board of Education*, 342 U.S. 429, 434–35 (1952), the Court held that a taxpayer could not litigate a constitutional objection absent a direct and substantial personal injury. Likewise, in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992), the Court reaffirmed that standing requires a concrete, particularized injury traceable to the challenged action and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Bond’s asserted injury is not that Congress invaded *her* constitutional prerogatives, but that Congress allegedly intruded on state criminal authority. That is a sovereign injury, not a personal one.

Nor does the Tenth Amendment transform Bond into the proper champion of state interests. The Amendment is a reservation of power to the States or the people, not a roving license for private defendants to demand strict federalism review in every prosecution. The Court in *Lane County v. Oregon*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 71, 76–77 (1869), treated the Tenth Amendment as a structural limitation protecting state authority. And in *Coyle v. Smith*, 221 U.S. 559, 567–68 (1911), the Court invalidated a federal condition interfering with a State’s sovereign prerogatives because the injury was to the State itself. Those cases confirm the point: the proper party to assert a Tenth Amendment injury is the State whose sovereignty is allegedly impaired. See also *Kansas v. Colorado*, 206 U.S. 46, 83–84 (1907) (recognizing the State’s capacity to vindicate its own quasi-sovereign interests).

The Third Circuit correctly understood that *Tennessee Electric Power* forecloses Bond’s claim. The court of appeals need not invent a new standing doctrine where the Supreme Court has already spoken. *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 326 (1941), and *Cummings v. Missouri*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 277, 320–21 (1867), both reflect the broader principle that constitutional litigation must be tied to the claimant’s own legal rights. Bond’s prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 229 may raise questions about Congress’s power as a matter of separation of powers or federalism, but those are not questions she may raise in her own right unless she can show an injury personal to her constitutional status. She cannot.

Bond’s best response is that she is directly prosecuted and therefore injured. But standing doctrine requires more than the existence of prosecution; it requires that the prosecution itself violate a right personal to the defendant. That is not so here. A defendant may of course challenge whether a statute criminalizes her conduct or whether the Government proved an offense. But she may not, absent some personal constitutional entitlement, invoke the Tenth Amendment as a substitute prosecutor for the States. *Frothingham*, 262 U.S. at 488–89; *Tennessee Electric Power*, 306 U.S. at 143–44. To hold otherwise would permit every federal defendant to convert a state-sovereignty objection into a personal defense, a result inconsistent with the Court’s repeated insistence that federal courts are not forums for abstract disagreement over the distribution of power between sovereigns. *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 486–88 (1923); *Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co.*, 206 U.S. at 237–38.

Because Bond lacks standing, the Court should affirm the judgment without reaching the merits of her Tenth Amendment attack on § 229. If the statute exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers, the States may assert that claim in an appropriate case. Bond may not.
