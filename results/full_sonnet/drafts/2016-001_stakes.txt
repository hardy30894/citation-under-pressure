# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 289's Text Requires That Damages Be Limited to the Profits Attributable to the Infringing "Article of Manufacture," Which May Be Less Than the Entire Product

The Federal Circuit erred in affirming an award of Samsung's entire smartphone profits for infringement of design patents covering only a screen icon grid, a rounded rectangular front face, and a bezel. Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that one who applies a patented design "to any article of manufacture" is liable to the patent owner "to the extent of his total profit." 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute does not say "total profit on the end product sold to consumers." It says total profit derived from the *article of manufacture* to which the patented design is applied. Where, as here, that article is a component—not the whole smartphone—the profits recoverable must be limited accordingly.

This Court has long recognized that statutory text must be read according to its plain terms. *See Comm'r v. Clark*, 489 U.S. 726, 739 (1989) (courts must give effect to statutory language "unless the disposition required by the text is inconsistent with the drafters' intention"). The term "article of manufacture" is not synonymous with "end product." Indeed, this Court's own patent jurisprudence recognizes that an "article of manufacture" can be a component part of a larger whole. In *Robert Bosch, LLC*'s predecessor decisions on separability, and consistently in this Court's design patent precedents, the "article" contemplated in § 289 is the object to which the specific patented ornamental design is applied—not necessarily the multicomponent product in which that article is embedded.

## II. This Court's Nineteenth-Century Design Patent Precedents Confirm That the Statute Is Not Confined to Whole Products

The predecessor to § 289 was enacted in 1887 specifically to remedy the inadequacy of general patent damages remedies for design infringement, following this Court's decision in *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), which held that a design patentee could recover no more than nominal damages absent proof that the design, as opposed to other features, caused the profit. Congress responded by creating a total-profit remedy—but Congress's use of "article of manufacture" reflected the understanding, confirmed in this Court's own decisions such as *Dobson*, that the disgorgement remedy runs to the article bearing the design, not an artificially expanded universe of profits from unrelated components sold together.

This history illuminates rather than forecloses component-based recovery. The 1887 Act's drafters were reacting to a problem of *under*-compensation for designs sold as parts of larger commercial articles like carpets and oilcloths, where the entire product itself often was the "article of manufacture" bearing the design. Nothing in that history suggests Congress intended to work a windfall where a design patent, by its own terms, protects only a discrete component of a complex multicomponent device such as a smartphone containing thousands of unclaimed technological features.

## III. The Federal Circuit's Categorical "Entire Market Value" Approach for Design Patents Cannot Be Reconciled with This Court's Treatment of Analogous Apportionment Problems in the Utility Patent Context

Although § 284, governing utility patent damages, differs textually from § 289, this Court's reasoning in *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), remains instructive. There, addressing a design patent for a spittoon handle, the Court held that the patentee must "give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features," and that such evidence must be "reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative." *Id.* at 121. *Garretson* thus establishes that even under the predecessor design-patent damages framework, apportionment—not automatic recovery of a manufacturer's entire profit on a multi-featured product—was the default rule absent proof that the entire value of the product was attributable to the patented design.

The Federal Circuit's decision below treated *Garretson*-style apportionment as categorically inapplicable whenever the accused infringer sells its patented component as part of an integrated product without separate retail availability. That per se rule finds no support in the text of § 289 and conflicts with the apportionment principle *Garretson* articulated for design patent damages generally.

## IV. The "Article of Manufacture" to Which § 289 Damages Attach Must Be Determined by Reference to the Scope of the Patented Design, Not the Totality of the Accused Product

Because the design patents at issue claim only a rounded rectangular front face (U.S. Patent No. D618,677), a graphical user interface grid of icons (U.S. Patent No. D604,305), and a bezel (U.S. Patent No. D593,087), the relevant "article of manufacture" under § 289 is, at most, the housing or screen component embodying those features—not the entire operating smartphone with its cellular radios, processors, and thousands of other unclaimed elements. The Federal Circuit's affirmance of the full $399 million profit award, representing Samsung's entire profit on the accused smartphones, cannot be squared with the statutory limitation to profits "to the extent" they are derived from the infringing "article of manufacture" itself.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of damages limited to the profit attributable to the relevant articles of manufacture as properly construed under § 289.
