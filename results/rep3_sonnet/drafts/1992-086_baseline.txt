# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT'S DENIAL OF ACCESS BASED SOLELY ON THE RELIGIOUS VIEWPOINT OF LAMB'S CHAPEL'S SPEECH VIOLATED THE FREE SPEECH CLAUSE

The First Amendment's guarantee of free speech applies with full force even where government has no obligation to open its property to any speaker. Once a government creates a forum for expressive activity—even a limited one—it "may not exclude speech where its distinction is not 'reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum,' nor may it discriminate against speech on the basis of . . . viewpoint." *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (citation omitted). This principle controls here regardless of how the forum is characterized.

### A. The District's Facilities Were a Limited Public Forum Open to Discussion of Family and Child-Rearing Issues

The Second Circuit correctly recognized that Center Moriches created a "limited public forum" by permitting community groups to use school facilities after hours for a wide range of social, civic, and educational purposes. *See Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983) (describing property that government has "opened for use by the public as a place for expressive activity"). The District permitted lectures on parapsychology and the human psyche, musical performances, and other community programming addressing the human condition. Having opened its property to speakers addressing family life, child development, and human psychology from secular perspectives, the District could not selectively exclude a speaker addressing the *same subject matter*—family values and child-rearing—merely because that speaker's perspective was religious.

This Court has made clear that when government permits discussion of a subject, it may not then discriminate against a particular viewpoint on that subject. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that a university that created "a forum generally open for use by student groups" could not deny access to a religious student group, because doing so would constitute "invidious viewpoint discrimination." *Id.* at 267, 277. The Court explained that an "equal access policy would not itself violate the Establishment Clause," *id.* at 271, and that the university's asserted interest in avoiding entanglement with religion did not justify viewpoint-based exclusion. The same logic applies here: the District opened its doors to discussions of family and psychological well-being, then closed them only when the speaker approached those topics from a religious viewpoint.

### B. Exclusion Based on Religious Viewpoint Is Impermissible Even in a Limited Public Forum

The court below erred in concluding that because no *prior* speaker had used explicitly religious content, the District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel was merely a permissible limitation on subject matter rather than impermissible viewpoint discrimination. This reasoning cannot be reconciled with this Court's forum doctrine. The relevant subject matter—family values and child-rearing—was plainly within the scope of matters the District permitted others to discuss. The District did not exclude all speech on this topic; it excluded only speech that approached the topic from a religious perspective. That is the essence of viewpoint discrimination.

As this Court has held, "[t]he government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses on an otherwise includible subject." *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. Local Rule 7's blanket prohibition on "religious purposes" does not merely regulate subject matter—it targets religious viewpoints on subjects the District otherwise permits to be discussed. The District's own correspondence confirms this: the Clerk denied Lamb's Chapel's application specifically because the film had "religious connotations" and was "church related," not because the subject of family life was itself excluded from the forum.

### C. The District's Justification Cannot Satisfy the Applicable Standard

Even under the more permissive standard applicable to limited public forums, exclusion of speech must be both viewpoint-neutral and reasonable. *See Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46. The District's rule fails this test because it singles out religious viewpoints for exclusion while permitting comparable secular treatment of the same subjects. A speaker may discuss child-rearing from a psychological, sociological, or humanistic perspective, but not from a religious one. This is precisely the kind of content discrimination this Court condemned in *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of University of Virginia*—though decided after the Second Circuit's ruling here, its reasoning follows directly from *Widmar* and *Cornelius*: viewpoint discrimination is "an egregious form of content discrimination" that is "presumed impermissible" even in limited forums.

## II. PERMITTING LAMB'S CHAPEL EQUAL ACCESS WOULD NOT VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE

The District's fear that granting access would run afoul of the Establishment Clause is unfounded and cannot justify viewpoint discrimination. This Court's decision in *Widmar* squarely forecloses this argument. There, the Court held that opening university facilities on an equal basis to religious and secular groups alike satisfies the *Lemon* test: it has the secular purpose of promoting free speech, its primary effect is not to advance religion because "the University's provision of benefits to religious groups would be *incidental* to the [University's] provision of a forum" for many groups, and it does not foster excessive entanglement. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75.

The same reasoning governs here. The film series at issue addressed family values and child-rearing from a religious perspective—precisely the kind of viewpoint that, under *Widmar*, does not lose First Amendment protection merely because it is religious. Allowing Lamb's Chapel to use school facilities after hours, on the same terms as other community groups, would not signal District endorsement of religion any more than permitting the Southern Harmonizers to perform sacred music did. No reasonable observer would conclude that an after-hours film series, open to the public and unconnected to any school-sponsored activity, constitutes governmental establishment of religion.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Second Circuit and hold that the District's denial of equal access to Lamb's Chapel constituted impermissible viewpoint discrimination in violation of the First Amendment's Free Speech Clause.
