**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien beyond the 90-day removal period. The text permits detention “beyond the removal period” only “pending removal,” a phrase that presupposes an eventual end to custody. When removal becomes impossible, continued detention ceases to serve any statutory purpose and instead becomes punitive.

This construction is compelled by the canon requiring avoidance of serious constitutional doubts. Indefinite civil detention of a person who has effected entry into the United States implicates the core liberty interest protected by the Due Process Clause. See Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (“Freedom from bodily restraint has always been at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause.”). The government may not detain an alien “indefinitely” merely because no country will accept him; such detention lacks both a legitimate non-punitive purpose and a reasonable relation to that purpose. See United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule—that detention may continue so long as “good faith efforts to effectuate deportation continue”—cannot be reconciled with these principles. That standard would permit detention for years or decades whenever repatriation negotiations stall, transforming a removal statute into a general preventive-detention scheme. The Court has rejected analogous attempts to convert immigration authority into open-ended incarceration. See Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 218–19 (1953) (Jackson, J., dissenting) (warning against “a power of indefinite imprisonment”).

Section 1231(a)(6) therefore contains an implicit temporal limitation: detention may last only for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the statute no longer authorizes continued custody. The habeas court must then order release under appropriate conditions of supervision. This reading respects both the statutory text and the constitutional requirement that detention bear a reasonable relation to its legitimate purpose.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
