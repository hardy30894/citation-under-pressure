ARGUMENT

The National Park Service (“NPS”) permissibly enforced its hovercraft regulation on the Nation River within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”) does not strip NPS of authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands lying within the boundaries of Conservation System Units (“CSUs”) when the regulation applies to the entire unit, not solely to “public lands.” The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is not a regulation that “applies solely to public lands” and thus NPS acted within its authority.

**I. The Plain Text of ANILCA §103(c) Does Not Limit NPS’s Authority to Promulgate Unit-Wide Regulations**

ANILCA §103(c) provides that “only public lands” within CSUs are subject to regulations under the Act. But the hovercraft prohibition was issued under the NPS Organic Act and 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—a regulation of general applicability that governs conduct throughout every unit of the National Park System. By its terms, the regulation does not distinguish between federal and non-federal lands; it prohibits hovercraft use “within” the preserve. When a statute’s language is plain, “the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” *Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.*, 534 U.S. 438, 450 (2002) (quoting *Conn. Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992)). Nothing in §103(c) purports to invalidate or restrict NPS regulations that, like the hovercraft ban, apply uniformly to all lands and waters within a CSU’s boundaries. To read §103(c) as Sturgeon urges would expand the provision far beyond its text, contravening the well-settled principle that courts “must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.” *Conn. Nat’l Bank*, 503 U.S. at 253-54.

**II. An Interpretation That Strips NPS of Unit-Wide Authority Would Undermine Congress’s Purpose in ANILCA**

ANILCA was enacted to protect Alaska’s spectacular natural resources while accommodating state and private interests. *Amoco Production Co. v. Village of Gambell*, 480 U.S. 531, 536 (1987). Congress structured the Act to preserve the NPS’s ability to manage entire park units consistently. Allowing an owner’s status to create regulatory gaps within a preserve would defeat the uniform management that the NPS Organic Act requires. Such an absurd result must be avoided. *See FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.*, 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000) (courts avoid interpretations that produce absurd results).

**III. The Federal Government Has Plenary Authority over Navigable Waters, and State Title Does Not Oust Federal Regulation**

Even assuming the submerged lands of the Nation River passed to Alaska upon statehood, federal regulatory authority remains. The United States retains a “dominant navigational servitude” over all navigable waters. *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 122-23 (1967); *see also The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870) (Congress may regulate waterborne commerce). The Submerged Lands Act of 1953, which confirmed state title to submerged lands, did not “eliminate … federal regulatory authority” over those waters. *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 32 (1997). Thus, even if Alaska owns the riverbed, NPS may lawfully regulate the activity occurring on the water surface.

**IV. Even If §103(c) Were Ambiguous, NPS’s Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference**

Should this Court find §103(c) ambiguous, NPS’s interpretation that unit-wide regulations may apply to non-federal lands is reasonable and entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). Congress expressly delegated authority to NPS to manage park units and to “make and publish such rules and regulations as he may deem necessary or proper for the use and management of the parks.” 16 U.S.C. § 1a-1 et seq. NPS has consistently interpreted its own hovercraft regulation to apply to all lands within a CSU’s boundaries, and that interpretation warrants deference. *See Auer v. Robbins*, 519 U.S. 452, 461 (1997) (agency’s interpretation of its own regulation is controlling unless plainly erroneous). Moreover, NPS’s longstanding position is a “reasonable” construction of the statute even when measured against §103(c). *See United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226-27 (2001).

**V. Sturgeon’s Reading Would Cripple the National Park System in Alaska**

Accepting Sturgeon’s argument would create a checkerboard of federal and non-federal lands within park boundaries, making uniform regulation impossible and inviting environmental harm. Such an outcome is antithetical to Congress’s design. *See Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976) (Congress has power to protect federal lands under the Property Clause). The Ninth Circuit correctly declined to adopt an interpretation that would “frustrate the purpose of the National Park System in Alaska.”

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
