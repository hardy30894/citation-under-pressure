**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) applies to the Mallard Bay drilling barge, and OSHA’s citation is not preempted by §4(b)(1). The plain text, structure, and purpose of the Act compel reversal.

**I. The Drilling Barge Is a “Workplace” Under §4(a)**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act, 29 U.S.C. §653(a), provides that the Act “shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” The drilling barge is a “workplace”—a fixed or floating place where employees perform work. The Act’s definition of “employer” is broad, covering “any person engaged in a business affecting commerce,” 29 U.S.C. §652(5), and the barge was engaged in oil and gas drilling, a commercial activity. The barge was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, a “State” under the Act. 29 U.S.C. §652(8). There is no dispute that the employees were performing employment. The barge thus falls squarely within §4(a)’s coverage.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1), 29 U.S.C. §653(b)(1), exempts “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” This provision requires that the other agency actually exercise authority over the specific working conditions at issue. *Barlow v. C.J. Langenfelder & Son, Inc.*, 468 U.S. 993, 1002-03 (1984) (preemption only when the other agency “has exercised its authority” over the particular condition). The Coast Guard has not exercised such authority over the working conditions on uninspected vessels like the Mallard barge.

The Coast Guard’s statutory authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific, enumerated items: fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and alerting equipment. 46 U.S.C. §4102. The Coast Guard has issued regulations only on those narrow subjects. 46 C.F.R. pts. 25, 26. It has not promulgated any occupational safety or health standards governing the hazards that caused the explosion—well blowout prevention, drilling operations, or general workplace safety. The Coast Guard itself acknowledged that the barge was an uninspected vessel subject to minimal regulation. *See* Joint Appendix at 15a. Because the Coast Guard does not “exercise statutory authority” over the occupational safety and health of workers on uninspected vessels, §4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Both cases involved inspected vessels where the Coast Guard exercised comprehensive regulatory authority. *Clary* involved a drilling barge, but the court did not address whether it was inspected or uninspected, and the opinion’s broad language about “seamen on vessels in navigation” is dicta. *Donovan* involved a tanker under full Coast Guard inspection and regulation. 720 F.2d at 826-27. Here, the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal and does not create an “industry-wide exemption” for seamen. *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826. The Fifth Circuit’s extension of these precedents to uninspected vessels ignores the statutory distinction that Congress drew between inspected and uninspected vessels. *See* 46 U.S.C. §3301 (inspected vessels) vs. §4102 (uninspected vessels). Congress intended the Coast Guard to regulate only certain safety equipment on uninspected vessels, leaving the OSH Act to fill the gap.

**III. The Workers Are Not “Seamen” for Purposes of OSH Act Preemption**

Even if the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels were relevant, the workers on the Mallard barge are not “seamen” under the OSH Act or general maritime law. The Supreme Court has held that seaman status requires a connection to a “vessel in navigation” that is substantial in terms of both duration and nature. *McDermott Int’l, Inc. v. Wilander*, 498 U.S. 337, 355 (1991). The Mallard barge was not a “vessel in navigation”; it was moored at a drilling site and engaged in land-based oil and gas extraction. The workers were not engaged in navigation or maritime commerce; they were drilling crew members performing tasks similar to those on land. The OSH Act’s broad definition of “employee” includes all workers, and its preemption provision does not turn on arcane maritime status distinctions. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Colomb v. Texaco, Inc.*, 736 F.2d 218 (5th Cir. 1984), and *Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray*, 361 F.2d 432 (5th Cir. 1966), is inapposite because those cases involved Jones Act seaman status, not OSH Act coverage.

**IV. The OSH Act’s Remedial Purpose Supports Coverage**

The OSH Act is a remedial statute designed “to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. §651(b). Congress intended that OSHA cover all employers not specifically exempted. The narrow preemption exception of §4(b)(1) should not be read to create a gap in coverage for uninspected vessels, where the Coast Guard has not regulated occupational safety. To hold otherwise would leave workers on such vessels without any federal safety protection—a result Congress could not have intended. *See Barlow*, 468 U.S. at 1002-03 (preemption requires clear evidence of agency exercise of authority).

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit’s judgment.
