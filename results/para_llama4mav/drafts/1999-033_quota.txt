The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted expansion of the Equal Protection Clause.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); Loving v. Virginia, 388 U.S. 1 (1967); Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256 (1979). The Clause is not a general prohibition on arbitrary or irrational government action. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961) ("the constitutional safeguard is offended only if the classification rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State's objective").

The Seventh Circuit's "class of one" theory ignores the requirement that a plaintiff must be a member of a identifiable class or group to bring an equal protection claim. See, e.g., Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); Hernandez v. Texas, 347 U.S. 475 (1954). In Yick Wo, the Court held that a San Francisco ordinance was unconstitutional because it was enforced in a discriminatory manner against Chinese laundry owners. 118 U.S. at 373-74. Similarly, in Hernandez, the Court held that Mexican-Americans were entitled to equal protection under the Fourteenth Amendment because they were a distinct class. 347 U.S. at 479-80.

In contrast, Olech does not allege that she is a member of a particular class or group. Rather, she claims that the Village's demand for a 33-foot easement was motivated by a desire to "get" her. This is not a cognizable equal protection claim. See, e.g., Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944) (holding that a claim of unequal treatment by a state official does not state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause unless it is based on membership in a particular class).

Furthermore, allowing "class of one" claims would turn the Equal Protection Clause into a federal "due process" clause, which would undermine the distinction between the two clauses. See, e.g., Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327 (1986) (holding that the Due Process Clause is not a guarantee against incorrect or ill-advised government decisions); Collins v. Harker Heights, 503 U.S. 115 (1992) (holding that the Due Process Clause does not impose a duty on municipalities to provide certain minimal levels of safety and security).

The Seventh Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the Supreme Court's holding in F.C.C. v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307 (1993), which established that a statutory classification is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Id. at 313. Olech's claim does not involve a statutory classification, but the same principle should apply.

In addition, the Court has held that government action is not unconstitutional simply because it is arbitrary or irrational. See, e.g., Minnesota v. Clover Leaf Creamery Co., 449 U.S. 456 (1981) (holding that a state statute is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest). The Village's demand for a 33-foot easement may have been arbitrary or irrational, but that does not make it a constitutional violation.

In conclusion, the Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Seventh Circuit's holding is an unwarranted expansion of the Clause, and it is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's precedents. This Court should reverse the Seventh Circuit's decision and hold that Olech's claim is not cognizable under the Equal Protection Clause.
