**Argument**

I. **Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat.**

This case turns on the meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) and the scope of the “true threat” exception. A criminal statute does not lightly dispense with mens rea. Where Congress is silent as to a culpable mental state, this Court presumes “the existence of a scienter requirement” is necessary to separate wrongful from innocent conduct. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-63 (1952). Section 875(c) prohibits transmitting “any communication containing any threat,” but it does not specify whether the speaker must intend the communication as a threat, merely foresee that it may be taken as one, or intend to intimidate. That silence should be resolved in favor of a subjective-intent requirement. *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723, 735-38 (2015).

The Constitution points the same way. The First Amendment leaves room for “true threats,” but that category exists because the law may punish “serious expression[s] of intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). In *Black*, the Court explained that intimidation is constitutionally unprotected because it is “a type of true threat, where a speaker directs a threat to a person or group of persons with the intent of placing the victim in fear of bodily harm or death.” *Id.* at 360. That formulation ties the exception to the speaker’s purpose, not merely the listener’s reaction. An objective-only test collapses the constitutional line between protected expression and punishable threat by criminalizing speech based on how a hypothetical audience might react, even if the speaker meant something else. *Id.* at 359-60.

The Third Circuit’s “reasonable speaker” standard is especially problematic because it criminalizes speech without proof of a guilty mind. *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), held that a statement is a true threat when “a reasonable speaker would foresee” it would be understood as threatening. But *Kosma* predates *Black*, and *Black*’s reasoning is irreconcilable with a rule that permits conviction absent proof that the defendant intended the communication as a threat. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359-60. A foreseeability standard may describe negligence; it does not establish the intent Congress ordinarily requires in a federal felony. *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 737-38; *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263.

Nor can the government defend the conviction by recasting the First Amendment inquiry as one of victim fear alone. While threats may cause fear, the Constitution does not permit punishment of speech merely because it frightens. The State’s interest in protecting people from “fear of violence” cannot justify dispensing with mens rea. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360; *Mary Gormley v. Director, Connecticut State Department of Adult Probation*, 449 U.S. 1023 (1980) (White, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (noting that while threats of physical violence are unprotected, the government may not unduly infringe speech merely because it is offensive or alarming). A speaker who posts violent lyrics, hyperbole, or venting may be reckless or crude, but absent proof of subjective intent to threaten, he is not guilty under § 875(c). *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 737-38.

The record here underscores the constitutional danger of an objective standard. The jury was told that a “true threat” could be found based on the statements’ context and how they would be understood, without finding that Elonis meant them as threats. That instruction allowed conviction even if Elonis was merely ranting, role-playing, or expressing violent imagery without the purpose of placing anyone in fear. Under *Black* and *Elonis*, that is too little. 538 U.S. at 359-60; 575 U.S. at 737-38.

Because § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat, Elonis’s conviction cannot stand on an objective-only jury instruction. The judgment should be reversed.
