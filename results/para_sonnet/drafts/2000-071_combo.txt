# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Facilities Constitute a Nonpublic Forum, and Its Policy Excluding Religious Worship Is a Reasonable, Viewpoint-Neutral Regulation of Access.

Government need not throw open every parcel of public property to every mode of expression. As this Court held in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), "the State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Id.* at 47. A school building used after hours pursuant to a limited community-use statute is not transformed into a traditional public forum comparable to the streets and parks described in *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), which "time out of mind" have been held in trust for public assembly and discussion. *Id.* at 515. Milford's building retains its essential character as a place dedicated to education, and the community-use policy merely permits limited, categorical access for civic and recreational purposes.

Within such a nonpublic forum, exclusion of a category of activity is constitutionally permissible so long as it is reasonable and does not discriminate among viewpoints on a subject otherwise open for discussion. Milford's policy operates precisely on that basis: it excludes *all* groups, regardless of creed, that seek to use the building for religious worship or devotional instruction. The Club was not excluded because of the particular religious perspective it holds, but because its meetings—opening prayer, devotional singing, scriptural memorization tied to salvation, and an "invitation" for six-year-olds to accept Christ as personal savior—constitute the very worship service the policy reserves against, for every faith alike.

*Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), does not compel a different result. There, a municipality applied a standardless permit scheme to deny a public park—an archetypal public forum—to a religious group while granting it to others. *Id.* at 272. Milford's policy contains no such standardless discretion; it draws a single, religion-neutral line between instruction about values, including from a religious perspective, and religious worship itself, and applies that line evenhandedly to every applicant.

## II. Even If the Free Speech Clause Otherwise Protected the Club's Access, the Establishment Clause Independently Justified Milford's Exclusion.

This Court has long recognized that public educational property occupies a special place under the Establishment Clause. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Court explained that the Clause was designed to erect "a wall of separation between Church and State," *id.* at 16, foreclosing the use of the machinery of public education to advance religious ends.

That principle finds its most direct application in *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948). There the Court struck down a program permitting religious teachers to enter public school buildings for voluntary religious instruction, holding that the arrangement impermissibly used the "tax-supported public school system" to aid the propagation of religious doctrine. *Id.* at 209–10. The Court in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), later upheld an off-premises released-time program, but only by carefully distinguishing *McCollum* on the ground that there "the classrooms were used for religious instruction and the force of the public school was used to promote that instruction." *Id.* at 315. Milford's building, unlike the off-site facilities in *Zorach*, is the very schoolhouse in which the Club sought to conduct its meetings—precisely the arrangement *McCollum* condemned.

This Court's school-prayer decisions confirm that public school property demands heightened vigilance against apparent religious endorsement directed at children. In *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), the Court held that "it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," *id.* at 425, and in *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), the Court articulated the governing test: "what are the purpose and primary effect of the enactment[?] If either is the advancement or inhibition of religion then the enactment exceeds the scope of legislative power." *Id.* at 222. A weekly meeting held within the schoolhouse, involving young children still associating the building with the school day, in which the leader invites first-graders to "believe on the Lord Jesus" and pray a conversion prayer, carries precisely the primary effect *Schempp* forbids and precisely the appearance of official religious sponsorship *Engel* condemns.

*Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), teaches that free exercise and free speech, however fundamental, "may be regulated" where the community's legitimate interests—here, avoiding the appearance that impressionable schoolchildren are being led in worship within the schoolhouse itself—so require. *Id.* at 306. Milford's judgment that its own building could not host the Club's devotional program without incurring the very risk this Court's precedents forbid was, at minimum, reasonable, and the Establishment Clause independently justifies the exclusion the Second Circuit upheld below.
