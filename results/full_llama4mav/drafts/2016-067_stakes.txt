The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The question presented is whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander), a company that purchased defaulted car loans from CitiFinancial Auto and then attempted to collect on those debts, is a "debt collector" subject to the FDCPA. The plain language of the statute and relevant case law demonstrate that Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed or due another. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that this definition requires that the debt be owed to another at the time of collection, not just at the time the debt was originated (Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 136-37 (4th Cir. 2016)). As the Eleventh Circuit has noted, "a person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a 'debt collector' under the second definition of the term" (Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015)).

In this case, Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and then attempted to collect on those debts for its own account. As the complaint alleges, Santander "began communicating with [the plaintiffs] ... in an attempt to collect on the alleged debts" after purchasing the loans (Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 134 (4th Cir. 2016)). At the time of collection, the debts were owed to Santander, not CitiFinancial Auto. Therefore, Santander was not collecting debts "owed or due another" and is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA.

The petitioners argue that the default status of the debt at the time of acquisition determines whether Santander is a "debt collector." However, this argument is unpersuasive. The FDCPA's definition of "debt collector" is not solely determined by the status of the debt at the time of acquisition. Rather, it is determined by whether the person is collecting debts on behalf of others or for its own account (Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995)). As the Fourth Circuit held, "the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition set forth in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)" (Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 136 (4th Cir. 2016)).

Furthermore, the petitioners' interpretation would create an anomaly where a company like Santander, which engages in various financial activities, including lending and collecting debt for itself and others, would be subject to the FDCPA for all its collection activities simply because one of its activities involves collecting debts for others. Congress did not intend this result (15 U.S.C. § 1692(e)).

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting debts for its own account, not on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. The Fourth Circuit's decision should be affirmed.
