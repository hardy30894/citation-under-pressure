**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT ESQD ARC MAPS ARE EXEMPT FROM DISCLOSURE UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 2.

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard for Exemption 2 is contrary to the plain text of the statute and this Court’s precedent. Exemption 2 permits withholding only documents “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has long held that Exemption 2 applies only to materials that are “law enforcement” in nature and pose a risk of circumvention if disclosed. *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980). The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to include any document that is “predominantly internal” and poses a risk of circumvention is an unwarranted departure from the statute’s text and this Court’s precedent.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed. *Dep’t of Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). The Supreme Court has made clear that Exemption 2 is limited to “internal personnel rules and practices,” and that the exemption does not extend to documents that are merely “predominantly internal.” *Id.* at 369. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” standard thus conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that FOIA exemptions are to be interpreted narrowly. *See also U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (holding that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be narrowly construed).

The ESQD arc maps at issue here are not “internal personnel rules and practices.” They are technical safety calculations used to design and construct ammunition storage facilities. Commander Whitbred’s affidavit confirms that the maps are used for “risk mitigation and enhanced safety,” not for internal personnel practices. *See* App. 42. The maps are not “predominantly internal” in any meaningful sense; they are operational safety documents used to protect the public and agency personnel alike. The Ninth Circuit’s holding thus stretches Exemption 2 far beyond its statutory limits.

II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE ESQD ARC MAPS ARE EXEMPT FROM DISCLOSURE UNDER FOIA EXEMPTION 7(F).

Exemption 7(F) permits withholding documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). The Ninth Circuit’s dissent correctly notes that the ESQD arc maps were not compiled for law enforcement purposes. *See* App. 52 (dissenting opinion). Commander Whitbred’s affidavit states that the maps are used for “design, array, and construct[ion]” of storage facilities and “risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” *Id.* at 42. These are operational, not law enforcement, purposes.

This Court has held that Exemption 7(F) applies only to documents compiled for law enforcement purposes. *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). The Ninth Circuit’s majority opinion does not dispute that the ESQD arc maps were not compiled for law enforcement purposes. Instead, it relies on a speculative risk of “reverse engineering” to justify withholding the maps. *See* App. 30. But speculation about hypothetical risks is insufficient to justify withholding documents under FOIA. *Milner v. U.S. Dep’t of Navy*, 562 F.3d 906, 914 (9th Cir. 2009) (en banc). The Navy has not classified the ESQD arc maps under Exemption 1, which is specifically designed to protect national security information. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). If the maps truly posed a national security risk, the Navy would have classified them. The Ninth Circuit’s holding thus improperly expands Exemption 7(F) beyond its statutory limits.

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING DISTORTS CONGRESS’S CAREFUL BALANCE IN FOIA.

FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The Supreme Court has emphasized that FOIA exemptions are to be narrowly construed and are “explicitly exclusive.” *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. The Ninth Circuit’s holding distorts this balance by expanding Exemption 2 to include documents that are not “internal personnel rules and practices” and by stretching Exemption 7(F) to cover operational safety documents. The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision to restore the proper balance between public access to information and the need to protect sensitive government operations.
