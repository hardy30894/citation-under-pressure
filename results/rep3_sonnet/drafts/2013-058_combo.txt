# ARGUMENT

## I. The Retirement-Fund Exemption Must Be Construed According to Its Purpose, Not by the Historical Label Attached to an Asset

This Court has long recognized that the animating purpose of the Bankruptcy Act's exemption provisions is to give the honest debtor "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That purpose is forward-looking: exemptions exist to preserve a debtor's capacity for future support, not to shield assets that are already available for present consumption. *See Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294 (1903) (exemption statutes must be construed in light of the object they serve).

Petitioners ask this Court to read "retirement funds" in 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) atemporally—so that any account that once served a retirement purpose in a decedent's hands remains exempt forever, regardless of its function in the debtor's hands. That reading elevates a label over reality, precisely the error this Court has repeatedly condemned. As the Court explained in *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892), "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." An inherited IRA carries the word "IRA," but it does not carry the substance of a retirement account: contributions are barred, rollovers are barred, and distribution must begin immediately, often completed within five years. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); § 402(c)(11)(A). It is retirement in name only.

## II. The Exemption's Purpose Does Not Reach Funds That No Longer Function as Retirement Savings

This Court has cautioned against literalism that produces results "plainly at variance with the policy of the legislation as a whole." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Section 522's retirement exemption reflects a specific bargain: Congress protected funds set aside for a debtor's own future support because doing so serves the fresh-start policy underlying bankruptcy relief. *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 244. That bargain is not served by protecting an inherited account that Heidi Heffron-Clark may spend at will, today, for any purpose whatsoever, and which she must substantially deplete long before her own retirement.

This Court's spendthrift-trust jurisprudence confirms that the law protects funds from creditors because of their function as support, not merely because they once belonged to a fund with that character. In *Nichols v. Eaton*, 91 U.S. 716 (1875), the Court recognized that property held for a beneficiary's maintenance may be insulated from creditors precisely because its terms confine it to that supportive purpose; the protection is not detached from the asset's continuing function. An inherited IRA has no such confining function—the beneficiary may withdraw the entire balance the day after probate closes and spend it on anything at all. Nothing about the account's current operation resembles a fund "held for retirement."

The same functional inquiry governs this Court's construction of "property of the estate" in bankruptcy. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court determined whether a right belonged to the bankruptcy estate by asking whether it was sufficiently connected to the debtor's own pre-bankruptcy economic position—not by resting on formal characterization alone. The same functional method should govern here: whether an asset is "retirement funds" within the meaning of § 522 depends on how the asset presently operates in the debtor's hands, not on the label it carried when someone else owned it.

## III. Substance, Not Form, Controls Characterization of Tax-Favored Accounts

This Court's tax jurisprudence reinforces that courts must look to substance rather than nominal form when characterizing financial arrangements created under the Internal Revenue Code—the very code from which § 522(b)(3)(C) borrows its cross-references. In *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), the Court refused to credit a transaction's formal compliance with statutory terms where its substance defeated the statute's purpose, explaining that a contrary result would "exalt artifice above reality and . . . deprive the statutory provision in question of all serious purpose." *Id.* at 470. The same reasoning applies with full force here. Treating an inherited IRA as "retirement funds" merely because Congress used the word "IRA" to describe the tax-deferral vehicle would exalt the account's nominal pedigree above the economic reality that it is, in petitioners' own hands, a present, freely accessible pool of wealth—no different in substance from any other inherited asset.

## Conclusion

Because an inherited IRA does not function as retirement savings for the debtor who holds it, it falls outside the purpose—and therefore the coverage—of § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
