**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle for the Attorney General’s scheduling authority.**

The non-delegation doctrine requires that Congress provide an "intelligible principle" to guide the exercise of delegated authority. *J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) satisfies this requirement by enumerating specific factors the Attorney General must consider before adding a substance to the schedules. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). These factors include the substance’s potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, the state of current scientific knowledge, the history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, the risk to public health, dependence liability, and whether the substance is an immediate precursor of a controlled substance. Id. Furthermore, the temporary scheduling provision requires the Attorney General to consider the history, current pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse, as well as the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These explicit standards provide a clear, legislative directive to the Attorney General, satisfying the "intelligible principle" test.

**II. The non-delegation doctrine does not require a stricter standard for delegations that create crimes.**

The Toubys argue that a more stringent analysis applies because the delegated power creates criminal penalties. However, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the non-delegation doctrine is a "dormant" constraint and that Congress has wide latitude in delegating legislative power. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 109 S.Ct. 647, 102 L.Ed.2d 714 (1989). The Court reiterated that "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution, mandate that Congress generally cannot delegate its legislative power to another Branch." *Id.* at 654. However, the Court emphasized that the Constitution does not prohibit Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches. *Id.* The Court has upheld every congressional delegation of power that has been presented to it, even where Congress has provided negligible standards for the delegatee to follow. *Id.* at 655. The only cases striking down delegations are *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), which are narrow exceptions. The standards in the CSA are far more specific than the "negligible standards" the Court has previously upheld. Therefore, the delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional.

**III. The Attorney General may delegate authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency.**

The Attorney General possesses the authority to delegate the functions of the CSA to the DEA. Pursuant to 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), the Attorney General promulgated a regulation subdelegating the performance of the functions vested in him by the CSA to the DEA Administrator. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). The statute does not prohibit this sub-delegation. The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA Administrator was a valid exercise of his statutory authority, and the subsequent temporary scheduling of 4-methylaminorex was lawfully executed.
