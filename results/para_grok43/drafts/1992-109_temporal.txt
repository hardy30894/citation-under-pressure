The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply any standard other than that set forth in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), when determining a defendant's competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. That decision holds that a defendant is competent if he has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Id. at 402.

Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirms that due process requires a hearing whenever the record raises a bona fide doubt as to competency, yet it applies the Dusky formulation without differentiation according to the stage of proceedings. Id. at 385. The Court there treated the question of competency as unitary, turning on the defendant's capacity to understand the nature of the charges and to participate in his defense. Ibid.

Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), likewise imposes no distinct mental-capacity test for waiver of counsel. It requires only that the waiver be "an intelligent relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," judged by the particular facts and circumstances surrounding the case. Id. at 464. The inquiry remains whether the defendant possesses the understanding necessary to forgo the assistance of counsel, not whether he can make a "reasoned choice" among strategic alternatives.

Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), addresses the voluntariness of a guilty plea and requires that the record affirmatively disclose a knowing and intelligent waiver of constitutional rights. Id. at 242. It does not, however, prescribe a competency standard different from Dusky. The decision focuses on the trial court's duty to ensure the plea is voluntary, not on elevating the threshold of mental functioning required to enter the plea.

Precedent therefore supplies a single constitutional measure of competency. A defendant who satisfies the Dusky test possesses the capacity to waive counsel and plead guilty; due process demands nothing more. The Ninth Circuit's imposition of a heightened "reasoned choice" standard finds no support in these authorities and cannot be reconciled with the uniform standard they establish.
