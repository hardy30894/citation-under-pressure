**ARGUMENT**

I. **Ray’s state-law tort claims are preempted by federal aviation law.**

Congress has broadly displaced state regulation of airline services through the Airline Deregulation Act. The ADA preempts a state “law, regulation, or other provision having the force and effect of law” related to an air carrier’s “price, route, or service.” 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1); *Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 374, 383–84 (1992). The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that the statute is to be given “a broad scope,” id. at 383, and that state-law rules affecting an airline’s operations, including boarding, scheduling, and passenger handling, may not stand. *Northwest, Inc. v. Ginsberg*, 572 U.S. 273, 279–80 (2014); *American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens*, 513 U.S. 219, 223–24 (1995).

Ray’s false-imprisonment and negligence claims attack American’s decisions during an extraordinary weather diversion: whether to permit deplaning, when to reopen the cabin, how to respond to flight delays, and how to manage passengers awaiting departure. Those decisions are quintessential “service” choices. This Court has held that passenger-service claims are preempted when they seek to regulate an airline’s “operations and procedures for boarding, baggage handling, in-flight food and beverage service, and the like.” *Smith v. Comair, Inc.*, 134 F.3d 254, 259 (4th Cir. 1998); see also *Botz v. Omni Air Int’l*, 286 F.3d 488, 495–96 (8th Cir. 2002), abrogated on other grounds by *Wolens*, 513 U.S. at 228–29. Courts have likewise found preemption where claims challenge an airline’s conduct in managing delays and passenger confinement. *Lyn-Lea Travel Corp. v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 283 F.3d 282, 289–90 (5th Cir. 2002); *Charas v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 160 F.3d 1259, 1261–62 (9th Cir. 1998) (en banc).

Ray’s theory would impose a state-law duty on American to provide deplaning opportunities at particular intervals during a tarmac delay, to ensure passenger comfort, and to supply different food, water, and restroom arrangements. That is exactly the sort of patchwork regulation Congress forbade. *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 390–91; *Rowe v. New Hampshire Motor Transp. Ass’n*, 552 U.S. 364, 371–72 (2008) (state laws “having a connection with or reference to” federally regulated transportation services are preempted). If each state could define when a delay becomes actionable false imprisonment or negligence, airline passenger-service standards would vary from state to state, undermining the federal policy of “maximum reliance on competitive market forces.” *Wolens*, 513 U.S. at 230.

Nor does Ray evade preemption by recasting her complaint as a tort action rather than a direct regulation. The Supreme Court has rejected efforts to “repackage” preempted claims as state common-law duties. *Ginsberg*, 572 U.S. at 281–82. Whether framed as negligence or false imprisonment, Ray’s claims depend on evaluating whether American should have released passengers, procured buses sooner, or handled the diversion differently. Those claims “relate to” service and are therefore preempted. *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 383–84; *Botz*, 286 F.3d at 495–96.

II. **Even if not preempted, summary judgment was proper because Ray cannot prove the elements of false imprisonment.**

False imprisonment under Arkansas law requires a restraint of personal liberty without lawful justification. *Headrick v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 293 F.3d 1078, 1085 (8th Cir. 2002); *Kimmel v. McAlpine*, 565 F.2d 406, 410 (8th Cir. 1977). The restraint must be actual and unlawful; mere discomfort, inconvenience, or pressure to remain is not enough. *Headrick*, 293 F.3d at 1085.

Ray chose twice to remain on the plane after American expressly offered passengers the opportunity to deplane by bus. Her own testimony shows that the pilot announced that passengers could leave, and that she and her husband decided to stay because they believed they lacked sufficient funds for alternate transportation. A person who voluntarily remains aboard after being told she may leave is not falsely imprisoned. See *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 35 cmt. h (1965); *Headrick*, 293 F.3d at 1085. American did not lock the doors, physically bar the exits, or threaten unlawful force to prevent deplaning. The most Ray asserts is that the pilot said passengers who left would be “on their own.” Even accepting that statement as true, it described the consequences of deplaning; it did not amount to a legal restraint. See *Kimmel*, 565 F.2d at 410.

III. **Ray’s negligence claim also fails because American owed no state-law duty inconsistent with federal aviation regulation, and Ray cannot show causation or injury.**

A negligence claim requires duty, breach, causation, and damages. *Yanmar Co. v. Slater*, 786 F.3d 948, 955 (8th Cir. 2015). Ray’s theory of duty—that American should have released passengers, supplied more food and water, or better handled the tarmac delay—again seeks to impose state-law standards on airline service and operations, which is preempted for the reasons above. *Morales*, 504 U.S. at 383–84; *Rowe*, 552 U.S. at 371–72.

In any event, Ray cannot establish legally cognizable injury caused by American. She alleges only transient discomfort, including a one-day stomach irritation for which she sought no medical care. Such evidence is insufficient to support more than speculative damages. *Baker v. John Morrell & Co.*, 220 F.3d 869, 871–72 (8th Cir. 2000). And her own conduct breaks any causal chain: she did not notify the crew about the lavatory issue, did not use another restroom, and remained aboard after repeated offers to deplane. *Headrick*, 293 F.3d at 1085; *Yanmar*, 786 F.3d at 955.

For these reasons, the district court’s judgment should be affirmed on the alternative ground that Ray’s state-law claims are preempted, and in any event because she failed to establish false imprisonment or negligence as a matter of law.
