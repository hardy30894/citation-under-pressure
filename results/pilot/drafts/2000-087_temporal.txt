**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in the foundational cases of *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570 (1968), *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), and *United States ex rel. La Vigne v. Smith*, 339 U.S. 152 (1950), forbids indefinite detention without a clear and constitutionally sufficient basis. The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not provide such a basis when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.

The Government’s argument that Congress may authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien rests upon a misreading of statutory authority and a disregard of fundamental due process principles established long before the modern immigration regime. In *United States v. Jackson*, this Court held that a statute which imposes a penalty of death upon a defendant’s choice to stand trial violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution protects the individual against the arbitrary exercise of governmental power,” 390 U.S. at 580, and that “no person may be deprived of liberty without due process of law.” Id. at 581. The same principle applies here: when the Government seeks to detain an individual beyond a reasonable period in which removal is possible, it must do so under a statute that clearly authorizes such confinement and that does not permit arbitrary or indefinite detention.

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. That case upheld the detention of an alien at the border who had not yet entered the United States, and whose removal was not yet final. The Court in *Mezei* recognized that “the power to exclude aliens is a fundamental sovereign attribute exercised by the Government’s political departments,” 345 U.S. at 212, and that “the exclusion of aliens is a matter of political discretion.” Id. at 213. But *Mezei* did not address the detention of a resident alien who has been lawfully ordered removed and who has already been admitted to the United States. The distinction is critical. In *United States ex rel. La Vigne v. Smith*, 339 U.S. 152 (1950), this Court held that a person who has been admitted to the United States and who is not a threat to public safety may not be detained indefinitely without a judicial determination of continued necessity. The Court stated that “the right to liberty is not to be abridged without a clear and compelling justification,” 339 U.S. at 155, and that “the Government may not hold a person in custody without a valid legal basis.” Id. at 156.

Here, the Government has not demonstrated that continued detention is necessary to secure removal. The record shows that Zadvydas has been in custody for more than three years beyond the removal period, and that no country—Germany, Lithuania, or any other—has accepted him. The INS has made no meaningful effort to secure his removal in the foreseeable future. The Government’s claim that “good faith efforts” continue is hollow when no country will accept him and no diplomatic or administrative mechanism exists to effectuate removal. In *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), this Court struck down a statute that authorized the removal of individuals from government employment without a hearing or judicial review. The Court held that “the power to remove a person from office is not unlimited,” and that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary action by the Government.” 328 U.S. at 315. The same principle applies to detention: the power to detain is not unlimited, and the Constitution requires that such detention be justified by a clear and present need.

The Government’s argument that the statute permits detention until removal is accomplished is untenable. The statute does not define “removal,” nor does it specify a time limit. But in the absence of a clear statutory mandate, the Court must interpret the statute in light of constitutional constraints. In *United States v. Jackson*, the Court held that “a statute which makes the penalty depend upon the exercise of a constitutional right is invalid.” 390 U.S. at 581. Similarly, a statute that permits indefinite detention without a judicial determination of necessity violates due process. The Constitution does not permit the Government to hold a person in custody indefinitely simply because it has not yet found a country willing to accept him.

The Government’s position would allow the Executive to detain any alien indefinitely, regardless of the likelihood of removal. That is precisely the kind of arbitrary power the Constitution was designed to prevent. In *United States ex rel. La Vigne v. Smith*, the Court emphasized that “the right to liberty is not to be abridged without a clear and compelling justification.” 339 U.S. at 155. Here, there is no such justification. The alien has been ordered removed, but no country will accept him. The Government has not shown that removal is reasonably foreseeable, nor has it demonstrated that continued detention is necessary to ensure his presence at the moment of removal. The Constitution does not permit such detention.

For these reasons, the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The judgment of the district court must be affirmed.
