# ARGUMENT

## I. Congress Had Authority to Enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951

The Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, reaches robbery that "in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce." This language reflects the full scope of the Commerce Clause power recognized in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937), where the Court held that Congress may regulate intrastate activities that "have such a close and substantial relation to interstate commerce that their control is essential or appropriate to protect that commerce from burdens and obstructions." *Id.* at 37. That power extends even to conduct with only an indirect effect on commerce, so long as the aggregate effect is not trivial. *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 125 (1942) (Congress may reach intrastate activity that "exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce").

Applying these principles directly to § 1951, this Court's sister circuits and the Supreme Court have sustained the statute where the robbery depletes the assets of a business engaged in interstate purchasing, as Mr. Goodcents was here. *See Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960) (recognizing that Congress intended the Hobbs Act "to use all the constitutional power Congress has to punish interference with interstate commerce"). Mr. Pearson preserves the argument that, notwithstanding this line of authority, § 1951 as applied to a purely local robbery of a sandwich shop exceeds the outer bounds of the commerce power identified in *Jones & Laughlin*, because the depletion-of-assets theory threatens to erase any meaningful limit on federal jurisdiction over garden-variety local crime.

## II. The District Court Erred in Excluding Mr. Pearson's Alibi Evidence

The exclusion of Mr. Pearson's alibi testimony and corroborating jewelry-store receipt violated his right to present a defense. As the Supreme Court held in *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), "the right to offer the testimony of witnesses, and to compel their attendance, if necessary, is in plain terms the right to present a defense, the right to present the defendant's version of the facts." A rule that mechanically excludes reliable, timely-discovered defense evidence "cannot rationally be defended" where, as here, the prosecution suffered no genuine prejudice and had ample opportunity to investigate the watch-repair receipt before trial. *See id.* at 22.

The error was compounded by the weakness of the government's identification evidence. Ms. Starks could not identify Mr. Pearson from a photo array shortly after the crime, yet identified him in court only after seeing his photograph broadcast on television and observing that he was the only person of the relevant description in the courtroom. The Supreme Court has long recognized that "the vagaries of eyewitness identification are well-known; the annals of criminal law are rife with instances of mistaken identification." *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 228 (1967). Given this unreliability, the alibi evidence was critical, and its exclusion cannot be deemed harmless.

## III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Conviction

A conviction must be reversed unless, "taking the view most favorable to the Government," there is substantial evidence to support it. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942). Here, the only evidence placing Mr. Pearson at the scene was the compromised identification of Ms. Starks and the testimony of an accomplice, Mr. Martin, who had every incentive to minimize his own culpability by shifting blame. Mr. Pearson's alibi—corroborated by the jewelry store receipt and employee testimony—was consistent with innocence. On this record, the evidence does not rise to the level of substantial evidence required under *Glasser* to sustain a conviction on any count.

## IV. The District Court's Felony-Murder Instruction Was Erroneous

An instruction that fails to require the jury to find every element of the offense charged cannot stand. *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 613 (1946) (reversing where the instruction left the jury with "a false impression" of the law governing an essential element). Here, the district court's felony-murder charge did not adequately require the jury to find that Mr. Pearson himself participated in the underlying robbery with the requisite intent before it could hold him responsible for the death of Ms. Montgomery. Because the instruction blurred this essential distinction, it created the same risk of an unreliable verdict condemned in *Bollenbach*, and reversal is required.

## V. The District Court Erred in Refusing Mr. Pearson's Lesser-Included-Offense Instructions

A defendant is entitled to an instruction on a lesser included offense whenever the evidence would permit a jury rationally to convict on the lesser charge and acquit on the greater. *Sansone v. United States*, 380 U.S. 343, 349–50 (1965); *see also Berra v. United States*, 351 U.S. 131, 134 (1956) (a lesser-offense instruction is proper "if there was a rational basis for the jury's determination" that the elements of only the lesser offense were satisfied). This principle traces to *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 63–64 (1895), which recognized that a jury may properly return a verdict on a lesser offense embraced within the indictment when the evidence supports it. Given the sharply disputed evidence regarding Mr. Pearson's role, the district court's refusal to submit the requested lesser-included-offense instructions deprived the jury of its proper function and requires reversal.
