**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATES RETAIN BROAD AUTHORITY TO SHAPE THE ADMISSIBILITY AND EFFECT OF EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE WITHOUT VIOLATING DUE PROCESS.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of his mental disease or defect to negate *mens rea* when the State has chosen to channel such evidence exclusively to the insanity defense. This Court has long recognized that the definition of the elements of a criminal offense is a matter of state law. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 795 (1952) (holding that the defense of insanity is separate from the issue of specific intent). Because Arizona may define the mental element of first-degree murder as a cognitive capacity distinct from the abnormal mental condition that gives rise to an insanity plea, the State may correspondingly limit the evidence admissible on each issue.

In *Leland*, this Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant and restricted the effect of insanity evidence to that affirmative defense. The Court stated that Oregon’s scheme was not in conflict with the Constitution, finding no requirement that insanity evidence be considered on the issue of intent. *Id.* at 800. So too here: Arizona law separates the insanity defense—an affirmative excuse—from the State’s burden to prove *mens rea*. The evidence Clark sought to introduce went not to whether he intended to kill the officer, but to whether his delusional beliefs rendered him blameless. The State may constitutionally confine such evidence to the insanity inquiry.

**II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY SHIFT THE PROSECUTION’S BURDEN OF PROOF.**

The cornerstone of criminal due process is that the State must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). But Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of this burden. The State must still prove that Clark acted intentionally—a mental state that, under Arizona law, is entirely compatible with a severe mental illness. The rule merely excludes irrelevant or misleading evidence from the *mens rea* determination.

When a defendant claims that his mental condition prevented him from forming intent, the State may cabin that evidence to the insanity defense because the two inquiries are logically distinct. As the Court explained in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the presumption of sanity is a rebuttable inference that may be overcome by proof of insanity. *Id.* at 487-88. The question of sanity is a question of fact that, once raised, the State must disprove. *Id.* But nothing in *Davis* requires that the same evidence be allowed to contest *mens rea*.

This Court has consistently rejected claims that due process compels the unbounded admission of expert psychiatric testimony. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court held that the trial court has wide discretion in determining the admissibility of evidence. *Id.* at 463. And in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), the Court recognized that the mental condition of the accused may be the subject of proof, but that the state may decide how such evidence is to be considered. *Id.* at 583. That principle does not extend to dictating how the evidence may be used.

**III. MULTIPLE PRECEDENTS UPHOLD STATE CONTROL OVER THE INTERPLAY OF INSANITY AND MENS REA.**

Since the earliest days of this Court, the States have been permitted to define the relationship between mental illness and criminal responsibility. In *Leland*, the Court reiterated that a State may place the burden of proving insanity on the defendant without offending due process, so long as the State retains the burden of proving the elements of the offense. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 795. Arizona has done precisely that: the State retains the burden of proving intent; the defendant bears the burden on insanity. Evidence supporting insanity is not automatically evidence negating intent.

Long before *Leland*, this Court recognized that insanity is a defense distinct from the issue of specific intent. *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 487. In *Hopt*, the Court observed that the mental condition of the accused may be such that he cannot form a criminal intent, but that the state may treat that condition as an excuse rather than a negation of an element. *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 583. And in *Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413 (1902), the Court held that a defendant claiming a condition such as irresistible impulse must prove that condition to the satisfaction of the jury, even though the government retained the burden on general intent. *Id.* at 418.

The Court in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), rejected the argument that due process requires a special evidentiary rule for chronic alcoholism to negate *mens rea*, holding that the criminal law is not powerless to punish conduct committed under circumstances that would normally be deemed criminal. *Id.* at 532. Clark’s position—that he must be allowed to use delusional beliefs to negate intent—similarly seeks to constitutionalize a particular evidentiary regime that no prior decision of this Court has mandated.

**IV. CLARK’S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD DISRUPT THE STATE’S CAREFULLY CRAFTED INSANITY SCHEME.**

Arizona has chosen to treat insanity as an affirmative defense requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(C). Allowing the same evidence to refute *mens rea* would permit a defendant to circumvent the higher burden and the specialized adjudication of insanity. This Court has never held that due process requires such a result. To the contrary, in *Leland*, the Court approved Oregon’s scheme requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt—a higher standard than Arizona’s—without any separate channel for the evidence in the *mens rea* context. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798-99.

Because Arizona’s rule respects the distinct roles of *mens rea* and insanity, does not relieve the State of its burden, and reflects a balanced legislative judgment, it does not violate the Due Process Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
