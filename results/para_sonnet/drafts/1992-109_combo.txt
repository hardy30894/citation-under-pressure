**ARGUMENT**

**The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Standard for Determining Competency to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel**

**I. The Sole Constitutional Measure of Mental Competency Is the Dusky Standard, Which Applies Uniformly Throughout Criminal Proceedings.**

This Court has already defined the constitutional floor for mental competency. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether [the defendant] has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. Nothing in *Dusky* confines that standard to the moment a jury is empaneled; it defines the minimum mental capacity a defendant must possess to participate in *any* stage of a criminal prosecution consistent with due process.

*Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reinforces that a single competency threshold governs the proceeding as a whole: the constitutional evil is the prosecution of a defendant who is "legally incompetent," a status defined by the same mental faculties described in *Dusky*. *Id.* at 378. Nothing in *Pate* suggests that a defendant who is competent to be tried by a jury and to instruct counsel becomes incompetent merely because he instead chooses to plead guilty or to proceed without counsel. The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard invents a second, higher tier of competency untethered to any test this Court has ever announced, and untethered to *Dusky* itself.

**II. The Additional Safeguards Due Process Requires for Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Are Supplied by the "Knowing and Voluntary" Inquiry, Not by Inflating the Competency Standard.**

This Court has long recognized that decisions to waive counsel or to plead guilty implicate a distinct inquiry—not whether the defendant is *more* competent than *Dusky* requires, but whether his particular waiver was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court explained that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. That inquiry asks what the defendant understood and intended respecting the specific right at issue; it does not require a freestanding, elevated mental-capacity test.

*Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), confirms the point in the context of waiver of counsel: a defendant "may waive his Constitutional right to assistance of counsel if he knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open." *Id.* at 279. The touchstone is knowledge and voluntariness of the choice, assessed against the *Dusky* baseline of competency—not a separate, heightened psychiatric threshold. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise requires that a waiver of counsel be made "with an apprehension of the nature of the charges, the statutory offenses included within them, the range of allowable punishments thereunder, possible defenses to the charges and circumstances in mitigation thereof, and all other facts essential to a broad understanding of the whole matter." *Id.* at 724. That "apprehension" is precisely what a trial court's searching colloquy is designed to elicit; it is a procedural safeguard, not evidence that the Constitution demands a different mental-competency standard.

The same principle governs guilty pleas. Because, as this Court explained in *Kercheval v. United States*, 274 U.S. 220 (1927), "[a] plea of guilty differs in purpose and effect from a mere admission or an extrajudicial confession; it is itself a conviction," *id.* at 223, and because, as *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), later reaffirmed, a guilty plea is "itself a conviction," *id.* at 242, this Court has required trial courts to satisfy themselves that the plea is voluntary and intelligent on the record. *Id.* That requirement—searching inquiry into voluntariness and understanding of the specific rights forgone—already guards against the precise danger the Ninth Circuit purported to address. No decision of this Court holds that due process additionally demands proof of a superior mental capacity beyond *Dusky*'s rational- and factual-understanding test before such a plea may be accepted.

**III. *Massey v. Moore* Does Not Establish a Heightened Competency Standard.**

Respondent's reliance on *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), is misplaced. There the Court recognized that a defendant who lacked the capacity to conduct his own defense could not be permitted to proceed without counsel, and remanded for a proper competency determination. *Id.* at 108. *Massey* addressed the *consequence* of a finding that a defendant lacks the capacity described in what later became the *Dusky* formulation; it did not purport to create a second, more demanding competency test applicable only to waiver decisions. Read together with *Dusky*, *Pate*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, and *Adams*, *Massey* stands only for the unremarkable proposition that a defendant who fails the single constitutional competency standard may not be permitted to represent himself—not that a defendant who satisfies that standard must clear some higher, undefined bar of "reasoned choice."

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard finds no footing in this Court's precedents. The Due Process Clause requires only that a defendant possess the competency described in *Dusky v. United States*, coupled with a knowing and voluntary waiver as required by *Johnson v. Zerbst*, *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, *Kercheval v. United States*, and *Boykin v. Alabama*. The judgment below should be reversed.
