**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Live Controversy Remains Because Petitioners Seek Nominal Damages for a Completed Injury**

A case is not moot when the plaintiff seeks nominal damages for a completed injury. *Linda R.S. v. Richard D.*, 410 U.S. 614, 619 n.3 (1973) (“[A] federal court’s adjudication of [a] claim for damages would be meaningful, since it would determine whether [the plaintiff] is entitled to nominal damages.”); *see also Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978) (holding that plaintiffs suing under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 may recover nominal damages for a completed constitutional violation). The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), is erroneous and should be reversed.

The Court has long recognized that nominal damages preserve a live controversy even after the challenged policy is repealed. *See, e.g., City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 n.10 (1982) (noting that a claim for nominal damages “would be sufficient to keep the case alive”); *Honig v. Doe*, 484 U.S. 305, 318 n.6 (1988) (same). This principle applies with equal force to constitutional claims. *See Memphis Cmty. Sch. Dist. v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 n.11 (1986) (acknowledging that nominal damages are available for constitutional violations). The fact that Petitioners’ claims for prospective relief are moot does not eliminate their right to seek redress for past injuries. *See Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013) (“[A] case is moot only when it is impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever to the prevailing party.” (internal quotation marks omitted)).

The Eleventh Circuit’s rule in *Flanigan’s* conflicts with this settled understanding. The court held that a request for nominal damages cannot save an otherwise moot claim, but it offered no persuasive justification for this departure from Supreme Court precedent. *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256–57. The Court should reject this reasoning and reaffirm that nominal damages preserve a live controversy.

**II. Petitioners’ Claims Are Not Moot Because the Voluntary-Cessation Doctrine Does Not Apply**

A defendant’s voluntary cessation of an allegedly unconstitutional policy does not moot a case unless there is no reasonable expectation that the policy will be revived. *See City of Mesquite*, 455 U.S. at 289; *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). The burden is on the defendant to show that the challenged policy is not “capable of repetition, yet evading review.” *See Weinstein v. Bradford*, 423 U.S. 147, 149 (1975) (per curiam).

Here, Respondents revised their policies to permit expressive activities anywhere on campus without permits, rendering Petitioners’ claims for prospective relief moot. However, Respondents have not met their burden to show that the challenged policies are not capable of repetition. *See, e.g., Spencer v. Kemna*, 523 U.S. 1, 15 (1998) (holding that a claim is not moot if the challenged conduct could reasonably be expected to recur). Because Respondents have not demonstrated that the policies will not be reinstated, Petitioners’ claims are not moot.

**III. Petitioners’ Nominal Damages Claim Is Sufficient to Preserve a Live Controversy**

Petitioners seek nominal damages for a completed constitutional violation, which is sufficient to preserve a live controversy. *See Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266; *Linda R.S.*, 410 U.S. at 619 n.3. The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule in *Flanigan’s* is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent and should be rejected.

The Court has repeatedly recognized that nominal damages provide redress for completed injuries. *See, e.g., Memphis Cmty. Sch. Dist.*, 477 U.S. at 308 n.11; *Stachura*, 477 U.S. at 308 n.11. The fact that Petitioners’ claims for prospective relief are moot does not eliminate their right to seek nominal damages for past violations. *See Chafin*, 568 U.S. at 172.

**IV. Conclusion**

The judgment below should be affirmed because Petitioners’ claims for nominal damages preserve a live controversy. However, the Eleventh Circuit’s rule in *Flanigan’s* conflicts with Supreme Court precedent and should be rejected. The Court should clarify that a request for nominal damages is sufficient to preserve a live controversy in cases involving completed constitutional violations.
