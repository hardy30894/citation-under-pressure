**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding it had subject matter jurisdiction over this declaratory judgment action. The Pressls’ claim arises solely under Virginia state law and concerns the interpretation and enforcement of a private flowage easement—matters entirely within the province of state courts. The district court’s invocation of federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p was misplaced. Because the federal question presented is not “arising under” federal law in the constitutional sense, and because the federal statute cited does not confer jurisdiction over disputes between private parties over easement rights, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. This Court must vacate the dismissal and remand for proceedings in state court.

**I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Because the Pressls’ Claim Does Not Arise Under Federal Law**

The threshold inquiry in any federal jurisdictional analysis is whether the case “arises under” federal law. *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 312 (2005). A case “arises under” federal law only when a federal question is “presented by the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint.” *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946). Here, the Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under a private flowage easement, a matter governed exclusively by Virginia property law. The complaint does not allege any violation of federal statutes, nor does it seek relief based on federal law. It is a classic state law dispute over the scope of a private easement.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a suit may not be deemed to arise under the Constitution or laws of the United States merely because a federal question is raised in defense.” *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987). The mere presence of a federal agency’s license or a federal regulatory scheme does not automatically confer federal jurisdiction. *See also* *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank*, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936) (“The mere fact that a federal statute is involved in the case does not make the case one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.”). The Pressls’ claim is not “federal in character” simply because APCO operates under a FERC license. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 560 U.S. 442, 448 (2010) (holding that a claim “arising under” federal law must be “federal in character” and not merely “federal in origin”).

**II. Section 825p Does Not Confer Jurisdiction Over Private Easement Disputes**

APCO relies on 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants FERC jurisdiction over hydroelectric projects and authorizes the agency to “issue licenses” for such projects. However, this statute does not confer federal question jurisdiction on district courts over disputes between private landowners and utility companies over the scope of flowage easements. *See* *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952) (holding that “a statute creating a federal right does not automatically confer jurisdiction on federal courts unless the statute itself provides for such jurisdiction”).

The plain language of § 825p is limited to “any proceeding” brought by the United States or FERC to enforce the terms of a license. It does not authorize private parties to bring suit in federal court. *See* *Barnes*, 560 U.S. at 448 (“The mere fact that a federal agency has jurisdiction over a subject does not mean that federal courts have jurisdiction over every dispute related to that subject.”). The Supreme Court has held that “a federal statute must be more than a source of substantive law to confer federal jurisdiction.” *Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 808 (1986). Here, § 825p is not such a statute. It does not create a private right of action, nor does it authorize federal courts to hear disputes over the interpretation of private easements.

**III. The “Federal Question” Exception in *Grable* Does Not Apply**

APCO argues that this case falls within the *Grable* exception to the general rule that federal jurisdiction requires a federal question in the plaintiff’s complaint. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. But *Grable* applies only when a state law claim “necessarily raise[s] a federal issue that is both substantial and capable of resolution in federal court without disrupting the federal-state balance.” *Id.* at 313. Here, no such federal issue is presented. The Pressls’ claim does not challenge the validity of FERC’s license, nor does it seek to enjoin the operation of the dam. It is a straightforward dispute over the meaning of a private easement—exactly the kind of matter traditionally resolved in state courts.

*See* *Barnes*, 560 U.S. at 448 (“The fact that a federal agency has jurisdiction over a subject does not mean that federal courts have jurisdiction over every dispute related to that subject.”). The district court’s conclusion that the Pressls must first seek relief from FERC before filing suit further underscores that the federal agency is not a party to this dispute and that the claim is not one “arising under” federal law. *See* *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 630 (2002) (holding that “a federal question must be presented by the plaintiff’s claim, not by a defense or a procedural requirement”).
