**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION**

The text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) is broad, but the Supreme Court interpreted it narrowly to prevent indefinite detention. The Court held that the statute limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal from the United States. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 2498 (2001). The Court explicitly stated that the statute "does not permit indefinite detention." *Id.* at 2498. This construction is necessary because the statute applies broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including minor visa violations, and not merely to "a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals." *Id.* at 2499.

**II. THE CONSTITUTION IMPOSES A "REASONABLE TIME" LIMITATION**

The Constitution prohibits indefinite detention without a warrant or probable cause. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2495. The Supreme Court concluded that indefinite detention of aliens in this category would raise serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 2495. Consequently, the statute must be read to avoid a serious constitutional threat. *Id.* at 2503. The Court held that "interpreting the statute... to avoid a serious constitutional threat, we conclude that, once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." *Id.* at 2503.

**III. THE "REASONABLY FORESEEABLE" STANDARD GOVERNS**

The standard for reasonableness is whether removal is reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2504. The Court instructed that "the habeas court must ask whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal." *Id.* at 2504. The Court further held that "if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute." *Id.* at 2504. While the Court established a six-month presumption, the ultimate inquiry is whether removal is reasonably foreseeable.

**IV. APPLICATION TO THE FACTS**

Here, petitioner has been detained for over three years following the expiration of the removal period. No country has agreed to accept him. Under the Supreme Court’s holding, once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2503. Because there is no realistic chance of removal, petitioner’s continued detention exceeds the period reasonably necessary to secure his removal and violates the statute.
