**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does **not** impose a situs-of-injury limitation on coverage under 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The text, structure, and purpose of the statute confirm that Congress tied coverage to the **source of the injury-producing operations**, not to the happenstance of where the fatal blow fell. See **43 U.S.C. § 1333(b)**; **Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986)**.

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act compensation to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” for oil and gas activities. **43 U.S.C. § 1333(b)**. Congress thus chose a causal formulation—“result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf”—rather than a geographic one focused on where the injury happened. The statute says nothing about injury situs, and courts should not add a limitation Congress omitted. See **Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co., 534 U.S. 438, 461-62 (2002)**.

The Supreme Court’s decision in **Tallentire** does not compel the contrary result. There, the Court addressed OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, not § 1333(b)’s workers’ compensation extension. **477 U.S. at 209, 219 n.2**. The Court expressly stated that § 1333(b) “has no bearing on this case.” **Id. at 219 n.2**. A footnote addressing a provision not before the Court cannot override the statute’s actual text or create a limitation Congress did not write. See **United States v. Rodgers, 461 U.S. 677, 706 (1983)** (courts should not “turn to isolated phrases” divorced from context).

Nor does the structure of OCSLA support a situs-of-injury rule. Congress enacted OCSLA to create a comprehensive federal regime governing offshore resource development. **Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969)**; **Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 217-18 (1986)**. Section 1333(b) was meant to ensure compensation coverage for injuries arising out of offshore operations, thereby avoiding the anomalies that would result if coverage turned on whether an offshore employee was injured on a platform, on a vessel, or during land-based work integral to shelf operations. See **Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Serv., Inc., 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988)**.

The Fifth Circuit’s situs-of-injury rule in **Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc)** is unpersuasive because it adds words to the statute. Section 1333(b) does not say “injury occurring on the outer Continental Shelf”; it says “injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” **43 U.S.C. § 1333(b)**. That causal language is broader than situs and naturally includes injuries on land when those injuries arise from offshore operations. See **Curtis, 849 F.2d at 810-11**. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, the better reading is a substantial-nexus approach, which gives effect to the statutory words without collapsing them into a geographic restriction. See **Pacific Operators Offshore, LLP v. Valladolid, 565 U.S. 207, 218-19 (2012)**.

The respondent’s position is also consistent with the LHWCA’s remedial character. The Longshore Act is to be construed liberally in favor of coverage. **Northeast Marine Terminal Co. v. Caputo, 432 U.S. 249, 268 (1977)**; **Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs v. Perini North River Associates, 459 U.S. 297, 315-16 (1983)**. Congress incorporated that compensation scheme into OCSLA to protect offshore workers and their dependents. See **43 U.S.C. § 1333(b)**. A situs-of-injury limitation would arbitrarily deny benefits to workers injured in the course of offshore operations merely because the accident happened on shore, even though the injury was caused by those offshore operations. That reading is inconsistent with the statute’s text and remedial design. See **Curtis, 849 F.2d at 811**; **Pacific Operators Offshore, 565 U.S. at 220-21**.

The judgment below should therefore be affirmed. On this record, Valladolid’s death resulted from operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf, and OCSLA coverage does not turn on the fortuity that the forklift strike occurred on land. **43 U.S.C. § 1333(b)**; **Pacific Operators Offshore, 565 U.S. at 221-22**.
