**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief—particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to appeal.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires a district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal following a guilty plea. This requirement is not a jurisdictional mandate, but rather a procedural safeguard designed to ensure that defendants are fully informed of their appellate rights. The rule’s purpose is to promote fairness and informed decision-making, not to create a per se basis for vacatur of a valid sentence. As this Court has long recognized, “the failure to comply with a procedural rule does not necessarily result in the reversal of a conviction or sentence unless the error affects substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Here, no such substantial right was affected.

The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review to the Rule 32 violation. Under *Olano*, a reviewing court may consider plain error only if it “affects substantial rights,” and even then, only if it “seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” 507 U.S. at 732–33. The Third Circuit properly concluded that the failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was harmless because the record clearly established that Peguero was aware of his right and chose not to exercise it. The district court conducted an evidentiary hearing and credited testimony showing that Peguero had consulted with counsel, understood the appellate process, and deliberately decided not to appeal. This finding is entitled to deference under *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985), and is not clearly erroneous.

The Court has previously held that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his right to appeal negates the prejudice that might otherwise result from a failure to inform him.” *United States v. Bynum*, 987 F.2d 1058, 1061 (5th Cir. 1993); see also *United States v. Mendoza*, 978 F.2d 1455, 1458 (9th Cir. 1992) (holding that “a defendant’s awareness of his right to appeal renders the failure to advise harmless”). The rule’s purpose—to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights—is satisfied when the defendant is, in fact, aware. As this Court stated in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), “the rule’s function is to ensure that the defendant knows he has a right to appeal, not to create a right to appeal where none exists.” The rule does not require a mechanical recitation of rights; it requires only that the defendant be informed. When the defendant is already informed, the rule’s purpose is fulfilled.

Peguero’s claim rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of Rule 32(a)(2). He argues that any violation of the rule, regardless of context, warrants habeas relief. But this would transform a procedural safeguard into a constitutional right, which the rule was never intended to be. The Court has consistently rejected such a per se approach. In *United States v. Bledsoe*, 477 F.3d 107 (3d Cir. 2007), the Third Circuit held that “a failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) is not automatically reversible error,” especially where the defendant was aware of the right. The same reasoning applies here.

Moreover, the policy implications of granting habeas relief in every case where a court fails to advise a defendant of his right to appeal would be staggering. It would open the floodgates to collateral attacks on every sentence where a judge failed to recite the standard warning—even when the defendant knew his rights and made a deliberate choice not to appeal. Such a result would undermine the finality of criminal judgments and impose an undue burden on the federal courts. The Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of finality in criminal proceedings. *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 166 (1982) (“The finality of criminal judgments is a cornerstone of our system of justice.”).

The Court should not permit a technical violation of a procedural rule to override the defendant’s actual knowledge and voluntary decision not to appeal. The Third Circuit correctly applied harmless-error analysis and concluded that the Rule 32 violation did not affect substantial rights. The judgment should be affirmed.

**For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.**
