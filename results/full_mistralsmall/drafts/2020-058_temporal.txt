**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF THE IMMIGRATION AND NATIONALITY ACT (INA) DEMONSTRATE THAT A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER § 1255**

The plain text of the INA compels the conclusion that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) constitutes an “admission” for purposes of § 1255. Section 1254a(f)(4) explicitly provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) (emphasis added). This statutory language equates TPS recipients with nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted. As this Court has recognized, “[t]he words of statutes—especially those used in a technical legal sense—should be interpreted, if possible, in their natural, ordinary, and commonly understood meaning.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The term “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” in § 1254a(f)(4) plainly refers to a status that can only be attained through lawful admission. *See also* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13) (defining “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer”).

The Government’s argument that “lawful status” is distinct from “admission” ignores the statutory framework. In *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), the Supreme Court rejected a similar attempt to divorce statutory terms from their ordinary meaning, holding that “[w]here Congress has defined a term, that definition must be applied.” *Id.* at 199. Here, Congress has defined “admission” in § 1101(a)(13), and the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4) demonstrates that TPS recipients are treated as nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted. The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced because *Hanif* did not address the interplay between § 1254a(f)(4) and § 1255. *See also* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a) (requiring that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to adjust status).

II. **CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND STATUTORY PURPOSE SUPPORT THE CONCLUSION THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION**

The legislative history of the INA confirms that Congress intended TPS to function as a form of lawful admission for those who would otherwise be removable. In *INS v. Phinpathya*, 464 U.S. 183 (1984), the Supreme Court emphasized that “[t]he starting point in every case involving construction of a statute is the language itself.” *Id.* at 189. Here, the language of § 1254a(f)(4) plainly treats TPS recipients as nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted. This interpretation aligns with Congress’s broader purpose in creating TPS: to provide temporary relief to nationals of countries facing extraordinary conditions while maintaining their lawful presence in the United States. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1) (authorizing TPS for nationals of countries experiencing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions).

The Government’s argument that TPS does not constitute an admission would render § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528 (1955), “[a] statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Id.* at 538-39. If TPS did not constitute an admission, § 1254a(f)(4) would serve no purpose in the context of § 1255, as TPS recipients would remain statutorily barred from adjusting their status. This Court must avoid an interpretation that would render part of the statute superfluous. *See also* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k) (providing an exception to the admission requirement for certain employment-based adjustments, further demonstrating Congress’s intent to allow flexibility in adjustment of status where appropriate).

III. **PRECEDENT FROM THIS COURT AND OTHER CIRCUITS SUPPORTS THE CONCLUSION THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION**

This Court has long recognized that statutory terms should be given their plain and ordinary meaning. *See United States v. Alpers*, 338 U.S. 680, 682 (1950) (“The words of a statute must be read in their ordinary, everyday senses unless the legislature has clearly indicated otherwise.”). The plain text of § 1254a(f)(4) demonstrates that TPS recipients are treated as nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted. This interpretation is consistent with the decisions of other circuits that have addressed this issue prior to 1970. *See, e.g., United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 200 (1957) (holding that statutory terms should be given their plain meaning in immigration contexts).

The Government’s reliance on *Hanif*, 694 F.3d 479, is misplaced because *Hanif* did not address the interplay between § 1254a(f)(4) and § 1255. The Third Circuit’s decision in this case departs from the plain text of the statute and ignores the clear intent of Congress. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), “[t]he function of the courts is to enforce the will of Congress as expressed in the statute.” *Id.* at 542. Here, the will of Congress is clear: TPS recipients are to be treated as nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted for purposes of adjusting their status under § 1255.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that the Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under § 1255. The plain text of the INA, the legislative history, and the purpose of the TPS program all support the conclusion that TPS recipients are eligible to adjust their status under § 1255. This Court should reverse the decision below and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
