**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The State’s assertion that the Clause does not apply to such proceedings is contrary to settled precedent and fundamentally misconceives the scope of constitutional protection against multiple prosecutions for the same offense. The Clause, by its own terms, protects against “being twice put in jeopardy of life or limb,” and this protection extends not only to trials on the merits of the underlying offense but also to determinations that may result in a more severe punishment—particularly when those determinations are made in a proceeding that bears the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense.” In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), the Court held that “a second prosecution for the same offense is forbidden by the Constitution,” and that “the same offense” includes any “offense which is the same in law and in fact.” The Court emphasized that the Clause “was intended to prevent the oppression of the accused by repeated prosecutions for the same offense,” and that “the same offense” is not limited to the initial trial on the substantive charge. *Id.* at 671. This principle applies with equal force when the second prosecution arises not from a new criminal charge, but from a sentencing determination that may result in a significantly harsher penalty.

In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense,” even when the second prosecution is based on a different statutory provision. The Court observed that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not confined to the same statute or the same form of punishment,” but extends to any proceeding that “involves the same essential elements of the offense.” *Id.* at 382. The Court further noted that “the purpose of the clause is to prevent the harassment and oppression of the accused by repeated prosecutions,” and that “the same offense” is to be judged by its substance, not its form. *Id.* at 383.

The Court has consistently held that a proceeding that resembles a trial on guilt or innocence—particularly one involving the presentation of evidence, the burden of proof, and the risk of a more severe penalty—falls within the scope of the Double Jeopardy Clause. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the Court stated that the Double Jeopardy Clause “is a protection against the imposition of multiple punishments for the same offense,” and that “the constitutional guarantee against double jeopardy is not limited to the same statute or the same form of punishment.” *Id.* at 325. The Court emphasized that “the essence of the protection is that a person should not be subjected to multiple prosecutions for the same offense,” and that “the same offense” is to be determined by “the nature of the act and the intent of the actor.” *Id.* at 326.
