**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that the three-year statute of limitations in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) requires a plaintiff to have “actual knowledge” not only of the underlying transaction but also of the alleged imprudence of that transaction. The plain text, structure, and purpose of ERISA, reinforced by centuries of limitations jurisprudence, compel the opposite conclusion: once a plaintiff has been provided with all relevant information, he has “actual knowledge” regardless of whether he chose to read or now recalls having read it.

**I. The Text of § 1113(2) Requires Knowledge of the Facts, Not a Legal Conclusion.**

The statute bars any action commenced more than three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The “breach or violation” is the fiduciary’s act—the investment decision, the disclosure, the failure to monitor. Nothing in the statute requires the plaintiff to also know that the act was imprudent or unlawful. This Court has long held that a limitations period is triggered by knowledge of the facts giving rise to the claim, not by knowledge of the legal significance of those facts. *Baker v. Cummings*, 181 U.S. 117, 120 (1901) (knowledge of the transaction sufficient); *Curtis v. Connly*, 257 U.S. 260, 262 (1921) (statute runs from “the existence of the cause of action,” not from discovery of legal wrong). The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that the plaintiff know the investments were “imprudent” impermissibly reads a legal element into the statute.

**II. Pre-1970 Precedent Confirms That Disclosure of Facts Starts the Clock.**

A century of this Court’s decisions establishes that when a defendant discloses the operative facts, the plaintiff has the requisite knowledge to trigger limitations. In *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435, 448 (1918), the Court held that the statute of limitations on a patent fraud claim began to run from the date of issuance, not from discovery, because the facts were publicly recorded. Here, Intel disclosed all investment decisions in Fund Fact Sheets, plan documents, and websites. That disclosure equates to knowledge. *Accord*: *American Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 160, 166 (1898) (bond’s notice requirement satisfied when plaintiff had “knowledge of the facts” from written reports). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—requiring the plaintiff to affirmatively read and remember—effectively reads the statute out of existence.

**III. The Purpose of Statutes of Limitations Bars the “Ostrich” Defense.**

Statutes of limitations exist to encourage diligence and prevent stale claims. *Unexcelled Chemical Corp. v. United States*, 345 U.S. 59, 63 (1953) (limitations period “begins to run when the cause of action accrues,” not when plaintiff discovers it). A plaintiff who chooses not to read disclosed information cannot later claim ignorance to avoid the bar. *See* *United States v. A. S. Kreider Co.*, 313 U.S. 443, 448 (1941) (limitations period runs from the event, not from plaintiff’s subjective awareness). Here, Sulyma accessed the disclosure websites but testified he “did not recall” the information. That is precisely the kind of willful blindness that this Court has rejected. *United States v. Wurts*, 303 U.S. 414, 417 (1938) (refund limitations period runs from allowance, not payment, because the government “had the means of knowledge”).

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Nullify the 1987 Amendment.**

Congress repealed the constructive-knowledge provision in 1987, leaving only “actual knowledge.” That repeal did not convert the statute into a subjective-awareness test. To the contrary, “actual knowledge” means knowledge of the facts, not constructive notice. But the Ninth Circuit’s requirement of knowledge of *imprudence* elevates the standard far beyond the pre-1987 constructive-knowledge test, which was satisfied when a “report … could reasonably be expected” to provide knowledge. The Ninth Circuit’s rule allows a plaintiff to defeat summary judgment simply by saying “I didn’t read it.” That is not what Congress intended. *United States v. Goldman*, 277 U.S. 229, 234 (1928) (limitations period controlled by “the clear language of the statute”). *Jaben v. United States*, 381 U.S. 214, 220 (1965) (limitations tolled by complaint, not by subjective awareness).

**V. Sulyma’s Lack of Recall Does Not Create a Genuine Dispute.**

The Ninth Circuit concluded that Sulyma’s declaration that he was “unaware” and “did not recall” created a material fact dispute. But this Court has never permitted a plaintiff to avoid summary judgment based on self-serving denials of knowledge when the evidence shows disclosure. *Curtis v. Connly*, 257 U.S. at 263 (no tolling where plaintiff “had the means of knowledge”). The district court properly found that the undisputed disclosures gave Sulyma actual knowledge.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
