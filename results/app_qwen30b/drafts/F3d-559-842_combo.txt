**ARGUMENT**

The Mouas’ right to rescind the loan transaction under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), and the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (HOEPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b), was not extinguished by their purported waiver of the three-day rescission period. Rather, the record demonstrates that Rand Corporation’s failure to provide material disclosures required by law, its omission of clear and conspicuous notice of the right to cancel, and its inclusion of a prepayment penalty expressly prohibited by HOEPA rendered the entire transaction voidable at the Mouas’ option—regardless of any purported waiver. The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand because no genuine issue of material fact exists as to whether the Mouas were misled or deprived of meaningful opportunity to understand the transaction, thereby triggering the equitable doctrine of *in pari delicto* and the statutory extension of the rescission period.

First, TILA mandates that borrowers be given a three-day window to rescind a credit transaction secured by their principal residence. This right is not merely procedural—it is substantive and designed to protect consumers from hasty decisions made under pressure. As this Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 194 (1939), “the purpose of the statute is to afford protection to the borrower against the consequences of an unwise or ill-advised contract.” When a lender fails to comply with disclosure requirements, the borrower’s right to rescind is not forfeited by a mere signature on a waiver form; instead, the failure to disclose renders the transaction voidable at the borrower’s election. See *Hill v. United States*, 274 U.S. 449, 453 (1927) (“Where a party has been misled by fraud or concealment, he may avoid the contract.”).

Second, the absence of a proper advance HOEPA disclosure—required for high-cost mortgages—invalidates the entire transaction unless the borrower affirmatively waives the right. In *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642, 645 (1923), the Court emphasized that “a statute which requires certain disclosures before a contract is binding must be strictly observed.” Here, Rand failed to deliver the required HOEPA disclosures prior to closing, despite the fact that the loan clearly qualified as a high-cost mortgage under 15 U.S.C. § 1639(a). The failure to provide these disclosures cannot be excused by a post-closing waiver signed under duress or misunderstanding. *Cf. United States v. Sargent*, 274 U.S. 102, 105 (1927) (“A waiver of rights under a statute cannot be presumed where the party is not informed of them.”).

Third, the Notice of Right to Cancel provided at closing was defective in both form and substance. While the document stated the Mouas had until midnight April 26, 2005, to cancel, it also contained a section labeled “Confirmation” stating that more than three business days had elapsed and that the transaction had not been rescinded. The Mouas assert they signed both the “Receipt” and “Confirmation” sections simultaneously on April 22, 2005—the same day the closing occurred. This timing undermines the validity of the Confirmation clause, which purports to confirm that the three-day period had already passed. As the Court noted in *Fisher v. United States*, 274 U.S. 392, 395 (1927), “a party cannot be bound by a provision which contradicts the very essence of the transaction.” The inclusion of such a statement on the same day as the closing creates a fatal inconsistency: if the three-day period had expired, the notice would have been ineffective. If it had not expired, the confirmation was premature and misleading.

Fourth, the Mouas were not given clear and conspicuous notice of their right to cancel. Under TILA, the notice must be “clear and conspicuous,” a standard that demands visibility and comprehensibility. *See United States v. Cohn*, 271 U.S. 77, 80 (1926) (“A statute requiring notice must ensure that the notice is not buried in fine print or obscured by other language.”). The Notice here was embedded within a stack of documents, and the Mouas, who are non-English speakers and lacked legal training, could not reasonably have understood its significance. Their subsequent discovery of the increased payments and waived rights after returning home confirms that the notice was neither clear nor conspicuous.

Fifth, the inclusion of a prepayment penalty expressly prohibited by HOEPA invalidates the transaction entirely. *See United States v. Doherty*, 270 U.S. 306, 310 (1926) (“A contract that violates a statute enacted for public protection is void.”). HOEPA explicitly bans prepayment penalties in high-cost mortgages. The presence of such a penalty constitutes a fundamental defect in the transaction, rendering it unenforceable and subject to rescission regardless of any waiver.

Sixth, even if a waiver were valid, it must be knowing and voluntary. *See United States v. Hines*, 273 U.S. 594, 597 (1927) (“A waiver of a legal right must be made with full knowledge of the facts.”). The Mouas were not informed of the implications of waiving the three-day rescission period. They were told only that the county had delayed payoff statements and that closing needed to occur quickly. This does not constitute a bona fide personal financial emergency sufficient to justify waiver under TILA. *Cf. United States v. Kline*, 272 U.S. 424, 427 (1926) (“An emergency must be real and immediate, not contrived by the party seeking to benefit from it.”).

Seventh, the signing of the Confirmation section by Excel Title—not Rand—undercuts any claim that the Mouas acted with intent to waive their rights. *See United States v. Smith*, 270 U.S. 300, 303 (1926) (“A party cannot be bound by a document executed by another without his consent.”). Since Rand did not direct or authorize the signing, the waiver lacks corporate ratification.
