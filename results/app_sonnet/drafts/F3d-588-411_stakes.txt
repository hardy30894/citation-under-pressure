**ARGUMENT**

**A. The District Court Erred in Its Supplemental Instruction Defining "Shielding" Under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii)**

A jury instruction "may not be judged in artificial isolation," but must be assessed for whether it fairly and accurately conveys the governing law. *Cupp v. Naughten*, 414 U.S. 141, 146–47 (1973). Where a statutory term is undefined by Congress and susceptible to more than one reasonable reading, courts must be especially careful that a supplemental definition does not expand criminal liability beyond what the statute was designed to reach. *See United States v. Santos*, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008) (plurality opinion) (explaining that the rule of lenity requires ambiguous criminal statutes to be construed in favor of the defendant).

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) makes it a felony to "conceal[], harbor[], or shield[] from detection" an alien known to be unlawfully present. Congress did not define "shielding," leaving courts to give the term content. Several circuits confronting this gap have adopted a uniform, narrowing construction: conduct that "tend[s] substantially to facilitate an alien's remaining in the United States illegally and to prevent government authorities from detecting the alien's unlawful presence." *United States v. Ozcelik*, 527 F.3d 88, 100 (3d Cir. 2008); *accord United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567, 574 (2d Cir. 1999); *United States v. Lopez*, 521 F.3d 1201, 1206 (9th Cir. 2008). That formulation requires the government to prove not merely that a defendant's conduct made an alien's detection somewhat less likely, but that the defendant's conduct was substantially and materially connected to the alien's continued unlawful presence and evasion of the authorities.

The district court's supplemental instruction dispensed with that limiting principle. Rather than requiring the jury to find that Ye's conduct substantially facilitated his employees' continued unlawful presence and materially impeded the government's ability to find them, the instruction permitted the jury to convict on proof that Ye engaged in any conduct that made detection more difficult in some undefined degree. That formulation swept ordinary, lawful employer conduct—paying employees in cash, failing to keep certain payroll records, providing housing to workers—into the ambit of a felony offense without requiring the substantial, causally connected concealment that the statute's use of "shield" contemplates. Every person who employs an illegal alien makes that alien's detection somewhat less likely simply by employing him; if that is sufficient for "shielding," the term becomes coextensive with the misdemeanor hiring offense under § 1324a(a)(1)(A), collapsing Congress's deliberate distinction between the two offenses.

Because the instruction permitted the jury to convict Ye of a felony on a standard broader than that adopted by other circuits construing the identical statutory language, and because any ambiguity in the term must be resolved against expansive criminal liability, *Santos*, 553 U.S. at 514, the conviction should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial with a properly narrowed instruction consistent with *Ozcelik*, *Kim*, and *Lopez*.

**B. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to Prevent the Government From Detecting His Illegal Alien Employees**

Even under the district court's own instruction, the government was required to prove that Ye acted with intent to prevent government detection of his employees' unlawful status. A conviction must be reversed if, "after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution," no "rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

The evidence here showed, at most, that Ye failed to comply with employment-verification requirements, paid workers in cash, and provided housing and transportation to employees he knew to be unauthorized. But these facts establish knowing employment of illegal aliens—the separate misdemeanor offense under § 1324a(a)(1)(A)—not a specific intent to shield those employees from government detection. Ye did not conceal his employees from investigators: when agents visited Buffet City in April 2005 and again in August 2005, they observed the Hispanic workers openly on the premises. Ye did not deny their presence, did not instruct them to hide, and did not obstruct the agents' investigation. He candidly told agents he lacked the required documentation and explained why. When agents returned in December 2005, they were again able to locate and arrest the illegal aliens working at the restaurant without impediment from Ye.

Providing housing and transportation, without more, is conduct incidental to employment and does not itself establish an intent to prevent detection by the government; nor does a failure to maintain immigration paperwork demonstrate an affirmative effort to conceal workers from investigators who were, in fact, repeatedly able to find them. At most, the evidence shows Ye's indifference to immigration law, not an intent to defeat detection by federal authorities. Under the substantial-facilitation standard adopted in *Ozcelik*, 527 F.3d at 100, and *Kim*, 193 F.3d at 574, this evidence cannot sustain a felony shielding conviction.

Because no rational jury could have found, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Ye intended to shield his employees from government detection rather than merely to employ them unlawfully, this Court should reverse Ye's conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and direct entry of a judgment of acquittal on that count.
