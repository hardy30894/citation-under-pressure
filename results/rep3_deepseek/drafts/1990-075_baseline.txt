**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The Constitution permits Congress to obtain assistance from coordinate branches so long as it lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies this standard.

**A. Congress Provided an Intelligible Principle Guiding the Attorney General’s Discretion.**

Section 811(h) authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only when necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is far more precise than delegations the Court has repeatedly upheld. *See, e.g., Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374 (upholding delegation requiring sentencing guidelines to serve “the purposes of sentencing”); *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944) (upholding delegation to set prices that would be “fair and equitable”).

Moreover, Congress cabined the Attorney General’s authority in multiple ways. The Attorney General must consider three specific factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). The Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit it to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be considered. *Id.* § 811(h)(4). Temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. *Id.* § 811(h)(2). These constraints provide “more than an ‘intelligible principle.’” *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 545 (9th Cir. 1988).

**B. The Delegation Does Not Violate the Nondelegation Doctrine Simply Because It Involves Criminal Penalties.**

Petitioners argue that stricter scrutiny applies because the delegation authorizes the creation of crimes. But the Supreme Court has never adopted such a categorical rule. In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld a delegation to the Sentencing Commission that directly determined the length of criminal sentences. 488 U.S. at 371-79. And in *Yakus*, the Court upheld a delegation authorizing criminal penalties for violating price controls. 321 U.S. at 423-27. The “intelligible principle” test applies regardless of whether the delegated power touches criminal law.

The two cases petitioners invoke—*Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935)—are distinguishable. In *Panama Refining*, Congress provided “no criterion to govern the President’s course.” 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, Congress gave the President “virtually unfettered” discretion to approve codes of fair competition. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress supplied detailed criteria, procedural requirements, and temporal limits. Section 811(h) is nothing like the “sweeping delegation of legislative power” struck down in those cases. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 n.7.

**C. The Preclusion of Judicial Review Does Not Render the Delegation Unconstitutional.**

Section 811(h)(6) provides that a temporary scheduling order “is not subject to judicial review.” But this bar applies only to factual or procedural challenges, not to constitutional ones. *See Emerson*, 846 F.2d at 544; *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1428 (10th Cir. 1987). Petitioners’ constitutional challenge is therefore reviewable. Moreover, the availability of judicial review is not a prerequisite for a valid delegation. *See Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 444 (upholding delegation despite limited judicial review). The “intelligible principle” test focuses on the guidance Congress provided, not on the availability of post-deprivation review.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL PROPERLY DELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DRUG ENFORCEMENT ADMINISTRATION.**

**A. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Subdelegate.**

The Controlled Substances Act provides that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions and duties under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This language is unambiguous and plenary. It authorizes the Attorney General to delegate temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator. *See United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 168 (D. Del. 1987) (holding that § 871(a) “clearly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act”).

**B. The Attorney General Properly Exercised His Subdelegation Authority.**

Pursuant to § 871(a), the Attorney General promulgated 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns to the DEA Administrator “[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This regulation encompasses the temporary scheduling power. The DEA Administrator then followed the statutory procedures: he published notice, considered comments from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and issued a temporary scheduling order for Euphoria. 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174, 38,225 (1987). The subdelegation was therefore lawful.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
