**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were moot upon Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) abandonment of its challenged “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct,” and that the claim for nominal damages could not revive a case otherwise rendered moot. This Court should affirm.

### I. A Claim for Nominal Damages Cannot Save a Moot Constitutional Challenge

The doctrine of mootness ensures that federal courts adjudicate live controversies. *See* *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). When a party’s injury ceases and there is no reasonable expectation of future harm, the case becomes moot, and the court lacks jurisdiction to decide it. *See* *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), *cert. denied*, 138 S. Ct. 1055 (2018). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *See* *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976).

Here, GGC revised its policies in February 2017—before the district court ruled on the motion to dismiss—eliminating the speech zones and removing the provision that prohibited open-air speech. The revised policy permits students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The district court found that GGC “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” This finding is not contested on appeal. The change was not a mere procedural adjustment; it was a substantive, permanent alteration of the policy that rendered the prior restrictions obsolete.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “a plaintiff’s desire to vindicate a constitutional right is not sufficient to maintain standing once the challenged conduct has ceased and the plaintiff has no reasonable expectation of future harm.” *See* *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 102 (1983). The same principle applies when the challenged policy is replaced with a constitutionally compliant one. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255 (“A claim for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.”). The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied *Flanigan’s* in concluding that the claim for nominal damages could not “save” the otherwise moot constitutional claims.

### II. Nominal Damages Are Not a Substitute for Injunctive or Declaratory Relief

Appellants argue that their request for nominal damages preserved a live controversy. But nominal damages are not a remedy for the absence of a live injury. They are “a token sum awarded to recognize a legal wrong when no actual damages are proven.” *See* *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). They are not a substitute for injunctive or declaratory relief, which are designed to prevent future harm. *See* *Harris v. McRae*, 448 U.S. 297, 317 (1980) (“The purpose of a declaratory judgment is to settle and afford relief from uncertainty and insecurity with respect to rights, status, and other legal relations.”).

Appellants’ complaint never alleged any actual injury—no financial loss, no emotional distress, no physical harm. Instead, they claimed only that they suffered “irreparable harm” from the violation of their constitutional rights. *See* *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (“Compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the ‘abstract value’ of the constitutional rights that may have been violated.”). The district court correctly concluded that the complaint sought only nominal damages, which are insufficient to sustain standing when the underlying injury has ceased.

Appellants’ attempt to recharacterize their claim as seeking compensatory damages fails. They did not plead any facts supporting such a claim. The prayer for relief requested only “nominal damages.” The complaint never specified the nature or extent of any injury. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (“The mere assertion of a right to damages does not transform a moot claim into a live one.”). The Eleventh Circuit properly rejected this after-the-fact attempt to reframe the claim.

### III. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend

Appellants contend the district court abused its discretion by denying their request to amend the complaint to seek compensatory damages. But they failed to comply with the procedural requirements for seeking leave to amend. *See* *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (“[W]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.”). Appellants did not file a motion to amend; they merely requested leave in a response to a motion to dismiss. This is not the proper procedural vehicle.
