### Argument

**Johnson announced a substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review.**

Under **Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989)**, new substantive rules apply retroactively because they “alter[] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” **Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004)**. By contrast, procedural rules merely regulate “the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability” and ordinarily do not apply retroactively. **Id.** Johnson is substantive. It narrowed the scope of the Armed Career Criminal Act by striking the residual clause as void for vagueness, leaving the statute to apply only to offenders whose prior convictions satisfy the enumerated-offenses clause or the elements clause. **Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591, 597-606 (2015)**.

The Court has repeatedly held that a rule is substantive when it “narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms” or places certain punishments “beyond the State’s power to impose.” **Schriro, 542 U.S. at 351-52**; **Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 200-01 (2016)**. Johnson did exactly that. It did not announce a new procedure for deciding ACCA cases; it invalidated one of the statute’s three paths to enhanced punishment. A defendant who was previously exposed to ACCA’s fifteen-year minimum solely through the residual clause is now outside the statute’s reach unless his prior convictions qualify under a remaining clause. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. **Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120, 129-31 (2016)**.

Welch’s contrary position is foreclosed by the retroactivity framework the Court has already adopted for vagueness decisions. In **Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998)**, the Court explained that decisions narrowing the meaning of a criminal statute apply retroactively because they are substantive. Likewise, in **Fiore v. White, 531 U.S. 225, 228-29 (2001)**, the Court held that a defendant may not be punished for conduct the statute did not actually cover. Johnson fits that same category: it changes what ACCA means and whom it punishes. **Johnson, 576 U.S. at 597-606**.

The government is therefore wrong to characterize Johnson as merely procedural because it required courts to evaluate risk in a new way. The Court has squarely rejected the notion that all vagueness rulings are procedural. In **Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 198-200**, the Court emphasized that substantive rules include decisions that “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” Johnson created exactly that risk for prisoners sentenced under the residual clause. Those sentences were imposed under a statutory provision that the Court later held cannot constitutionally support punishment at all. **Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606**.

Nor does **Teague** bar relief because Johnson was not a “watershed” procedural rule; it was not procedural in the first place. The Court in **Schriro, 542 U.S. at 352-58**, distinguished substantive rules from procedural rules and made clear that substantive rules apply retroactively on collateral review. Johnson falls squarely on the substantive side of that line. The Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized this in **In re Rivero, 797 F.3d 986, 990-91 (11th Cir. 2015)**, and the same conclusion follows from the Court’s own retroactivity decisions.

Welch’s sentence rested on ACCA’s residual clause. Because **Johnson** held that clause unconstitutional, his sentence exceeds the lawful maximum unless sustained by some other ACCA predicate. That is precisely the kind of error retroactivity doctrine permits collateral review to correct. **Welch, 578 U.S. at 128-31**; **Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 200-01**; **Bousley, 523 U.S. at 620-21**. The judgment should be affirmed.
