# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD, AS APPLIED, IS ARBITRARY AND CAPRICIOUS

The Board's requirement that an employer possess "good-faith reasonable doubt" before polling employees—identical to the standard for outright withdrawal of recognition—renders the polling mechanism nearly useless and cannot be reconciled with the Act's purpose of ensuring that bargaining representatives actually enjoy majority support. This Court has recognized that an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority status, but that presumption exists to promote stability, not to insulate unions from legitimate inquiry into their actual support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987).

The fundamental flaw in the Board's approach is one of internal logic. If the same evidentiary showing suffices both to withdraw recognition unilaterally *and* to conduct a poll—a procedure with far greater safeguards, including secret balloting supervised by a neutral third party—the polling option becomes superfluous. As the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have recognized, polling should be a "useful and legitimate tool" available to employers who harbor genuine uncertainty, even where that uncertainty falls short of the threshold required for outright withdrawal. *See Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1298 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982). These courts held that "an employer may poll its employees to determine their union sentiment if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal." *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867.

The Board's professed rationale—that polling is "potentially, if not inherently . . . disruptive of the collective-bargaining relationship" and "unsettling to the employees involved," *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989)—cannot justify equating the polling standard with the withdrawal standard. If anything, the Board's own reasoning that RM elections are the "preferred" method for testing support, because Board-supervised elections offer superior safeguards, "lead[s] squarely to the conclusion that the polling standard should be more stringent than that for RM elections," not identical to the withdrawal standard. This internal inconsistency renders the Board's rule arbitrary. An agency's interpretation of its governing statute, while entitled to deference, must still be "rational and consistent with the Act." *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786-87 (1990). A standard that collapses three distinct statutory mechanisms—withdrawal, RM election, and polling—into a single undifferentiated test, while simultaneously proclaiming a preference ordering among them, fails that test.

## II. SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE DID NOT SUPPORT THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT PETITIONER LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT

Even accepting the Board's stringent standard, the Board's finding that Allentown Mack lacked good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. This Court's review of Board factfinding must ensure that the Board's conclusions rest on the record "as a whole," not on selective disregard of probative evidence. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951).

The Board arbitrarily discounted reliable indicia of union disaffection. It refused to credit Mike Ridgick's statement that "there was no need for a union" on the theory that job-interview statements are inherently suspect, and it discounted Dennis Marsh's statement that he was not "getting his money's worth" from union dues as mere dissatisfaction with representation rather than opposition to the union itself. But these categorical rules improperly substitute per se exclusion for holistic assessment of the totality of circumstances. The Board likewise excluded the statements of Kermit Bloch and union steward Ron Mohr—the latter of whom directly stated that "if a vote was taken, the union would lose"—as unreliable hearsay, notwithstanding that Mohr, as union steward, was uniquely positioned to know the sentiments of his fellow employees.

The Board's approach effectively requires employers to produce testimony from every individual employee whose sentiments are relayed, an evidentiary burden this Court has never imposed and one inconsistent with the practical realities of workplace communication. Where, as here, multiple employees from different departments independently conveyed anti-union sentiment, and where a union steward himself predicted the union would lose a vote, the cumulative weight of that evidence created at minimum a genuine, good-faith doubt as to majority support—precisely the doubt the Board's own standard purports to require.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
