The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is arbitrary and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, and the NLRB has developed a standard for permitting employers to conduct polls. However, the NLRB's standard, as established in Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), is overly restrictive. Several courts of appeals have rejected this standard in favor of a more lenient "substantial, objective evidence" standard, which better balances the employer's interest in ascertaining employee sentiment with the need to protect employees' rights (NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d 1141 (5th Cir. 1961); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863 (6th Cir. 1962); NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (8th Cir. 1965)).

The NLRB's standard fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment. In Celanese Corp. of America, 95 N.L.R.B. 664 (1951), the NLRB acknowledged that an employer may have a good-faith doubt about a union's majority status. The Stoner Rubber Co. decision, 123 N.L.R.B. 1440 (1959), further supports the notion that an employer's poll can be lawful even without objective evidence of a loss of majority support.

Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union. This evidence is similar to that considered sufficient in NLRB v. Gulfmont Hotel Co., 362 F.2d 588 (5th Cir. 1966). The NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The NLRB's finding is based on an overly narrow interpretation of the evidence, ignoring the totality of the circumstances.

The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of substantial evidence in supporting the NLRB's decisions (Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951)). The Court has also held that the NLRB's findings must be reviewed to ensure they are not arbitrary or capricious (NLRB v. Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. 656 (1949)). The NLRB's decision in this case fails to meet this standard.

Furthermore, the NLRB's standard, as established in Struksnes Constr. Co., is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships, as established in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1 (1937). By restricting employers' ability to conduct polls, the NLRB's standard may actually destabilize collective-bargaining relationships.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is arbitrary and inconsistent with the NLRA. The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The court should reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful, as it was conducted in good faith and based on substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.
