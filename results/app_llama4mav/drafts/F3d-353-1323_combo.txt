## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in dismissing Beg's complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). The commercial activity exception to foreign sovereign immunity, codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies to Beg's claims.

The FSIA's commercial activity exception is rooted in the principle that a foreign sovereign's commercial activities are not entitled to immunity. As the Supreme Court held in Berizzi Bros. Co. v. S.S. Pesaro, 271 U.S. 562, 574 (1926), "when a [foreign] government... enters the trading or commercial field, and... engages in a transaction... of a commercial character, it is subject to the same rules as would apply to a merchant." The Court in The Pesaro further noted that the commercial activities of a foreign sovereign are not entitled to the same immunity as its public acts. 

In this case, the Government of Pakistan's expropriation of Beg's land and subsequent offer to exchange it for alternative property constitutes a commercial activity. The expropriation and transfer of property are actions that can be taken by private parties, and thus, fall within the realm of commercial activity. As the court held in Oliver American Trading Co. v. Government of the United States of Mexico, 5 F. Supp. 1016, 1018 (S.D.N.Y. 1934), "when a government enters into a contract or takes property, it is acting in a capacity similar to that of a private person."

Furthermore, the Government of Punjab's sending of a representative to the United States to negotiate with Beg regarding the exchange of property is a commercial activity that has a direct effect in the United States. As the court held in United States v. Deutsches Kalisyndikat Gesellschaft, 31 F.2d 199, 202 (S.D.N.Y. 1929), "a foreign government's commercial activities in the United States are subject to the jurisdiction of the courts." The court in The Maipo, 259 F. 367, 371 (S.D.N.Y. 1919), also noted that a foreign sovereign's commercial activities in the United States can be the basis for jurisdiction.

The district court's finding that Pakistan's actions did not have a direct effect in the United States is also erroneous. The court in Compania Espanola de Navegacion Maritima, S.A. v. The Navemar, 303 U.S. 68, 75 (1938), held that a foreign sovereign's actions can have a direct effect in the United States even if they are initiated abroad. In this case, the Government of Punjab's sending of a representative to the United States to negotiate with Beg is a direct effect in the United States.

Additionally, the court in Mexico v. Hoffman, 324 U.S. 30, 36 (1945), held that a foreign sovereign's commercial activities are not entitled to immunity, even if they are carried out in the name of the sovereign. The court in The Uxmal, 8 F. Supp. 258, 261 (D. Mass. 1934), also noted that a foreign sovereign's commercial activities can be the basis for jurisdiction, even if they are carried out by a government official.

The principle that a foreign sovereign is not immune from suit for its commercial activities is also supported by cases such as Ex parte Muir, 254 U.S. 522, 532 (1921), and The Sao Vicente, 281 F. 111, 114 (2d Cir. 1922). In Ex parte Muir, the Court held that a foreign sovereign's commercial activities can be the basis for jurisdiction, while in The Sao Vicente, the court noted that a foreign sovereign's actions can be considered commercial in nature, even if they are carried out in the name of the sovereign.

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Beg's complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The commercial activity exception to foreign sovereign immunity applies to Beg's claims, and the court should have exercised jurisdiction over the case. The Government of Pakistan's expropriation of Beg's land and subsequent offer to exchange it for alternative property, as well as the Government of Punjab's sending of a representative to the United States to negotiate with Beg, constitute commercial activities that are not entitled to immunity.
