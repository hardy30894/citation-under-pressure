I. The District Court Erred in Denying Judgment as a Matter of Law on Invalidity.

A patent is presumed valid, and the party asserting invalidity must prove facts supporting the defense by clear and convincing evidence. Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. Partnership, 564 U.S. 91, 95 (2011). Judgment as a matter of law is required when the evidence permits only one reasonable conclusion. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000). Here, no reasonable jury could have found that Facebook carried its burden under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b).

A. The Record Contains No Clear and Convincing Evidence That the Pre-Critical-Date Leader2Leader® Product Embodied the Asserted Claims.

The on-sale and public-use bars apply only when the invention is “ready for patenting.” Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc., 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998). Readiness requires either reduction to practice or enabling disclosure. Id. The asserted claims require dynamic association of metadata with data and applications, tracking of user movement between environments, and automatic updating of metadata to reflect the new environment. The only evidence that Leader2Leader® practiced these limitations was McKibben’s testimony that the claims cover the “underlying engine.” That testimony supplies no element-by-element comparison and therefore cannot constitute clear and convincing proof that every limitation was met. See Zenith Elecs. Corp. v. PDI Commc’n Sys., Inc., 522 F.3d 1348, 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (invalidity requires proof that “each and every limitation” is present).

The 2002 white paper likewise fails to supply the missing proof. It describes a “Big Board” and browser access but contains no disclosure of metadata tagging that follows users across environments or of automatic updating upon movement between user environments. Absent such evidence, the jury could not lawfully conclude that the offered product anticipated the claims.

B. Facebook Failed to Prove a Commercial Offer for Sale of the Claimed Invention Before the Critical Date.

Even assuming the invention was ready for patenting, the on-sale bar requires a commercial offer. Pfaff, 525 U.S. at 67. Leader’s January 2002 submission to Wright Patterson Air Force Base was a proposal to supply 20,000 licenses of a product still described as operating “with low user volumes.” Such exploratory communications do not constitute the “definite offer” necessary to trigger § 102(b). See Group One, Ltd. v. Hallmark Cards, Inc., 254 F.3d 1041, 1047 (Fed. Cir. 2001). The record contains no evidence of acceptance, price terms, or delivery obligations before December 10, 2002.

C. The Evidence Does Not Establish Public Use.

Public use under § 102(b) requires that the invention be used “by a person other than the inventor” in its “natural and intended way.” Egbert v. Lippmann, 104 U.S. 333, 336 (1881). Leader’s demonstrations were limited to prospective customers under circumstances that did not reveal the internal metadata mechanisms. No evidence shows that third parties operated the system to perform the claimed tracking and updating steps. Accordingly, the public-use bar was not satisfied.

Because the evidence is insufficient to support the verdict under the clear-and-convincing standard, the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law must be reversed.
