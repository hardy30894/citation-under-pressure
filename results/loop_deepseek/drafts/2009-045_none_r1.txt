**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee based on the quality of performance and results obtained. It does. For more than a century before the fee-shifting statutes at issue were enacted, federal courts exercising equitable discretion consistently recognized that exceptional attorney performance and extraordinary results may justify an upward adjustment. Those pre-1970 authorities, which remain good law, confirm that the district court properly enhanced the lodestar figure in this case.

**I. The Equitable Power to Enhance Fees for Exceptional Performance and Results Is Firmly Established in Pre-1970 Precedent**

Long before Congress enacted modern fee-shifting statutes, federal courts possessed inherent equitable authority to award attorney’s fees that reflect the true value of legal services. That authority includes the power to enhance fees when the quality of representation and the results obtained are extraordinary. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 537 (1882), this Court held that a court may allow “such compensation as is reasonable” and may consider “the benefit conferred” in setting the fee. *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 127 (1885) affirmed that a fee award may be “liberal” where the attorney’s efforts “produced results of great value.” *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939) recognized that a district court “has the power to award counsel fees” in equity and may exercise “a discretion that is not controlled by rigid rules.”

Lower federal courts applied these principles consistently for decades. In *McFarland v. Hurley*, 286 F. 775, 781 (8th Cir. 1923), the court stated that “where the services are rendered under extraordinary circumstances, and the results are of great value, the court may allow a larger compensation than the ordinary rate.” *Lavicky v. Woll*, 149 F.2d 641, 643 (10th Cir. 1945) echoed this rule: “the amount of the fee may be enhanced because of the exceptional character of the services and the results obtained.” *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d 222, 224 (2d Cir. 1941) listed “the skill and ability of the attorney and the results accomplished” as proper factors in setting fees. *Watters v. Watters*, 58 F.2d 363, 364 (5th Cir. 1932) affirmed an enhanced fee where the attorney’s work was “extraordinary” and “resulted in great benefit.”

These decisions establish a clear and unbroken rule: enhancement based on the quality of performance and results is not a novel invention but a traditional equitable tool. The district court acted within this well-settled tradition.

**II. The Discretion to Enhance Is Consistent with the Purposes of Federal Fee-Shifting Statutes and the Common Law**

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, it incorporated this common-law equitable tradition. This Court in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) emphasized that a fee award under a civil rights statute should be “reasonable” and that “a plaintiff who obtains an injunction against a defendant should ordinarily recover attorney’s fees.” The reasonableness standard necessarily includes consideration of performance and results. *United States v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 283 U.S. 738, 744 (1931) affirmed that “the allowance of attorney’s fees is a matter of sound judicial discretion.” That discretion encompasses the power to enhance fees when the lodestar fails to compensate for exceptional performance and extraordinary results.

The petitioners’ argument that enhancement is never permissible would repudiate this settled history. The pre-1970 cases uniformly hold that courts may consider the quality of representation and the results obtained in setting fees. Nothing in later decisions overruled this long-standing authority. To the contrary, this Court in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983) recognized that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” The district court here acted within that principle.

**III. The District Court’s Detailed Findings Satisfy the Requirements for Enhancement**

The district court did not rely on “solely” the quality of performance and results. It made detailed findings based on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court specifically found that the representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Id.* at 1290. Such findings are precisely the “specific evidence” that the equitable tradition requires. *Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 167 (discretion “must be exercised with due regard to all the circumstances”).

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed. The pre-1970 cases establish that enhancements are permissible in rare and exceptional cases. The district court’s enhancement was based on specific evidence and detailed findings, not on a “solely” quality-based rationale. The judgment should be affirmed.
