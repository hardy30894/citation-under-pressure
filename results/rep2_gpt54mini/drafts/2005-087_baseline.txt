**Argument**

Arizona’s rule does not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense,” but that principle has never been understood to require a State to admit every category of evidence a defendant wishes to offer on every theory he prefers. *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986); *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). States retain broad authority to define the elements of offenses and to structure affirmative defenses, including insanity. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201-07 (1977); *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952).

Arizona’s rule fits comfortably within that authority. The State permitted Clark to present expert mental-health evidence to support his insanity defense; it simply limited that same evidence from being repurposed to negate mens rea apart from insanity. That allocation does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt. Rather, it channels mental-health evidence into the State’s chosen insanity framework. Due process does not forbid that choice. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 56 (1996) (plurality opinion) (“the right to have a jury consider ‘evidence of voluntary intoxication’” to negate mens rea is not a fundamental principle of justice); id. at 58-59 (Ginsburg, J., concurring in the judgment) (States may define the mens rea elements and evidence rules together). A State may “enact its own rules of evidence” so long as they do not offend a fundamental principle of justice deeply rooted in our traditions. *Chambers*, 410 U.S. at 302.

This Court’s due process cases confirm that Arizona may limit expert evidence offered to rebut intent when the limitation reflects a valid substantive policy judgment. In *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006), the Court upheld the very Arizona rule at issue here. The Court explained that “due process does not require that a State admit evidence of mental disease and capacity to negate mens rea,” and it emphasized the “validity of Arizona’s rule restricting consideration of mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense.” *Id.* at 770, 779. The Court reasoned that the State may avoid the risk that psychiatric testimony will confuse the jury and will invite “end runs” around its chosen insanity standard. *Id.* at 774-79. That reasoning is controlling here.

Clark’s contrary position rests on the mistaken premise that the Constitution requires States to permit “diminished capacity” evidence whenever the prosecution must prove intent. But this Court has never recognized such a freestanding due process right. To the contrary, *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), held only that the prosecution must prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged; it did not prevent a State from defining how mental disease evidence may be used. Likewise, *Patterson* upheld New York’s decision to place the burden of proving extreme emotional disturbance on the defendant because the State had not shifted any element of the offense. 432 U.S. at 210-16. Arizona’s rule is even more modest: it leaves the State’s burden on intent untouched while directing mental-disease evidence to insanity.

Nor does *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), help Clark. *Ake* held that an indigent defendant must have access to psychiatric assistance when sanity is likely to be a significant factor at trial. *Id.* at 83. It did not hold that expert psychiatric evidence must be admissible for every forensic purpose. Arizona satisfied *Ake* by allowing Clark to present psychiatric evidence on insanity; due process does not require more.

Clark’s proposed rule would also destabilize settled state authority over the insanity defense. This Court has repeatedly recognized that insanity doctrines vary widely and that “no particular formulation” has constitutional status. *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. at 752; *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 536-37 (1968) (plurality opinion); *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 800. If the Constitution compelled States to admit mental-disease evidence on mens rea whenever the defendant chose, States could not meaningfully maintain an insanity defense with distinct evidentiary limits. Due process does not force that result. See *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 57-58.

Finally, Arizona’s rule is especially defensible because it concerns expert psychiatric testimony, not ordinary factual proof. This Court has recognized the special risk that such testimony can usurp the jury’s role or distract it from the legally relevant inquiry. *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. at 774-77. Limiting that evidence to insanity preserves the jury’s ability to decide mens rea from the surrounding circumstances and permits the State to define insanity as the exclusive channel for clinical evidence of mental disease.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed. Arizona’s rule does not deny due process; it is a permissible exercise of the State’s longstanding authority to define criminal defenses and regulate the use of psychiatric evidence at trial.
