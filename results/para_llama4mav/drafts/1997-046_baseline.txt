I. An Inmate Does Not Have a Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects against deprivations of life, liberty, or property without due process of law. However, the Supreme Court has held that a state's clemency procedure does not create a protected life or liberty interest. In Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court considered a challenge to Connecticut's pardon and commutation procedures. The Court held that the decision to grant or deny clemency is not subject to due process scrutiny, as it is not traditionally the business of courts. Id. at 464. The Court's decision in Dumschat controls here, and Ohio's clemency procedure, like Connecticut's, does not create a protected life or liberty interest.

Furthermore, the Court has distinguished between state-created liberty interests and inherent constitutional rights. In Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), the Court held that a state's creation of an appellate procedure as an integral part of its adjudicatory process triggers due process protection. However, Evitts does not extend to clemency proceedings, which are distinct from the adjudicatory process. The "great distance" between the trial and appeal stage and the executive clemency stage, as noted by Judge Nelson, suggests that clemency proceedings are not entitled to the same level of due process scrutiny.

II. The Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate an Inmate's Fifth Amendment Rights

The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination. However, Ohio's clemency procedure, as outlined in the Ohio Department of Rehabilitation and Correction regulation, does not compel an inmate to participate in an interview or answer questions. The regulation explicitly states that the inmate "shall be present at the interview" only if they request it, and that they may say "as much or as little" as they wish. This is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), that a state's disciplinary procedure did not violate an inmate's Fifth Amendment rights when the inmate was not compelled to answer questions.

Moreover, any potential risk of self-incrimination is speculative and premature, as the inmate may choose not to answer incriminating questions or may not be prosecuted for any new crimes. The district court's task on remand would be to engage in a highly theoretical assessment of the risk of self-incrimination, which is not justified at this stage. As Judge Nelson noted, the complaint does not allege any concrete risk of self-incrimination, and the regulation does not impose a waiver requirement.

In conclusion, Ohio's clemency procedure does not create a protected life or liberty interest, and the option to participate in a clemency interview does not violate an inmate's Fifth Amendment rights. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.
