ARGUMENT

I. A DEFENDANT'S TENDER TO THE NAMED PLAINTIFF CANNOT EXTINGUISH THE CLAIMS OF THE CLASS BECAUSE THE REPRESENTATIVE ACTION VESTS AN INTEREST IN THE WHOLE BODY OF PERSONS SIMILARLY SITUATED FROM THE MOMENT SUIT IS FILED.

The representative-suit doctrine did not originate with Rule 23; it is a creature of equity older than the Rule itself. In Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288 (1853), the Supreme Court sustained a suit brought by a handful of representatives on behalf of numerous, dispersed claimants precisely because "the parties interested are numerous, and it is impracticable to bring them all before the court." Id. at 303. The Court did not treat the controversy as belonging solely to the named representatives; it treated the suit as brought for the benefit of the entire class from its inception, binding absent members to the result. Id.

The Court reaffirmed this principle in Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356 (1921), holding that where members of a class are "so numerous as to make it impracticable to bring them all before the court," a suit by representative parties who "fairly represent the interest of all" proceeds for the benefit of the class as a unit. Id. at 363. The controversy belongs to the class from the outset, not merely to the individuals whose names appear in the caption.

That principle controls here. Lucero filed his complaint "on behalf of himself and all others similarly situated," Aplt.App. 10, invoking exactly the representative capacity that Smith v. Swormstedt and Ben-Hur v. Cauble recognize as vesting an interest belonging to the class as soon as the suit is commenced. BCR's post-answer tender to Lucero alone could satisfy his personal claim, but it could not retroactively unwind the representative character of the suit that these long-settled authorities say exists from filing. The district court's contrary holding, Lucero, 716 F.Supp.2d at 1097, treated the class interest as though it sprang into being only upon formal certification—a proposition foreign to the equitable tradition from which the representative action descends.

II. EVEN IF LUCERO'S PERSONAL CLAIM WERE SATISFIED, THE CONTROVERSY SURVIVES BECAUSE THE UNDERLYING DISPUTE IS CAPABLE OF REPETITION YET EVADING REVIEW.

Federal courts have long declined to dismiss as moot controversies that, by their nature, will recur but will always outrun the pace of litigation. In Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498 (1911), the Supreme Court held that a case was not moot notwithstanding the expiration of the specific order under review, because the underlying question was "capable of repetition, yet evading review." Id. at 515. The Court in St. Pierre v. United States, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), likewise recognized that a controversy otherwise moot as to the individual litigant may nonetheless demand adjudication where the issue would perpetually escape review if mootness were mechanically applied. Id. at 42-43.

The Court applied the same reasoning in Moore v. Ogilvie, 394 U.S. 814 (1969), refusing to dismiss a challenge to ballot-access requirements even though the particular election had already occurred, because the problem was "capable of repetition, yet evading review." Id. at 816.

Small-claim consumer collection suits present precisely this recurring, evanescent posture. A defendant who can tender the maximum recoverable statutory award to each successive named plaintiff before certification can perpetually avoid adjudication of the class claim, repeating the tactic in every future suit while the merits of its practices toward the class as a whole are never reviewed. That is the very evil Southern Pacific Terminal, St. Pierre, and Moore v. Ogilvie were designed to prevent. This Court should hold, consistent with those decisions, that the class controversy here is not moot.

III. BCR'S TENDER OPERATES AS A TACTICAL CESSATION OF THE CHALLENGED CONDUCT AS TO THE NAMED PLAINTIFF ONLY, WHICH DOES NOT DEPRIVE THE COURT OF THE POWER TO ADJUDICATE THE CLASS CLAIM.

In United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629 (1953), the Supreme Court held that a defendant's voluntary abandonment of challenged conduct does not automatically moot a case, because "the defendant is free to return to his old ways." Id. at 632. The Court explained that dismissal for mootness on account of a defendant's own voluntary act is appropriate only when it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." Id. at 633.

BCR's Rule 68 offer is functionally identical to the voluntary cessation at issue in W.T. Grant. It did not disclaim the challenged debt-collection practice as to the class; it merely purchased Lucero's silence as an individual, leaving BCR free to continue the identical conduct against every other member of the class Lucero sought to represent. Nothing in this record establishes, as W.T. Grant requires, that BCR's practices "could not reasonably be expected to recur" as to absent class members. Id. Under W.T. Grant's teaching, a defendant should not be permitted to manufacture jurisdictional mootness through a unilateral, self-selected act aimed only at the individual plaintiff while the class-wide wrong persists unremedied.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction and remand for further proceedings on Lucero's motion for class certification.
