ARGUMENT

I. The Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus to the Searched Premises.

A search warrant requires “a nexus … between the suspected crime and the place to be searched.” United States v. Ribeiro, 397 F.3d 43, 48 (1st Cir. 2005) (quoting United States v. Feliz, 182 F.3d 82, 88 (1st Cir. 1999)). “The critical element … is … reasonable cause to believe that the specific ‘things’ to be searched for and seized are located on the property to which entry is sought.” Zurcher v. Stanford Daily, 436 U.S. 547, 556 (1978). The affidavit supplied no such link to the parents’ home.

The only recent information was SJ’s report that Joubert had moved computer components inside the residence after learning of the investigation. That statement, standing alone, did not establish probable cause that evidence of sexual assault or exploitation would be found there. The remaining allegations concerned events years earlier at other locations and supplied no basis to believe contraband remained at the parents’ address. See United States v. Zayas-Diaz, 95 F.3d 105, 111 (1st Cir. 1996) (stale information insufficient without “facts indicating ongoing or continuous criminal activity”).

II. Admission of Uncharged Molestation Evidence Was an Abuse of Discretion.

Evidence of uncharged acts is admissible only if its probative value is not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. Fed. R. Evid. 403; United States v. Varoudakis, 233 F.3d 113, 118-19 (1st Cir. 2000). The testimony concerning decades-old, uncharged conduct against SJ bore minimal similarity to the charged offenses and created a substantial risk that the jury would convict on the basis of propensity. See United States v. Gilbert, 229 F.3d 15, 24 (1st Cir. 2000).

III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Authorize These Convictions.

Congress may not regulate noneconomic, intrastate activity lacking a substantial effect on interstate commerce. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 561 (1995). Sections 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) as applied to purely local conduct exceed that limit. See United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 617-18 (2000).

IV. The 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable.

A sentence must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary,” to achieve the purposes of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 50 (2007). The district court’s sentence, driven by uncharged conduct and exceeding the guidelines range by more than a decade, failed to account for Joubert’s age, health, and lack of recent criminal history. See United States v. Rivera-Moreno, 613 F.3d 1, 10 (1st Cir. 2010).
