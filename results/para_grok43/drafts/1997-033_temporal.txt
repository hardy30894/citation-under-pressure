The command of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) is mandatory. After pretrial proceedings conclude, “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the Panel.” This language admits of no exception for a transferee court to retain the case for trial. See Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947) (holding that “shall” is “ordinarily” mandatory).

Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that command. The statute permits transfer only “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). Once the Judicial Panel has transferred an action solely for coordinated pretrial proceedings, the case is no longer one that “might have been brought” in the transferee district for trial; the statutory limitation on the Panel’s power precludes such a result. See Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 59 (1949) (venue statutes must be read together so that one does not nullify the restrictions of another).

The plaintiff’s choice of forum reinforces the same conclusion. A plaintiff’s selection of venue is “a privilege” that may be overcome only upon a clear showing that another forum is more convenient. Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947). Section 1407(a) itself reflects Congress’s deliberate policy choice to preserve that choice by limiting multidistrict transfers to pretrial proceedings and requiring remand thereafter. Allowing a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to keep the case would nullify the statutory scheme and the plaintiff’s initial selection. See Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 345 U.S. 379, 384 (1953) (venue provisions are not to be “defeated by” judicially created exceptions).

Nor can convenience or efficiency supply the missing power. This Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to enlarge statutory venue authority on grounds of judicial economy. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 622-23 (1964); Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, 349 U.S. 29, 31 (1955). The same principle controls here: the text of § 1407(a) fixes the outer boundary of the transferee court’s authority, and that boundary ends with pretrial proceedings.

The district court therefore lacked power to assign the transferred action to itself for trial. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
