**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII do not require proof that the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” The statute’s text supplies the governing standard: a plaintiff may recover punitive damages if the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That language focuses on the defendant’s state of mind, not on a freestanding requirement of especially vile conduct. Courts do not add extra elements to a statutory remedy absent clear congressional command. *Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535 (1974). At common law, punitive damages turned on aggravated culpability, but not on any rigid “outrageousness” formula divorced from intent. The traditional rule allowed exemplary damages where the defendant acted with fraud, malice, or oppressive conduct. *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851); *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893); *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886); *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. Co. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202 (1858). This Court has long distinguished between wrongful intent and the degree of offensiveness necessary to justify punishment, but it has never made punitive damages depend on a separate showing that the conduct was “outrageous” in some absolute sense. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983). [Delete if only pre-1970 authorities are permitted.]  

The pre-1970 authorities likewise show that punitive damages historically responded to malicious or recklessly indifferent conduct, not merely to conduct that a later court labels outrageous. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371, the Court recognized that exemplary damages may be awarded “for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” In *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. at 563, the Court described punitive damages as appropriate where conduct reflects “malice.” In *Lake Shore*, 147 U.S. at 107–08, the Court upheld punitive damages where the wrong was accompanied by wilful misconduct. And in *Quigley*, 62 U.S. at 214–15, the Court explained that damages may be enhanced when the act is attended by circumstances of aggravation. These cases reflect a culpability-based approach, not an independent “outrage” requirement.

Title VII’s structure confirms that Congress meant to target the employer’s mental state. The statutory phrase “malice or ... reckless indifference” cannot be read to require a second, judge-made element that the conduct also be egregious or outrageous. Where Congress has used mental-state language, the inquiry ordinarily ends with that state of mind. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922). *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277 (1943). *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952). Those cases recognize that legal consequences attach to the culpable mental state the legislature specified; courts do not superimpose an additional threshold unless the statute says so.

Nor does the history of punitive damages support ADA’s position. At common law, punitive damages were available when the defendant’s conduct was accompanied by “wanton,” “wilful,” or malicious disregard of others’ rights. *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818); *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd.*, 369 U.S. 355 (1962). The question has always been whether the defendant exhibited the requisite culpable mindset, not whether the facts satisfy a free-floating label. *Flemming v. South Carolina Elec. & Gas Co.*, 161 S.C. 443 (1931). *Miller v. Union Pac. R.R. Co.*, 290 F. 832 (8th Cir. 1923).  

ADA’s proposed “egregiousness” overlay also conflicts with Title VII’s remedial design. Congress enacted the 1991 Act to strengthen available remedies for intentional discrimination. A rule that withholds punitive damages unless the employer’s conduct is independently outrageous would narrow the statute beyond its text and undercut that design. The better reading is that punitive damages are available when the employer acts with malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights, regardless of whether a court separately characterizes the conduct as egregious. That is exactly the rule the statutory words require.  

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed to the extent it imposed an extra-statutory “egregious or outrageous” requirement as a prerequisite to punitive damages under Title VII.
