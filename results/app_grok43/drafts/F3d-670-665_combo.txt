ARGUMENT

The writ of habeas corpus issues to prevent fundamental injustice where a prisoner demonstrates actual innocence. Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 93 (1807) (Marshall, C.J.) (“the great object of [the writ] is the liberation of those who may be imprisoned without sufficient cause”). Courts of equity have long relieved against strict application of time bars when enforcement would work manifest injustice. See Penn v. Lord Baltimore, 1 Ves. Sen. 444, 27 Eng. Rep. 1132 (Ch. 1750); see also Ex parte Watkins, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202 (1830) (habeas reaches “cases of palpable error”).

A credible showing that another committed the crime constitutes such injustice. Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 334 (1915) (due process violated where “the whole proceeding is a mask”); Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923) (mob-dominated trial “void” despite procedural regularity). Perjured testimony by the true culprit likewise voids the conviction. Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (per curiam) (“a conviction obtained through the knowing use of perjured testimony” denies due process).

The writ’s equitable character permits tolling of limitations when innocence is shown. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938) (habeas “safeguard[s] … fundamental rights”); Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 105 (1942) (per curiam) (writ available “where the conviction has been obtained in violation of any constitutional right”). No additional showing of post-conviction diligence is required once the constitutional violation appears on the face of the record. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 458 (1953) (habeas “tests … the very truth of the matter”); Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963) (“the federal habeas corpus statute … vests in the federal courts … the duty to afford the petitioner a full and fair hearing”).

The affidavits here, if credited, establish that Jones—not Perkins—committed the homicide. That evidence was unavailable at trial and directly negates guilt. Under the authorities above, such a showing warrants equitable relief from the statute of limitations without further inquiry into diligence. The district court therefore erred in requiring reasonable diligence as a precondition to considering the merits.
