**ARGUMENT**

A federal habeas court may not consider a claim that a state prisoner procedurally defaulted in state court unless the prisoner demonstrates cause for the default and actual prejudice from the alleged violation of federal law. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991); *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause, but only if the prisoner first establishes that the ineffective-assistance claim itself was not defaulted or that cause and prejudice excuse its default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986).

The Sixth Circuit held that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may nevertheless serve as cause for the default of Carpenter’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. That conclusion conflicts with *Coleman* and *Carrier*. There, the Court explained that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488-89. Allowing an unexhausted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would permit prisoners to circumvent state procedural rules simply by alleging attorney error, undermining the “independent and adequate state ground” doctrine. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 729.

The court of appeals’ rule also ignores the requirement that cause must be external to the defense. *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488. A prisoner’s failure to present an ineffective-assistance claim in the manner prescribed by state law is not external; it is the very default that must be justified. *See Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446, 451 (2000) (noting that the Sixth Circuit’s approach would “render the exhaustion requirement . . . a nullity”).

Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself dismissed as untimely under Ohio law, and he has shown neither cause nor prejudice for that default, the claim cannot excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency claim. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
