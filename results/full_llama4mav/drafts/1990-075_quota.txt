The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), allows Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 372 (quoting J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, listing specific factors to consider when scheduling substances, including the substance's potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). This guidance satisfies the "intelligible principle" test, as seen in cases such as Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), where the Court upheld a delegation of power to the Price Administrator to regulate prices during World War II.

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is also constitutional. Although it grants the Attorney General broad authority to temporarily schedule substances, it requires consideration of specific factors, including the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This provision is similar to others upheld by the Court, such as the delegation of power to the Federal Sentencing Commission in Mistretta. The fact that the temporary scheduling provision is subject to limited judicial review, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), does not render it unconstitutional, as the Court has held that the availability of judicial review is not a dispositive factor in determining the constitutionality of a delegation. See Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952).

The Attorney General's delegation of authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also authorized. The CSA grants the Attorney General the power to delegate his functions to other officials, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and the Attorney General has properly exercised this authority by delegating the temporary scheduling power to the DEA Administrator. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This sub-delegation is consistent with the Court's holding in United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505 (1974), that the Attorney General has broad authority to delegate his functions under the CSA.

The Toubys' argument that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the Attorney General to create crimes is unpersuasive. The Court has upheld delegations of power that involve the creation of crimes, such as in Yakus, where the Price Administrator was authorized to regulate prices and impose criminal penalties for violations. The CSA's delegation of power to the Attorney General is similarly constitutional, as it is guided by intelligible principles and subject to various checks and balances.

Other courts have considered the constitutionality of the CSA's delegation of power and have upheld it. See, e.g., United States v. Emerson, 846 F.2d 541 (9th Cir. 1988); United States v. Spain, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987). The Third Circuit's decision in this case is consistent with these holdings, and the Toubys' convictions should be affirmed.

The Supreme Court has consistently upheld broad delegations of power, as seen in cases such as Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), and Lichter v. United States, 334 U.S. 742 (1948). In Fahey, the Court upheld a delegation of power to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to regulate savings and loan associations, despite the lack of specific guidance. In Lichter, the Court upheld a delegation of power to the War Department to recover excessive profits made by contractors during World War II. These cases demonstrate the Court's willingness to uphold delegations of power that are guided by intelligible principles, even if they are broad in scope.

In conclusion, the CSA's delegation of power to the Attorney General is constitutional, as it is guided by intelligible principles and subject to various checks and balances. The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the DEA is also authorized, and the Toubys' convictions should be affirmed.
