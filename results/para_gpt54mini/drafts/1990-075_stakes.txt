**ARGUMENT**

I. **Congress did not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General.**

The Controlled Substances Act satisfies the nondelegation doctrine because Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s exercise of the temporary-scheduling power. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989). The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld broad delegations where Congress identifies the policy, the relevant factors, and the limits of the delegated authority. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79; *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420-27 (1944).

Section 811(h) does precisely that. Congress authorized temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), and required the Attorney General to consider specified factors drawn from the statute’s abuse-and-risk criteria. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3), incorporating § 811(c)(4), (5), and (6). Congress further limited the temporary order to one year, with one possible six-month extension, and required notice in the Federal Register and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4), (2). Those constraints are materially comparable to other delegations upheld by the Court. See *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420-27; *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947); *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79.

Petitioners rely on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), but those decisions involved sweeping delegations with no meaningful standards governing the executive’s choice. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415-30; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 521-51. Here, by contrast, Congress cabined the Attorney General’s discretion with a concrete trigger—an “imminent hazard to the public safety”—and with specific statutory factors and temporal limits. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). Under *Mistretta*, that is enough. 488 U.S. at 372-79.

Nor is the fact that temporary scheduling has criminal consequences constitutionally decisive. The Court has upheld delegations whose exercise affects criminal liability where Congress itself established the substantive policy and standards. *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420-27; *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521-31 (1911). Congress may authorize an executive officer to “fill up the details” so long as Congress supplies the governing law. *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 517. That is what Congress did here.

II. **The Attorney General was authorized to subdelegate temporary-scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator.**

The Controlled Substances Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions to subordinates. Section 871(a) provides that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Supreme Court has recognized that such a delegation statute authorizes subdelegation unless Congress indicates otherwise. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 512-23 (1974); *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 694-96 (1974). Where Congress has affirmatively empowered the Attorney General to delegate his functions, and where the subdelegatee remains within the Department, the subdelegation is valid unless the statute clearly limits it. *Giordano*, 416 U.S. at 514-23.

Nothing in § 811(h) withdraws the general delegation authority conferred by § 871(a). Section 811(h) identifies who may act, but it does not state that the Attorney General alone must personally perform the temporary-scheduling function. Congress knew how to require personal action when it wished to do so, and it did not do so here. See *Nixon*, 418 U.S. at 694-96. The Attorney General therefore acted within his statutory authority when he delegated temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, an officer within the Department of Justice. See 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b); *Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 759, 767-69 (3d Cir. 1990).

III. **The Third Circuit correctly upheld the temporary scheduling of Euphoria.**

The court of appeals properly recognized that § 811(h)(6)’s bar on judicial review does not foreclose constitutional review, but that petitioners’ facial nondelegation challenge fails on the merits. *Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 759, 762-67 (3d Cir. 1990). The Supreme Court presumes judicial review absent clear congressional intent to preclude it, particularly on constitutional questions. *Webster v. Doe*, 486 U.S. 592, 603-05 (1988); *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). But once review is available, the statute still must be judged under settled nondelegation principles, and those principles are satisfied here. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-79; *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 420-27.

Because Congress supplied an intelligible principle and meaningful limits, and because the Attorney General’s subdelegation was authorized by statute, the judgment below should be affirmed.
