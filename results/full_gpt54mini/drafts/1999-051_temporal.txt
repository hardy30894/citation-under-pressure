**ARGUMENT**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim as “cause” to excuse the default of another claim. This Court has long held that federal habeas review is barred when the prisoner has failed to comply with an adequate and independent state procedural rule, unless the prisoner can show cause and prejudice. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). And the cause inquiry itself is bounded by the ordinary rules of procedural default: a claim asserted as cause must have been properly presented to the state courts, or it too is defaulted. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 489 (1986), explained that “the exhaustion doctrine... generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” When the alleged cause is itself forfeited under state law, federal courts do not excuse one default with another.

That rule follows directly from the structure of habeas doctrine and from this Court’s repeated insistence on comity and finality. A federal court may not adjudicate claims that the state courts have had no fair opportunity to resolve. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963), recognized that the habeas writ does not license “deliberate bypass” of state procedures. Likewise, *Wainwright* made clear that a prisoner who fails to adhere to a state contemporaneous-objection rule cannot obtain federal review absent a showing that respects state procedural law. 433 U.S. at 87. If a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim itself could always serve as cause, then a petitioner could evade state rules simply by relabeling a defaulted claim as attorney error. That would nullify the very default doctrine *Wainwright* preserved.

The Court’s ineffective-assistance cases confirm the point. In *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964), the Court recognized that constitutional errors by counsel can matter on habeas, but only when properly presented and supported. And in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), the Court emphasized that federal habeas is not an alternative forum for bypassing state processes. Nothing in those cases suggests that a prisoner may rely on an ineffective-assistance theory never fairly exhausted in state court to resurrect another defaulted ground for relief. To the contrary, *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443 (1965), held that a procedural default may be excused only where the state has had a fair chance to pass on the issue and the default does not serve a legitimate state interest. That rationale is incompatible with treating an unexhausted ineffective-assistance claim as automatic cause.

Nor does the Sixth Circuit’s rule comport with *Fay*’s understanding of habeas waiver. *Fay* treated federal relief as unavailable where the petitioner’s own litigation choices amounted to a deliberate abandonment of state remedies. 372 U.S. at 438-39. A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim is no different in principle: it is itself a claim the state court was not asked to decide through the required procedure. To permit it to do double duty as cause would let a petitioner profit from the very default that bars the claim, contradicting the settled rule that federal habeas does not reward procedural noncompliance. See *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 87; *Henry*, 379 U.S. at 447-48.

The better rule is simple and administrable: only a properly preserved ineffective-assistance claim may serve as cause to excuse another default. That rule preserves the State’s interest in enforcing its own procedures, respects the exhaustion requirement, and prevents habeas from becoming a vehicle for end-runs around state adjudication. Because respondent’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself procedurally defaulted in state court, it cannot supply cause for the default of the underlying sufficiency claim. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed on the ground that the underlying claim remains barred.

If the Court reaches the merits of cause and prejudice, the result is the same. Cause requires an objective factor external to the defense that impeded compliance with the state rule. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488. A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim, standing alone, is not such an external factor; it is simply another claim of constitutional error subject to the same state-law forfeiture. And prejudice requires more than speculation; the petitioner must show “actual and substantial disadvantage.” *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 170 (1982). The record here does not establish that respondent’s defaulted appellate-ineffectiveness claim, even if considered, meets that demanding standard.

Accordingly, this Court should reject petitioner’s attempt to bootstrap one defaulted claim into cause for another and should affirm the denial of habeas relief.
