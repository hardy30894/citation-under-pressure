**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not expressly govern employer polling of employee support for an incumbent union. The Board, however, has crafted a standard that permits polling only when an employer already possesses “sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition from the union.” *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984). This standard renders polling a nullity. If an employer already has evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition, it has no need to poll; it may simply cease bargaining. The Board’s rule thus forces employers into an all-or-nothing choice: either withdraw recognition based on incomplete evidence, risking an unfair labor practice finding, or forgo any inquiry into the union’s actual support.

The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have correctly rejected this approach. Those courts hold that an employer may poll employees if it possesses “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant*, 736 F.2d at 1299. This standard gives meaning to the poll as a “useful and legitimate tool for the employer who is in sincere doubt of the union’s majority status.” *Mingtree Restaurant*, 736 F.2d at 1299.

The Board’s contrary rule is internally inconsistent. The Board itself acknowledges that “RM elections” are the “preferred method for testing employee support of the union.” *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). Yet the Board applies the same evidentiary standard for polling as for RM elections. If RM elections are preferred, the standard for polling—a less formal, unsupervised procedure—should be *more* stringent, not identical. As the D.C. Circuit observed below, the Board’s reasoning “lead[s] squarely to the conclusion that the polling standard should be more stringent than that for RM elections.” *Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB*, 83 F.3d 1483, 1487 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (quoting Joan Flynn, *A Triple Standard at the NLRB: Employer Challenges to an Incumbent Union*, 1991 Wis. L. Rev. 653, 660). The Board’s failure to reconcile this anomaly renders its standard arbitrary.

Moreover, the Board’s standard undermines the Act’s goal of promoting stable collective bargaining. An employer who doubts a union’s majority status faces a dilemma: bargain with a union that may lack majority support, violating § 8(a)(2), or refuse to bargain, risking a § 8(a)(5) violation. *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867. Polling offers a safe harbor—a way to resolve doubt without legal risk. The Board’s rule eliminates that safe harbor, forcing employers to act on incomplete evidence. This Court should not defer to an agency interpretation that defeats the statute’s purpose. *See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (Board’s interpretation must be “rational and consistent with the Act”).

**II. The Board’s Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

Even under the Board’s flawed standard, its factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The record contains overwhelming, unrefuted evidence that the union had lost majority support.

First, seven employees made direct statements repudiating the union. The Board discounted these statements on grounds that are arbitrary and inconsistent with its own precedent. For example, the Board refused to credit employee Mike Ridgick’s statement that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union,” because it was made during a job interview. But the Board’s own precedent holds that such statements are reliable when the employer has not created a coercive atmosphere. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), *enforced*, 590 F.2d 4 (5th Cir. 1979). Here, there is no allegation of coercion. Similarly, the Board discounted employee Dennis Marsh’s statement that he was “not getting his money’s worth” from union dues, characterizing it as mere dissatisfaction. Yet dissatisfaction with representation is a classic indicator of lost support. *See Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995).

Second, the Board ignored evidence of widespread anti-union sentiment among the workforce. Employee Kermit Bloch reported that the entire night shift—five or six employees—did not want the union. The Board rejected this as hearsay, but hearsay is admissible in NLRB proceedings and may constitute substantial evidence. *See NLRB v. Walton Manufacturing Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 408 (1962). Employee Ron Mohr, the union steward, told management that if a vote were taken, the union would lose. The Board dismissed this as an “off-the-cuff” remark, but Mohr was in a unique position to gauge employee sentiment.

Third, the Board’s reliance on the date of January 25, 1991, as the relevant date for assessing doubt is arbitrary. The poll was conducted on February 1, 1991. The Board offered no explanation for why statements made between January 25 and February 1 were irrelevant. This rigid line excludes probative evidence without justification.

Finally, the Board’s finding ignores the poll results themselves. Nineteen employees voted against the union; only 13 voted in favor. This 19-to-13 vote is powerful evidence that the union lacked majority support. The Board’s refusal to consider the poll results as confirmation of the company’s doubt is irrational. *See Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board where “overwhelming unrefuted evidence” showed loss of support).

In sum, the Board’s factual finding is not supported by substantial evidence when viewed in light of the entire record. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Court should reverse the judgment below and deny enforcement of the Board’s order.
