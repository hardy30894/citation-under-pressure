# ARGUMENT

## I. Guam's Section 107 Cost-Recovery Claim Is Timely Because the 2004 Consent Decree Did Not "Resolve" Guam's CERCLA Liability

CERCLA provides two distinct paths to recovery: a six-year cost-recovery action under Section 107(a), and a three-year contribution action under Section 113(f). *See* 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607, 9613(f); *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004). Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only where a party "has resolved its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action" in "an administrative or judicially approved settlement." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 Consent Decree did no such thing. It resolved a Clean Water Act enforcement action—not a CERCLA liability determination—and it expressly disclaimed any "finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." Consent Decree at 3, J.A. 92.

This Court has recognized that CERCLA's remedial scheme must be construed to further its central purpose: ensuring that "responsible parties foot the bill" for hazardous waste cleanup. *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). That purpose is defeated, not advanced, by treating a Clean Water Act consent decree—silent on CERCLA liability and expressly disclaiming any admission of fault—as triggering a CERCLA contribution action that expired before Guam could reasonably have known its rights had accrued.

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the Decree's substantive obligations—closing the dump, designing a cover system—necessarily "resolved" liability within the meaning of Section 113(f)(3)(B), notwithstanding the Decree's explicit disclaimer. This Court has cautioned against interpretations of CERCLA that produce results Congress could not have intended. *Cf. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. at 55 (CERCLA responds to "serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution" and should be construed to serve that remedial purpose). Reading a Clean Water Act settlement, entered without any CERCLA cause of action pending, without any finding of liability, and addressing statutory violations under an entirely different environmental regime, as silently triggering and then extinguishing a CERCLA contribution right is precisely the kind of technical, purpose-defeating construction this Court should reject.

## II. The Consent Decree's Express Disclaimer of Liability Forecloses Section 113(f)(3)(B) Treatment

The plain terms of the Consent Decree state that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." Consent Decree at 3, J.A. 92. Courts have long recognized that such disclaimers matter to the Section 113(f)(3)(B) inquiry. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (concluding a consent decree did not resolve liability where "the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability").

The court below acknowledged that "a disclaimer of liability may weigh against the conclusion that the parties intended to resolve liability," but nonetheless discounted the disclaimer as insufficient standing alone. This approach inverts the proper inquiry. Where, as here, the underlying action arose under an entirely separate statute—the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA—and the settling parties expressly reserved judgment on liability, there is no principled basis for treating the settlement as CERCLA's triggering mechanism. This Court should hold that an express disclaimer of liability, combined with a settlement's failure to arise under CERCLA in the first instance, forecloses application of Section 113(f)(3)(B).

## III. Equitable and Statutory Considerations Favor Guam's Position

Congress structured CERCLA's dual remedies to encourage settlement while preserving injured parties' ability to recover cleanup costs. *See Cooper Industries*, 543 U.S. at 167 (Section 113 provides "two express avenues for contribution" designed to incentivize settlement). Interpreting an ambiguous, non-CERCLA settlement as retroactively triggering—and then barring—a contribution claim undermines this framework. It punishes parties like Guam for settling environmental disputes promptly, discouraging the very cooperative resolution CERCLA seeks to promote.

Guam operated in a unique posture: a former U.S. possession that inherited a dump built and used for decades by the Navy for military waste disposal, including alleged munitions and dangerous chemicals such as DDT and Agent Orange. The Navy was designated a potentially responsible party in 1988, yet the United States now seeks to escape liability through a limitations technicality arising from a settlement that never mentioned CERCLA, never adjudicated liability, and predates any indication that Guam's rights against the Navy were even in question.

This Court has recognized that statutory limitations periods must be applied according to their terms, *see Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia*, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958), but that principle cuts in Guam's favor: Section 113(f)(3)(B)'s limitations period cannot run from a settlement that never triggered the statute in the first place. Because the 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve CERCLA liability, Guam's Section 107 cost-recovery action—filed well within the six-year window—remains timely.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the D.C. Circuit and hold that Guam may proceed under CERCLA Section 107(a).
