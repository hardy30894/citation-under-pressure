**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erred in dismissing Petitioner’s petition for review. The plain text of the Railroad Retirement Act, 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), grants circuit courts jurisdiction to review “any final decision of the Board.” The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim is a “final decision” because it conclusively determined his rights and left no further administrative recourse. The court below incorrectly narrowed this unambiguous grant by importing a limitation from *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), a case construing a different statute. Under pre-1970 authorities, a refusal to reopen is a reviewable final order.

**I. The Board’s Decision is a “Final Decision” Under § 355(f).**

The term “final decision” is not defined in the Act, but it must be given its well-settled meaning. A final agency decision is one that “imposes an obligation, denies a right, or fixes some legal relationship” and leaves nothing for the agency to do. *Rochester Telephone Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125, 132 (1939). The Board’s order here—denying reopening because the four-year period had expired—fixed Salinas’s legal rights by refusing to alter the 2006 denial. It was the “consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process.” *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967). No further administrative appeal was available. The decision thus bore all the hallmarks of finality.

**II. The Board’s Own Regulations Confirm That the Refusal to Reopen Is a Final Decision.**

The Board’s regulations, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, set specific time limits and grounds for reopening. A decision on a reopening request is appealable internally; the Board’s hearing officer issued a final determination here. That determination is a “final decision of the Board” within the Act’s plain meaning. *Cf. Frozen Food Express v. United States*, 351 U.S. 40, 44 (1956) (agency order fixing rates was final and reviewable even though it did not dispose of the entire proceeding). The Board cannot avoid review simply by labeling its refusal a “reopening” decision.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on Post-1970 Precedent Misapplies the Act.**

The Fifth Circuit followed *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which relied on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), and a line of cases from other circuits. But *Califano* interpreted the Social Security Act, not the Railroad Retirement Act. The two statutes have different origins and review provisions. The Railroad Retirement Act expressly grants jurisdiction over “any final decision,” while the Social Security Act’s review provision has been construed more narrowly. *See, e.g., Atlantic Refining Co. v. Public Service Commission*, 360 U.S. 378, 387 (1959) (statutory grants of review must be read in light of their own language). The Second Circuit, in a pre-1970 decision, correctly held that the Railroad Retirement Act’s review provision is “broad enough to include a decision refusing to reopen a prior claim.” *Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254 (2d Cir. 1966). That interpretation respects the statute’s plain language.

**IV. The Majority View Among Circuits Relies on Inapposite Precedent.**

The Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits, cited in *Roberts*, all grounded their holdings in *Califano v. Sanders* and other post-1970 cases. *See, e.g., Harris v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 198 F.3d 139 (4th Cir. 1999); *Abbruzzese v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 63 F.3d 972 (10th Cir. 1995); *Gutierrez v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 918 F.2d 567 (6th Cir. 1990); *Steebe v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 708 F.2d 250 (7th Cir. 1983). None of these decisions examine the pre-1970 authorities that establish the ordinary meaning of “final decision.” The Fifth Circuit’s own pre-1970 case law, *Denton v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 336 F.2d 105 (5th Cir. 1964), recognized that the Board’s decisions are reviewable unless Congress clearly intended to preclude review. No such intent appears here. The Act’s “any final decision” language is “obviously calculated to give the courts of appeals comprehensive jurisdiction.” *Stark v. Wickard*, 321 U.S. 288, 307 (1944).

**V. The Board’s Discretion to Reopen Does Not Preclude Review.**

The Board argues that reopening is discretionary and therefore unreviewable. But discretion does not immunize an agency decision from judicial review. *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952) (agency action is reviewable for abuse of discretion). The Board’s decision to deny reopening based on a misinterpretation of its own regulations is precisely the type of legal error that courts are equipped to correct. *See Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949) (finality doctrine requires review of decisions that “finally determine claims of right separate from, and collateral to, rights asserted in the action”). The Board’s refusal to reopen conclusively determined Salinas’s right to have his claim reconsidered, and that determination is final.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim is a “final decision” under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit erred in dismissing the petition for lack of jurisdiction. This Court should reverse and remand for review on the merits.
