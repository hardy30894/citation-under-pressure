## ARGUMENT

### I. The district court abused its discretion by striking four experts as a discovery sanction.

A district court may impose sanctions for discovery noncompliance, but dismissal-like sanctions must be tied to a clear record of delay or contumacious conduct and consideration of lesser measures. *National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc.*, 427 U.S. 639, 642-43 (1976); *Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs., Inc.*, 765 F.2d 511, 514-15 (5th Cir. 1985); *Malautea v. Suzuki Motor Co.*, 987 F.2d 1536, 1542-44 (11th Cir. 1993). This Court has emphasized that sanctions under Rule 37 must be just and proportionate to the violation. *Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs., Inc.*, 765 F.2d 511, 514 (5th Cir. 1985); *Chrysler Corp. v. Blackmon*, 841 F.2d 1253, 1259-60 (5th Cir. 1988).

Here, the district court struck four experts after Plaintiffs failed to complete depositions by July 30, 1993. But the record shows a scheduling dispute over whether the experts were prepared to render final opinions, followed by Plaintiffs’ request for a protective order and the court’s own directive that depositions be completed by a date certain. The court did not first consider lesser sanctions, nor did it make findings sufficient to justify the effective preclusion of critical expert testimony. That is especially problematic where the sanction eliminated the proof necessary to survive summary judgment. See *Batson*, 765 F.2d at 514-15; *Shepherd v. American Broadcasting Cos., Inc.*, 62 F.3d 1469, 1478-79 (D.C. Cir. 1995).

The Supreme Court has cautioned that the sanction must be commensurate with the misconduct and that the judicial system favors decisions on the merits. *National Hockey League*, 427 U.S. at 643; *Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee*, 456 U.S. 694, 707 (1982). Because the exclusion order here functioned as a case-dispositive sanction without the requisite showing, it was an abuse of discretion. See *Batson*, 765 F.2d at 514-15; *Chrysler Corp.*, 841 F.2d at 1261.

### II. The court erred in excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under Daubert.

Under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589-95 (1993), expert testimony is admissible if it is both relevant and reliable, and the district court’s gatekeeping inquiry must remain flexible. *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 141-42 (1999). The Court in *Daubert* rejected rigid tests in favor of an inquiry focused on scientific validity and “fit.” 509 U.S. at 592-93. This Court reviews the admissibility of expert testimony for abuse of discretion, but that discretion is not boundless. *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 141-43 (1997); *Moore v. Ashland Chemical Inc.*, 151 F.3d 269, 276 (5th Cir. 1998) (en banc).

The record here does not show that Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson offered opinions so speculative or methodologically unsound that exclusion was warranted under *Daubert*. Rather, the district court appears to have conflated incompleteness in discovery with unreliability in methodology. That is not the *Daubert* standard. Reliability addresses the principles and methods employed, not whether the expert had satisfied the court’s scheduling expectations. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 592-93; *Kumho Tire*, 526 U.S. at 149-52.

Because exclusion of these experts eliminated the only evidence supporting causation, the court was required to apply the reliability inquiry carefully and not as a substitute for discovery enforcement. *Joiner*, 522 U.S. at 146; *Moore*, 151 F.3d at 276-77. The order excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson should therefore be reversed.

### III. Summary judgment cannot stand because the evidentiary rulings were erroneous and because genuine disputes remained.

Summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-24 (1986). If the district court’s exclusion orders were erroneous, the summary judgment necessarily falls, because Defendants’ motions depended on the absence of admissible expert proof. See *Little v. Liquid Air Corp.*, 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir. 1994) (en banc); *Topalian v. Ehrman*, 954 F.2d 1125, 1131 (5th Cir. 1992).

Even apart from the expert rulings, the court was required to view the evidence in the light most favorable to Appellants and draw all reasonable inferences in their favor. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255; *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150-51 (2000). The claims at issue involve exposure, causation, and damages in a toxic-tort setting—questions that commonly turn on disputed expert and circumstantial proof. *Borel v. Fibreboard Paper Products Corp.*, 493 F.2d 1076, 1084-87 (5th Cir. 1973); *Wicker v. Conoco, Inc.*, 868 F.2d 1313, 1318-20 (5th Cir. 1989). The district court’s rulings prevented that proof from being considered and thereby short-circuited the merits.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
