**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional limitation on executive power: the prohibition against indefinite, non-punitive detention without a reasonable prospect of removal. The Fifth Circuit’s initial conclusion that indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is lawful so long as the Government makes “good faith efforts” to deport the alien, regardless of the alien’s actual prospects of removal, is irreconcilable with the Constitution and this Court’s holding in *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491 (2001). That decision, which this Court has now made definitive, establishes that the statute does not authorize indefinite detention and that continued confinement beyond a “reasonable time” is unconstitutional when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that “the statute [§ 1231(a)(6)], read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal from the United States. It does not permit indefinite detention.” Id. at 2498. This construction is not merely a matter of statutory interpretation; it is a constitutional imperative. The Court emphasized that the statute “does not apply narrowly to ‘a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals’... but broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations.” Id. at 2499. Detention for such a broad class of individuals—many of whom are not threats to public safety—cannot be justified by the mere possibility of future removal. To allow indefinite detention under such circumstances would transform the immigration system into a mechanism of indefinite administrative confinement, violating the core principle of due process.

The Court further clarified the habeas court’s role: “The habeas court must ask whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. It should measure reasonableness primarily in terms of the statute’s basic purpose, namely assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” Id. at 2504. This standard is not satisfied by the Government’s mere assertion of “good faith efforts.” As the Court noted, “the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing” once the alien demonstrates “good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future.” Id. at 2505. The burden is not on the alien to prove impossibility of removal, but on the Government to demonstrate that removal remains reasonably foreseeable.

In this case, Zadvydas has been in custody for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him. His reapplication for Lithuanian citizenship remains pending, but no country has indicated a willingness to receive him. The Government has not presented any evidence that removal is reasonably foreseeable in the near term. Indeed, the Court in *Zadvydas* observed that “as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink.” Id. at 2505. After more than three years, the “reasonably foreseeable future” has effectively vanished. The Government’s continued detention of Zadvydas, therefore, exceeds any reasonable time necessary to secure removal and violates the constitutional limits established in *Zadvydas*.

The Fifth Circuit’s prior opinion, which held that detention was lawful so long as “good faith efforts to effectuate deportation continue,” misapprehended the Court’s holding. The Supreme Court explicitly rejected that standard, stating that “this standard would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable— which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” Id. at 2505. The Court did not require the alien to prove impossibility; it required the Government to show that removal remains reasonably foreseeable. The Government has failed to meet that burden.

The Court’s decision in *Zadvydas* was not a narrow ruling on a single case. It was a constitutional command: “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court’s order of release, based on a standard that effectively permits indefinite detention, is inconsistent with that command. The Court must now affirm the district court’s judgment, which correctly applied the constitutional standard established in *Zadvydas*.

In sum, the post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite confinement. The Constitution demands that detention be limited to a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. When removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable—particularly after more than three years of detention—continued confinement is unlawful. The judgment of the district court must be affirmed.
