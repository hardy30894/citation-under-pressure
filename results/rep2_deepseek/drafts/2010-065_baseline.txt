**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Criminal Defendant Lacks Standing to Invoke the Tenth Amendment as a Shield Against a Validly Enacted Federal Statute.**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States powers not delegated to the United States. U.S. Const. amend. X. But that structural protection is a limit on federal authority vis-à-vis the States themselves, not a source of individual rights that a private party may assert. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). In *Tennessee Electric*, the Court held that private utility companies “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* That holding is binding and dispositive here.

The Third Circuit correctly recognized that *Tennessee Electric* forecloses Bond’s Tenth Amendment challenge. *Bond v. United States*, 581 F.3d 128, 135 (3d Cir. 2009). Bond is a private individual, not a State. She does not represent Pennsylvania or any other sovereign. Her claim that § 229 intrudes on state prerogatives is an “undifferentiated, generalized grievance” that does not confer standing. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005). As the First Circuit explained, “the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes.” *Id.* at 35.

Bond’s attempt to distinguish *Tennessee Electric* as “dicta” fails. The Supreme Court dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both standing and the merits, making the standing holding an alternative ground, not obiter dictum. *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978). Moreover, because standing implicates Article III jurisdiction, the Court’s resolution of that question predominates. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998).

Every circuit to squarely address the issue after *Tennessee Electric* has held that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims. *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33-36; *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). The Third Circuit correctly joined this consensus.

**II. Even If Bond Had Standing, § 229 Is a Valid Exercise of Congress’s Treaty Power.**

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and Congress acted within its Article I authority when it enacted implementing legislation. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). In *Holland*, the Court upheld a statute implementing a migratory bird treaty, rejecting a Tenth Amendment challenge. *Id.* at 435. The Court reasoned that “if the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” *Id.* at 432.

The same logic applies here. The Convention requires each signatory to “adopt the necessary measures to implement its obligations,” including “enacting penal legislation.” 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993). Section 229 is a “necessary and proper” means to fulfill that obligation. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18. As the Second Circuit held in *United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998), “if the treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”

Bond’s conduct—repeatedly using toxic chemicals to poison another person—falls squarely within the statute’s prohibition. The statute is not vague as applied to her. *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). And the treaty power is not limited to matters of “interstate commerce” or other enumerated powers. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 433. As the Court observed, “there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
