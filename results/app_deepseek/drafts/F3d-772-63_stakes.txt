## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in dismissing Foley’s state-law claims. This Court reviews a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal *de novo*, accepting all well-pleaded facts as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat*, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014). Foley’s complaint plausibly alleges that Wells Fargo breached duties owed to him under the class-action settlement agreement and violated Massachusetts statutory and common law.

### I. Foley Stated a Claim for Breach of Contract as a Third-Party Beneficiary

Under Massachusetts law, a person who is not a party to a contract may enforce its terms if the contract “clearly expresses an intent to benefit” that person. *Maehler v. Michaels*, 442 Mass. 704, 708 (2004). The settlement agreement explicitly identifies “Settlement Class B Members”—including Foley—as the intended beneficiaries of its modification and servicing commitments. The agreement requires that such members “first shall be considered for a HAMP modification” and, if they do not qualify, “shall be considered for a MAP2R modification.” Wells Fargo’s failure to consider Foley for either modification before scheduling foreclosure breached that promise. Foley’s complaint alleges that Wells Fargo delayed sending a HAMP application for seven months, never evaluated him for MAP2R, and proceeded to foreclosure despite the agreement’s mandate. These allegations state a plausible claim for breach. *See Iantosca v. Benistar Admin. Servs., Inc.*, 463 Mass. 97, 104 (2012) (third-party beneficiary may sue for breach of promise made for his benefit).

### II. Foley Stated a Claim Under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 14

Massachusetts law strictly regulates foreclosure procedures. A mortgagee must comply with all statutory prerequisites before exercising the power of sale. *U.S. Bank Nat’l Ass’n v. Schumacher*, 467 Mass. 421, 427 (2014). The settlement agreement’s requirement that Wells Fargo consider Foley for a modification before foreclosing is a condition precedent to a valid foreclosure. Foley alleges that Wells Fargo failed to satisfy that condition. The district court’s dismissal of this count cannot be sustained because Foley’s complaint plausibly alleges that the foreclosure was initiated without the required consideration, rendering it unlawful under chapter 244. *See Eaton v. Federal Nat’l Mortg. Ass’n*, 462 Mass. 569, 581 (2012) (foreclosure invalid if mortgagee fails to comply with statutory requirements).

### III. Foley Stated a Claim for Unfair or Deceptive Acts Under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A

Chapter 93A prohibits “unfair or deceptive acts or practices” in trade or commerce. *Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A, § 2*. Wells Fargo’s conduct—promising to consider Foley for a modification, then ignoring that promise while simultaneously pursuing foreclosure—is precisely the type of “unfair” behavior the statute targets. *See Klairmont v. Gainsboro Rest., Inc.*, 465 Mass. 165, 174 (2013) (conduct “unfair” if it is “immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous”). Foley’s repeated unanswered calls, the bank’s failure to send a timely application, and its misleading letters about short sales all demonstrate a pattern of deception. The complaint adequately alleges a violation of chapter 93A.

### IV. Federal Law Does Not Preempt Foley’s State-Law Claims

Wells Fargo argues that the Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) preempts Foley’s claims. But HAMP is a voluntary federal program that does not occupy the field of mortgage modification. *See In re Bank of Am. Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) Contract Litig.*, 728 F.3d 18, 24 (1st Cir. 2013) (HAMP does not create a private right of action, but state-law claims based on independent duties are not preempted). Foley’s claims arise from the settlement agreement—a private contract—and from Massachusetts statutes, not from HAMP itself. The settlement agreement incorporates HAMP only as a reference point; the duty to consider Foley arises from the agreement, not from federal law. Preemption therefore does not apply. *See id.* at 25 (state-law claims for breach of contract and unfair practices are not preempted by HAMP).

### Conclusion

Foley’s complaint plausibly alleges that Wells Fargo breached its contractual and statutory obligations. The district court’s dismissal should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
