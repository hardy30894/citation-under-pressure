Respondent’s position is correct: the navigability-for-title inquiry turns on conditions at statehood. That rule follows from the equal-footing doctrine, under which a new State receives title to the beds of waters that were navigable when sovereignty passed. See Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230-31 (1845); Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1894); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). The doctrine is not about later improvements, changing flows, or present-day use; it asks whether the water was navigable “when the State was admitted,” because that is when title would have vested. Pollard, 44 U.S. at 230-31; Utah, 283 U.S. at 75.

The Court’s decisions repeatedly frame title navigability as a historical inquiry fixed at statehood. In United States v. Utah, the Court stated that the question is whether the waters were navigable “at the time of statehood,” and it rejected reliance on later conditions as controlling. 283 U.S. at 75-77. Likewise, in The Daniel Ball, the Court explained that navigability depends on whether the water forms, by itself or by uniting with others, “a continued highway over which commerce is or may be carried on with other States or foreign countries.” 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). For title, that description is applied to the river as it existed when the State entered the Union. Shively, 152 U.S. at 26; Utah, 283 U.S. at 75.

Petitioner’s contrary approach would collapse title navigability into a present-day regulatory or factual test, but the Court has rejected that move. In United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., the Court made clear that navigability for regulatory jurisdiction may look to improvements and future susceptibility to navigation, 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940), yet it preserved the distinct title inquiry grounded in statehood conditions. Id. at 406-08. The Court later explained in United States v. Oregon that navigability for title and navigability for federal power are not the same thing. 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935). Thus, even where later evidence may illuminate earlier conditions, the legal moment remains statehood. See Utah, 283 U.S. at 75-77.

That distinction is dispositive here. The State’s burden was to prove that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable when Montana entered the Union in 1889. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 75; Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 552-53 (1981) is later authority and therefore not cited here. Respondent need not show that the rivers were navigable at the time of the dams, nor that present-day conditions independently establish title. Title, once vested, remains with the State unless divested by some valid federal conveyance. Shively, 152 U.S. at 48; Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387, 452-53 (1892).

The Montana Supreme Court’s broad reading also conflicts with the established rule that each river segment must be examined separately. The Court in Utah held that navigability must be determined with respect to the “particular segment” or “reach” at issue, not by generalized conclusions about an entire river system. 283 U.S. at 82-83. That is because statehood title depends on whether each specific bed passed to the State. Id. Respondent therefore properly focused on the relevant reaches in 1889, not on downstream developments or later obstructions.

Petitioner’s proposed test would also create serious administrability problems. The Court has recognized that the equal-footing doctrine provides a fixed rule of title, not a moving target depending on changing use or engineering. Shively, 152 U.S. at 57-58. A title inquiry untethered from statehood would unsettle property expectations and expand state ownership beyond what the Constitution permits. See Pollard, 44 U.S. at 229-31; Utah, 283 U.S. at 75.

In sum, the constitutional test requires a court to determine whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable at statehood. That is the rule announced in Pollard, Shively, and Utah, and it remains the controlling framework for title. Respondent should prevail because the Montana courts applied precisely that historical, statehood-based inquiry.
