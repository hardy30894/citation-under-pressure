The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's holding relies on an overly broad interpretation of Exemption 2, which is not supported by the statutory language or relevant case law.

Exemption 2 exempts from disclosure matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has held that this exemption should be interpreted narrowly, Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361-62 (1976) (quoting S.Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)), and that the nine exemptions listed in § 552(b) are "explicitly exclusive," U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (quoting Adm'r FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

The Ninth Circuit's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard, as set forth in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C.Cir.1981) (en banc), is not supported by pre-1970 case law. In fact, the Supreme Court's decision in Rose suggests that Exemption 2 is intended to cover only "minor employment matters" that are not of "genuine and significant public interest." Rose, 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The Court's language in Rose implies that Exemption 2 is not intended to cover sensitive or significant internal agency matters.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's reliance on Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir.1980), is misplaced. While Hardy held that "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2," id. at 656, it did not hold that Exemption 2 applies to all "predominantly internal" materials. The D.C. Circuit's decision in Crooker, which adopted the "predominantly internal" standard, was decided after the relevant pre-1970 case law and is not controlling here.

The pre-1970 case law suggests that Exemption 2 is intended to be a narrow exemption. For example, in Cole v. Young, 351 U.S. 536 (1956), the Supreme Court held that a statute authorizing the discharge of government employees for "such cause as will promote the efficiency of the service" should be interpreted narrowly. Similarly, in United States Ex Rel. Touhy v. Ragen, 340 U.S. 462 (1951), the Court held that a regulation centralizing the discretion to submit records to the courts was lawful, but only because it was consistent with the statutory language and legislative intent.

Other pre-1970 cases also support a narrow interpretation of Exemption 2. In Fogarty v. United States, 340 U.S. 8 (1950), the Court held that a claim for relief under the Lucas Act required a "written request for relief" within the meaning of § 3 of the Act. The Court's decision turned on a narrow interpretation of the statutory language. Similarly, in Northern Pacific Railway Co. v. United States, 330 U.S. 248 (1947), the Court held that a statute governing the transportation of government property should be interpreted in light of its plain meaning and legislative history.

In United States v. MacDaniel, 32 U.S. 1 (1833), the Court held that a claim against the government could be offset by a counterclaim, even if the counterclaim was not strictly legal. However, the Court also noted that the counterclaim must be "equitable" and within the discretion of the head of the department. This decision suggests that Exemption 2 should be interpreted in light of its purpose and the broader statutory context.

In United States v. Mouat, 124 U.S. 303 (1888), the Court held that a paymaster's clerk was not an "officer" of the Navy within the meaning of a statute governing longevity pay. The Court's decision turned on a narrow interpretation of the statutory language and the relevant regulations.

In Hannum v. United States, 226 U.S. 436 (1913), the Court held that a statute governing the retirement of Navy officers should be interpreted in light of its plain meaning and legislative history. The Court's decision suggests that Exemption 2 should be interpreted narrowly, in light of its statutory language and purpose.

In Murray v. Joe Gerrick & Co., 291 U.S. 315 (1934), the Court held that a state workmen's compensation act did not apply to a Navy Yard, because the Yard was a federal enclave. The Court's decision turned on a narrow interpretation of the statutory language and the relevant legislative history.

These pre-1970 cases collectively suggest that Exemption 2 should be interpreted narrowly, and that the Ninth Circuit's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is not supported by relevant case law. The ESQD arc maps at issue in this case are not "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices" of the Navy, and their disclosure would not risk circumvention of agency regulation. Therefore, the Ninth Circuit erred in exempting these documents from disclosure under Exemption 2.
