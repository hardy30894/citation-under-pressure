**ARGUMENT**

I. THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE SPEAKER SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENTS TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS THREATS.

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam). But the Constitution does not permit conviction for speech merely because a listener feels threatened. *Id.* at 708. To avoid criminalizing speech that is protected by the First Amendment, the government must prove that the speaker subjectively intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Id.* This Court has long held that the First Amendment requires proof of a culpable mental state before speech may be punished. *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931) (invalidating a statute that punished the display of a red flag “unless such display ... constitute[d] a clear and present danger of a substantive evil”); *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242, 258 (1937) (“The statute must be judged as a whole ... and in its application to the conduct for which petitioner was prosecuted.”).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard—under which a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat—*United States v. Cox*, 957 F. Supp. 139, 144 (E.D. Pa. 1997)—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), this Court cautioned that speech may not be punished merely because it stirs people to anger or invites dispute. The Third Circuit’s standard would permit conviction for speech that a reasonable listener might misinterpret as threatening, even if the speaker did not intend it as such. That is precisely the danger the First Amendment was meant to avoid. *Id.*

This Court has repeatedly required proof of a defendant’s subjective intent when speech is at issue. In *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 279–80 (1964), the Court required proof that the speaker knew his statements were false or acted with reckless disregard of their truth or falsity. These cases reflect a fundamental principle: the First Amendment demands that the government prove a culpable mental state before it may punish speech. *Id.*

The Third Circuit’s objective standard flips this principle on its head. It permits conviction for speech that a reasonable person might find threatening, regardless of the speaker’s intent. That is not the law. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707. The First Amendment requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Id.*

II. *WATTS* AND *NEW YORK TIMES* RECOGNIZE THAT THE FIRST AMENDMENT PROHIBITS CONVICTION FOR SPEECH MERELY BECAUSE IT CAUSES FEAR.

The government’s objective standard would permit conviction for speech that causes fear, even if the speaker did not intend it as a threat. But this Court has long rejected such a standard. In *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707, the Court held that the First Amendment does not protect “true threats,” but it did not suggest that speech may be punished merely because it causes fear. *Id.* at 708. Similarly, in *New York Times*, 376 U.S. at 279–80, the Court required proof of a culpable mental state before speech could be punished. The government’s objective standard would eviscerate this protection. *Id.*

III. THE GOVERNMENT’S PROPOSED STANDARD WOULD CHILL PROTECTED SPEECH.

The government’s objective standard would chill protected speech. If speakers may be punished for statements that a reasonable person might find threatening, they will hesitate to engage in robust debate, satire, or hyperbole. *Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4. This is particularly true in the context of online speech, where tone and context are easily misinterpreted. *Id.* The First Amendment does not permit such a chilling effect. *Id.*

This Court has long recognized the danger of punishing speech based on its tendency to cause fear. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that speech may not be punished unless it is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action. The government’s objective standard would permit punishment for speech that merely causes fear, even if it does not incite lawless action. That is not the law. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The First Amendment requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. The Third Circuit’s objective standard is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. The Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with the First Amendment.
