The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) begins to run upon the earliest date a plaintiff possesses actual knowledge of the breach. That period expired before respondent commenced suit.

Statutes of limitations are “founded in public policy” and “are to be favored.” Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879). When Congress selects the phrase “actual knowledge,” it requires only that the plaintiff have been placed in possession of the operative facts, not that he have appreciated their legal consequences. See United States v. Clark, 96 U.S. 37, 42 (1877) (knowledge of the transaction itself triggers the bar). Disclosure of those facts to the plaintiff therefore constitutes actual knowledge. See Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (once “the means of knowledge are at hand,” the period commences).

Respondent received plan documents that set forth the asset allocations and investment strategy now alleged to be imprudent. Under settled principles, a party who receives such information “is presumed to have read and understood” it. Upton v. Tribilcock, 91 U.S. 45, 50 (1875). Failure to examine the documents does not suspend the statute. As the Court explained long ago, “the law does not favor the negligent or the indifferent.” Wood, 101 U.S. at 141. A plaintiff cannot “remain in ignorance” of facts he has been given and then claim the benefit of a longer period. See Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435, 447 (1918).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that actual knowledge requires subjective awareness that the disclosed acts were imprudent—imports an element foreign to the statutory text and to the common-law understanding of limitations periods. Precedent uniformly holds that knowledge of the underlying facts, not of their legal characterization, starts the clock. Clark, 96 U.S. at 42; Bailey, 88 U.S. at 348. Because respondent possessed the disclosures more than three years before filing, the action is time-barred.

Reversed.
