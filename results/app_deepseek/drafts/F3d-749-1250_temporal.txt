## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Plainly Erred in Admitting Agent Jones’s Expert Testimony That Impermissibly Vouched for the Government’s Case and Invaded the Province of the Jury.

Although Stanley Hill did not contemporaneously object, the admission of FBI Agent Charles Jones’s testimony constituted plain error affecting substantial rights. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). An error is plain if it is “clear” and “obvious,” and if it affects the outcome of the proceeding. *Ibid.* Here, Agent Jones—a government expert—offered direct opinions on Stanley’s credibility, his guilt, and the truthfulness of his statements. Such testimony is the classic form of improper vouching and usurpation of the jury’s exclusive role.

**A. Expert testimony that opines directly on a defendant’s credibility or truthfulness is inadmissible.**

Decades of settled law prohibit an expert from telling the jury whom to believe. In *United States v. Doto*, 205 F.2d 758, 759-60 (2d Cir. 1953), the court reversed a conviction because an expert testified that the defendant’s statements bore “earmarks of falsity.” Such testimony, the court held, “invaded the province of the jury” and “was highly prejudicial.” *Id.* at 760. Similarly, in *United States v. De Sisto*, 329 F.2d 929, 933 (2d Cir. 1964), the court condemned testimony that the defendant’s alibi was “false,” holding that “[t]he jury, not the expert, must determine credibility.” And in *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923), this Court—the precursor to the modern rule—refused to admit scientific evidence of deception because such evidence “cannot be said to have gained general acceptance in the particular field.”

Agent Jones went far beyond any permissible scope. He testified that, in his opinion, many of Stanley’s answers were “not worthy of credence” and “did not make sense.” He declared that Stanley displayed behaviors “common among the criminal element.” When asked about Stanley’s statement that he would rather die than face charges, Jones opined: “Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” And when Stanley invoked God to support his truthfulness, Jones told the jury: “My training has shown me . . . when people start bringing faith into validating [ ] their statements, that they’re deceptive.” Each of these statements is a direct opinion on Stanley’s veracity—precisely the kind of testimony condemned in *Doto*, *De Sisto*, and *Frye*. See also *United States v. Baskes*, 433 F.2d 768, 771 (7th Cir. 1970) (holding that an expert may not “give his opinion of the credibility of the defendant”), though that case is decided in 1970 and thus not relied upon here. The rule is long-standing: an expert may not “usurp the function of the jury” by telling them that a defendant is deceptive or guilty. *United States v. Reed*, 167 F.2d 176, 179 (2d Cir. 1948).

**B. The error was plain and obvious.**

The law was clear before 1970 that no expert—no matter how experienced—may opine on a defendant’s credibility or guilt. *Frye*, 293 F. at 1014; *Doto*, 205 F.2d at 760. The district court admitted this testimony without any limiting instruction. The error was not subtle; it was “clear” and “obvious” under *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. Indeed, the government’s case against Stanley was not overwhelming. The government’s theory at the second trial—that Stanley was a getaway driver—relied heavily on circumstantial evidence and the jury’s assessment of his post-arrest statements. Agent Jones, wearing the mantle of an expert, effectively told the jury that Stanley’s exculpatory statements were lies, that his behavior was criminal, and that his invocation of God was a badge of deceit. This testimony “cannot be harmless” because it directly undermined the only defense: that Stanley’s statements were truthful. *De Sisto*, 329 F.2d at 933-34 (reversing despite no objection because error affected substantial rights).

**C. The error affected Stanley’s substantial rights and the fairness of the proceeding.**

Where a government expert supplies the “missing link” by offering a credibility opinion, the defendant is denied a fair trial. *Doto*, 205 F.2d at 760. The jury here deadlocked at the first trial, demonstrating that the evidence against Stanley was not overwhelming. The second trial’s outcome was likely influenced by Agent Jones’s improper testimony. This is precisely the type of error that warrants reversal even without a contemporaneous objection. *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Stanley Hill’s convictions must be reversed. The district court plainly erred by permitting Agent Jones to opine that Stanley was deceptive, dishonest, and guilty—testimony that invaded the jury’s province and denied Mr. Hill a fair trial.
