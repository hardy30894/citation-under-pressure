**ARGUMENT**

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for the Ordot Dump Cleanup**

The D.C. Circuit erred in concluding that the 2004 Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability for the Ordot Dump cleanup. Under 42 U.S.C. § 113(f)(3)(B), a party may seek contribution only if it has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” A “settlement” under this provision requires a compromise of disputed claims, not merely a consent to a penalty or cleanup order. Here, the Consent Decree was based on the pleadings, “before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree at 3. This language is fatal to the government’s argument. As the Sixth Circuit held in *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015), a consent decree must contain “express language” that the parties “agree that this Settlement Agreement constitutes an administrative settlement for purposes of Section 113(f)(3)(B) of CERCLA.” The Consent Decree here contains no such language. It was a consent to a penalty and a cleanup, not a settlement of the underlying liability dispute. Consequently, the Consent Decree did not “resolve” Guam’s liability within the meaning of the statute.

**II. The “Exclusive Remedy” Rule Requires Strict Construction of the Statute’s Settlement Definition**

The D.C. Circuit’s interpretation of § 113(f)(3)(B) as the exclusive remedy for any party that pays for a response action—regardless of whether the payment was made pursuant to a true settlement—would render the contribution mechanism a nullity. Congress enacted CERCLA to ensure that “responsible parties foot the bill” for environmental cleanups, *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998), but it also established a specific mechanism for apportioning those costs among joint tortfeasors. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 162 (2004). The “exclusive remedy” language of § 113(f)(3)(B) is designed to prevent a settling party from being sued for contribution by a non-settling party, but it does not expand the definition of “settlement” to include administrative orders or consent decrees that lack a finding of liability. To read the statute otherwise would allow the EPA to force a PRP to pay for a cleanup under a CWA consent decree and then claim immunity from contribution, effectively making the PRP liable for the entire cost without the procedural protections of a formal contribution action. The Consent Decree here was a “consent” to a penalty and a cleanup, not a “settlement” that resolved liability. Therefore, Guam is not barred by the three-year statute of limitations and may proceed under § 107(a) to recover its costs.
