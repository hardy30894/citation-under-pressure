**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s role is advisory and the judge independently finds aggravating factors.**

In *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in the maximum punishment. *Id.* at 589. But *Ring* expressly distinguished Florida’s scheme. In *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), the Court had upheld Arizona’s judge-only sentencing, but *Ring* overruled *Walton* only as to schemes where the judge alone finds aggravating factors. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida’s scheme, by contrast, requires the jury to render an advisory verdict recommending life or death. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2). The judge then independently weighs aggravating and mitigating circumstances and imposes sentence. § 921.141(3). This Court has never held that the Sixth Amendment requires the jury to make specific factual findings on aggravators or to render a unanimous recommendation. Indeed, in *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638 (1989), the Court upheld Florida’s scheme against a Sixth Amendment challenge, concluding that “the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury.” *Id.* at 640–641. *Ring* did not disturb *Hildwin*; it merely overruled *Walton*’s holding that a judge could find aggravators without any jury involvement. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 608 n.6. Because Florida’s jury participates in sentencing, the scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment.

**II. The Eighth Amendment does not require a jury to determine mental retardation or to render a unanimous death recommendation.**

In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded, but left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” *Id.* at 317. Florida has done so: it requires the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence, and the trial court makes the ultimate determination. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4); *Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009). This Court has repeatedly recognized that States may assign the mental-retardation determination to the judge. *See Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6, 7 (2005) (per curiam) (remanding for state court to determine procedure); *Bobby v. Bies*, 556 U.S. 825, 831 (2009) (noting that *Atkins* left “to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction”). Hurst was allowed to present mental-retardation evidence as mitigation, and the trial court expressly found he was not mentally retarded. That procedure satisfies the Eighth Amendment.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require a unanimous jury recommendation. This Court has never held that unanimity is constitutionally required in capital sentencing. In *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242 (1976), the Court upheld Florida’s scheme despite its non-unanimous jury recommendation. *Id.* at 253. The “heightened reliability” demanded by the Eighth Amendment, *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), is achieved through the judge’s independent weighing of aggravating and mitigating circumstances, not through jury unanimity. Florida’s scheme ensures reliability by requiring the judge to make written findings and by providing for appellate review. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3); *Hurst v. State*, 819 So. 2d 689, 698 (Fla. 2002). The fact that other States require unanimity does not render Florida’s scheme unconstitutional. *See Walton*, 497 U.S. at 656 (noting that “the Constitution does not require the States to adopt any particular form of capital sentencing procedure”).

**III. Hurst’s claim that the jury must find mental retardation as a bar to execution is foreclosed by *Atkins* and *Ring*.**

*Ring* requires jury findings only for facts that make a defendant death-eligible—i.e., aggravating factors. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609. Mental retardation is a mitigating factor that bars execution, not an element of the offense. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. The Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find mitigating facts. *See Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000) (holding that only facts that increase the penalty beyond the statutory maximum must be found by a jury). Because mental retardation reduces punishment, it need not be submitted to the jury. Florida’s procedure—allowing the judge to decide mental retardation after the jury’s advisory verdict—is fully consistent with *Atkins* and *Ring*.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
