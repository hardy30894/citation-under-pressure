## ARGUMENT

The question presented is whether the Fourteenth Amendment requires Arizona to permit a criminal defendant to use expert evidence of mental disease or defect to negate the *mens rea* element of a crime, when the State has provided a separate insanity defense through which that same evidence may be presented. The answer is no. States possess broad authority to define the elements of criminal offenses and to structure the defenses available to a defendant. Arizona’s rule—channeling mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense—does not violate due process.

### I. States May Define the Elements of Crimes and the Structure of Defenses

The Constitution does not require a particular definition of insanity or a particular allocation of evidentiary burdens. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), this Court upheld an Oregon statute that required a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court recognized that the State could “require proof of insanity by a higher standard than a preponderance of the evidence.” *Id.* at 799. Critically, the Court observed that the jury could consider evidence of insanity “with all other evidence” on the question of premeditation and intent, but it did not hold that the Constitution *compelled* such consideration. *Id.* at 793. The State’s power to define the insanity defense includes the power to limit the use of mental-disease evidence to that defense.

### II. Insanity and Mens Rea Are Distinct Concepts

The insanity defense addresses a defendant’s capacity to understand the wrongfulness of his conduct, not whether he formed a specific intent. As Justice Rehnquist noted in his concurring opinion in *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), “the existence or nonexistence of legal insanity bears no necessary relationship to the existence or nonexistence of the required mental elements of the crime.” *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877, 880 (1976) (Rehnquist, J., concurring). A defendant may be legally sane yet unable to form a particular intent, or legally insane yet fully capable of forming intent. Arizona’s rule respects this distinction by treating mental disease as relevant only to the separate question of criminal responsibility, not to the elemental question of intent.

### III. The Defendant’s Right to Present a Defense Is Not Absolute

The Due Process Clause guarantees a defendant a meaningful opportunity to present a defense, but that right is subject to reasonable procedural and evidentiary rules. *See, e.g., Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). Arizona’s rule does not bar the defendant from presenting expert evidence of his mental condition; it merely channels that evidence to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof. The defendant remains free to argue that the prosecution has failed to prove *mens rea* beyond a reasonable doubt, and he may rely on lay testimony or other evidence to support that argument. The State’s limitation on the use of expert mental-disease evidence is a rational means of avoiding jury confusion and preserving the distinction between the insanity defense and the elements of the crime.

### IV. The State’s Rule Does Not Shift the Burden of Proof on Mens Rea

The prosecution retains the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of that burden. The defendant may still argue that the evidence—including any evidence of mental condition—raises a reasonable doubt about his intent. The only limitation is that he cannot use expert testimony of mental disease *as a matter of right* to negate *mens rea*; he must instead present that evidence through the insanity defense. This is a permissible procedural choice, not a denial of due process. *See Leland*, 343 U.S. at 797-98 (upholding Oregon’s allocation of the burden on insanity).

### V. The Insanity Defense Provides an Adequate Forum for Mental-Disease Evidence

A defendant who successfully raises the insanity defense avoids criminal punishment altogether. *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354, 363 (1983) (noting that an insanity acquittal “necessarily is stigmatized by the verdict itself”). The availability of that defense ensures that the defendant’s mental condition is fully considered. Arizona’s rule does not prevent the defendant from presenting his expert evidence; it simply requires him to do so within the framework of the insanity defense. That framework is constitutionally adequate.

### VI. Conclusion

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to allow a defendant to use expert evidence of mental disease to negate *mens rea* when the State has provided a separate insanity defense. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
