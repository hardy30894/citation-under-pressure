## ARGUMENT

### I. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73'S TERM LIMITS ON FEDERAL CONGRESSIONAL CANDIDATES VIOLATE THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution, found in Article I, Sections 2 and 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for members of Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency, and the Framers' decision to enumerate these qualifications was a deliberate choice to preclude additional state-imposed requirements. As the Supreme Court has held, the Qualifications Clauses "fix[] as qualifications for membership in Congress certain characteristics which cannot be altered by Congress or the States." Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 539-40 (1969). The Court has also recognized that the Constitution's enumeration of qualifications is exhaustive, and that states may not add to or modify these requirements. See id. at 550.

The term limits imposed by Amendment 73 on federal congressional candidates are, in effect, additional qualifications that are not authorized by the Constitution. By limiting the number of terms a member of Congress may serve, Amendment 73 effectively adds a new qualification that is not found in the Constitution. This is impermissible, as the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. See U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, 316 Ark. 251, 872 S.W.2d 349 (1994) (Brown, J., concurring) ("The Constitution of the United States sets the qualifications for membership in Congress, and the states are not free to add to or subtract from those qualifications.").

### II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT SECTION 3 OF AMENDMENT 73 IS INSEVERABLE FROM SECTIONS 1 AND 2.

The severability clause in Amendment 73 is insufficient to save the remainder of the amendment, as the unconstitutional term limits on federal congressional candidates are inextricably linked to the term limits on state officials. The ballot title and the Preamble to Amendment 73 refer to "elected officials" generally, without distinguishing between state and federal officeholders. This suggests that the voters were presented with an "all or nothing" package, and that the term limits on state officials were not severable from the term limits on federal congressional candidates.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly applied the test for severability, which requires a determination of whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). In this case, there is no evidence to suggest that the voters would have approved term limits on state officials if the term limits on federal congressional candidates had been excluded. Therefore, the entire amendment must fall.

The practice of coupling a legitimate objective with one of doubtful legality, as was done in Amendment 73, is not fair to voters and should be discouraged. By presenting the term limits on state and federal officials as a single package, the sponsors of Amendment 73 created uncertainty and potentially misled voters. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that this approach is not "voter-friendly" and that it undermines the integrity of the electoral process.

### CONCLUSION

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73's term limits on federal congressional candidates violate the U.S. Constitution, and that Section 3 of the amendment is inseverable from Sections 1 and 2. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
