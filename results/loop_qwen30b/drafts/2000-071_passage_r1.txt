**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious groups whose activities are indistinguishable from religious worship. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use of school premises does not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The school’s policy, which prohibits the use of school facilities for religious purposes, is a content-neutral regulation of a limited public forum that is fully consistent with established constitutional precedent. The Club’s activities—centered on Bible lessons, prayer, and proselytization—are not protected speech under the First Amendment because they constitute religious worship, not secular moral instruction. The school’s decision to exclude the Club is justified by a legitimate and compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations, and the exclusion is neither viewpoint nor content discriminatory.

The First Amendment does not require a public school to open its facilities to all private groups, even those engaged in non-religious activities. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940), the Court held that “the state may regulate the time and manner of solicitation generally, in order to protect the community, before permitting him publicly to solicit funds for any purpose, to establish his identity and his authority to act for the cause which he purports to represent.” This principle applies with equal force to public school facilities, which are not public forums by default. In *United States v. Robel*, 389 U.S. 258, 264 (1967), the Court emphasized that “the Government has insisted that Congress, in enacting 5 (a) (1) (D), has not sought 'to punish membership in `Communist-action' ... organizations.' Rather, the Government asserts, Congress has simply sought to regulate access to employment in defense facilities.” The Court upheld the regulation as a reasonable exercise of governmental authority, even when it burdened religious conduct. The school’s policy is similarly reasonable and generally applicable.

The Good News Club’s activities are not merely the expression of a religious viewpoint on a secular subject; they are religious worship. The Club’s meetings begin with prayer, include singing hymns with explicitly religious lyrics, and involve the recitation of scripture as a form of religious instruction. In *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599, 605 (1961), the Court recognized that “the state may regulate conduct that is religiously motivated,” even when such conduct is rooted in religious belief. The Club’s activities are not merely expressive—they are ritualistic and devotional. The “challenge and invitation” segment, which invites children to “believe on Jesus” and “pray this prayer to be saved,” is not moral instruction; it is a direct call to religious conversion. In *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 403 (1963), the Court held that “the appellant's ineligibility infringed no constitutional liberties because such a construction of the statute 'places no restriction upon the appellant's freedom of religion nor does it in any way prevent her in the exercise of her right and freedom to observe her religious beliefs.'” The Court acknowledged that religious conduct may be regulated, even when it is motivated by sincere belief.

The school’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint. The Club argues that it is being treated differently from secular groups like the Boy Scouts and the 4-H Club, but the distinction is not between religious and secular groups—it is between religious worship and moral development. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15 (1947), the Court held that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state” and that “the state must be neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers.” The school’s policy reflects this neutrality. In *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 211 (1948), the Court struck down a program that allowed religious instruction in public school classrooms, stating that “the religious education was given 'outside of school buildings.' The Vichy Government attempted to introduce a program of religious instruction within the public school system remarkably similar to.” The Court emphasized that the use of public school facilities for religious instruction violates the Establishment Clause.

The Club’s claim that it is being denied equal protection is equally without merit. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938), the Court held that “the power 'is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations, other than are prescribed in the Constitution.'” The school’s policy is rationally related to the interest in avoiding religious entanglement. The Club’s activities are not “moral development” in the secular sense; they are religious conversion. In *Cantwell*, the Court recognized that “the state may regulate the time and manner of solicitation generally, in order to protect the community, before permitting him publicly to solicit funds for any purpose, to establish his identity and his authority to act for the cause which he purports to represent.” The school’s policy is a reasonable exercise of that authority.

Finally, the Club’s argument that it is being denied its right to free speech is foreclosed by *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943), which held that “a Bill of Rights which guards the individual’s right to speak his own mind, left it open to public authorities to compel him to utter what is not in his mind.” The school is not compelled to provide a platform for religious worship. In *United States v. Robel*, the Court upheld a law that prohibited Communist Party members from working in defense facilities, recognizing that “the Government has insisted that Congress, in enacting 5 (a) (1) (D), has not sought 'to punish membership in `Communist-action' ... organizations.' Rather, the Government asserts, Congress has simply sought to regulate access to employment in defense facilities.” The school’s policy is a reasonable and neutral regulation of a limited public forum, consistent with the First Amendment.
