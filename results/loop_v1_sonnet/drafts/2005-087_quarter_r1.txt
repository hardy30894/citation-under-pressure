**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), a plurality of this Court declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, recognizing that questions of mens rea and the tests for assessing criminal responsibility have traditionally been left to experimentation by the States and that this Court has not derived a general constitutional doctrine governing them from the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 535–37. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Powell* leaves to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent element by element. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation between the defense of insanity and the prosecution's burden on the elements of the offense.

This Court has also sustained, as consistent with due process, the placement of the burden of proof on a defendant asserting insanity, recognizing that the formulation of rules governing criminal responsibility belongs to the States and to Congress in the federal system. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). And this Court has already upheld the exclusion of similar categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct the jury that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, concluding that the District of Columbia's adherence to a unitary standard of criminal responsibility, rather than a doctrine of partial responsibility, did not offend due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits the evidence to be considered on the question of insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend a principle of justice "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). The Fourteenth Amendment does not "assure uniformity of judicial procedure," *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 111 (1908), and States remain free "to abolish old and to create new remedies... in the exercise of a wide discretion." *Id.* at 110.

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, explaining that "the process of adjudication... normally requires the drawing of lines" and that state rules of evidence are entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), is whether "the absence of that fair play" so infects the proceeding as to violate due process. *Id.* at 236. Here, the trial court permitted Clark to introduce the full range of expert psychiatric testimony and to have it evaluated by the factfinder on the issue of insanity—the very evidence he now claims should also negate intent. Nothing in *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 487–88, which addressed the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be simultaneously admissible, unconstrained, for an entirely separate purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people," *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325, and this Court should affirm.
