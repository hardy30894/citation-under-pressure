**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations occurring aboard the *MR. BELDON*, an uninspected drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation *per se* preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act—particularly where, as here, the vessel is uninspected and the workers are not seamen within the meaning of maritime law. This Court should reverse.

**I. The OSH Act Applies to the *MR. BELDON* Because It Constitutes a “Workplace” Under § 4(a)**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the Act’s coverage: “The provisions of this Act shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to fixed, land-based facilities. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the Act’s coverage extends to any place where employees perform work,” including mobile or temporary workplaces. *Barnes v. OSHRC*, 453 U.S. 180, 185 (1981) (per curiam). In *Barnes*, the Court upheld OSHA jurisdiction over a construction site on a floating barge, emphasizing that “the Act’s reach is not confined to permanent structures.” Id. at 186.

The *MR. BELDON* was not merely a vessel in transit—it was a permanent, self-contained workplace where employees performed drilling operations for extended periods. It housed crew quarters, control rooms, machinery, and storage areas. The explosion occurred during routine drilling operations, not navigation. The workers were engaged in industrial, not maritime, labor. As the Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) correctly found, the *MR. BELDON* was a “workplace” under the OSH Act. The Fifth Circuit’s failure to address this threshold issue was error, as the existence of a workplace is a prerequisite to OSHA jurisdiction.

**II. The Coast Guard’s Authority Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction Over Uninspected Vessels**

The Fifth Circuit held that the Coast Guard’s exclusive authority over “working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation” preempts OSHA under § 4(b)(1), citing *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). But these cases do not support the broad, categorical preemption the court imposed.

*Clary* involved a seaman injured on a drilling barge due to a lack of color-coding on a deck plate. The court held that OSHA regulations did not apply because the Coast Guard had authority over “working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” 609 F.2d at 1122. However, the court did not address whether the barge was inspected or uninspected, nor did it purport to extend its holding to non-seamen or non-navigational workplaces. The decision was grounded in the *status* of the worker—seaman—and the *nature* of the work—maritime operations. The court did not purport to bar OSHA from regulating industrial workplaces on vessels, even if they are in navigation.

*Donovan* reaffirmed *Clary* but did so in the context of a seaman’s claim for injuries on a vessel. The court stated: “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels.” 720 F.2d at 827. Again, the holding was limited to seamen and maritime work. The court did not purport to bar OSHA from regulating non-seamen performing industrial work on vessels, nor did it address uninspected vessels.

The Fifth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Clary* on the ground that it did not consider uninspected vessels is misplaced. The *Clary* court’s holding was not contingent on inspection status. It was based on the *status* of the worker and the *nature* of the work. The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is not limited to inspection; it includes safety regulation under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 and 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. But the mere existence of Coast Guard safety regulations does not mean that OSHA is barred from regulating *other* aspects of workplace safety—especially where the workers are not seamen and the work is industrial, not maritime.

**III. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Where the Coast Guard’s Authority Is Not Exclusive or Comprehensive**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides: “The provisions of this Act shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision is not a blanket bar to OSHA jurisdiction. It applies only where another agency has *exclusive* and *comprehensive* authority over occupational safety and health.

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is neither exclusive nor comprehensive. It regulates specific safety equipment and procedures—fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and emergency signaling—but it does not regulate general workplace safety, ergonomics, hazard communication, or training programs. The Coast Guard has no authority to enforce standards on employee exposure to toxic substances, machine guarding, or fall protection—core areas of OSHA’s jurisdiction.
