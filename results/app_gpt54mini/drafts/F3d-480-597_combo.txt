## Argument

### I. The evidence was insufficient to prove that Ms. Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle was a machine gun.

A conviction may stand only if the Government proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. In a criminal case, due process requires the prosecution to prove the defendant’s guilt as to each essential fact, and a conviction resting on inference piled upon inference cannot stand. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970) is later authority not cited here; under earlier settled doctrine, the Supreme Court held that a verdict cannot be sustained unless the evidence supports each necessary element with legal certainty. See Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463 (1943); Smith v. United States, 223 U.S. 438 (1912).

Here, the Government had the burden to prove that Ms. Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm possessed the characteristics that made it a machine gun. The record shows at most that she knew a rifle was in the bag and that she knew it was an AK-47. That is not the same thing as knowledge that the weapon met the statutory definition of a machine gun. Criminal liability cannot be expanded by implication. “No person may be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law,” and due process forbids conviction on proof that leaves an essential element uncertain. Hammond Packing Co. v. Arkansas, 212 U.S. 322 (1909); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359 (1931).

The Supreme Court has long required strict proof where criminal statutes turn on hidden or technical characteristics of an object. In cases involving regulated articles or status-based offenses, the Court has insisted that the Government prove the proscribed condition, not merely possession of the item’s outer form. See United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250 (1922); Shevlin-Carpenter Co. v. Minnesota, 218 U.S. 57 (1910). But where criminal responsibility depends on a further fact known to the accused, the proof must show that fact, not simply a suspicion about it. See Bailey v. United States, 219 U.S. 219 (1911); United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277 (1943). The Government’s proof here falls short of that standard.

Nor can the conviction be upheld by treating the jury’s conclusion as conclusive if unsupported by substantial evidence. A verdict cannot rest on “mere suspicion or conjecture.” Galloway v. United States, 319 U.S. 372 (1943). The same principle appears in earlier cases emphasizing that criminal guilt must be established by competent proof, not speculation. See Sparf v. United States, 156 U.S. 51 (1895); United States v. Johnson, 319 U.S. 503 (1943). At most, the evidence established knowledge of a rifle’s presence and general type. It did not establish knowledge of automatic fire capability, much less of the statutory machine-gun status.

The Government may contend that Ms. Nieves-Castaño’s statement that she saw an AK-47 is enough. It is not. A defendant’s awareness of a weapon’s appearance does not prove awareness of the internal mechanism that distinguishes a semiautomatic rifle from a machine gun. Criminal law has never permitted conviction by category alone when the statute requires proof of a more specific factual condition. See Bailey v. United States, 219 U.S. 219 (1911); Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463 (1943). The jury therefore lacked a legally sufficient basis to conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that Ms. Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm was a machine gun.

### II. The school-zone conviction also cannot stand because the statute’s reach was not proved in a constitutionally reliable way.

Section 922(q)(2)(A) criminalizes possession of a firearm within a school zone. Where a criminal statute defines its prohibited area by distance, due process requires fair notice and a determinate standard of enforcement. See Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926); Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939). The Supreme Court has repeatedly invalidated laws that fail to provide persons of ordinary intelligence with a usable standard for compliance. Connally, 269 U.S. 385; Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242 (1937); Winters v. New York, 333 U.S. 507 (1948).

On this record, however, the Government did not introduce proof establishing the statutory zone by a reliable, legally intelligible method tied to the actual location of the apartment. When the criminal law depends on a geographic boundary, the boundary must be shown with certainty. See Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927); Nash v. United States, 229 U.S. 373 (1913). A conviction cannot rest on an undefined or arbitrary measurement method. See Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359 (1931); Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939).

For these reasons, both convictions should be reversed. At minimum, the machine-gun conviction must be vacated for insufficient evidence of mens rea, and the school-zone conviction must be vacated because the record does not establish the statute’s application with the clarity due process requires. See Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463 (1943); Galloway v. United States, 319 U.S. 372 (1943).
