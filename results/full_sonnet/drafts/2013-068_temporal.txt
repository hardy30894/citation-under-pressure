# ARGUMENT

## I. The Act Is a Valid, Content-Neutral Time, Place, and Manner Regulation

This Court has long held that government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech occurring on public property so long as the regulation is content-neutral, serves a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), this Court unanimously upheld a licensing requirement for parades on public streets, explaining that "civil liberties, as guaranteed by the Constitution, imply the existence of an organized society maintaining public order." *Id.* at 574. The Act challenged here is precisely such a regulation: it does not silence any message, but merely establishes a fixed thirty-five-foot zone around clinic entrances that applies with equal force to speakers of every viewpoint.

This Court reached the same result in *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), sustaining an ordinance banning loud sound trucks on public streets because "[u]nrestrained use throughout a municipality of all sound amplifying devices would be intolerable." *Id.* at 87. And in *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court upheld a municipal permit requirement for speeches in a public park, confirming that the constitutional guarantee of free speech "does not deprive a municipality of authority to control the streets and parks reasonably." *Id.* at 405. The Act likewise regulates only where petitioners may stand, not what they may say.

## II. Public Streets and Sidewalks, Though Traditional Public Fora, Remain Subject to Reasonable Regulation

Petitioners invoke the special status of sidewalks as public fora, but this Court has never treated that status as immunizing speakers from reasonable regulation. In *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court recognized that streets and parks "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" for expressive purposes, *id.* at 515, but it did not hold that such use is beyond regulation; rather, use of the public forum "must not, in the guise of regulation, be abridged or denied." *Id.* at 516. The Act does not abridge or deny use of the sidewalks outside clinics—it merely shifts the precise point of contact by thirty-five feet, leaving petitioners free to picket, chant, sing, hold signs, distribute literature, and pray on the same sidewalks, just beyond the buffer.

Nor does the Act's location on government-adjacent property render it suspect. In *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), this Court upheld convictions for trespass where demonstrators refused to leave jail grounds, explaining that "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Id.* at 47. Massachusetts's interest in dedicating a narrow strip of sidewalk to unobstructed passage into medical facilities is fully consonant with that principle.

## III. The Commonwealth's Interest in Protecting Ingress and Egress to Medical Facilities Is Substantial and Directly Analogous to Interests This Court Has Already Sustained

The interest at the core of the Act—ensuring that persons may enter and leave a building without obstruction, harassment, or confrontation—is not novel. This Court confronted precisely this interest in *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611 (1968), where it upheld a Mississippi statute making it unlawful to picket "in such manner as to obstruct or unreasonably interfere with free ingress or egress" to a courthouse. *Id.* at 613. The Court rejected vagueness and overbreadth challenges, holding that the statute was "a precise and narrowly drawn regulatory statute [which] evinces a legislative judgment that... unimpeded ingress to and egress from... buildings are compelling state interests." *Id.* at 617. If the State may protect access to a courthouse from picketers pressing a political message, it may equally protect access to a medical clinic from picketers pressing a moral one. The buffer zone here is narrower in physical scope than the regulation sustained in *Cameron* and, like that statute, is keyed to the specific harm of obstructed passage rather than to the content of any speaker's message.

This Court reached a similar conclusion in *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536 (1965), upholding a statute prohibiting picketing "near" a courthouse "with the intent of interfering with, obstructing, or impeding the administration of justice." *Id.* at 559. The Court recognized that the State's interest in unimpeded access to sensitive facilities justifies a measured buffer even though the picketing itself communicated a viewpoint on a matter of public concern. The same logic governs here: the Commonwealth need not wait for violence or physical obstruction to occur before acting to secure a corridor of safe passage.

## IV. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels of Communication

Even where a regulation restricts the location of speech, it survives constitutional scrutiny if ample alternatives remain, as this Court found in both *Cox v. New Hampshire* and *Kovacs*. The record confirms that petitioners retain the same tools of persuasion—voice, signs, literature, and prayer—merely displaced by thirty-five feet. That is the paradigm of a reasonable time-place-manner accommodation, not a suppression of speech.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
