## Argument

The District violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because the proposed films were “church related.” Once a state creates a forum for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech on the basis of viewpoint. Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983); Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educ. Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 800 (1985). Even in a limited public forum, the government “may not discriminate against speech on the basis of its viewpoint.” Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995); see also Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 533 U.S. 98, 106-07 (2001).

Here, the School District opened its facilities after hours for community use, including film presentations and other meetings. Having done so, it could not exclude Lamb’s Chapel merely because the films would present family-values material “from a Christian perspective.” Lamb’s Chapel sought to use the premises for a subject otherwise available in the forum—family and child-rearing issues. The District’s stated reason for refusal was not that the topic was outside the forum, but that the presentation was “church related.” That is viewpoint discrimination. Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831 (“Discrimination against speech because of its message is presumed to be unconstitutional”); Good News Club, 533 U.S. at 107 (“The restriction is not a restriction based on subject matter, but rather on viewpoint.”).

The Supreme Court’s decision in Lamb’s Chapel compels reversal. There, as here, a school district denied a church access to school facilities for after-hours films dealing with family values and child rearing because the films were religious in nature. Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 387-88 (1993). The Court held that the district violated the First Amendment because the exclusion was “on the basis of the religious viewpoint of the films” and because the school had permitted other speakers to address similar subject matter. Id. at 393-94. The Court rejected the school district’s effort to characterize the exclusion as a permissible subject-matter limitation, explaining that allowing discussion of family issues while forbidding them “from a religious standpoint” is viewpoint discrimination. Id. at 394.

That is precisely what happened here. The District did not close its premises to all discussions of family values or child rearing. It selectively barred Lamb’s Chapel because its perspective was religious. The First Amendment does not allow a public school to “convey[] the message that the government endorses” or disapproves a viewpoint by selectively excluding speakers from an otherwise available forum. Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 835; see also Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 248-49 (1990) (school’s equal access to noncurricular student groups could not exclude religious speech simply because it was religious). The government “may not regulate speech based on its substantive content or the message it conveys,” Reed v. Town of Gilbert, 576 U.S. 155, 163 (2015), and it may not do so under the lesser label of “religious” use when the real objection is the viewpoint expressed. Lamb’s Chapel, 508 U.S. at 394; Good News Club, 533 U.S. at 107-08.

The District’s reliance on establishment concerns is no answer. In Lamb’s Chapel itself, the Court held that permitting the religious film series would not violate the Establishment Clause. 508 U.S. at 395. Indeed, “there is no realistic danger that the community would think that the District was endorsing religion or a religious viewpoint” by allowing equal access in a forum otherwise open to the public. Id. at 395. The same principle was reaffirmed in Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 273-75 (1981), and Good News Club, 533 U.S. at 113-19. Equal access to a limited public forum does not establish religion; exclusion of religious viewpoints does not cure any supposed endorsement problem, but instead violates the Free Speech Clause. Widmar, 454 U.S. at 276; Lamb’s Chapel, 508 U.S. at 395.

The Second Circuit’s attempt to uphold the exclusion because the school had not opened the forum to “religious uses” cannot be squared with Supreme Court precedent. The relevant question is not whether the District permitted all forms of religious activity, but whether it permitted the subject matter of the speech and then excluded one perspective. Lamb’s Chapel, 508 U.S. at 393-94; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831. A rule that permits discussion of family values but forbids discussion of family values “from a Christian perspective” is not viewpoint neutral. Good News Club, 533 U.S. at 112-13.

Because the District denied Lamb’s Chapel access solely on the basis of religious viewpoint, the judgment below should be reversed.
