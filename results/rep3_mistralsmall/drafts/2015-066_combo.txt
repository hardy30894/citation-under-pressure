**ARGUMENT**

I. The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts, and an uncounseled conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right is fundamental and applies to all criminal prosecutions, regardless of the forum. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963) (“The right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours.”); *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”). The Court has made clear that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal prosecutions but extend to state proceedings as well. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344-45; *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273-74 (1948) (holding that the Sixth Amendment applies to state prosecutions for “serious crimes”).

Tribal courts are not exempt from the Sixth Amendment’s reach. While tribal sovereignty is a recognized principle of federal law, see *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 322-23 (1978), the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not displaced by tribal sovereignty. The Court has long held that the Bill of Rights applies to tribal governments when they exercise authority delegated by Congress. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 382-83 (1896) (holding that the Fifth Amendment does not apply to tribal governments); *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 570-71 (1883) (same). However, where Congress has expressly or implicitly subjected tribal governments to constitutional constraints, those constraints apply. *Organized Village of Kake v. Egan*, 369 U.S. 60, 75 (1962) (“[T]ribal authority is subject to the federal Bill of Rights only where Congress has so provided.”). Here, Congress has not exempted tribal courts from the Sixth Amendment’s requirements. To the contrary, by incorporating tribal convictions into federal law as predicate offenses under § 117(a), Congress has implicitly subjected those convictions to the same constitutional scrutiny as state and federal convictions.

An uncounseled conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under § 117(a) because it violates the Sixth Amendment. The Court has repeatedly held that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is violated where a defendant is tried without counsel and sentenced to imprisonment. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344-45; *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 37 (1972) (extending *Gideon* to misdemeanors where imprisonment is imposed). Even where a defendant is not sentenced to imprisonment, the Court has held that the use of an uncounseled conviction to enhance punishment violates the Sixth Amendment. *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967) (“[A] conviction obtained without counsel cannot be used to support guilt of another offense.”); *United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 448 (1971) (holding that a sentencing court may not consider an uncounseled conviction in imposing sentence).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is consistent with this precedent. The court held that an uncounseled tribal court conviction could not be used as a predicate offense in a federal prosecution because it violated the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 1394-96. The court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state courts but apply to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. *Id.* at 1395. This reasoning is sound and should be affirmed.

II. The use of an uncounseled conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause requires that criminal proceedings be fundamentally fair. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941) (“In criminal trials a State can no more violate the constitutional requirements of due process than can the Federal Government.”). The use of an uncounseled conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) is fundamentally unfair because it relies on a conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115 (“[A] conviction obtained without counsel cannot be used to support guilt of another offense.”).

The Court has long held that the use of an uncounseled conviction to enhance punishment violates due process. *Tucker*, 404 U.S. at 448; *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954) (holding that a conviction obtained in violation of due process cannot be used to support a sentence enhancement). The same principle applies here. The use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) is a violation of due process because it relies on a conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment.

III. The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not frustrate the purpose of § 117(a) or undermine tribal sovereignty.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision does not frustrate the purpose of § 117(a) or undermine tribal sovereignty. To the contrary, it ensures that tribal courts comply with the Sixth Amendment’s requirements when prosecuting defendants who may later face federal prosecution under § 117(a). The Court has long held that tribal sovereignty is not absolute and is subject to federal law. *Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323-24. Where Congress has enacted legislation to protect vulnerable populations, such as victims of domestic violence, tribal courts must comply with constitutional requirements. *See Organized Village of Kake*, 369 U.S. at 75.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also does not create a circuit split. The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). However, these decisions are inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s precedent on the Sixth Amendment and due process. The Ninth Circuit’s decision is correct and should be affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. An uncounseled conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because it violates the Sixth Amendment and the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with Supreme Court precedent and should be affirmed.
