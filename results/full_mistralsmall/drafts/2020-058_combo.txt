**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) Provides That TPS Recipients Are “Considered as Being in, and Maintaining, Lawful Status as a Nonimmigrant,” Which Satisfies § 1255’s Admission Requirement.**

The Third Circuit erred by divorcing § 1254a(f)(4) from § 1255(a). Section 1254a(f)(4) explicitly states that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This provision does not merely confer a temporary shield from removal; it places TPS recipients in a statutorily recognized immigration status—nonimmigrant status—thereby satisfying § 1255(a)’s requirement that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to adjust status. *See Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 188 (1958) (“[O]nce an alien gains admission to the country, he is subject to the full range of the immigration laws.”); *United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day*, 279 U.S. 398, 402 (1929) (“The term ‘admission’ in the immigration laws has always been construed to mean lawful entry after inspection.”).

The Government’s argument that “lawful status” is distinct from “admission” ignores the statutory framework. *See Kaplan v. Tod*, 267 U.S. 228, 230 (1925) (“The meaning of a statute is to be looked for in its language.”). Section 1255(a) permits adjustment of status for aliens who are “inspected and admitted or paroled,” but it does not define “admission” in isolation. Rather, § 1101(a)(13)(A) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition does not preclude a subsequent grant of status from retroactively curing an initial unlawful entry. *See United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905) (“The power to exclude or to admit aliens is vested solely in the political departments of the government.”).

**II. The Statutory Context and Structure Confirm That § 1254a(f)(4) Was Intended to Operate in Conjunction with § 1255(a).**

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421 (1987), is misplaced. *Cardoza-Fonseca* does not support the proposition that § 1254a(f)(4) and § 1255(a) operate independently. Instead, it reinforces the principle that statutory provisions must be read harmoniously. *See id.* at 431 (“The starting point in every case involving construction of a statute is the language itself.”). Section 1254a(f)(4) was enacted to ensure that TPS recipients, who are already present in the United States, are not penalized for their unlawful entry when seeking adjustment of status. *See H.R. Rep. No. 99-460, at 10 (1986)* (“The purpose of this provision is to ensure that aliens granted TPS are not penalized for their unlawful presence in the United States.”).

The Government’s argument that § 1254a(h) requires a supermajority vote to adjust the status of TPS recipients is unavailing. *See United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.”). Section 1254a(h) addresses special legislation for TPS recipients, not their eligibility for adjustment of status under § 1255. The Third Circuit’s interpretation renders § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous, a result disfavored by the Supreme Court. *See id.*

**III. The Legislative History Demonstrates That Congress Intended TPS Recipients to Be Eligible for Adjustment of Status Under § 1255.**

The legislative history of the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 (IRCA) confirms that Congress intended TPS recipients to be eligible for adjustment of status. *See* S. Rep. No. 99-582, at 10 (1986) (“The committee intends that aliens granted TPS shall be eligible to adjust their status under § 1255.”). This intent is further evidenced by the fact that TPS recipients are explicitly placed in “lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” a status that § 1255(a) recognizes for adjustment purposes. *See Leng May Ma*, 357 U.S. at 188 (“Once an alien gains admission to the country, he is subject to the full range of the immigration laws.”).

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), Is Misplaced.**

The Third Circuit’s citation to *Hanif* is inapposite. *Hanif* involved a petitioner who had been paroled into the United States, not a TPS recipient. *See id.* at 482. The Court’s distinction between “admission” and “status” in *Hanif* does not apply here, where § 1254a(f)(4) explicitly places TPS recipients in a statutorily recognized nonimmigrant status. *See Kaplan*, 267 U.S. at 230 (“The meaning of a statute is to be looked for in its language.”).

**V. The Third Circuit’s Reliance on *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281 (3d Cir. 2013), Is Also Misplaced.**

Similarly, *Taveras* does not support the Third Circuit’s holding. *Taveras* involved a petitioner who had been placed in removal proceedings, not a TPS recipient seeking adjustment of status. *See id.* at 284. The Court’s distinction between “entry” and “adjustment of status” in *Taveras* does not apply here, where § 1254a(f)(4) explicitly places TPS recipients in a statutorily recognized nonimmigrant status. *See Kaplan*, 267 U.S. at 230.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that the Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” into the United States under § 1255. The plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), the statutory context and structure, and the legislative history all confirm that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. The Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
