**Argument**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because a reasonable jury could find Bora Corp. liable under § 905(b) for breaching the vessel’s “active control” duty. Under **Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981)**, a vessel that retains control over operations in the area of injury must exercise reasonable care to avoid exposing longshoremen to harm. The Supreme Court explained that, when a vessel “actively involves itself in the cargo operations and negligently injures a longshoreman,” liability may attach. **Id. at 167.** The Court also recognized that the vessel must act with reasonable care when it “remains in active control of the area or equipment used by the stevedore.” **Id. at 167-68.**

That standard is met here. The record shows that the snatch blocks were part of the ship’s own hatch-cover system, were fixed to the hatch covers, weighed about 100 pounds each, and were moved by the ship’s crew. The crew’s function was not passive; it operated the pulley system, opened and closed the hatch covers, and was responsible for returning the blocks to the up position after each hatch was opened. A jury could readily conclude that Bora retained operational control over the snatch block at issue and over the dangerous condition created by leaving it in the down position.

This Court’s decisions applying Scindia confirm that the inquiry turns on whether the vessel retained control over the relevant instrumentality and work area. In **Gravatt v. City of New York, 226 F.3d 108 (3d Cir. 2000)**, the Court held that active control exists where the vessel retains authority over the manner in which the work is being performed or over the instrumentality causing the injury. **Id. at 121-22.** Likewise, in **Hill v. Reederei F. Laeisz G.m.b.H., 435 F.3d 404 (3d Cir. 2006)**, the Court emphasized that the vessel’s duty arises when it “retains active control over the area or equipment” and the longshoreman is injured by a danger associated with that control. **Id. at 413-14.** Bora’s crew, not Serbin or his stevedore employer, controlled the hatch-cover mechanism and the placement of the snatch blocks. The danger was thus created and perpetuated by the vessel’s own operational activity.

The district court appears to have treated Serbin’s decision to move the block as dispositive. That was error. A longshoreman’s effort to address a hazardous shipboard condition does not eliminate vessel negligence as a matter of law. Under Scindia, the vessel’s duty is breached if its own active operations create or maintain a hazard, even if the longshoreman attempts to work around it. **Scindia, 451 U.S. at 167-68.** And under ordinary negligence principles governing § 905(b), comparative fault is for the trier of fact. **Edmonds v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique, 443 U.S. 256, 259-60 (1979)** (recognizing that vessel negligence may be apportioned with longshoreman fault). Serbin testified that he moved the block to avoid delay in unloading fruit and because he had moved such blocks before. That testimony may support comparative negligence, but it does not compel judgment for Bora.

Nor can Bora avoid liability by characterizing the snatch block as a condition within the stevedore’s exclusive sphere. The ship’s crew had the responsibility to reset the blocks, the blocks were fixed components of the ship’s hatch system, and the crew used them as part of the vessel’s own cargo-handling equipment. Courts have repeatedly held that where the vessel’s own gear is in use and under its control, the vessel may be liable for negligent operation. See **Lieberman v. Matson Navigation Co., 300 F.2d 661, 664-65 (9th Cir. 1962)** (vessel liable where its crew’s handling of ship’s gear created unsafe condition). The same principle applies here.

At minimum, the evidence creates a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether Bora retained active control of the snatch block and whether the crew’s failure to reposition it was negligent. Summary judgment is therefore improper. See **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986)**. Because a reasonable jury could find that Bora breached its active operations duty under Scindia and that this breach contributed to Serbin’s fall and knee injury, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
