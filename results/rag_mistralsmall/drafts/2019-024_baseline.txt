**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Held That “Actual Knowledge” Under ERISA § 1113(2) Requires Knowledge That The Alleged Breach Occurred And That It Was A Violation Of ERISA.**

The text of ERISA § 1113(2) provides that an action “may not be commenced more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this provision to require that a plaintiff have actual knowledge both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was imprudent under ERISA. *See Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee v. Sulyma*, 912 F.3d 573, 582 (9th Cir. 2019) (“[A]ctual knowledge of the breach” under § 1113(2) requires knowledge that the investments occurred and that they were imprudent). This interpretation is consistent with the statutory text and the Supreme Court’s guidance on ERISA’s fiduciary duties.

In *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996), the Supreme Court explained that ERISA imposes a duty of care on fiduciaries to act “solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries” and to exercise “the care, skill, prudence, and diligence ... that a prudent man acting in a like capacity ... would use.” This duty is the foundation for claims alleging imprudent investment decisions. *See id.* Thus, to have “actual knowledge of the breach or violation” under § 1113(2), a plaintiff must know both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was imprudent—that is, a violation of the duty of care.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also aligns with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co.*, 571 U.S. 99, 103 (2013), which emphasized that ERISA’s limitations provisions are designed to balance the interests of plan participants and fiduciaries. Allowing a limitations period to run from mere disclosure of investment decisions—without requiring knowledge that those decisions were imprudent—would undermine this balance by barring claims before participants could reasonably be expected to understand the legal significance of the information.

**II. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Rejected Intel’s Argument That Sulyma’s Receipt Of Disclosures Alone Triggered The Statute Of Limitations.**

Intel argues that Sulyma’s receipt of disclosures about the investment funds’ asset allocations and strategies was sufficient to trigger the three-year limitations period under § 1113(2). This argument misreads the statute and the Ninth Circuit’s precedent. The statute requires “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not merely knowledge of the underlying facts. *See Sulyma*, 912 F.3d at 582.

The Supreme Court has recognized that ERISA’s limitations provisions are designed to protect participants from being unfairly barred from bringing claims before they can reasonably be expected to understand their legal significance. *See Heimeshoff*, 571 U.S. at 103. In *Sulyma*, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that receipt of disclosures alone does not equate to actual knowledge of a breach or violation, particularly where the plaintiff did not read or recall the disclosures. *See Sulyma*, 912 F.3d at 582. This interpretation ensures that the limitations period does not begin to run until the participant has actual knowledge of the legal significance of the fiduciary’s actions.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Does Not Create A Circuit Split Or Undermine ERISA’s Policy Objectives.**

Intel argues that the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation creates a circuit split and undermines ERISA’s policy objectives by allowing plaintiffs to delay filing claims indefinitely. This argument is unpersuasive. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation is consistent with the statutory text and the Supreme Court’s guidance on ERISA’s fiduciary duties. *See Sulyma*, 912 F.3d at 582; *Lockheed*, 517 U.S. at 887.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation does not create a circuit split. Other circuits have similarly held that actual knowledge under § 1113(2) requires knowledge of the legal significance of the fiduciary’s actions. *See, e.g., Glista v. Feinberg*, 921 F.3d 16, 24 (1st Cir. 2019) (holding that actual knowledge requires knowledge of the breach and its legal significance). This interpretation ensures that the limitations period does not begin to run until the participant has actual knowledge of the legal significance of the fiduciary’s actions, which is consistent with ERISA’s policy objectives.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires knowledge that the alleged breach occurred and that it was a violation of ERISA. This interpretation is consistent with the statutory text, the Supreme Court’s guidance on ERISA’s fiduciary duties, and the policy objectives of ERISA’s limitations provisions. The Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s decision.
