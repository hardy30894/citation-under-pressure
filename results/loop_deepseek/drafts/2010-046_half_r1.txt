## ARGUMENT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar the three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy ongoing Eighth Amendment violations. The order satisfies each of the PLRA’s requirements: it is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the constitutional violations, and is the least intrusive means of doing so. The three-judge court expressly found that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the unconstitutional conditions and that no other relief would remedy the violation. Those findings are amply supported by the record and are entitled to deference.

Federal courts have long possessed the equitable power to remedy constitutional violations by ordering appropriate relief tailored to the wrong. The conditions in California’s prisons—pervasive inadequate medical care, untreated mental illness, and preventable deaths—violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 371 (1910) (holding that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that involve “torture” or a “lingering death” and that the Amendment’s protections extend beyond “the forms of punishment” historically recognized). The systemic failures in California’s prisons are precisely the kind of barbarous treatment the Eighth Amendment forbids.

This Court has repeatedly affirmed the breadth of federal equitable authority to remedy constitutional wrongs. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954), the Court declared that in fashioning equitable relief, courts must be guided by “equitable principles” and that “traditional equity” requires “the elimination of a great variety of obstacles.” *Id.* at 499. The Court further explained that courts may “require the defendants to make a prompt and reasonable start toward full compliance” and “take such additional steps as may be necessary” to achieve a non-discriminatory school system. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955). A court may, in its remedial discretion, require the necessary steps to bring a State into constitutional compliance, even when those steps involve the release of prisoners.

Similarly, in *Pennsylvania v. Board of Directors of City Trusts*, 353 U.S. 230 (1957), this Court reaffirmed that federal courts possess the authority to ensure that state actors comply with constitutional requirements, holding that a state agency could not administer a racially restrictive trust consistent with the Fourteenth Amendment. *Id.* at 231. The principle is clear: a federal court’s equitable power may reach state policies and practices that violate the Constitution.

The PLRA does not displace these fundamental equitable principles. The PLRA’s requirements—that relief be “narrowly drawn,” “extend no further than necessary,” and be the “least intrusive means”—echo traditional equitable standards. They do not erect a per se bar to population caps when the court finds, as it did here, that nothing short of a significant reduction in the inmate population will cure the constitutional violation.

Moreover, the three-judge court complied with the PLRA’s procedural requirements. The order was issued by a three-judge panel convened under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The court made the requisite findings that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations and that “no other relief” would suffice. The court also found that the order is the “least intrusive means” of correcting the violation. These findings are supported by voluminous evidence, including the State’s own admissions that for years it failed to provide constitutionally adequate medical care and that overcrowding made compliance impossible.

The State’s suggestion that the PLRA forbids any order requiring the release of prisoners is incorrect. The PLRA permits a prisoner release order if the three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violation and that “no other relief” will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). The court made both findings here. The State’s argument would effectively read these provisions out of the statute, rendering the PLRA a bar to any remedy regardless of the severity of the constitutional violation.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts may enforce the Constitution even against recalcitrant States. In *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346 (1880), the Court held that “the Constitution of the United States, in its own efficacy and by its own operation” reaches state officers, and that “the judicial power of the United States can be exerted to compel” compliance with constitutional duties. *Id.* at 346-347. If a State defaults in its duty, the judicial power may be exercised to compel performance. The three-judge court acted within that tradition.

Finally, this Court has long recognized that federal courts may hold persons in contempt for defiance of orders necessary to effectuate constitutional remedies. In *Ex parte Robinson*, 86 U.S. 505, 510-511 (1874), the Court affirmed that the power to punish for contempt is “inherent in all courts of justice” and is “essential to the preservation of order in judicial proceedings, and to the enforcement of the judgments, orders, and writs of the courts.” The State consented to the original remedial orders and to the appointment of the Receiver; it cannot now claim immunity from the consequences of its own failure to comply.

The PLRA does not insulate California from its constitutional obligations. The three-judge court’s order is a lawful exercise of equitable authority, fully consistent with the statute and with this Court’s precedents. The judgment should be affirmed.
