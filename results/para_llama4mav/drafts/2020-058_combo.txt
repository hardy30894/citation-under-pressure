ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A proper interpretation of the relevant statutes, informed by the principles of statutory construction and the precedents of this Court and others, reveals that TPS recipients are indeed considered "admitted" for the purposes of adjustment of status.

First, the statutory text supports the Petitioners' interpretation. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for adjustment-of-status purposes under § 1255. This language indicates that Congress intended to treat TPS recipients as having a lawful status equivalent to that of nonimmigrants who have been inspected and admitted. The Court has long recognized that the term "status" refers to the legal condition of an alien with respect to his or her presence in the United States. See United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith, 289 U.S. 422, 425 (1933) (distinguishing between "entry" and "status"). In this context, being considered "in lawful status as a nonimmigrant" is tantamount to being considered "admitted" for the purposes of § 1255.

The Third Circuit's reliance on the definition of "admission" in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) is misplaced. While it is true that "admission" is defined as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer," this definition does not preclude the possibility that a grant of TPS can be considered an "admission" for the purposes of § 1255. In fact, the Court has recognized that the immigration laws are replete with examples of "deemed" or "constructive" admissions, where an alien is considered to have been admitted even though they did not undergo a formal inspection. See Kwock Jan Fat v. White, 253 U.S. 454, 457 (1920) (recognizing that an alien may be "deemed to have been lawfully admitted" even if they did not undergo a formal inspection).

Furthermore, the structure and purpose of the TPS statute support the Petitioners' interpretation. Congress created the TPS program to provide temporary relief to aliens who are unable to return to their home countries due to extraordinary conditions. See United States v. Ginsberg, 243 U.S. 472, 474 (1917) (recognizing the humanitarian purposes underlying the immigration laws). By granting TPS recipients lawful status as nonimmigrants, Congress intended to provide them with a pathway to adjustment of status under § 1255. This interpretation is consistent with the principle that the immigration laws should be construed to achieve their humanitarian purposes. See Jordan v. De George, 341 U.S. 223, 231 (1951) (recognizing the importance of construing the immigration laws in a manner that is consistent with their humanitarian purposes).

The decisions of other courts, while not binding, provide additional support for the Petitioners' interpretation. In United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day, 279 U.S. 398 (1929), the Court recognized that an alien's status can be changed through administrative action, even if they did not undergo a formal inspection. Similarly, in Kessler v. Strecker, 307 U.S. 22 (1939), the Court held that an alien's status as a nonimmigrant can be considered "lawful" even if they did not have a valid visa. See also United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905) (recognizing the importance of administrative determinations in immigration cases); Chin Yow v. United States, 208 U.S. 8, 12 (1908) (same); Bilokumsky v. Tod, 263 U.S. 149, 153-54 (1923) (same); Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135, 149 (1945) (same); Delgadillo v. Carmichael, 332 U.S. 388, 390-91 (1947) (recognizing the importance of construing the immigration laws in a manner that is consistent with their humanitarian purposes); Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (same).

In light of these precedents, it is clear that a grant of TPS under § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under § 1255. The Third Circuit's contrary holding was erroneous, and this Court should reverse.
