ARGUMENT

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the statute of limitations period. When a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal from the conspiracy before that period, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member during the relevant time. The court below erred by placing the burden of persuasion on the defendants.

I. The Burden of Proof on Every Element, Including Membership Within the Limitations Period, Rests on the Government

The fundamental principle that the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt applies to every element of the offense. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). For a conspiracy charge, the government must prove that an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy occurred within the statute of limitations. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). The statute of limitations runs from the last overt act, and the government must establish that the defendant’s membership continued into that period. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (if a defendant withdraws, “the statute of limitations would run from the date of withdrawal”). Thus, the government’s burden includes proving that the conspiracy was still active and that the defendant was a member during the limitations period.

II. Withdrawal Does Not Shift the Burden of Persuasion to the Defendant

Withdrawal from a conspiracy requires an affirmative act inconsistent with the conspiracy’s objectives. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. at 369. Once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw and remained a member within the limitations period. *United States v. Cohen*, 145 F.2d 82, 87 (2d Cir. 1944) (“the burden of proving that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the period of the statute of limitations rests upon the government”); *United States v. Brennan*, 136 F.2d 787, 789 (2d Cir. 1943) (government must prove membership within limitations period, and if defendant shows withdrawal, government must prove continued membership). The defendant’s evidence of withdrawal does not require the defendant to prove it; rather, it triggers the government’s obligation to prove the defendant’s ongoing membership. *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 845 (2d Cir. 1939) (government must prove that conspiracy continued into limitations period).

III. The D.C. Circuit Erred by Placing the Burden on the Defendants

The court below held that the defendants bore the burden of proving that the conspiracy ended before the limitations period, and that the government had no burden to prove that the conspiracy continued. This contravenes the settled rule that the government must prove every element of the charge beyond a reasonable doubt. *Stringer v. United States*, 233 F. 19, 22 (9th Cir. 1916) (burden on government to prove all elements, including that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period). The leaders’ split before the limitations period raised a factual question whether the conspiracy continued. The government introduced no evidence of any overt act within the period. Under *Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 607, the government’s failure to prove an overt act within the limitations period is fatal. The court below compounded this error by requiring the defendants to prove the conspiracy’s termination.

IV. Conclusion

The government failed to prove that the conspiracy, or the defendants’ membership in it, continued into the statute of limitations period. The judgment should be reversed.
