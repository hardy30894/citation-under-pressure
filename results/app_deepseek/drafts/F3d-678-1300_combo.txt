ARGUMENT

I. The Jury’s Invalidity Finding Lacks Substantial Evidence Because Facebook Did Not Prove That the Leader2Leader® Product Embodied Each Claimed Limitation Before the Critical Date.

A patent is invalid under § 102(b) only if the prior public use or sale was of “the identical invention” as claimed. *Coffin v. Ogden*, 85 U.S. 120, 124 (1873). The evidence must show that every element of the asserted claims was present in the allegedly barring device. *Consolidated Fruit-Jar Co. v. Wright*, 94 U.S. 92, 95 (1876). Here, Facebook’s proof rests on a white paper and a few demonstrations that do not establish that the Digital Leaderboard® engine, as used in the Leader2Leader® product, actually performed the claimed “dynamically associating metadata” and “tracking movement” steps before December 10, 2002. No witness testified that the product’s metadata tagging was “dynamic” as required by the claims. The jury’s inference that the product necessarily practiced those limitations is speculation, not substantial evidence. *Pennock v. Dialogue*, 27 U.S. 1, 19 (1829) (public use must be “of the thing patented” in its complete form).

II. Any Use of the Leader2Leader® Product Was Experimental and Therefore Not a Public Use Under § 102(b).

Even if the product embodied the claimed invention, the record shows that Leader’s pre-critical-date activities were experimental, not public. “The use of an invention by the inventor himself, or by any other person under his direction, by way of experiment, and in order to bring the invention to perfection, has never been regarded as a public use.” *Elizabeth v. Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 134 (1877). Leader’s founder testified that the product was still under development through 2002, and the white paper describing the product as “operational now with low user volumes” was part of a confidential proposal, not a public offer. *Andrews v. Hovey*, 123 U.S. 267, 275 (1887) (experimental use includes “test[ing] the machine” to ensure it “would answer the purpose intended”). Facebook presented no evidence that Leader had abandoned control over the product or that the demonstrations were for commercial exploitation rather than testing. *Smith v. Goodyear Dental Vulcanite Co.*, 93 U.S. 486, 494 (1876) (public use requires “the public had a right to use it” without restriction).

III. The Alleged Offer for Sale to Wright Patterson Air Force Base Was Not a Definite Commercial Offer for a Completed Invention.

The on-sale bar requires a “completed sale or offer to sell” of the invention. *McClurg v. Kingsland*, 42 U.S. 202, 206 (1843). Leader’s January 2002 white paper was a preliminary proposal offering 20,000 licenses, but the paper did not identify a specific price, delivery date, or binding terms. Moreover, the invention was not yet “actually reduced to practice” because the metadata functionality was still being refined. *Christie v. Seybold*, 55 F. 69, 72 (6th Cir. 1893) (reduction to practice requires “the embodiment of the invention in some tangible form that shows its practical efficiency”). Without a completed invention, an invitation to negotiate does not trigger the bar. *Egbert v. Lippmann*, 104 U.S. 333, 336 (1881) (public use requires “the invention was in public use or on sale, with the consent and allowance of the inventor”).

IV. The District Court Erred in Denying Leader’s Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law Because No Reasonable Jury Could Have Found Invalidity by Clear and Convincing Evidence.

The presumption of patent validity requires a challenger to prove invalidity by clear and convincing evidence. *Hildreth v. Mastoras*, 257 U.S. 27, 32 (1921). Facebook’s evidence was at best ambiguous: the white paper described a system that could be interpreted as practicing the claims, but the witnesses could not confirm that the claimed dynamic metadata tracking was actually implemented. The jury’s finding of invalidity rests on inference piled upon inference, which is insufficient to meet the clear-and-convincing standard. *The Telephone Cases*, 126 U.S. 1, 538 (1888) (public use must be proved by “clear and satisfactory proof”). Because the evidence was legally insufficient, the district court should have granted Leader’s motion. *De Forest Radio Co. v. General Electric Co.*, 283 U.S. 664, 685 (1931) (judgment as a matter of law proper when no substantial evidence supports verdict).

For these reasons, the judgment of invalidity should be reversed, or, in the alternative, a new trial should be ordered.
