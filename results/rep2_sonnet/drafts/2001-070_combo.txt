**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, forbids "cruel and unusual punishments." Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962). But this Court's precedents establish that the Amendment's proportionality inquiry proceeds punishment-by-punishment, not class-by-class. An offender's mental condition is properly weighed by the sentencer as mitigation; it has never been treated as an absolute constitutional bar to an otherwise valid punishment. Because the death penalty itself is not "cruel and unusual" under any test this Court has applied, and because Virginia's capital-sentencing procedure permitted Atkins to place his intellectual limitations before the jury as mitigation, his sentence comports with the Eighth Amendment.

**I. The Eighth Amendment Regulates Modes and Degrees of Punishment, Not Categories of Otherwise-Culpable Offenders.**

Since Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130 (1879), this Court's cruel-and-unusual-punishments jurisprudence has focused on the character of the punishment itself. Wilkerson held that the Clause forbids "punishments of torture, . . . and all others in the same line of unnecessary cruelty," id. at 136, while approving execution by firing squad as a permissible mode of carrying out a valid sentence. In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), likewise upheld electrocution, explaining that death is not "cruel" within the Constitution's meaning unless it involves "something inhuman and barbarous, something more than the mere extinguishment of life." Id. at 447. And in Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), the plurality reaffirmed that the Clause forbids only "the infliction of unnecessary pain in the execution of the death sentence," id. at 463—not the penalty of death for a properly convicted offender.

Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), does not depart from this framework. Weems held that a particular sentence was disproportionate to a particular offense; it did not hold that an entire class of offenders is constitutionally immune from a punishment otherwise valid for the crime committed. Weems teaches only that "a principle to be vital must be capable of wider application than the mischief which gave it birth," id. at 373—a principle of flexible proportionality applied to the punishment measured against the offense, not a license to carve blanket exemptions for classes of defendants based on a personal characteristic such as intelligence.

Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), confirms this understanding. The plurality observed that "the Amendment must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," id. at 101, but applied that standard to hold that denationalization was disproportionate to the offense of wartime desertion—a punishment-specific inquiry, not the creation of an offender-status exemption from a concededly permissible penalty.

**II. Mental Retardation Bears on Individualized Culpability and Is Properly Addressed as Mitigation, Not a Categorical Bar.**

This Court has consistently left questions of mental capacity and criminal responsibility to individualized adjudication rather than fixing a constitutional rule keyed to diagnostic labels. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court sustained Oregon's insanity standard against due process attack, recognizing the wide disagreement among those who have studied the relationship between mental impairment and responsibility, id. at 800-01, and declining to constitutionalize any single test for how capacity bears on culpability. Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9 (1950), similarly held that the procedure for resolving an offender's competency in connection with execution is a matter for the States, sustaining Georgia's practice of committing that determination to the Governor rather than a court. Both decisions confirm that mental-capacity questions are for the processes each State provides, not for a categorical exemption imposed from without.

Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), reinforces the point. Rejecting the argument that Robinson v. California, which held that punishing the mere "status" of narcotic addiction—"even though [the defendant] has never touched any narcotic drug within the State," 370 U.S. at 667—violates the Eighth Amendment, compelled a similar rule for chronic alcoholics prosecuted for public drunkenness, the plurality distinguished punishment for status from punishment for a criminal act. Atkins was not punished for a status. He was punished, after individualized capital-sentencing proceedings, for abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder that a properly instructed jury found him capable of committing with the requisite culpability. His asserted intellectual limitations were placed before that jury through psychological testimony, and the jury, so informed, returned a sentence of death. That individualized process—not a categorical judicial exemption—is what the Eighth Amendment, as construed in Wilkerson, Kemmler, Francis, Leland, and Solesbee, contemplates.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
