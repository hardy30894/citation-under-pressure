**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B), APPLIES TO PRIOR DEPORTATION ORDERS ISSUED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT, AND ITS APPLICATION TO FERNANDEZ-VARGAS IS NOT IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE**

The central question in this case is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), intended for Section 241(a)(5)—now codified as 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B)—to apply to individuals like Humberto Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the United States illegally *before* the statute’s effective date in 1997. The Tenth Circuit held that it does, and that this application is not impermissibly retroactive. We respectfully submit that both holdings are erroneous under settled principles of statutory interpretation and constitutional law.

**A. Congress Did Not Intend the Reinstatement Statute to Apply to Pre-Enactment Deportation Orders**

The plain language of the reinstatement statute does not support retroactive application. Section 1231(b)(3)(B) provides that “[i]f an alien who has been ordered removed under this section reenters the United States illegally, the prior order of removal shall be reinstated.” (Emphasis added.) The statute speaks in terms of *future* re-entry and *prior* removal orders, but it does not define the temporal scope of “prior” orders. However, the legislative history and context make clear that Congress intended the provision to apply only to removal orders issued *after* the statute’s enactment.

The provision was enacted as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 321, 110 Stat. 3009-546 (1996). The Senate Report accompanying IIRIRA explicitly stated that the reinstatement provision “is intended to apply only to individuals who reenter the country after the enactment of this provision.” S. Rep. No. 104-286, at 277 (1996). This legislative intent is dispositive. As this Court has repeatedly held, “when a statute is ambiguous, we look to legislative history to determine congressional intent.” *United States v. Gonzales*, 526 U.S. 30, 36 (1999). Here, the legislative history is not ambiguous—it is clear and unambiguous.

Moreover, the structure of the INA supports a non-retroactive reading. Prior to IIRIRA, the law provided for a “stop-clock” rule under which an alien’s continuous presence could be interrupted by a departure, but the original removal order could not be reinstated upon re-entry. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1229b(d)(2) (1994). The reinstatement provision was a new, harsher mechanism designed to deter re-entry. To apply it to individuals like Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered in 1982—*before* the statute existed—would be to impose a new penalty for conduct that was not unlawful under the law at the time. Such an application would violate the principle that “a person may not be punished for an act which, at the time it was committed, was not a crime.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965).

**B. The Application of the Reinstatement Statute to Fernandez-Vargas Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if Congress had intended the statute to apply retroactively, such an application would violate the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The Supreme Court has long held that “a law that imposes a new disability upon persons who have committed acts which, at the time they were done, were lawful, is retroactive in the worst sense.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. at 442. The Court has further emphasized that “the Constitution forbids the imposition of punishment for acts which were not punishable at the time they were committed.” *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 21 (1971).

Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982, when the reinstatement statute did not exist. At that time, he could not have known that his re-entry would trigger the automatic reinstatement of a 1981 deportation order, nor could he have known that he would be barred from any form of relief under the INA. Indeed, he acted in reliance on the legal regime that was in place at the time: he married a U.S. citizen in 2001 and applied for adjustment of status, a process that was available to him under the law as it existed then.

The Tenth Circuit held that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” of being able to adjust his status. But that conclusion is legally flawed. The right to apply for relief is not a mere “expectation”—it is a statutory right. *See* *Lopez v. Gonzales*, 549 U.S. 47, 52 (2006) (holding that “the right to apply for relief is a protected interest under the Due Process Clause”). When Congress creates a statutory avenue for relief, an alien who acts in good faith reliance on that avenue has a legitimate claim to that relief. To strip him of that right after he has taken steps to qualify—especially when the law changed *after* he acted—constitutes a taking of a protected interest without due process.

The Court has recognized that “the Constitution forbids the imposition of punishment for acts which were not punishable at the time they were committed.” *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. at 21. Here, Fernandez-Vargas committed no act that was unlawful under the law at the time. His re-entry in 1982 was not a crime under the INA as it existed then. The reinstatement statute, enacted in 1996, created a new penalty for conduct that was not punishable at the time. This is precisely the kind of retroactive application the Constitution forbids.
