**Argument**

The Florida Supreme Court correctly rejected Hurst’s Sixth and Eighth Amendment challenges.

I. **Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, as applied here, satisfied Ring and the Sixth Amendment.**

Ring v. Arizona held that “the Sixth Amendment requires jury finding of any aggravating circumstance necessary for imposition of the death penalty.” 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002). But Ring did not announce a broader rule requiring the jury to perform every sentencing function in a capital case. The Court’s holding was tied to the Apprendi principle: any fact that increases the maximum punishment must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Ring, 536 U.S. at 602-03; Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000).

Florida’s scheme, as upheld below, fits within that constitutional rule because the jury convicted Hurst of first-degree murder, and the sentencing process preserved the jury’s role in finding the facts relevant to death eligibility. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a State may assign different components of a capital sentencing process to judge and jury so long as the Sixth Amendment fact-finding requirement is honored. See Harris v. Alabama, 513 U.S. 504, 515 (1995); Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639, 648 (1990), overruled on other grounds by Ring, 536 U.S. 584.

Hurst’s argument that the jury must unanimously recommend death also goes beyond Ring. The Sixth Amendment guarantees a jury determination of the facts that make a defendant death-eligible, not a unanimous sentencing recommendation. The Court has never held that unanimity is constitutionally required in capital sentencing recommendations. See Johnson v. Louisiana, 406 U.S. 356, 359-62 (1972); Apodaca v. Oregon, 406 U.S. 404, 410-12 (1972). Florida’s advisory recommendation procedure therefore did not violate the jury-trial right merely because the recommendation here was 7-5.

Nor does Hurst’s mental-retardation claim alter the Sixth Amendment analysis. In Atkins v. Virginia, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of the mentally retarded, but it expressly left “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction” to the States. 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002). States may assign that determination to the judge, the jury, or both, and the Constitution does not require a particular procedure. See Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353-54 (2004). Florida’s decision to allow a court to determine mental retardation as a bar to execution, while permitting the defendant to present such evidence in mitigation, is therefore permissible under Atkins.

II. **Florida’s sentencing scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment.**

The Eighth Amendment requires heightened reliability in capital sentencing, but it does not mandate unanimity in every aspect of the process. See Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976); Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). The constitutional question is whether the procedure creates an unacceptable risk of arbitrary death sentences. Florida’s scheme does not.

The Supreme Court has upheld capital sentencing systems that vest important sentencing determinations in the judge after the jury’s participation. See Proffitt v. Florida, 428 U.S. 242, 251-53 (1976); Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 464-65 (1984), overruled on other grounds by Hurst. Those decisions reflect that the Eighth Amendment does not require the specific unanimity rule Hurst urges.

Likewise, the exclusion of mental retardation as a freestanding defense before the jury did not offend the Eighth Amendment. Atkins prohibits execution of mentally retarded offenders, but it leaves the manner of implementation to the States. 536 U.S. at 317. Florida’s procedure allowed Hurst to present evidence of intellectual impairment and brain abnormalities as mitigation, and the sentencing judge expressly considered that evidence. That is all the Eighth Amendment requires. See Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978); Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 113-15 (1982).

Finally, the Florida Supreme Court’s decision accords with the Court’s repeated recognition that death sentences may be imposed through carefully guided procedures that channel sentencing discretion. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 189-95 (1976). Hurst received a jury trial, a capital sentencing hearing, and judicial review. The Constitution does not require more.

**Conclusion**

Because Florida’s sentencing procedure complied with Ring, Atkins, and the Eighth Amendment’s reliability requirements, the judgment below should be affirmed.
