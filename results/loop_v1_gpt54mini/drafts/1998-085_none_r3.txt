Respondent’s punitive-damages claim fails because Title VII does not make exemplary damages automatic upon proof of intentional discrimination. Congress authorized punitive damages only when the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights” of the plaintiff. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That statutory text requires a heightened culpable mental state; it does not impose punitive liability whenever a plaintiff proves a discriminatory employment decision. The court below therefore correctly refused to treat this case as punishment-worthy absent additional aggravating circumstances.

The common law confirms that punitive damages are exceptional. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court recognized that exemplary damages are not the ordinary consequence of liability. In The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818), the Court explained that punitive damages depend on aggravated fault rather than mere wrongfulness. Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), likewise treated punitive recovery as limited to cases of serious culpability. Those decisions reflect the settled principle that punishment is reserved for especially blameworthy conduct, not every proven violation.

The Court’s civil-rights precedents follow the same line. Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880), condemned racial exclusion from juries, but it did not suggest that every constitutional violation warrants punitive sanctions. Virginia v. Rives, 100 U.S. 313 (1880), and Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339 (1880), also invalidated discriminatory state action without equating liability with punishment. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), similarly established unlawful discrimination in the administration of law, not an automatic right to exemplary damages. These cases show that discrimination and punitive entitlement are distinct questions.

That distinction matters here. Petitioner proved, at most, that ADA unlawfully failed to promote her. The jury’s back-pay award addressed that injury. But punitive damages are a different matter: they are reserved for cases in which the defendant’s conduct is sufficiently aggravated to justify punishment. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), and Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251 (1946), confirm that the law distinguishes between compensatory recovery and enhanced damages. Nothing in those decisions suggests that exemplary damages follow automatically from liability.

Petitioner’s approach would collapse Congress’s separate “malice or reckless indifference” requirement into the bare fact of intentional discrimination. That construction would deprive the statutory limitation of independent force. Courts do not read statutes that way. Ex parte Virginia, 100 U.S. 339 (1880), and Yick Wo, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), both reflect that discriminatory liability turns on proof of unlawful action, but punitive sanctions require something more. Congress’s inclusion of a distinct punitive standard therefore must be given effect.

The better reading, consistent with longstanding punitive-damages doctrine, is that Title VII permits punitive damages only where the employer’s conduct reflects the kind of aggravated blameworthiness traditionally associated with exemplary awards. Day, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363; The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546; Lake Shore, 147 U.S. 101. Petitioner’s evidence supported a finding of discrimination and back pay, but not the additional showing necessary for punitive relief under 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1).

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
