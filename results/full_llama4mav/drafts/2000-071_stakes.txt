The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum. 

The Supreme Court has held that, in a limited public forum, the government may reserve the forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics and may exclude others based on subject matter or speaker identity. See Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The key consideration is whether the exclusion is viewpoint-neutral. Id. at 829-30. 

Here, Milford's community use policy excludes religious worship and instruction, and the school applied this policy to exclude the Good News Club. The Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit correctly determined that the Club's activities were "quintessentially religious" and not merely the teaching of morals from a religious perspective. See Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). 

The Club's activities, which included prayer, Bible lessons, and singing songs with religious content, went beyond the discussion of morals and character from a religious viewpoint. The Supreme Court has recognized that while a religious perspective can be a viewpoint on a secular subject, it is not always possible to neatly separate viewpoint from subject matter. See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District, 508 U.S. 384, 394-95 (1993). However, in this case, the Club's activities were not merely a discussion of morals and character from a religious viewpoint but were instead focused on religious instruction and worship.

Furthermore, the Establishment Clause concerns that arise when a public school permits religious worship or instruction on its premises justify Milford's exclusion of the Good News Club. The Supreme Court has held that the Establishment Clause prohibits government actions that have the effect of endorsing or promoting religion. See County of Allegheny v. American Civil Liberties Union Greater Pittsburgh Chapter, 492 U.S. 573, 592-94 (1989). By excluding the Club, Milford avoided the risk of being perceived as endorsing or promoting the Club's religious message.

In sum, Milford's exclusion of the Good News Club from its facilities was a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the school's concern about violating the Establishment Clause justified its actions. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
