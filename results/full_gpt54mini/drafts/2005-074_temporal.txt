Petitioner respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) does not reach him, and if it does, its application here is impermissibly retroactive. The Court should reverse.

I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date

A statute is presumed to operate prospectively absent “the legislative intent to the contrary.” **Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964)**. Courts do not lightly infer that Congress meant to attach new legal consequences to completed conduct. **Sohn v. Waterson, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 596, 599-600 (1873)**. That principle is especially strong where the statute would extinguish an alien’s opportunity to seek relief that existed when he reentered and remained in the United States.

Section 241(a)(5) speaks in the present tense to an alien who “has reentered” illegally after prior removal, but the question is not whether petitioner fits a grammatical description; it is whether Congress clearly intended the provision to reach pre-enactment reentries. In **Union Pacific Railroad Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co., 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913)**, the Court held that statutes “are not to be given a retroactive effect unless such construction is required by explicit language or by necessary implication.” Nothing in § 241(a)(5) supplies that explicit command.

To the contrary, where Congress intends retroactive reach, it knows how to say so. The absence of express retroactive language here is decisive under the settled presumption against retroactivity. **United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806)**; **McClurg v. Kingsland, 42 U.S. (1 How.) 202, 206 (1843)**. Because petitioner reentered in 1982, long before the statute became effective in 1997, the Court should construe § 241(a)(5) not to govern his case.

II. If Section 241(a)(5) Reaches Petitioner, Its Application Is Impermissibly Retroactive

A law is retroactive when it “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability” in respect to past transactions. **Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)**. This Court has long refused to give statutes that effect absent unmistakable congressional direction. **McCullough v. Virginia, 172 U.S. 102, 123-24 (1898)**.

Applied here, § 241(a)(5) unquestionably attaches a new disability to petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry and long-term residence. At the time he reentered, the immigration laws permitted him to remain while pursuing whatever relief might then become available. The new statute instead converts that past entry into a permanent bar and strips all relief. That is precisely the kind of “new disability” disfavored by the retroactivity canon. **Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, 22 F. Cas. at 767**.

The retroactive effect is particularly severe because petitioner did not merely commit a past act and walk away; he lived in the United States for nearly two decades under the prior legal regime. He later married a United States citizen and sought adjustment of status. Under longstanding retroactivity principles, a statute may not upset settled expectations by attaching new legal consequences to conduct that was complete before enactment. **Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534 (1922)**; **Sturges v. Carter, 114 U.S. 511, 519 (1885)**.

The Government may argue that petitioner had no “vested right” to discretionary relief. But the retroactivity inquiry is not limited to vested property rights. It also forbids statutes that “destroy or impair” the legal significance of past conduct. **Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, 22 F. Cas. at 767**. Petitioner’s claim is not that he had a guaranteed entitlement to adjustment; it is that Congress may not later convert his earlier reentry into a basis for mandatory reinstatement and categorical ineligibility for relief without clear notice.

Moreover, the equities reinforce the presumption against retroactivity. Petitioner remained in the country for years while the law afforded him a pathway to regularization. To apply § 241(a)(5) to his pre-enactment reentry would make the law operate on “past facts” to impose “new consequences.” **Union Pacific Railroad Co., 231 U.S. at 199**. That is retroactivity in its classic form.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that § 241(a)(5) does not apply to petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry. If the Court concludes otherwise, it should further hold that applying the statute to him is impermissibly retroactive and reverse the order of removal.
