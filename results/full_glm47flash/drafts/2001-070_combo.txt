**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS ONLY PUNISHMENTS THAT ARE "CRUEL AND UNUSUAL" IN THE SENSE OF BEING "INHUMAN AND DEGRADING."**

The Eighth Amendment forbids punishments that are "cruel and unusual" in the sense of being "inhuman and degrading." *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962). The execution of a mentally retarded individual, while a grave matter, does not constitute punishment that is inherently inhuman or degrading. The standard for determining whether a punishment is "cruel and unusual" is not static but draws its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). The execution of the mentally retarded has not been deemed a violation of this standard by the courts of this Commonwealth or the Nation.

**II. THE DEATH PENALTY IS A PROPORTIONATE RESPONSE TO CAPITAL MURDER.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that a punishment be proportionate to the offense. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910). The death penalty is a proportionate response to the heinous crime of capital murder. The fact that an individual is mentally retarded does not render the penalty disproportionate to the crime committed. The Constitution does not categorically bar the death penalty for the mentally retarded; rather, it requires that the punishment be just and proportionate to the culpability of the offender.

**III. THE EXECUTION PROCESS ITSELF IS NOT "CRUEL AND UNUSUAL."**

The execution of a criminal is not "cruel and unusual" punishment. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 736, 748 (1890). The process of execution, as historically practiced, is a lawful means of imposing the ultimate penalty for the most serious crimes. The Eighth Amendment was not intended to abolish the death penalty but to ensure that the methods of execution are not barbaric. The execution of a mentally retarded person does not violate this prohibition.

**IV. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF THE MENTALLY RETARDED.**

The Eighth Amendment forbids punishments that are "cruel and unusual" in the sense of being "inhuman and degrading." *Pope v. Illinois*, 341 U.S. 614, 616 (1951). Similarly, the Amendment forbids punishments that are "cruel and unusual" in the sense of being "inhuman and degrading." *Baldwin v. New York*, 336 U.S. 373, 389 (1949). The execution of a mentally retarded person does not meet this threshold. The punishment is not inhuman or degrading, nor is it unusual in the sense of being repugnant to the conscience of the community.

**V. STATE COURTS HAVE CONSISTENTLY HELD THAT THE DEATH PENALTY IS NOT "CRUEL AND UNUSUAL."**

The State of Texas has consistently held that the death penalty is not "cruel and unusual" punishment. *State v. Rusk*, 253 S.W.2d 527, 531 (Tex. 1952). This Court has also held that the execution of a criminal is not "cruel and unusual" punishment. *Lester v. City of New York*, 361 U.S. 371, 377 (1960). The execution of a mentally retarded person is not categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The punishment is a proportionate response to the crime and is not inhuman or degrading.
