**ARGUMENT**

**I. A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255 BECAUSE IT IS A LAWFUL ENTRY AFTER INSPECTION AND AUTHORIZATION.**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States *after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.*” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) (emphasis added). The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that TPS does not constitute an admission—rests on an artificial distinction between “status” and “admission” that finds no support in the statute. *See Sanchez v. Mayorkas*, 992 F.3d 409, 416–17 (3d Cir. 2021). When an alien is granted TPS, the Attorney General (now the Secretary of Homeland Security) *inspects* the alien’s eligibility and *authorizes* their continued presence in the United States. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(a), (b). This is precisely the “inspection and authorization” contemplated by § 1101(a)(13)(A). *Cf. INS v. Aguirre-Aguirre*, 526 U.S. 415, 424 (1999) (recognizing that the INA’s terms must be construed “in light of the [INA’s] overarching structure and purpose”).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced. *Hanif* did not address TPS, nor did it hold that “admission” requires a *physical* entry. Rather, it merely reiterated that “admission” is distinct from “status.” *Id.* at 485. The court here conflates that distinction with a requirement that “admission” must occur at the *border*. But the INA does not impose such a temporal or geographic limitation. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) (defining admission as “lawful entry *after inspection and authorization*” (emphasis added)). A grant of TPS is a lawful entry *after inspection and authorization*—it is an admission.

**II. CONGRESS INTENDED § 1254A(F)(4) TO DEEME TPS RECIPIENTS “ADMITTED” FOR PURPOSES OF § 1255.**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Third Circuit erroneously concluded that this provision does not also deem TPS recipients “admitted.” *Sanchez*, 992 F.3d at 417–18. But the statute’s text and structure compel the opposite conclusion.

First, § 1254a(f)(4) is not a standalone provision—it operates in conjunction with § 1255. *See* 8 U.S.C. §§ 1254a(f)(4), 1255(a). Congress knew how to expressly waive the admission requirement when it wished to do so. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i) (waiving admission requirement for certain special immigrants and visa lottery winners). Its failure to do so for TPS recipients does not evince an intent to exclude them from § 1255’s benefits. *Cf. Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.*, 534 U.S. 438, 452 (2002) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”) (internal quotation marks omitted).

Second, the Third Circuit’s reading renders § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous. If TPS does not confer “admission,” then § 1254a(f)(4) would only preserve lawful *status*—but § 1255(c)(2) already excuses lapses in lawful status for those who were *admitted*. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(c)(2). The Third Circuit’s interpretation thus strips § 1254a(f)(4) of meaningful effect. *Cf. Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions.”).

Third, the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) has recognized that an adjustment of status constitutes an admission. *See In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). TPS recipients, like those adjusting status, are granted a lawful immigration status *after* inspection and authorization. There is no principled reason to treat them differently.

**III. THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION CONTRADICTS THE PURPOSE OF TPS AND § 1255.**

The Third Circuit feared that allowing TPS to serve as an admission would “pave a clear path to status adjustment” for those who entered illegally, undermining § 1254a(h)(2)’s supermajority requirement. *Sanchez*, 992 F.3d at 419. But this concern is unfounded. TPS remains a *temporary* status—it does not guarantee permanent residence. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1) (TPS is granted for “temporary periods” due to “extraordinary conditions”). The statute’s purpose is to provide *relief from removal*, not a shortcut to a green card. *See id.* Allowing TPS recipients to adjust status under § 1255 does not alter TPS’s temporary nature—it merely permits those who qualify for permanent residence to obtain it.

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s interpretation forces TPS recipients who entered illegally to leave the country and reenter lawfully—a process that is both impractical and dangerous for those fleeing unsafe conditions. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 964 (9th Cir. 2017). This result is inconsistent with Congress’s intent to provide humanitarian relief. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (courts must construe humanitarian provisions “in a manner consistent with their charitable purposes”).

**IV. THE MAJORITY OF CIRCUITS TO ADDRESS THIS ISSUE HAVE HELD THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION.**

The Third Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Ninth and Sixth Circuits, which have held that TPS recipients are “inspected and admitted” for purposes of § 1255. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960–61; *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 554 (6th Cir. 2013). The Eleventh Circuit has also recognized that TPS does not waive the admission requirement, but it did not address whether TPS itself constitutes an admission. *See Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011). This Court should follow the majority view and hold that TPS recipients are “admitted” under § 1101(a)(13)(A).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully request that this Court reverse the Third Circuit’s decision and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
