**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution Are Exclusive and May Not Be Altered by the States**

The Constitution vests in Congress the sole authority to determine the qualifications for its Members. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 547 (1969) ("[T]he Constitution leaves to each House the determination of the qualifications of its own Members."). This authority is not merely procedural but substantive, as the Framers expressly rejected state-imposed qualifications for federal office. *Id.* at 548 (citing *The Federalist No. 60* (Hamilton)). The Qualifications Clauses—Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 (House) and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 (Senate)—set forth the exclusive criteria for membership: age, citizenship, and residency. *Id.* at 522-23. Any attempt by a state to add or alter these qualifications is unconstitutional. *Id.* at 547 ("[T]he framers were well aware of the potential for abuse inherent in the power to judge the qualifications of one’s own members, and they vested that power in each House precisely to forestall such abuse.").

This Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are not merely directory but mandatory. *Barry v. Cunningham*, 277 U.S. 572, 575 (1928) ("The qualifications for membership in Congress are prescribed by the Constitution, and cannot be added to by state action."). The Court has struck down state-imposed restrictions on ballot access for federal officeholders as violative of the Qualifications Clauses. *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 318 (1941) ("The right of the people to choose their representatives in Congress is a privilege of national citizenship protected by the Constitution."). Arkansas’s Amendment 73, which purports to limit the number of terms a person may serve in Congress, is no different. It adds a qualification not found in the Constitution and thus violates the Qualifications Clauses. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 547.

**II. The Arkansas Supreme Court Correctly Held That Amendment 73 Violates the Qualifications Clauses**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s decision in this case is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See Powell*, 395 U.S. at 547; *Barry*, 277 U.S. at 575. The court recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that states may not impose additional qualifications for federal office. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 312 Ark. 14, 30 (1993) ("The terms for members of Congress can be limited only by amending the United States Constitution."). The court further held that the severability clause in Amendment 73 could not save the unconstitutional provisions. *Id.* at 31 (citing *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750 (1964) ("The test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion.")).

The Arkansas court’s reasoning is sound. The Qualifications Clauses are a structural safeguard of the federal system, ensuring uniformity in the qualifications for federal office. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 547. Allowing states to impose term limits on federal officeholders would undermine this uniformity and invite a patchwork of qualifications across the states. *Id.* The court correctly held that Amendment 73 is unconstitutional because it seeks to do precisely that.

**III. The Arkansas Supreme Court Did Not Err in Declining to Enforce the Severability Clause**

The Arkansas Supreme Court properly declined to enforce the severability clause in Amendment 73 because the unconstitutional provisions were inextricably linked to the constitutional ones. *Combs*, 237 Ark. at 750. The court recognized that the voters were presented with an "all or nothing" package, and there was no way to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the unconstitutional provisions. *Thornton*, 312 Ark. at 31. This is consistent with this Court’s precedents, which hold that severability clauses are not enforceable when the unconstitutional provisions are so intertwined with the constitutional ones that the latter cannot stand alone. *See, e.g., Frost v. Corporation Comm’n of Okla.*, 278 U.S. 515, 525 (1929) ("[I]f what is left is so changed in its purpose and effect as to be a different thing from that which the legislature intended to enact, it cannot be said to be the same statute.").

**Conclusion**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73 violates the Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution and that the severability clause cannot save the unconstitutional provisions. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
