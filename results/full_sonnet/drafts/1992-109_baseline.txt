# ARGUMENT

## THE COMPETENCY STANDARD FOR PLEADING GUILTY AND WAIVING COUNSEL IS THE SAME AS THE COMPETENCY STANDARD FOR STANDING TRIAL

The Ninth Circuit held that due process requires a heightened "reasoned choice" standard before a defendant may waive counsel or plead guilty—a standard higher than the competency standard this Court articulated for standing trial in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). That holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's precedents, with the structure of the criminal trial, or with sound principles of federalism governing habeas review of state convictions.

### A. This Court's Competency Standard Is Unitary and Does Not Vary With the Decision at Hand

In *Dusky*, this Court held that the test for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and has "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." 362 U.S. at 402. This Court reaffirmed that standard in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), explaining that competency requires "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 171 (quoting *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402). Nothing in *Dusky* or *Drope* suggests that this standard fluctuates depending on which right the defendant chooses to exercise or waive.

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard rests on the theory that waiving counsel or pleading guilty demands "a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial." Pet. App. 14 (quoting *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1983)). This premise is mistaken. A defendant who stands trial retains counsel, must participate in complex strategic decisions, and confronts the same constitutional rights—to confront witnesses, to remain silent, to a jury trial—that are implicated when a defendant pleads guilty. See *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) (guilty plea waives "the right to trial by jury, the right to confront one's accusers, ... the right against compulsory self-incrimination," among others). There is no logical basis for concluding that the mental capacity necessary to stand trial—where a defendant must understand and participate in this same universe of rights—is meaningfully less demanding than the capacity necessary to waive them.

Indeed, a defendant who is competent to stand trial has already demonstrated the capacity to understand the charges, the available defenses, and the consequences of conviction. See *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 171. A defendant who pleads guilty need not exercise any greater cognitive function; if anything, the decision to forgo trial simplifies rather than complicates the defendant's task.

### B. The Ninth Circuit's Standard Improperly Conflates Competency With the Separate Requirement That a Waiver Be Knowing and Voluntary

The Ninth Circuit's error stems from its conflation of two distinct inquiries: (1) whether a defendant is *competent*, and (2) whether a waiver is *knowing and voluntary*. This Court has long treated these as separate questions. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), this Court held that waiver of the right to counsel must be an "intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right," *id.* at 464, focusing on the voluntariness and awareness of the waiver, not on some heightened mental-capacity threshold distinct from trial competency.

Similarly, in *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), this Court held that a defendant has a right to self-representation once the waiver of counsel is made "knowingly and intelligently," *id.* at 835, again addressing the *voluntariness* inquiry, not competency. The voluntariness inquiry asks whether the defendant understands the consequences of the decision and has made it free of coercion; the competency inquiry asks only whether the defendant has the requisite mental capacity described in *Dusky*. These inquiries are analytically distinct, and trial courts remain fully equipped to protect defendants' rights through the voluntariness inquiry without importing a new and ill-defined "reasoned choice" competency standard.

The Ninth Circuit's cases—*Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny—erroneously merged these inquiries, demanding that trial courts assess not merely whether the waiver was knowing and voluntary, but whether the defendant was mentally capable of "reasoned choice." Pet. App. 14. That standard has no textual or doctrinal grounding in this Court's precedents and introduces a vague, subjective inquiry into "reasoned choice" that provides no more protection than the existing *Dusky* standard combined with a robust voluntariness inquiry under *Boykin* and *Faretta*.

### C. A Single Competency Standard Serves the Practical Administration of Criminal Justice

This Court has recognized that "practicality" and "avoid[ing] disruption of the fair administration of justice" are legitimate considerations in shaping constitutional criminal procedure. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 209 (1977). Requiring trial courts to apply one standard to determine competency to stand trial and a different, higher standard to determine competency to plead guilty or waive counsel invites confusion and inconsistent rulings, particularly where—as here—a defendant's mental state may be assessed by psychiatric examiners unfamiliar with the "reasoned choice" formulation. The court below faulted the state trial court for relying on psychiatric reports that assessed only "competency to stand trial," Pet. App. 24, but the *Dusky* standard for trial competency is the only constitutionally required benchmark; no separate examination is constitutionally mandated.

### D. Any Additional Protection Moran Sought Was Available Through the Voluntariness Inquiry, Which the State Court Properly Applied

Even accepting that heightened vigilance is warranted before accepting a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea, that vigilance is supplied by the requirement that waiver be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent, see *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 242, not through a freestanding competency standard untethered from *Dusky*. The record shows the state trial court engaged Moran directly regarding his rights and his decision, consistent with the "utmost solicitude" required under *Boykin*. *Id.* at 243. This satisfies due process; no further "reasoned choice" competency showing was constitutionally required.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that the standard for competency to plead guilty and waive counsel is the same standard articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), for competency to stand trial.
