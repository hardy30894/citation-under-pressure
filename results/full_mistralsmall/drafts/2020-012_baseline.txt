**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s Decision Denying Reopening Is a Final, Reviewable Agency Action

The Railroad Retirement Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 disability application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”), 5 U.S.C. § 704. The Supreme Court has made clear that “[a]gency action is final for purposes of judicial review when it ‘mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process’ and ‘determine[s] rights or obligations.’” Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997) (quoting Port of Boston Marine Terminal Ass’n v. Rederiaktiebolaget Transatlantic, 400 U.S. 62, 71 (1970)). Here, the Board’s denial of Salinas’s reopening request satisfies both prongs.

First, the Board’s refusal to reopen is the consummation of its decisionmaking process. The Board’s regulations provide that a denial of a reopening request is a final, non-appealable decision unless the claimant seeks reconsideration within the statutorily prescribed timeframe. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. The Board’s hearing officer conducted a full evidentiary hearing and issued a written decision denying reopening based on the four-year time limitation for new and material evidence or administrative error. This is the “last step in the administrative process.” Abbott Labs. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967).

Second, the denial determines Salinas’s rights. By refusing to reopen the 2006 application, the Board foreclosed any possibility of revisiting the denial of benefits for that period. This effectively extinguished Salinas’s claim to disability benefits for the years preceding his 2013 application. See Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99, 107 (1977) (noting that a refusal to reopen a prior claim “may amount to a decision on the merits”).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is inconsistent with this framework. *Roberts* held that § 355(f) of the Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”) does not authorize review of reopening decisions, but it overlooked the APA’s independent grant of jurisdiction. The APA provides that “[a]gency action made reviewable by statute and final agency action for which there is no other adequate remedy in a court are subject to judicial review.” 5 U.S.C. § 704. The RRA does not expressly preclude review of reopening decisions, nor does it provide an alternative remedy for such decisions. Thus, the APA’s jurisdictional grant applies. See Bowen v. Michigan Academy of Family Physicians, 471 U.S. 369, 375–76 (1985) (holding that the Medicare Act’s judicial review provisions did not impliedly repeal the APA’s general grant of review).

II. The Board’s Decision Is Arbitrary and Capricious

Even if the Board’s decision were not final, it is arbitrary and capricious under the APA, 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The Board denied reopening based solely on the four-year time limitation, without considering whether equitable tolling or other exceptions applied. This was an abuse of discretion.

The Supreme Court has held that agencies must “examine the relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action including a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made.” Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983). Here, the Board failed to consider Salinas’s argument that his untimely reconsideration request in 2006 was the result of mental incapacity—a factor that may warrant equitable tolling. See Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89, 95–96 (1990) (holding that equitable tolling may apply to administrative deadlines in appropriate cases). The Board’s refusal to address this argument renders its decision arbitrary and capricious.

III. The Circuit Split Warrants This Court’s Review

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Roberts* conflicts with the D.C. Circuit’s holding in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which permits judicial review of reopening decisions. This split underscores the need for this Court’s intervention to resolve the question of whether reopening decisions are final and reviewable. See Hutto v. Davis, 454 U.S. 370, 375 (1982) (per curiam) (noting that “[w]hen a particular issue raises a conflict among the Courts of Appeals, this Court’s certiorari jurisdiction is most appropriately invoked”).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s denial of Salinas’s reopening request is a final, reviewable decision under the APA. It is also arbitrary and capricious. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary precedent is erroneous and conflicts with other circuits. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the split and ensure consistent application of the law.
