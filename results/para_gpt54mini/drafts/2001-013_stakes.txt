**Argument**

**I. Section 4(b)(1) does not withdraw OSHA jurisdiction over uninspected vessels merely because the Coast Guard has some regulatory authority.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act’s preemption clause is limited. By its terms, OSHA does not apply only where “another Federal agency” has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations “affecting occupational safety or health” with respect to the relevant working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that this provision requires more than a general grant of authority to another agency; it requires that the other agency actually exercise authority over the specific working conditions at issue. *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 241–42 (2002); *Southern Railway Co. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission*, 539 F.2d 335, 337–38 (4th Cir. 1976).

That limitation is dispositive here. The Coast Guard’s regulatory regime for uninspected vessels is narrow and focused on a handful of subjects, including fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and certain alerting equipment. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. pts. 25–26. As the Court explained in *Chao*, “[t]he Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific requirements,” and do not amount to the comprehensive regulation necessary to displace OSHA. 534 U.S. at 241–43. Because the citations here concern workplace hazards not specifically regulated by the Coast Guard, OSHA retained jurisdiction. *Id.* at 243–44.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s broad reading of Coast Guard exclusivity cannot survive the Supreme Court’s decision in Chao.**

The Fifth Circuit held that *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), created an across-the-board exemption for “seamen on vessels in navigation.” But *Chao* rejected that approach. The Court held that the Fifth Circuit “erred in treating the Coast Guard’s authority over inspected vessels as sufficient to pre-empt OSHA with respect to uninspected vessels.” 534 U.S. at 245. The Court emphasized that “the Coast Guard had exercised only limited regulatory authority over uninspected vessels,” and therefore OSHA’s authority was not displaced. *Id.* at 242.

That holding controls here. While the Fifth Circuit focused on the Coast Guard’s general authority over maritime safety and its regulations for some uninspected-vessel equipment, the Supreme Court made clear that such limited regulation does not trigger § 653(b)(1) preemption for all workplace hazards aboard uninspected vessels. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 241–44. The Act does not permit a “blanket exemption” from OSHA based on partial Coast Guard regulation. *Id.* at 244.

**III. The Act’s structure and purpose confirm that OSHA coverage remains unless the other agency has occupied the field of the specific hazard cited.**

Congress enacted the OSH Act to assure “safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). Section 4(b)(1) is an exception, not the rule, and exceptions are construed narrowly. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243; *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119, 123–24 (2d Cir. 1978). The Court in *Chao* stressed that Congress did not intend “to leave employees without the protections of either OSHA or another agency’s regulations.” 534 U.S. at 246.

That concern is especially acute on uninspected vessels. The Coast Guard’s regulations plainly do not address every occupational hazard that may arise on a drilling barge or similar floating workplace. Where, as here, the specific hazard is unregulated, OSHA may act unless and until another agency has exercised authority over that working condition. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243–45; *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386, 388–89 (5th Cir. 1976).

**IV. Because the Coast Guard did not regulate the specific working conditions cited by OSHA, the citations were proper.**

The record shows only that the Coast Guard investigated the casualty and noted the rig was uninspected. That is not enough. Investigation is not regulation, and a general statutory mandate is not the same as exercised authority under § 653(b)(1). *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 242–43. The dispositive question is whether the Coast Guard had issued standards or regulations affecting the specific occupational safety and health hazards for which OSHA cited Mallard. *Id.* at 241. It had not.

Accordingly, OSHA’s jurisdiction was not preempted, and the citations should be sustained. The Supreme Court has already resolved the question presented: minimal Coast Guard regulation of an uninspected vessel does not oust OSHA under § 4(b)(1). *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235 (2002).
