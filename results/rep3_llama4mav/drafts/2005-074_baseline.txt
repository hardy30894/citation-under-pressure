The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. Even if it does, its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.

First, the statutory text and legislative history do not clearly indicate that Congress intended the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Court established a two-step test for determining whether a statute applies retroactively. The first step is to determine whether Congress has clearly indicated the statute's temporal reach. Id. at 280. If not, the court proceeds to the second step, assessing whether the statute has an impermissible retroactive effect. Id. Here, the INA's reinstatement provision lacks clear congressional intent regarding its application to pre-enactment re-entries.

The government may argue that the statute's language is broad enough to encompass pre-enactment re-entries. However, as the Court held in INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the absence of explicit language regarding retroactivity suggests that Congress did not intend the statute to apply retroactively. Id. at 320. The St. Cyr Court also emphasized that the "familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations" underlie the presumption against retroactivity. Id. at 321. Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982 and remained here for nearly two decades, forming significant ties to the community and relying on the availability of discretionary relief under the INA. Applying the reinstatement statute to him would upset these settled expectations.

Even if the statute is deemed to apply to pre-enactment re-entries, its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive. Under Landgraf's second step, a statute has an impermissible retroactive effect when it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." 511 U.S. at 270. The reinstatement statute denies Fernandez-Vargas the opportunity to adjust his status, a form of relief he was pursuing when the statute was applied to him. This denial attaches new legal consequences to his pre-enactment re-entry, rendering the statute's application retroactive.

The Tenth Circuit's holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is unpersuasive. As the Court noted in St. Cyr, aliens who re-entered the United States before the enactment of the reinstatement statute had a reasonable expectation of being eligible for discretionary relief. 533 U.S. at 323-24. Fernandez-Vargas's reliance on this expectation is evident in his decision to remain in the United States and pursue adjustment of status. The reinstatement statute's application to him impermissibly disrupts this reliance.

In conclusion, the reinstatement statute does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if it does, its application is impermissibly retroactive, as it attaches new legal consequences to his pre-enactment re-entry and disrupts his settled expectations. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
