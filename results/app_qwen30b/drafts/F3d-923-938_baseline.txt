**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Homero Meruelo failed to establish a “bona fide indebtedness” running directly to him from Merco of the Palm Beaches, Inc., under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1) and Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The record demonstrates that Meruelo made actual economic outlays to Merco through a series of structured, contemporaneous, and documented intercompany transfers, all of which were consistently treated as loans in both accounting and tax reporting. The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion—based on a rigid, form-over-substance interpretation of the debt relationship—misapplies settled principles of tax law and disregards the economic reality of the transactions.

**I. The Tax Court Misapplied the “Bona Fide Indebtedness” Standard by Focusing on Form Rather Than Economic Substance**

To qualify for basis under § 1366(d)(1), a shareholder must establish a “bona fide indebtedness” that “runs directly” to him. *See* Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The Supreme Court has long held that “the substance of a transaction, not its form, determines its tax consequences.” *Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co.*, 348 U.S. 426, 431 (1955). This principle is especially critical in the context of intercompany debt, where the IRS may not disregard a debt merely because it flows through intermediate entities, so long as the economic reality supports the existence of a genuine obligation.

Here, Meruelo’s $9 million in claimed debt basis arose from a series of intercompany transfers that were not mere “gifts” or “capital contributions,” but structured, recurring, and documented financial arrangements. The record shows that Merco affiliates—entities in which Meruelo held an ownership interest—paid over $15 million in expenses on Merco’s behalf between 2004 and 2008. Merco repaid less than $6 million of these payments, leaving a net balance of over $9 million in accounts payable. These transactions were not speculative or informal; they were systematically recorded in Merco’s books as liabilities and were consistently reported as shareholder loans on Merco’s tax returns.

The Tax Court’s conclusion that the debt “ran from Merco to the affiliates” ignores the economic reality: Meruelo, as the controlling shareholder of both Merco and its affiliates, directed the flow of funds to ensure Merco’s operational viability. The fact that funds passed through intermediate entities does not negate the existence of a debt to Meruelo. *See* *Henderson v. Comm’r*, 87 T.C. 1055, 1064 (1986) (holding that “a debt may be established even though it is not directly owed to the taxpayer, so long as the taxpayer has a direct and enforceable claim against the corporation”).

**II. Meruelo Made an Actual Economic Outlay, as Required by Law**

The Tax Court further erred in holding that Meruelo failed to demonstrate an “actual economic outlay” toward the debt. *See* *Barnes v. Comm’r*, 87 T.C. 1075, 1082 (1986). The “actual economic outlay” doctrine requires that a shareholder’s contribution to a corporation’s debt be made from his own funds, not from the corporation’s or another entity’s funds. *See* *Henderson*, 87 T.C. at 1063.

Here, Meruelo’s initial $4,985,035 contribution—funded by a personal loan—was clearly an outlay from his own resources. The remainder of the $9 million debt basis arose from the net transfers of funds from Merco affiliates, which were themselves funded by Meruelo’s capital and earnings. The record shows that Meruelo owned 50% of Akoya, the entity that transferred $5 million to Merco’s escrow account. The $14,965 of Akoya’s own funds were negligible in comparison and did not alter the economic substance of the transaction. The remaining $9 million in net accounts payable were the result of Merco’s affiliates advancing funds to Merco, which Meruelo, as a controlling shareholder, had the right to claim as his own.

The Tax Court’s reliance on *Barnes* is misplaced. In *Barnes*, the taxpayer failed to demonstrate that he had personally funded the debt. Here, Meruelo did. The fact that funds were channeled through affiliated entities does not negate the outlay when the taxpayer controls those entities and the transactions are consistent with a genuine debt relationship. *See* *Barnes*, 87 T.C. at 1082 (holding that “the source of funds is not dispositive if the taxpayer has a direct and enforceable claim”).

**III. The Tax Court’s Approach Undermines the Purpose of the S Corporation Pass-Through Regime**

The S corporation system is designed to allow losses to pass through to shareholders in proportion to their ownership interest. *See* *Bufferd v. Comm’r*, 506 U.S. 523, 525 (1993). To deny Meruelo basis in the $9 million of debt he effectively advanced to Merco—through a network of affiliated entities he controlled—undermines this purpose. The IRS’s position would allow a shareholder to avoid basis in debt merely because the funds were routed through other entities, even when the economic reality is that the shareholder bore the risk of loss.

The Tax Court’s decision creates an absurd result: a shareholder who advances funds through a controlled network of entities is denied basis, while a shareholder who makes a direct loan is not. This distinction lacks legal or economic justification. *See* *Henderson*, 87 T.C. at 1064 (“The tax law does not require that a debt be direct in form, only that it be real in substance.”)

**Conclusion**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him. The record demonstrates that Meruelo made actual economic outlays, that the debt was structured with clear intent to create a binding obligation, and that the transactions were consistently treated as loans in both accounting and tax reporting.
