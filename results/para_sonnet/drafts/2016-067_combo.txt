**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text of § 1692a(6) Distinguishes "Debt Collectors" From "Creditors" by Reference to For Whom the Debt Is Collected, Not When It Was Acquired.**

Where a statute's language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 1692a(6) defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as one who "regularly collects or attempts to collect ... debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Nothing in that text asks whether the debt was in default when the collector acquired it; it asks only whether the collector is gathering money for itself or for someone else. Petitioners' proposed default-based test is not found in the words Congress chose, and this Court has long refused to import tests the statutory text does not contain. *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485.

**II. Courts Must Give Effect to Every Word of the Statute, Not Superimpose an Unwritten Default Requirement.**

It is "the duty of the court to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute." *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883). Likewise, "the meaning of a statute is to be looked for, not in any single section, but in all the parts together, and in their relation to the end in view." *Fox v. Standard Oil Co. of N.J.*, 294 U.S. 87, 95 (1935). Reading § 1692a(6) as a whole, Congress built the "debt collector" definition around the *relationship* between the collector and the debt—collection "for another"—and separately built an *exclusion*, § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), for persons collecting non-defaulted debt "owed or due another." The exclusion presupposes that the person already satisfies the threshold definition of collecting for someone else. It does not, and cannot, transform every purchaser of defaulted paper into a debt collector; doing so would collapse the statute's two-step structure into one, ignoring words Congress placed there for a reason. *Montclair*, 107 U.S. at 152.

**III. The Word "Another" Governs Both Halves of the Definitional Clause, Confirming That Collection on One's Own Account Is Excluded.**

When a qualifying clause follows a series of terms and applies equally to all of them, "the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all." *Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920). In § 1692a(6), the word "another" modifies both "owed or due" and "asserted to be owed or due." A person who purchases a debt and thereafter collects it for its own account is not collecting a debt "owed ... another"—the debt is owed to the purchaser itself. Santander, having bought petitioners' installment contracts outright, was collecting sums owed to Santander, not to CitiFinancial Auto or anyone else. Under the ordinary grammatical structure that this Court has long applied, that places Santander's conduct outside the statutory definition entirely.

**IV. Congress's Narrow, Express Treatment of Creditors Elsewhere in § 1692a(6) Shows That No Broader, Unstated Default-Based Exception Was Intended.**

Congress knew how to bring a creditor within the debt-collector definition when it wished to do so: § 1692a(6) expressly includes "any creditor who, in the process of collecting his own debts, uses any name other than his own which would indicate that a third person is collecting." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Under the settled principle that the expression of one thing in a statute implies the exclusion of others not mentioned, *see Botany Worsted Mills v. United States*, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929), Congress's specific, narrow inclusion of one category of creditor conduct—assumed-name collection—confirms that it did not intend a second, unstated category based on the timing of a debt's default. Had Congress meant to sweep in every creditor that purchases defaulted debt, it would have said so as plainly as it did for assumed-name collection.

**V. This Court Should Not Supply an Omission That Congress Did Not Make.**

Petitioners ask the Court, in substance, to add a provision to § 1692a(6) making a debt's default status at acquisition the touchstone of "debt collector" status. But "what the government asks is not a construction of a statute, but, in effect, an enlargement of it by the court, so that what was omitted ... may be included within its scope. To supply omissions transcends the judicial function." *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926). If Congress wishes to treat purchasers of defaulted consumer debt differently from other creditors, it may amend the statute; courts may not do so by interpretation.

**VI. A Regulatory Statute Should Be Confined to the Conduct Its Text Describes, Not Expanded by Inference to Reach Additional Actors.**

This Court has repeatedly declined to stretch federal regulatory statutes beyond the conduct Congress actually described, even where a broader reading might serve the statute's general remedial aims. *See FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349 (1941). As the Court has explained, "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, Congress's words describe a debt collector as one gathering debts "owed ... another." Santander, collecting on debts it owns, is a creditor. The judgment below should be affirmed.
