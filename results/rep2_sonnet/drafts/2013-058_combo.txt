**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Exemption Reaches Only Funds That Retain the Character of Retirement Savings**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds" that enjoy tax-favored status under specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. The statutory text does not say "any funds that once were retirement funds" or "any funds derived from a retirement account." This Court has long instructed that "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed," and that courts must enforce clear statutory language according to its terms. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The ordinary, present-tense meaning of "retirement funds" describes money presently dedicated to retirement, not money that once served that purpose in someone else's hands.

That reading is reinforced by the statutory neighborhood in which the phrase appears. This Court has repeatedly applied the principle that "a word is known by its associates," refusing to give an ambiguous term unintended breadth divorced from its surrounding context. *Jarecki v. G. D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961). Congress placed the "retirement funds" exemption alongside provisions tied to tax-code sections governing accounts maintained *for* the current holder's retirement—accounts subject to early-withdrawal penalties and minimum distribution ages keyed to the owner's own retirement. An inherited IRA, by contrast, is stripped of every attribute that makes an account a retirement vehicle for its current holder: no further contributions may be made, and distribution must begin and often conclude years before the beneficiary's retirement. Reading "retirement funds" to include such an account would give the term a breadth the surrounding statutory scheme does not support.

**II. Exemption Status Is Fixed by the Character of the Property at the Time of Filing, Not by Its History**

This Court has consistently held that a debtor's exemption rights are determined by the nature of the property as it exists when the bankruptcy petition is filed, not by what the property once was. In *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310 (1924), the Court held that exemption rights become fixed as of the date the petition is filed, and are governed by the character the property then bears. Similarly, in *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459 (1913), the Court looked to the debtor's actual interest in property as of the commencement of the case to determine what passed to creditors and what remained protected.

That temporal principle applies with full force here. Whatever protective character the funds bore while Ruth Heffron held them as her own retirement account, that character was a function of *her* relationship to the funds—her inability to withdraw without penalty, her obligation to defer distribution until her own retirement years. Once the account passed to Heidi Heffron-Clark, none of those constraints survived; the funds became fully accessible for present consumption, subject only to a mandatory payout schedule wholly unrelated to Heidi's own retirement. Under the settled rule that exemption analysis asks what the property *is* at the time of filing, the funds here are not retirement funds in the Clarks' hands, regardless of what they were in the decedent's.

**III. Exempting Inherited IRAs Would Exceed the Fresh-Start Rationale That Justifies the Retirement-Funds Exemption**

The retirement-funds exemption, like other Bankruptcy Act and Code exemptions, exists to serve a specific purpose: enabling the honest debtor to preserve the means of *future* self-sufficiency. As this Court explained in the seminal case describing the aims of bankruptcy relief, the object is "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor... a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That rationale is inherently forward-looking: it protects a debtor's capacity to work, save, and rebuild—not a windfall inheritance already available for present spending.

This Court has cautioned against construing bankruptcy provisions so broadly that they defeat the balance the Act strikes between debtor relief and creditors' legitimate claims. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966), the Court observed that the definition of estate property is meant "to secure for creditors everything of value the bankrupt may possess in alienable or leviable form when he files his petition," while fresh-start provisions are calibrated to protect only what genuinely serves the debtor's future needs. Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would upset that balance: it would shield a freely spendable asset from creditors merely because a relative once used it for retirement savings, converting what Congress designed as a limited tax-deferral mechanism into a permanent creditor-proof estate-planning tool. This Court likewise recognized in *Perry v. Commerce Loan Co.*, 383 U.S. 392 (1966), that exemption and discharge provisions must be applied consistently with the fresh-start policy they were enacted to serve, not stretched beyond it.

Finally, where a literal but unmoored reading would produce results contrary to a statute's evident purpose, this Court has looked to that purpose to guide construction, recognizing that "there is... no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress's words—and the fresh-start purpose they embody—confirm that inherited IRAs, severed from any link to the beneficiary's own retirement, fall outside the exemption.
