# ARGUMENT

## I. Bryant Has Standing to Enforce the Arbitration Award Rendered in His Favor.

The district court erred in holding that Bryant, as an individual bargaining-unit employee, lacks standing to enforce an arbitration award obtained under the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA. This Court has long recognized that an individual employee retains the right to sue for breach of a collective bargaining agreement notwithstanding the union's role as exclusive bargaining representative. In *Smith v. Evening News Ass'n*, 371 U.S. 195 (1962), the Supreme Court held that Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act "was not meant to divest individual employees of protection against breach by the union of its duty of fair representation," and that an employee may bring suit even though the union might also have sued on the same facts. Id. at 200. The Court in *Evening News* squarely held that "the fact that a suit may be a proper subject for an action under § 301 of the [LMRA] does not preclude an action under [other law]," recognizing that individual rights arising under a labor contract survive independent of union prerogative. Id.

That principle applies with particular force here because Bryant is not attempting to relitigate the merits of the arbitration but merely to enforce an award already rendered in his favor. The Supreme Court has distinguished between suits attacking an arbitration decision and suits to enforce one, and has cautioned that a union's control over the grievance process does not extinguish the individual employee's interest in the fruits of an award actually won. See *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 186 (1967) (recognizing that "the wrongfully discharged employee may adequately be protected" through mechanisms independent of the union's litigation control). Because Bryant seeks only to compel compliance with a favorable award, and because no provision of the collective bargaining agreement "clearly and unmistakably" reserves that enforcement right to the union alone, see *Wright v. Universal Maritime Service Corp.*, 525 U.S. 70, 80 (1998), the district court's dismissal for lack of standing cannot stand.

## II. The District Court Failed to Apply the Supreme Court's Clarification of the McDonnell Douglas Framework in *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*

The district court's summary judgment order treated Bryant's Title VII and Section 1981 claims as if he were required, after establishing a prima facie case and evidence of pretext, to introduce additional, independent evidence of discriminatory animus. That approach misapplies the burden-shifting standard set forth in *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), as clarified by the Supreme Court in *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000).

In *Reeves*, the Court held that "a plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated." 530 U.S. at 148. The Court expressly rejected the notion that a plaintiff must always present evidence beyond the prima facie case and proof of pretext, explaining that "it is not enough to disbelieve the employer; the factfinder must believe the plaintiff's explanation of intentional discrimination," but that such belief may permissibly rest on disbelief of the employer's proffered reason together with the prima facie showing. Id. at 147.

Here, Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic's stated justification for his discipline and termination—rigid enforcement of the Red Letter Day policy—was pretextual, given the CWA's unrebutted offer to have coworkers perform Bryant's assignments, Bell Atlantic's refusal of that accommodation, and its selective enforcement of the policy against Bryant despite his consistent efforts to comply. Under *Reeves*, this evidence, combined with Bryant's prima facie case, was sufficient to permit a reasonable jury to find intentional discrimination. The district court's failure to apply this standard, and its apparent demand for additional independent proof of animus, constitutes reversible error.

## III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment.

Summary judgment is appropriate only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact." *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). In evaluating the record, a court must remember that "the evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor." *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). The district court failed to honor this standard.

The record here reflects disputed facts material to Bryant's discrimination and retaliation claims: whether Bell Atlantic's rejection of the CWA's proposed accommodation was motivated by legitimate operational needs or by discriminatory intent; whether similarly situated employees outside Bryant's protected class received more favorable treatment under the Red Letter Day policy; and whether the timing and severity of Bell Atlantic's escalating discipline—culminating in termination shortly after Bryant's medical excuse expired—support an inference of pretext or retaliatory motive. These are precisely the kind of credibility and inference-laden questions that *Anderson* reserves for the jury, not the court on summary judgment. 477 U.S. at 255. Because "the evidence presents a sufficient disagreement to require submission to a jury," id. at 251-52, the district court's grant of summary judgment must be reversed.
