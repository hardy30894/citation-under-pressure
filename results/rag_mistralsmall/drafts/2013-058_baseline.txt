**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Seventh Circuit Correctly Held That an Inherited IRA Is Not Exempt Under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) Because It Is Not "Retirement Funds" in the Debtor’s Hands.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempts from a debtor’s estate “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code, including § 408 (governing IRAs). *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit correctly concluded that an inherited IRA does not qualify for this exemption because it is not “retirement funds” in the hands of the debtor-beneficiary. *Clark v. Rameker*, 714 F.3d 559, 563-65 (7th Cir. 2013).

The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but the Supreme Court has recognized that exemptions under § 522 are to be construed in light of their purpose: to protect funds actually set aside for retirement. *Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 325-26 (2005) (exempting IRAs under § 522(d)(10)(E) because they are “plainly ‘funds set aside for retirement’”). An inherited IRA, by contrast, is not a fund set aside for the beneficiary’s retirement. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. Unlike a traditional IRA, an inherited IRA must begin distributing its assets within one year of the original owner’s death, and the beneficiary cannot make additional contributions or roll over the funds into another retirement account. *Id.* at 563-64 (citing 26 U.S.C. §§ 408(d)(3)(C), 401(a)(9)(B)). The beneficiary may withdraw funds at any time without penalty, and the account is not dedicated to the beneficiary’s retirement. *Id.* at 564. Thus, as the Seventh Circuit held, an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *Id.*

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary decision in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), errs by focusing on the account’s tax-exempt status rather than its function as retirement savings. *See id.* at 490-91. The Supreme Court has cautioned that exemptions are not to be read atemporally or mechanically. *See Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987) (“Deciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed to the achievement of a particular objective is the very essence of legislative choice”). The Bankruptcy Code’s reference to “retirement funds” in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is not satisfied by funds that were once retirement funds in another’s hands but are no longer set aside for retirement in the debtor’s. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565-66.

**II. The Seventh Circuit Properly Rejected the Argument That the Inherited IRA’s Tax-Exempt Status Alone Suffices to Qualify It for Exemption.**

The Clarks argue that the inherited IRA’s tax-deferred status under § 408 is sufficient to qualify it as “retirement funds” under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). This argument conflates tax status with functional purpose. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565. The Supreme Court has recognized that IRAs are exempt under the Bankruptcy Code because they are “funds set aside for retirement,” not merely because they are tax-deferred. *Rousey*, 544 U.S. at 325-26. An inherited IRA, however, is not set aside for the beneficiary’s retirement; it is a vehicle for immediate distribution. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564-65. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the tax-deferral benefits of an inherited IRA do not transform it into “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands. *Id.* at 565.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Does Not Conflict With Other Exemptions That Lack Temporal Restrictions.**

The Clarks contend that § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) omits the word “debtor’s,” suggesting that the exemption is not limited to funds set aside for the debtor’s retirement. This argument misreads the statute. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 566. The Supreme Court has recognized that exemptions under § 522 are to be construed in light of their purpose, and the purpose of the retirement-funds exemption is to protect funds actually set aside for retirement. *Rousey*, 544 U.S. at 325-26. The absence of the word “debtor’s” does not abrogate this purpose. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 566. The Seventh Circuit correctly analogized this issue to the homestead exemption, which requires that the debtor’s domicile be used as a home, not merely that it once was someone else’s home. *Id.* at 567 (citing 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A)).

**IV. The Seventh Circuit’s Decision Does Not Frustrate the Purpose of Encouraging Retirement Savings.**

The Clarks argue that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage retirement savings by allowing parents to pass IRAs to their children free from creditors’ claims. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 567. This policy argument, however, is for Congress, not the courts. *See Rodriguez*, 480 U.S. at 525-26. The Supreme Court has cautioned that courts must not “simplistically” assume that whatever furthers a statute’s purpose must be the law. *Id.* The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement-funds exemption is limited to funds actually set aside for retirement, and an inherited IRA does not meet this requirement. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 567-68.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is not “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands. The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
