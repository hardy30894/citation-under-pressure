**ARGUMENT**

I. The Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 (“Act”) does not divest the Secretary of Labor of jurisdiction over the working conditions of employees aboard vessels such as Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52.

Section 4(a) of the Act provides that it applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Section 4(b)(1) further provides that the Act does not apply “to working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit has long recognized that the Coast Guard’s authority over vessel safety is not exclusive of OSHA’s jurisdiction where the Coast Guard has not affirmatively exercised its authority to regulate a particular working condition. *See, e.g., Calbeck v. Travelers Ins. Co.*, 119 F. Supp. 800, 804 (E.D. La. 1954) (“The mere fact that the Coast Guard has some regulatory power over vessels does not necessarily mean that it has exclusive jurisdiction over all matters pertaining to the safety of those vessels.”), *aff’d*, 222 F.2d 46 (5th Cir. 1955); *Vick v. United States*, 168 F. Supp. 809, 812 (S.D. Tex. 1958) (“The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is not exclusive where it has not undertaken to regulate a particular safety matter.”).

The Coast Guard’s authority over vessel safety is not plenary. The relevant statute provides that the Coast Guard “shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States covering all matters not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department.” 14 U.S.C. § 2 (1946). This language does not grant the Coast Guard exclusive jurisdiction over all matters affecting occupational safety on vessels. Rather, it permits concurrent jurisdiction where another federal agency has been delegated authority to regulate occupational safety. *See Calbeck*, 119 F. Supp. at 804.

II. The Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue in this case.

The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly held that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is not exclusive where it has not affirmatively regulated a particular working condition. *See, e.g., Calbeck*, 119 F. Supp. at 804; *Vick*, 168 F. Supp. at 812. In *Calbeck*, the court held that the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act applied to longshoremen working on vessels in navigable waters, notwithstanding the Coast Guard’s general authority over vessel safety. 119 F. Supp. at 804. The court reasoned that the Coast Guard had not exercised its authority to regulate the specific safety conditions affecting longshoremen. *Id.*

Similarly, in *Vick*, the court held that the Federal Employees’ Compensation Act applied to a federal employee injured while working on a vessel, notwithstanding the Coast Guard’s general authority over vessel safety. 168 F. Supp. at 812. The court reasoned that the Coast Guard had not exercised its authority to regulate the specific safety conditions affecting federal employees. *Id.*

Here, the Coast Guard investigated the explosion on Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 but did not issue any citations or regulations addressing the specific working conditions at issue. The Coast Guard’s general authority over vessel safety does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction where it has not affirmatively regulated the particular working conditions at issue. *See Calbeck*, 119 F. Supp. at 804; *Vick*, 168 F. Supp. at 812.

III. The Act’s legislative history supports the conclusion that OSHA retains jurisdiction over working conditions on vessels where the Coast Guard has not affirmatively regulated those conditions.

The Act was enacted to “assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b). The legislative history reflects Congress’s intent to cover all workplaces, including those on vessels, unless another federal agency has affirmatively regulated the specific working conditions. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 91-1291, at 17 (1970) (“The committee intends that the Act cover all employees, including those working on vessels, unless another federal agency has exclusive jurisdiction over the particular working conditions.”).

The Fifth Circuit has recognized that the Act’s coverage is broad and should not be narrowly construed to exclude working conditions on vessels. *See Calbeck*, 119 F. Supp. at 804. The court has held that the Act applies to working conditions on vessels where the Coast Guard has not affirmatively regulated those conditions. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed. OSHA retains jurisdiction over the working conditions of employees aboard vessels such as Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 where the Coast Guard has not affirmatively regulated those conditions.
