**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL.**

The Controlled Substances Act’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may delegate authority to executive officers so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide their discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The temporary scheduling provision satisfies this standard.

Congress expressly limited the Attorney General’s discretion by directing that a substance may be temporarily scheduled only if it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). The statute further specifies the factors the Attorney General must consider—including the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health—before exercising this authority. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). These statutory constraints provide sufficient guidance to prevent arbitrary or unchecked discretion. *See Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785 (1948) (upholding delegation where Congress required consideration of “fair and equitable” standards); *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (finding delegation valid where Congress provided “definite standards” to guide executive action).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In those cases, the Court invalidated delegations because Congress failed to provide any meaningful standards or criteria to guide executive discretion. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542. Here, by contrast, Congress has supplied clear statutory standards governing the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority. *See Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation where Congress provided “well-known and generally acceptable standards”).

Moreover, the temporary scheduling provision is analogous to other delegations upheld by the Court. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), the Court sustained a delegation of authority to fix price ceilings during wartime, where Congress required that prices be set “fair and equitable.” *Id.* at 420. Similarly, in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 325 U.S. 385 (1945), the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the SEC to regulate holding companies, where Congress provided detailed statutory standards. *Id.* at 397. The temporary scheduling provision fits comfortably within this line of precedent.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUBDELEGATION OF AUTHORITY TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is consistent with longstanding administrative practice and judicial approval. The Supreme Court has recognized that executive officers may subdelegate authority where Congress has not expressly prohibited it. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding subdelegation of rulemaking authority to subordinate officials); *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 693 (1892) (finding subdelegation permissible where Congress authorized the President to “perform his executive duties”).

Here, the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is expressly authorized by 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns to the DEA Administrator the “functions vested in the Attorney General by the Controlled Substances Act.” This regulation is a valid exercise of the Attorney General’s authority to organize and supervise the Department of Justice. *See United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260, 266 (1954) (recognizing the Attorney General’s broad authority to delegate and subdelegate executive functions).

The Toubys’ argument that the subdelegation is unauthorized ignores the well-established principle that executive officers may delegate authority to subordinate officials unless Congress has expressly prohibited it. *See Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 517; *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. at 693. No such prohibition exists in the Controlled Substances Act.

**III. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DEPRIVE DEFENDANTS OF DUE PROCESS.**

The temporary scheduling provision does not violate the Due Process Clause. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that procedural due process protections apply to administrative actions that affect individual rights, but the Court has never held that the non-delegation doctrine is incorporated into the Due Process Clause. *See Bi-Metallic Inv. Co. v. State Bd. of Equalization*, 239 U.S. 441, 445 (1915) (distinguishing between legislative and adjudicative actions for due process purposes); *Londoner v. City & County of Denver*, 210 U.S. 373, 385 (1908) (requiring notice and hearing for adjudicative actions).

Here, the temporary scheduling provision is a legislative action, not an adjudicative one. The Attorney General’s decision to temporarily schedule a substance is a rulemaking function, not an adjudication. *See United States v. Florida East Coast Ry. Co.*, 410 U.S. 224, 244 (1973) (distinguishing between legislative and adjudicative actions). As such, it is subject to the procedural requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act, not the heightened due process protections applicable to adjudicative actions. *See id.*

Moreover, the temporary scheduling provision includes procedural safeguards, such as notice-and-comment requirements and the opportunity for judicial review of constitutional challenges. *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (recognizing that constitutional challenges are presumptively reviewable). These safeguards ensure that the Attorney General’s actions are subject to meaningful judicial oversight.

**CONCLUSION**

The delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General is constitutional, the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is authorized, and the temporary scheduling provision does not violate the Due Process Clause. The judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
