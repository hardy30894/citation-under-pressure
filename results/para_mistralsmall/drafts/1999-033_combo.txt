**ARGUMENT**

The Village of Willowbrook respectfully submits that the Equal Protection Clause does not recognize a “class-of-one” theory of liability where a plaintiff fails to allege membership in a class or group. The Seventh Circuit’s decision below conflicts with this Court’s longstanding precedent, which requires that equal protection claims be premised on invidious discrimination against a class or group, not merely arbitrary treatment of an individual.

This Court has repeatedly held that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits “invidious discrimination” against persons “because of their race, color, or previous condition of servitude,” *Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303, 308 (1879), or other suspect classifications. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), this Court invalidated a facially neutral ordinance enforced discriminatorily against Chinese laundry operators, emphasizing that the Equal Protection Clause protects discrete groups from intentional discrimination. *See also* *United States v. Carolene Prods. Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938) (noting that “prejudice against discrete and insular minorities” is the principal concern of the Equal Protection Clause).

The Court has never extended the Equal Protection Clause to protect individuals from arbitrary treatment unrelated to group-based discrimination. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court rejected an equal protection claim brought by a candidate denied certification as a nominee, holding that the Clause does not “insure against all unequal treatment” but only against “invidious discrimination.” *Id.* at 8. Similarly, in *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17 (1960), the Court reaffirmed that equal protection violations require proof of “purposeful discrimination against a class or group.” *Id.* at 25.

The Seventh Circuit’s “class-of-one” theory—under which Olech alleges she was treated differently from others without proving membership in a protected class—finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence. In *Gomillion v. Lightfoot*, 364 U.S. 339 (1960), the Court invalidated a racially discriminatory redistricting plan, emphasizing that the Equal Protection Clause targets “racial classifications.” *Id.* at 347. The Court did not recognize a freestanding right to be free from arbitrary treatment. *See also* *McLaughlin v. Florida*, 379 U.S. 184 (1964) (invalidating a law targeting interracial couples); *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967) (same).

The Court’s decisions in *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), and *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm’rs*, 330 U.S. 552 (1947), further confirm that the Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit all irrational classifications. In *Railway Express*, the Court upheld a traffic regulation despite its underinclusive application, rejecting the argument that the Clause requires perfect consistency. *Id.* at 110. In *Kotch*, the Court deferred to legislative judgments about pilot qualifications, refusing to invalidate a system that treated similarly situated individuals differently. *Id.* at 556.

Olech’s claim—that the Village demanded an 18-foot easement from her while requiring only 15 feet from others—does not implicate the Equal Protection Clause. The Clause does not guarantee “equal treatment in the abstract” but only bars discrimination against groups. *See* *Hernandez v. Texas*, 347 U.S. 475, 478 (1954) (protecting discrete groups from intentional discrimination). Without alleging membership in a suspect class or group, Olech’s claim falls outside the Clause’s ambit.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision also conflicts with this Court’s holding in *Snowden*, which rejected a claim that a state official’s arbitrary denial of certification violated the Equal Protection Clause. *Id.* at 8. The Court explained that the Clause does not “insure against all unequal treatment” but only against “invidious discrimination.” *Id.* Olech’s claim—like the plaintiff’s in *Snowden*—is merely an assertion of unfairness, not a cognizable equal protection violation.

Finally, the Court’s decision in *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), underscores that the Equal Protection Clause targets systemic discrimination, not isolated instances of arbitrary treatment. *Id.* at 91. The Clause does not provide a remedy for every instance of unequal treatment; it redresses only discrimination against groups.

**Conclusion**
The Seventh Circuit’s “class-of-one” theory lacks support in this Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence. The Equal Protection Clause does not create a cause of action for arbitrary treatment unrelated to group-based discrimination. This Court should reverse the decision below and hold that Olech’s claim fails to state a cognizable equal protection claim.
