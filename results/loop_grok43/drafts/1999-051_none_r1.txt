ARGUMENT

The exhaustion doctrine, founded on comity between federal and state courts, precludes federal habeas relief on any claim not first presented to the state courts in compliance with state procedural rules. Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886); New York v. Eno, 155 U.S. 89, 92-93 (1894). A prisoner must “exhaust the remedies available in the courts of the State.” Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116 (1944). Claims omitted from direct appeal or post-conviction proceedings are therefore defaulted. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950).

A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel cannot constitute cause to excuse the default of a separate claim. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486-87 (1953). Every constitutional claim must be presented through available state remedies before federal review may proceed. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 419-20 (1963). Allowing one defaulted claim to revive another would undermine the independent and adequate state ground doctrine that governs federal habeas review of state convictions. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 317-18 (1963).

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, creates no exception to these exhaustion requirements. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 467-68 (1938). A prisoner who fails to raise an ineffective-assistance claim in the manner prescribed by state law does not preserve any federal right to invoke that claim as cause. Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 115 (1935). State procedural rules remain binding absent a showing of cause and prejudice arising from a properly exhausted federal claim. Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 329-30 (1915).

Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed by the Ohio courts as untimely, it supplies no basis for excusing the separate default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
