**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a Plaintiff’s Claims to Arise from or Relate to the Defendant’s Forum Contacts to Permit the Exercise of Specific Personal Jurisdiction.**

The Due Process Clause does not demand that a plaintiff’s claims arise from or relate to the defendant’s contacts with the forum state to permit the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction. *Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 447 (1952) ("[T]he issue is whether the activities of the corporation in the State of Ohio were sufficient to make it fair and reasonable to require the corporation to defend the particular suit which was brought there."). This Court has long recognized that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant whose activities in the forum render it reasonable to subject the defendant to the jurisdiction of the state’s courts, even if the plaintiff’s claims do not arise from those activities. *Id.* at 445-47.

The foundational principle that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on the defendant’s forum activities—regardless of whether the plaintiff’s claims arise from those activities—was established in *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945). In *International Shoe*, this Court held that the Due Process Clause permits a state to exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant if the defendant has "certain minimum contacts with [the state] such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend 'traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.'" *Id.* at 316 (quoting *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940)). The Court further explained that the "minimum contacts" inquiry focuses on the defendant’s activities in the forum state, not the plaintiff’s claims. *Id.* at 319 ("[T]he quality and nature of the activity in relation to the fair and orderly administration of the laws which is the basis for the jurisdiction.").

This principle was reaffirmed in *Perkins*, where the Court upheld the exercise of jurisdiction over a foreign corporation based on the corporation’s activities in the forum state, even though the plaintiff’s claims did not arise from those activities. *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 447 ("[T]he issue is whether the activities of the corporation in the State of Ohio were sufficient to make it fair and reasonable to require the corporation to defend the particular suit which was brought there."). The Court explained that the "minimum contacts" test is satisfied if the defendant’s activities in the forum state are "continuous and systematic," even if the plaintiff’s claims do not arise from those activities. *Id.* at 447-48.

Similarly, in *Wells Fargo & Co. Express v. Ford*, 238 U.S. 503 (1915), this Court upheld the exercise of jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on the defendant’s activities in the forum state, even though the plaintiff’s claims did not arise from those activities. *Id.* at 508 ("[T]he jurisdiction of the court is not dependent upon the nature of the plaintiff’s claim, but upon the defendant’s activities in the state."). The Court explained that the Due Process Clause does not require a plaintiff’s claims to arise from or relate to the defendant’s forum contacts to permit the exercise of jurisdiction. *Id.*

**II. Ford’s Systematic and Continuous Contacts with Montana Render the Exercise of Specific Personal Jurisdiction Consistent with Due Process.**

Ford’s systematic and continuous contacts with Montana render the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford in this case consistent with the Due Process Clause. *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 447-48. Ford’s contacts with Montana include advertising, selling, and servicing its vehicles and parts in the state, as well as deriving substantial revenue from Montana consumers. *Id.* at 447 ("[T]he issue is whether the activities of the corporation in the State of Ohio were sufficient to make it fair and reasonable to require the corporation to defend the particular suit which was brought there.").

This Court has long recognized that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on the defendant’s systematic and continuous contacts with the forum state, even if the plaintiff’s claims do not arise from those contacts. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319 ("[T]he quality and nature of the activity in relation to the fair and orderly administration of the laws which is the basis for the jurisdiction."). In *Perkins*, the Court upheld the exercise of jurisdiction over a foreign corporation based on the corporation’s systematic and continuous activities in the forum state, even though the plaintiff’s claims did not arise from those activities. *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 447-48.

Similarly, in *Minnesota Commercial Men’s Ass’n v. Benn*, 261 U.S. 140 (1923), this Court upheld the exercise of jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on the defendant’s systematic and continuous contacts with the forum state, even though the plaintiff’s claims did not arise from those contacts. *Id.* at 148 ("[T]he jurisdiction of the court is not dependent upon the nature of the plaintiff’s claim, but upon the defendant’s activities in the state."). The Court explained that the Due Process Clause does not require a plaintiff’s claims to arise from or relate to the defendant’s forum contacts to permit the exercise of jurisdiction. *Id.*

**III. The Exercise of Specific Personal Jurisdiction Over Ford in This Case Is Consistent with Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice.**

The exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford in this case is consistent with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316 (quoting *Milliken*, 311 U.S. at 463). Ford’s systematic and continuous contacts with Montana render it reasonable to subject Ford to the jurisdiction of Montana’s courts. *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 447-48. Ford has availed itself of the benefits and protections of Montana’s laws by advertising, selling, and servicing its vehicles and parts in the state, and by deriving substantial revenue from Montana consumers. *Id.* at 447.

This Court has long recognized that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on the defendant’s systematic and continuous contacts with the forum state, even if the plaintiff’s claims do not arise from those contacts. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. Ford*, 238 U.S. 503 (1915), this Court upheld the exercise of jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on the defendant’s systematic and continuous contacts with the forum state, even though the plaintiff’s claims did not arise from those contacts. *Id.* at 508. The Court explained that the Due Process Clause does not require a plaintiff’s claims to arise from or relate to the defendant’s forum contacts to permit the exercise of jurisdiction. *Id.*

**Conclusion**

The Due Process Clause does not require a plaintiff’s claims to arise from or relate to the defendant’s forum contacts to permit the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction. *Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 447. Ford’s systematic and continuous contacts with Montana render the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford in this case consistent with due process. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. The exercise of jurisdiction is also consistent with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. *Id.* at 316. Therefore, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
