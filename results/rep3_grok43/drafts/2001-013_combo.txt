The Occupational Safety and Health Act applies to employment performed in a workplace in a State. 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Section 4(b)(1) withdraws coverage only where another federal agency has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting occupational safety or health. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That withdrawal is not triggered by the mere existence of another agency's general maritime powers. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54 (1932) (statutory exceptions are construed narrowly to preserve the primary regulatory scheme). 

The Coast Guard's authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 and 46 U.S.C. § 6301 is limited to matters expressly delegated; it does not automatically displace other federal regulation of working conditions aboard uninspected vessels. Panama R.R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375, 388 (1924) (admiralty jurisdiction extends only to subjects within the constitutional grant and congressional enactments). Where Congress has not provided comprehensive standards for a particular class of vessels, overlapping federal authority remains available. O'Donnell v. Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co., 318 U.S. 36, 42-43 (1943) (remedial statutes are given liberal construction to effectuate their purpose).

Preemption requires actual exercise of regulatory power, not potential authority. Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (federal occupation of the field must be clear and manifest). The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels address only discrete equipment requirements. Southern Pac. Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917) (state and federal remedies coexist absent direct conflict). No comprehensive scheme governing the working conditions at issue here has been promulgated. Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc., 328 U.S. 1, 5 (1946) (concurrent remedies permitted where statutes do not conflict).

Statutes should be read to avoid rendering any provision superfluous. United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955). Treating the Coast Guard's limited rules as exclusive would nullify the Act's coverage of uninspected vessels operating in state waters. Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406, 409 (1953) (federal maritime law supplements, rather than supplants, other protective legislation). The same principle precludes implied repeal of an earlier statute by a later one absent irreconcilable conflict. Calbeck v. Travelers Ins. Co., 370 U.S. 114, 126 (1962).

The Fifth Circuit's contrary rule rests on an overbroad reading of general maritime authority that the cited decisions do not support. Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 543 (1960) (seamen's remedies are liberally construed). OSHA therefore retains jurisdiction to issue the citations.
