# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SATISFIES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

### A. The Act Regulates Location, Not Content or Viewpoint

The Massachusetts Act establishes a thirty-five-foot buffer zone around reproductive healthcare facility entrances, applicable to all persons regardless of what they say or believe. This is the hallmark of a valid time-place-manner restriction. In Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), this Court upheld a strikingly similar Colorado statute regulating activity within one hundred feet of healthcare facility entrances, recognizing that such a law targets a *location* rather than a *message*. The Colorado Court of Appeals had properly noted, as the record here confirms, that all patients—not merely those seeking a particular procedure—are subject to the same restriction. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 707-08 (noting that "all 60,000" of a clinic's patients "were subjected to the same treatment by the protesters," reinforcing that the regulation addressed conduct affecting everyone alike, not viewpoint).

The Act's four exemptions—for patients, employees, emergency personnel, and passersby—do not smuggle in content discrimination. They simply describe categories of persons whose presence in the zone is unrelated to protest activity. The First Circuit correctly held the Act "content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and a valid time-place-manner regulation." McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I), 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009).

### B. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve Significant Governmental Interests

This Court has repeatedly recognized the government's substantial interest in ensuring unobstructed access to healthcare facilities and protecting patients from confrontational sidewalk encounters. In Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), the Court upheld portions of an injunction creating a thirty-six-foot buffer zone, recognizing that measures preventing "increased stress and anxiety" among patients serve legitimate governmental purposes. See DeWitte v. United States, 520 U.S. 1133, 1133 (1997) (Stevens, J., respecting denial of certiorari) (describing the "substantial governmental interest in protecting the patients' physical and emotional health and safety").

The thirty-five-foot zone here is narrower than the one-hundred-foot floating zone approved in Hill and comparable to the thirty-six-foot fixed zone approved in Madsen. Unlike the fifteen-foot *floating* buffer zone struck down in Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y., 519 U.S. 357 (1997)—which moved unpredictably with individuals entering or leaving, creating enforcement uncertainty—the Massachusetts zone is fixed, clearly marked with painted arcs and posted signage, giving speakers unambiguous notice of where they may stand. This distinction matters: Hill itself was decided after Schenck, and this Court expressly distinguished fixed buffer zones from the floating zone problem. Hill, 530 U.S. at 708-09.

### C. Ample Alternative Channels of Communication Remain

The record compiled below demonstrates that Petitioners retain robust means of communicating their message. As the First Circuit found, Petitioners continue to engage in "oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups." McCullen v. Coakley, 708 F.3d 1, ___ (1st Cir. 2013). Petitioner Smith uses a loudspeaker; Petitioner McCullen displays signage from her car; Petitioner Shea holds large signs. By McCullen's own estimation, she persuaded approximately eighty women to forgo abortions even after the zones took effect—hardly evidence that the Act renders speech ineffective.

This showing satisfies the standard time-place-manner inquiry. The Act need not guarantee Petitioners' preferred method of communication—physical proximity sufficient for what they call "close personal contact." The Constitution requires only that "adequate," not "perfect," alternative channels remain. The record amply supports that conclusion.

## II. HILL v. COLORADO CONTROLS AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

### A. Hill Directly Governs This Case

Hill's core holding—that a state may create a buffer zone around healthcare facilities to protect patients' ability to access care without harassment—applies with full force here. The Massachusetts Act is materially similar to the Colorado statute in Hill: both target location rather than viewpoint, both apply to all speakers regardless of message, and both were designed to prevent the same category of harm—confrontational encounters between protesters and patients approaching medical facilities. Hill, 530 U.S. at 715 (recognizing sidewalks outside healthcare clinics as "quintessential" public forums while nonetheless upholding the buffer zone).

### B. Petitioners Offer No Basis for Overruling Hill

Petitioners' invocation of Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc., 564 U.S. 552 (2011), does not undermine Hill. Those cases addressed fundamentally different factual scenarios—corporate political speech, funeral protests, and pharmaceutical marketing data—not the unique problem of sidewalk confrontations outside abortion clinics. As the First Circuit correctly recognized, these decisions articulate only "conventional First Amendment principles" and cannot serve as "a wedge to subvert the Court's later decisions addressed to the much different problem of how the First Amendment operates when the special concerns of public-sidewalk protests around abortion clinics are at stake."

Stare decisis counsels strongly against overruling Hill. Legislatures across the country, including Massachusetts on two separate occasions, have relied on Hill's framework to craft buffer-zone statutes. Overruling Hill would destabilize this settled area of law without any showing that its reasoning has proven unworkable or that changed circumstances demand reconsideration.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
