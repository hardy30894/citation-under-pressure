**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it permit an impermissible delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). The delegation is fully consistent with the constitutional principle that Congress may delegate authority to administrative agencies so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of that power. This Court has long recognized that Congress may empower executive officers to act in the public interest under broad statutory mandates, provided the standards are not so vague or undefined as to render the delegation a surrender of legislative authority. The Act satisfies this test in every respect.

**I. Congress Has Provided an Intelligible Principle for Temporary Scheduling**

The Constitution does not prohibit delegation of legislative power; it only forbids the delegation of *unfettered* power. As this Court held in *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), “Congress may delegate authority to an administrative officer, provided it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the officer is directed to conform.” The principle must be “sufficiently clear and definite to guide the officer in the exercise of the delegated power.” *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 519 (1911). The temporary scheduling provision of the Controlled Substances Act meets this standard.

Section 811(h) requires the Attorney General to consider specific, enumerated factors before temporarily scheduling a substance: “(1) the actual or relative potential for abuse; (2) the history and current pattern of abuse; (3) the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and (4) the risk to public health.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). These criteria are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory, statutory standards that must be applied before any scheduling action may be taken. The requirement that the Attorney General consider these factors, and that he act only when there is an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” constitutes a clear and definite standard. The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where the standards were no more specific than those in § 811(h). In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court upheld a delegation to the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate rates, even though the statute provided only that the Commission “shall fix such rates as will be just and reasonable.” The Court found that “the standard of reasonableness” was “sufficiently definite to guide the Commission’s action.” Id. Similarly, in *United States v. Rock Royal Co-op., Inc.*, 307 U.S. 533, 540 (1939), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to regulate the marketing of agricultural products under a standard of “fair and equitable” practices. The Court held that “the terms used are not so vague as to make the delegation invalid.” Id.

Here, the standards in § 811(h) are no less definite than those in *American Trucking* or *Rock Royal*. The factors are not open-ended; they are rooted in public health, safety, and abuse patterns—fields in which administrative expertise is essential. The requirement of an “imminent hazard to the public safety” further limits the scope of the delegation, ensuring that temporary scheduling is reserved for emergency situations. This is not a delegation of power to create crimes at will; it is a delegation to act in response to a demonstrable threat, guided by clear statutory criteria.

**II. The Delegation to the Attorney General Is Not Unconstitutional Because It Creates Crimes**

The argument that delegations creating criminal penalties are inherently suspect is without foundation in precedent. The Court has never held that the creation of criminal liability renders a delegation unconstitutional per se. In *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 88 (1921), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to enforce the Packers and Stockyards Act, which included criminal penalties for violations. The Court stated: “The fact that the statute provides for criminal penalties does not render the delegation invalid.” Id. Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 125 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Attorney General to enforce the Sherman Act, even though violations were punishable by fine or imprisonment. The Court emphasized that “the power to enforce a law is not the power to make it.” Id. The delegation here is no different: Congress has defined the crime—unauthorized manufacture of a controlled substance—and the Attorney General is merely empowered to identify which substances fall within that category under emergency conditions.

The Toubys’ claim that the delegation is invalid because it allows the creation of new criminal prohibitions is directly contradicted by *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 402 (1930), where the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Interior to regulate the use of public lands, even though the regulations could result in criminal penalties. The Court held that “the mere fact that the regulations may lead to criminal prosecution does not make the delegation invalid.” Id. The same principle applies here: the delegation is to *identify* substances that are already subject to criminal penalties under the Act, not to *create* new crimes.

**III. The Subdelegation to the DEA Is Authorized and Proper**

The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is fully authorized by Congress. Section 811(h) does not prohibit subdelegation; it merely vests the power in the Attorney General. The Attorney General, as head of the executive branch, may delegate his statutory duties to subordinate officers, so long as the delegation is consistent with the statute and does not exceed the scope of the authority granted. This principle was affirmed in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 519 (1911), where the Court upheld the delegation of authority to the Secretary of the Interior to enforce the Lacey Act, including the power to issue regulations and conduct investigations. The Court stated: “The Secretary may delegate his authority to subordinate officers, provided the delegation is not contrary to law.” Id.
