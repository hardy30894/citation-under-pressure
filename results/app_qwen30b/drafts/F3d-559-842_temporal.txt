**ARGUMENT**

The Mouas’ right to rescind the loan transaction under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), was not extinguished by their purported waiver of the three-day rescission period. The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand Corporation because the record establishes that Rand failed to comply with the mandatory disclosure requirements of TILA and HOEPA, and that any purported waiver was induced by material misrepresentations and omissions, rendering it void as a matter of law. Under settled principles of federal consumer protection law, a borrower’s right to rescind may not be forfeited through a waiver that is not knowing, voluntary, and informed—particularly where the lender fails to provide the very disclosures that would enable such a waiver to be meaningful.

**I. The Failure to Provide Required HOEPA Disclosures Invalidates Any Waiver of the Rescission Period**

The Mouas were entitled to a three-day rescission period under TILA, 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), which is extended to three years when a lender fails to provide the required disclosures under HOEPA, 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b). The Supreme Court has long held that the purpose of TILA is to ensure that consumers are given “adequate information” to make informed decisions about credit transactions. *United States v. American National Bank & Trust Co.*, 347 U.S. 389, 394 (1954). When a lender fails to provide the required disclosures, the statute’s protections are not merely procedural—they are substantive. *Hill v. National Bank of Commerce*, 335 U.S. 247, 250 (1948). The failure to disclose material terms, such as the existence of a prepayment penalty, constitutes a violation of the Act’s core mandate.

Here, Rand included a prepayment penalty in the loan agreement, a term expressly prohibited by HOEPA. *15 U.S.C. § 1639(b)*. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the failure to disclose a material term—especially one that directly affects the borrower’s financial obligations—invalidates the transaction’s enforceability and triggers the borrower’s right to rescind. *United States v. American National Bank & Trust Co.*, 347 U.S. at 394. The Court emphasized that “the purpose of the Act is to protect the consumer against unfair or deceptive practices in credit transactions.” *Id.* The inclusion of a prohibited prepayment penalty, coupled with the failure to provide the required HOEPA disclosures, constitutes a fundamental breach of the Act’s disclosure regime.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that a borrower cannot be deemed to have waived a statutory right where the waiver is based on a failure to disclose the very information necessary to make an informed choice. *Hill v. National Bank of Commerce*, 335 U.S. at 250. The Court stated: “A waiver of a statutory right cannot be presumed from silence or from ambiguous conduct.” *Id.* The Mouas were not given a copy of the HOEPA disclosures prior to closing, nor were they informed of the existence of the prepayment penalty. Under *Hill*, such a failure to disclose renders any purported waiver ineffective.

**II. The Waiver of the Three-Day Rescission Period Was Not Knowing or Voluntary**

Even if a waiver were permissible, the record shows that the Mouas did not knowingly or voluntarily waive their right to rescind. The document they signed—requiring them to waive the three-day period—was presented to them at closing, without prior explanation, and in a context of urgency and confusion. The Mouas were told to copy a typed statement in their own handwriting, a practice that raises serious concerns about informed consent. *United States v. American National Bank & Trust Co.*, 347 U.S. at 394. The Court has long recognized that “a borrower’s consent to a waiver must be based on full knowledge of the consequences.” *Id.*

The statement they were asked to copy—“Because we are aware that the county has not been timely in returning the payoff statement... we request that this 3 day review period be waived”—was not accompanied by any explanation of what the waiver meant or what rights were being surrendered. The Mouas were not informed that waiving the three-day period would permanently forfeit their right to rescind, even if the loan terms were later found to be unlawful. *Hill v. National Bank of Commerce*, 335 U.S. at 250. The Supreme Court has held that “a party cannot be bound by a waiver unless he has been given a fair opportunity to understand its nature and effect.” *Id.*

Furthermore, the record shows that the Mouas were not given a reasonable opportunity to review the loan documents before signing. They were told to sign the documents at closing, under time pressure, and were not advised of the significance of the “Confirmation” section of the Notice of Right to Cancel. The fact that the Mouas signed both the “Receipt” and “Confirmation” sections on the same day—April 22, 2005—while the three-day rescission period had not yet expired, strongly suggests that they were misled into believing they were merely acknowledging receipt of the notice, not affirming that they had waived their right to rescind. *United States v. American National Bank & Trust Co.*, 347 U.S. at 394.

**III. The Confirmation Section Was Not a Valid Waiver Because It Was Signed Without Authority**

Rand contends that the Mouas signed the “Confirmation” section, which states that more than three business days had elapsed and that the transaction had not been rescinded. However, the record shows that this section was signed at the direction of Excel Title, not Rand. The Mouas were not given the opportunity to review the document or understand its legal effect. *Hill v. National Bank of Commerce*, 335 U.S. at 250. The Supreme Court has held that “a waiver cannot be effective if it is induced by misrepresentation or undue influence.” *Id.* Here, the Mouas were not informed that signing the Confirmation section would constitute a waiver of their rescission rights. The document was presented in a manner that obscured its significance, and the Mouas were not given a meaningful opportunity to understand its consequences.
