The ADEA does not reach appointed state judges. Congress did not manifest an unequivocal intention to regulate the tenure of state judicial officers, a subject traditionally reserved to the states. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819); Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 195 (1824); Collector v. Day, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 113, 124 (1871). Absent such a clear statement, the statute must be read to preserve the federal balance. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 176 (1803). The definition of “employee” therefore excludes “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges selected under the Non-Partisan Court Plan exercise precisely that function: their decisions establish binding rules of decision that fill gaps in statutes and precedents and govern future conduct throughout the state. The text and structure of the exemption confirm that it reaches judicial as well as executive and legislative officers.

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s constitutional retirement requirement would survive equal-protection scrutiny. The Fourteenth Amendment does not demand identical treatment of all persons. Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885). A classification survives if it rests on any state of facts that reasonably may be conceived to justify it. Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co., 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911); Tigner v. Texas, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940). The State may conclude that judges who have reached seventy are more likely to suffer the physical and mental decline that impairs the exacting work of the bench, that mandatory retirement eliminates case-by-case removal proceedings, and that the rule facilitates orderly succession and broader representation on the judiciary. Nebbia v. New York, 291 U.S. 502, 537 (1934). These considerations supply a rational basis. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
