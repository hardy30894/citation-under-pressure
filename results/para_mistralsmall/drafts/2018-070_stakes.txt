**ARGUMENT**

I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) does not permit the application of state law unless federal law contains a gap or void that state law is needed to fill.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with [federal law], the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State . . . are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has repeatedly held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law under the OCSLA only where federal law is “incomplete or inadequate.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969); see also Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Hammond, 726 F.2d 483, 490 (9th Cir. 1984) (“state law is incorporated only to the extent necessary to fill a significant void or gap in federal law”). The absence of a federal remedy or gap in coverage is a prerequisite to the assimilation of state law. See Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (state law applies only “to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law).

The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a comprehensive federal scheme that occupies the entire field of wage and hour regulation. See 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq.; see also Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc., 450 U.S. 728, 736-37 (1981) (FLSA establishes “a floor of minimum wages and overtime pay” and “a comprehensive federal scheme”). The FLSA contains an express savings clause permitting more protective state wage and hour laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218, but this clause does not create a gap in federal law. Rather, it preserves state authority where federal law is silent on a particular issue. See id.; see also Cipriano v. Board of Supervisors, 344 F. Supp. 312, 315 (E.D. La. 1972) (savings clause “does not create a void in federal law to be filled by state law”).

Here, the FLSA provides a complete remedy for wage and hour disputes, including overtime and meal period claims. See 29 U.S. C. §§ 206, 207. The FLSA’s savings clause does not create a gap; it merely allows states to impose more stringent requirements. See 29 U.S.C. § 218. Because federal law is not incomplete or inadequate, California’s wage and hour laws are not “applicable” under the OCSLA. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

II. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and therefore cannot apply on the outer Continental Shelf.

Even if state law were “applicable,” it must also not be “inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The FLSA’s savings clause does not immunize state laws from inconsistency analysis. See Cipriano, 344 F. Supp. at 315. California’s meal period requirements conflict with the FLSA’s framework, which does not mandate meal periods. See 29 C.F.R. § 785.19; see also Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd., 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004) (federal regulations define the scope of the FLSA).

Moreover, California’s requirement that employees receive a 30-minute meal period for every five hours worked is more onerous than the FLSA’s minimum standards. See Cal. Lab. Code § 512; 29 U.S.C. § 207. Because the FLSA does not require meal periods, California’s law is inconsistent with federal law and cannot apply on the OCS. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

III. The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedent and the OCSLA’s text.

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state law applies under the OCSLA whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, without requiring a gap in federal coverage. See Parker Drilling Mgmt. Servs., Ltd. v. Newton, 935 F.3d 1064, 1070 (9th Cir. 2019). This misreads the OCSLA’s text and this Court’s precedent. See Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 359; Cont’l Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision is not a blank check for state law to supplement federal law. Rather, it incorporates state law only where federal law is incomplete. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A); Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 359. The Ninth Circuit’s approach would permit states to impose additional requirements on OCS operations, undermining the uniformity of federal law. See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988) (OCSLA ensures “a uniform body of law”).

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA permits the application of state law only where federal law contains a gap or void that state law is needed to fill. Because the FLSA is a comprehensive federal scheme that provides a complete remedy for wage and hour disputes, California’s wage and hour laws do not apply on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
