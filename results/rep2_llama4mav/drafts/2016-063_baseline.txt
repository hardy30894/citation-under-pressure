The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), effectively overruled the D.C. Circuit's precedent in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and dictates that judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction lies in district court.

In Kloeckner, the Supreme Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court. 568 U.S. at 56. The Court's reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals. The statutory text, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), defines a mixed case as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB. The Kloeckner Court interpreted this language to mean that the action must be "appealable to the MSPB." 568 U.S. at 44. However, this language is not limited to procedural dismissals, as the D.C. Circuit suggested. Rather, it applies to all dismissals, including those on jurisdictional grounds.

The distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not supported by the statutory text or the Kloeckner decision. In both situations, the MSPB has determined that the employee's appeal is not properly before it. See Harms v. IRS, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007-08 (10th Cir. 2003) (suggesting that the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals may not be meaningful). The Kloeckner Court's awareness of the potential distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, as evidenced by the Justices' questions during oral argument, does not imply that the Court intended to preserve this distinction.

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's decision in Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), which held that Kloeckner did not apply to jurisdictional dismissals, is not persuasive. The Conforto court's reliance on the MSPB's discretion to excuse procedural errors, 713 F.3d at 1118 n.1, does not provide a meaningful basis for distinguishing between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals.

Perry's arguments, which the D.C. Circuit did not consider due to its adherence to Powell, demonstrate that the statutory scheme is best served by having judicial review of MSPB dismissals, including those on jurisdictional grounds, reside in district court. For instance, the statute contains deadlines that would be difficult to apply if the forum for judicial review varied based on the MSPB's ultimate decision about its jurisdiction. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(2), (e)(1)(B), (e)(2). Moreover, jurisdictional dismissals grounded in the voluntariness of the challenged employment action can overlap with the merits of a discrimination claim, making district court a more suitable forum for review.

In light of Kloeckner, the D.C. Circuit should have reconsidered its precedent in Powell and held that judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction lies in district court. This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit's decision and hold that Perry's case should be heard in district court.
