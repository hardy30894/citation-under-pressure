## ARGUMENT

### I. Congress Constitutionally Delegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, provided Congress lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act satisfies this standard.

**A. The Act Provides Detailed Standards and Procedural Safeguards**

Section 811(h) requires the Attorney General to find that temporary scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety” and to consider three specific factors: the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). These factors are drawn from the broader criteria in § 811(c), which the Attorney General must also consider. The Act further mandates notice in the Federal Register and transmission of the proposal to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be taken into account. § 811(h)(4). Temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. § 811(h)(2).

Such specificity far exceeds the “intelligible principle” that the Court has consistently upheld. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426–27 (1944), the Court sustained a delegation to the Price Administrator to fix maximum prices, guided by the general standard of “fair and equitable” prices and a list of factors to consider. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to appoint conservators, noting that “well-known and generally acceptable standards” in the banking field supplied sufficient guidance. Here, the Act supplies both specific statutory factors and the binding input of the Secretary of Health and Human Services, who can veto permanent scheduling. § 811(b). The temporary scheduling power is thus narrowly channeled.

**B. The Delegation Is Narrower Than Those Upheld in Prior Cases**

Congress has repeatedly delegated authority to executive officers to define criminal conduct. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521–22 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make regulations for national forests, the violation of which was a crime. The Court reasoned that Congress had “specified the purposes” and “the means to be employed.” *Id.* at 518. Similarly, in *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to set standards for imported tea, with penalties for violation. The Act here is even more constrained: the Attorney General must follow enumerated factors, obtain expert input, and act only to avoid an imminent hazard.

The two cases in which the Court invalidated delegations are readily distinguishable. In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the statute “declared no policy” and “established no criterion” to govern the President’s discretion. In *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 541–42 (1935), the statute gave the President “virtually unfettered” power to approve codes of fair competition. Here, Congress declared a clear policy—to protect public safety from hazardous drugs—and provided multiple criteria and procedures to guide the Attorney General. The delegation thus satisfies the “intelligible principle” test.

**C. The Delegation Is Consistent with Historical Practice**

From the earliest days, Congress has delegated authority to the Executive to determine the precise scope of criminal prohibitions. In *The Brig Aurora*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813), the Court upheld a statute authorizing the President to revive trade restrictions upon finding that certain conditions had been met. In *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 693 (1892), the Court upheld a delegation to the President to impose retaliatory tariffs. And in *Wayman v. Southard*, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825), Chief Justice Marshall recognized that Congress may delegate “subordinate” legislative powers when “the legislature itself could not act.” The temporary scheduling power is precisely such a subordinate power, necessary to respond quickly to emerging drug threats.

### II. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated Authority to the DEA Administrator

The Controlled Substances Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions. Section 871(a) provides: “The Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” The Attorney General exercised that authority by regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator the functions vested in the Attorney General under the Act.

This subdelegation is consistent with long-standing precedent. In *United States v. Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court upheld the President’s delegation of authority to the Alien Property Custodian, noting that “the President may delegate his authority to a subordinate.” In *United States v. Eaton*, 144 U.S. 677, 687 (1892), the Court upheld a regulation delegating arrest authority to subordinate officers. And in *Cincinnati, New Orleans & Texas Pacific Ry. Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 162 U.S. 184, 196 (1896), the Court recognized that administrative agencies may act through their subordinates. Because Congress expressly authorized delegation, the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was lawful.

### Conclusion

The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle and adequate procedural safeguards, satisfying the constitutional requirements for delegation. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was expressly authorized by statute. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
