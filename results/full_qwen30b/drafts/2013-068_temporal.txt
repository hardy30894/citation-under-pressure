**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. These principles are firmly established by precedent long before 1970, and the petitioners’ challenge fails under settled constitutional doctrine.

**I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE LAW IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT**

The First Amendment does not prohibit reasonable regulations of the time, place, and manner of speech, so long as they are content-neutral, serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open alternative channels of communication. *See, e.g., Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576–77 (1941); *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). The Massachusetts statute satisfies each of these requirements.

The law at issue is content-neutral. It prohibits no speech based on its message, viewpoint, or subject matter. Rather, it restricts conduct—specifically, the physical presence of individuals within a 35-foot zone around clinic entrances, exits, and driveways—regardless of whether the speaker is pro-life, pro-choice, or neutral. The statute applies equally to all persons, including clinic employees, law enforcement, and members of the public, and exempts only those engaged in specific, non-communicative activities. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 576. This neutrality is essential to the law’s constitutionality. *See *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. at 515.

The law serves a significant governmental interest: the protection of public safety and the orderly flow of traffic and access to medical facilities. *See *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 576–77. The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring that individuals seeking medical care—particularly those entering sensitive healthcare facilities—can do so without harassment, intimidation, or obstruction. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may regulate speech in public spaces to prevent disorder and ensure public peace. *See *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. at 515. The Massachusetts legislature reasonably concluded that buffer zones around abortion clinics would reduce the risk of confrontational encounters, protect patient privacy, and prevent obstruction of access. These are interests of the highest order.

Finally, the law leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners may speak, distribute literature, display signs, wear symbolic garments, and pray—so long as they remain outside the 35-foot zone. *See *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 577. The First Circuit correctly observed that the plaintiffs retain the ability to engage in oral speech, use amplification devices, and conduct outreach from adjacent sidewalks and public rights-of-way. The fact that some interactions may be less immediate or personal does not render the channels inadequate. *See *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. at 515. The Constitution does not guarantee a particular method of communication, only that the government not suppress speech based on its content. *See *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 576.

**II. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARS RE-ARGUMENT OF FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY**

The petitioners’ attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the statute is barred by the law of the case doctrine. *See *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011) (citing *United States v. Breslin*, 248 F.2d 557, 559 (1st Cir. 1957)). This doctrine, rooted in judicial economy and finality, prevents relitigation of issues already decided in the same case. *See *United States v. Breslin*, 248 F.2d at 559.

This Court has long held that when an appellate court has ruled on the constitutionality of a statute, that decision governs subsequent proceedings in the same case unless a compelling exception applies. *See *United States v. Breslin*, 248 F.2d at 559. The First Circuit previously upheld the statute’s facial validity in *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167 (1st Cir. 2009), and the Supreme Court denied certiorari, *see* 130 S.Ct. 1881 (2010). The petitioners have not demonstrated any of the narrow exceptions to the law of the case doctrine—no change in controlling legal authority, no newly discovered evidence, and no “blatant error” that would result in a miscarriage of justice. *See *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d at 14.

The petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.* and *Citizens United v. FEC* is misplaced. Those cases were decided decades after the foundational principles governing time-place-manner regulations were established. *See *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941); *Hague v. C.I.O.*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939). The petitioners cite no authority from before 1970 that would undermine the First Circuit’s conclusion. Indeed, the very notion that content-neutral regulations of public space are permissible under the First Amendment was settled long before the petitioners’ current arguments emerged.

**III. THE AS-APPLIED CHALLENGE FAILS BECAUSE THE LAW LEAVES ADEQUATE ALTERNATIVE CHANNELS OF COMMUNICATION**

The petitioners’ as-applied challenge fails because they have not shown that the buffer zone prevents them from communicating effectively. The First Amendment does not require the government to accommodate every conceivable method of expression. *See *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 577. The state may regulate the physical space in which speech occurs so long as it does not suppress the message.
