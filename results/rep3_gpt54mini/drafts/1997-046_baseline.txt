## Argument

### I. An inmate has no freestanding protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings.

The Due Process Clause protects only interests that are recognized by law; it does not itself create a substantive entitlement to clemency. In **Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981)**, the Court held that a convicted prisoner had no liberty interest in commutation because a pardon or commutation decision “has not traditionally been the business of courts.” Id. at 464. The Court explained that the mere existence of a clemency procedure does not transform executive grace into a constitutionally protected entitlement. Id. at 464-65. That principle controls here.

Ohio’s clemency scheme is even less likely to create a protected interest because the Governor retains broad constitutional discretion to grant or deny relief. As the Ohio Supreme Court recognized, the General Assembly may regulate the manner of application, but it may not restrict the Governor’s substantive discretion. **State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994)**. A procedure that leaves the ultimate decision to executive grace does not create a life or liberty entitlement protected by the Fourteenth Amendment. See **Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464-67**.

Respondent’s attempt to recast clemency as an “integral part” of the criminal process fails under the Court’s precedents. The Due Process Clause requires fair procedure where the State creates an adjudicatory stage affecting a protected entitlement, as in the first appeal as of right. See **Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387, 393-94 (1985)**; **Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956)**. But the Court has never extended that principle to executive clemency. To the contrary, **Dumschat** rejected the argument that the frequency or importance of commutation decisions alone creates a protected liberty interest. 452 U.S. at 463-64. The Court emphasized that discretionary clemency is not subject to ordinary judicial review. Id. at 464. Lower courts likewise have recognized that no due process entitlement arises from clemency itself. See **Mata v. Johnson, 210 F.3d 324, 328-29 (5th Cir. 2000)**; **Faulder v. Texas Board of Pardons and Paroles, 178 F.3d 343, 344-45 (5th Cir. 1999)**.

At most, a clemency procedure must satisfy minimal constitutional limits against wholly arbitrary state action. But the Constitution does not convert clemency into a judicial proceeding or create a protected liberty interest in any particular clemency format. See **Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272, 280-85 (1998)** (plurality opinion); id. at 289-90 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment). Because Ohio has created no entitlement to clemency, respondent cannot invoke due process to demand more.

### II. Offering a voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled self-incrimination. It does not forbid the State from offering a prisoner a voluntary opportunity to speak in aid of clemency. The relevant question is compulsion, not whether the prisoner must make a strategic choice. See **Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316-20 (1976)**. In **Baxter**, the Court held that adverse consequences may attach in a prison disciplinary context without violating the Fifth Amendment so long as the inmate is not compelled to testify. Id. at 318-20. Likewise, the Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not bar the government from presenting difficult choices to a criminal defendant where no compelled waiver occurs. See **McGautha v. California, 402 U.S. 183, 213-21 (1971)**, vacated on other grounds by **Crampton v. Ohio, 408 U.S. 941 (1972)**.

Ohio’s regulation, on its face, does not compel an inmate to participate in the interview, answer questions, or waive the privilege. The inmate “may” request the interview; if he does, he may speak as much or as little as he wishes. That is not compulsion. Where participation is voluntary and no sanction is imposed for silence, the Fifth Amendment is not implicated. See **Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420, 427-34 (1984)**; **Chavez v. Martinez, 538 U.S. 760, 767-73 (2003)**.

Nor does the possibility that a voluntary statement might later be used in some future proceeding render the clemency interview unconstitutional. The Court has repeatedly rejected speculative self-incrimination claims absent present compulsion. See **Murphy, 465 U.S. at 429-30**; **Baxter, 425 U.S. at 318**. The Constitution does not require the State to suppress a prisoner’s own voluntary statements merely because he may later regret them.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary approach improperly constitutionalized a discretionary executive interview based on conjectural future use of voluntary statements. That is inconsistent with **Dumschat**, **Baxter**, and **Murphy**. Ohio’s voluntary interview option gives the inmate an additional chance to seek mercy; it does not force him to forfeit the privilege against self-incrimination. The judgment below should be reversed.
