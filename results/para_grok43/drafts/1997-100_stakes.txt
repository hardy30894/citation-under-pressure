**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation once a reviewing court has found the evidence insufficient. The Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969).

In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), this Court held that double-jeopardy principles govern a capital sentencing proceeding that “resemble[s] a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 439. The Court identified the decisive features: the prosecution was required to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt before a jury, the defendant enjoyed the full panoply of trial rights, and the sentencer’s discretion was cabined by enumerated statutory criteria. *Id.* at 437-438. Because those procedures “ha[d] the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” an acquittal at sentencing precluded retrial. *Id.* at 439.

The same hallmarks are present here. California law requires the prosecution to prove a prior-conviction allegation “beyond a reasonable doubt.” *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 862 (1974). The defendant is entitled to a jury determination, Pen. Code § 1025, confrontation and cross-examination of witnesses, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996), and application of the rules of evidence, *People v. Myers*, 5 Cal. 4th 1193, 1201 (1993). These procedures are not optional; they are the exclusive means by which the State may obtain the enhanced sentence it seeks. When the Court of Appeal determined that the evidence failed to satisfy that standard, the State’s one “opportunity to offer whatever proof it could assemble” had been exhausted. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 11 (1978).

*Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), confirms that the constitutional label “sentencing” is not dispositive. There, a judge’s life-without-parole finding, though rendered in a non-jury proceeding, was held to be an acquittal for double-jeopardy purposes because it rested on a failure of proof under the statutory criteria. *Id.* at 211. The Court looked to the “character of the proceeding,” not its caption. *Id.* at 209.

The State’s contrary position—that double jeopardy never reaches noncapital sentencing—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. *Bullington* and *Rumsey* turned on the procedural character of the hearing, not on the penalty’s severity. Because the California prior-conviction proceeding is functionally indistinguishable from the capital sentencing hearings in those cases, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial.
