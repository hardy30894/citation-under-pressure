Ohio’s clemency process creates no protected life or liberty interest, and its optional interview does not compel self-incrimination.

I. Ohio’s clemency process creates no protected liberty interest.

Clemency has never been a constitutional entitlement. From the Founding era forward, the Court has treated pardon and commutation as matters of executive grace, not judicially enforceable right. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380–81 (1866); *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128, 147–48 (1872). The Court later reiterated that clemency is “the prerogative of the executive.” *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486–87 (1927). That principle forecloses any claim that a state creates a protected liberty interest merely by establishing a clemency procedure.

The Due Process Clause protects interests already recognized by law; it does not itself create a right to discretionary executive mercy. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369–70 (1886); *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473 v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895–99 (1961). Where the government retains discretion whether to grant or deny relief, there is no protected entitlement. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 569–71 (1972); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 599–603 (1972). Ohio’s clemency scheme leaves the Governor free to grant mercy or withhold it. That is discretion, not entitlement.

The Court’s clemency precedent confirms the point. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 463–67 (1981), the Court held that there is no liberty interest in commutation and no due process right to reasons for denial. *Dumschat* rests on the older rule that clemency is executive grace. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81; *Biddle*, 274 U.S. at 486–87. Ohio’s procedure therefore does not trigger procedural due process.

II. The optional clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

The Self-Incrimination Clause forbids compulsion, not voluntary speech. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 67–68 (1906); *McCarthy v. Arndstein*, 266 U.S. 34, 40–41 (1924). Ohio’s regulation does not require Woodard to attend an interview, answer questions, or waive any privilege. It merely offers him a chance to speak if he believes doing so will help his clemency petition. A voluntary choice of that kind is not compulsion.

The fact that silence may reduce the chance of clemency does not make the procedure unconstitutional. The Court has long recognized that the Fifth Amendment does not prevent the government from offering benefits conditioned on cooperation, so long as the individual remains free to refuse. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598–605 (1896); *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562–64 (1892). Nor does the Constitution bar adverse consequences flowing from a person’s own strategic decision to speak. *Mann v. People of State of Illinois*, 292 U.S. 602, 604–05 (1934); *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943).

That principle is especially clear here because the interview is optional and the prisoner may remain silent throughout. The Court has distinguished between compelled testimony and a choice to speak at one’s own risk. *United States ex rel. Vajtauer v. Commissioner of Immigration*, 273 U.S. 103, 106–07 (1927); *Maggio v. Zeitz*, 333 U.S. 56, 63–64 (1948). Ohio compels nothing. If Woodard chooses to speak in hopes of mercy, he does so voluntarily, and the Fifth Amendment is not implicated.

The contrary view would improperly constitutionalize a discretionary mercy process. The Court has cautioned against converting discretionary government procedures into judicially enforceable entitlements absent clear legal limits. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers*, 367 U.S. at 895–99; *McAndrews v. State of North Carolina*, 154 U.S. 391, 399–400 (1894). Ohio’s clemency interview is simply an optional opportunity to seek mercy, not an unconstitutional condition or compelled confession.

For those reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
