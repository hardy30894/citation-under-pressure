# ARGUMENT

## The Use of Bryant's Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Under § 117(a) Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment

### I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings in the First Instance

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches only in proceedings where the Constitution applies. Tribal courts, as instrumentalities of retained tribal sovereignty rather than arms of the federal or state government, are not bound by the Sixth Amendment's guarantee. This Court has long recognized that tribal sovereignty predates and exists independent of the Constitution, and that the Bill of Rights consequently does not constrain the exercise of that inherent sovereign authority. Bryant's tribal court convictions were therefore not constitutionally infirm; they were valid exercises of tribal criminal jurisdiction, conducted pursuant to the procedural protections Congress itself established in the Indian Civil Rights Act, not the Sixth Amendment.

Because Bryant's tribal convictions violated no constitutional right at the time they were entered, they are readily distinguishable from convictions obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment in state or federal court. This Court's recidivist jurisprudence has consistently permitted the use of prior valid convictions—even those obtained without counsel where counsel was not constitutionally required—to establish predicate status in subsequent proceedings. See Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948) (rejecting due process challenge to use of prior convictions to establish habitual offender status, explaining that "we cannot treat a mere error of state law, if one occurred, as a denial of due process").

### II. This Court's Recidivist-Statute Precedents Confirm That Valid Prior Convictions May Serve as Predicate Offenses

This Court has repeatedly upheld the use of prior convictions—including those obtained without the full panoply of constitutional protections later required—as elements of recidivist offenses, so long as the prior conviction was valid when rendered. In Carlesi v. New York, 233 U.S. 51 (1914), this Court upheld the use of a prior federal conviction, notwithstanding an intervening presidential pardon, to enhance punishment for a subsequent state offense, recognizing the state's legitimate interest in treating repeat offenders differently from first offenders.

Similarly, in Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448 (1962), this Court addressed the due process requirements attendant to habitual offender proceedings and confirmed that a habitual offender charge may be defeated by "proof that the prior convictions were not constitutionally valid as, for example, where one went to trial without a lawyer under circumstances where the appointment of someone to represent him was a requirement of due process." Id. at 452. The negative implication is clear: where appointment of counsel was *not* constitutionally required—as in tribal court—the prior conviction remains valid and usable.

This principle finds further support in Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554 (1966), where this Court upheld Texas's recidivist statute against constitutional challenge, recognizing the substantial state interest in using prior convictions to identify and punish repeat offenders more severely, an interest fully applicable to the federal scheme here.

### III. Burgett Is Inapplicable Because It Addressed Convictions Void for Violating the Sixth Amendment

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on the principle that constitutionally invalid convictions cannot support enhanced punishment, as articulated in Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), rests on a fundamental misapprehension. In Burgett, this Court held that a conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment—where counsel was constitutionally required but not provided—could not be used to support guilt or enhance punishment because such a conviction was "void" and its use would allow "the practical effect of erasing" the constitutional violation while simultaneously exploiting it. Id. at 115. But Burgett's rule presupposes a predicate constitutional violation. Bryant's tribal convictions suffered no such infirmity, because the Sixth Amendment never applied to those proceedings. Burgett cannot logically extend to convictions that were never unconstitutional to begin with.

### IV. Sound Federal Policy and Respect for Tribal Sovereignty Favor Permitting Use of These Convictions

The practical consequences of the Ninth Circuit's rule are severe. As this Court recognized in Chandler v. Fretag, 348 U.S. 3, 9 (1954), the "gravity" of habitual offender accusations underscores the importance of allowing the government to rely on prior valid convictions to identify and punish serial offenders. Congress designed § 117(a) specifically to address the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country, relying on tribal convictions—entered pursuant to tribal sovereign authority and the procedural protections Congress itself deemed adequate under the Indian Civil Rights Act—as evidence of a defendant's recidivism.

The reliability concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment, addressed in cases such as Sigler v. Losieau, 396 U.S. 988 (1969), and Price v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 488 (1910) (recognizing broad deference to prior valid adjudications in recidivist and extradition contexts), are adequately addressed by the procedural protections ICRA affords, including the right to counsel at tribal expense in appropriate circumstances and other due process guarantees tailored to tribal court systems. Just as this Court in Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196 (1948), emphasized that convictions must comport with the process actually due under the applicable sovereign's law, Bryant's convictions comported fully with the process due under ICRA.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that Bryant's uncounseled tribal court convictions were properly used as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).
