**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether the reinstatement statute of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1), applies to aliens who re-entered the United States before its enactment in 1997, and whether such application violates the constitutional prohibition against retroactive laws. The answer to both questions is no. The statute, enacted in 1996, operates retroactively to deprive petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas of relief he had a reasonable expectation of obtaining, and such application is impermissible under settled principles of due process and statutory construction.

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Pre-Enactment Re-Entries**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1), provides that a prior deportation order may be reinstated upon re-entry without a hearing or opportunity to apply for relief. However, this provision was not in effect when petitioner entered the United States in 1982. The statute’s retroactive application to conduct occurring before its enactment violates the fundamental principle that “a law cannot be applied to transactions which occurred before its passage.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 26 (1953). The Supreme Court has consistently held that “a statute which imposes a new disability or imposes a new obligation, or which takes away a right previously existing, cannot be applied to transactions which occurred before its passage.” *United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 20 (1948). Here, the statute imposes a new disability—denial of all forms of relief—on individuals who re-entered the country before its enactment. That disability did not exist at the time of petitioner’s re-entry in 1982.

The Court has long recognized that “the law is not to be applied retroactively to the prejudice of a person who has acted in good faith under a previous law.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948). Petitioner re-entered the United States in 1982, when no such statute existed. He lived in the country for nearly two decades, married a U.S. citizen, and, in good faith, applied for adjustment of status in 2001. He did so under the belief that he could seek relief under the immigration laws then in force. To now deny him that relief based on a law enacted after his re-entry is to apply the law retroactively to his conduct, which is impermissible.

**II. The Application of the Statute Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

The application of the reinstatement statute to petitioner’s case is not merely retroactive—it is *impermissibly* retroactive. The Supreme Court has held that “a statute which takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, or imposes a new disability, or increases the penalty for a past offense, is retroactive in the worst sense.” *United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 20 (1948). The statute here does all of these: it takes away a right—adjustment of status—that petitioner had a reasonable expectation of exercising under the law as it existed at the time of his re-entry.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the right to be heard before being deprived of liberty or property is a fundamental principle of justice.” *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954). The reinstatement statute denies petitioner any hearing or opportunity to apply for relief. This is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive deprivation of a right that existed at the time of his re-entry. The Court has held that “a law which deprives a person of a right which he had under the law existing at the time of the act complained of is retroactive.” *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 26 (1953).

Moreover, the Court has recognized that “the mere fact that a statute is not expressly made retroactive does not prevent its application to past conduct if the effect is to alter the legal consequences of past acts.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948). Here, the statute operates to alter the legal consequences of petitioner’s re-entry in 1982—consequences that were governed by a different legal regime. The law in 1982 did not bar adjustment of status for re-entrants with long-standing ties to the United States. The law in 1997 does. To apply the latter to the former is to apply the law retroactively.

**III. Petitioner Had a Protectable Expectation of Relief**

Petitioner had a protectable expectation of relief under the law as it existed at the time of his re-entry. The Court has held that “a person may have a legitimate expectation of protection under the law, even though the law is not written in a statute.” *United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 20 (1948). Petitioner lived in the United States for nearly twenty years after his re-entry, married a U.S. citizen, and raised a family. He acted in reliance on the immigration laws as they existed at the time. To now deny him relief based on a law enacted after his re-entry is to deprive him of a benefit he reasonably expected.

The Court has held that “a person who has acted in good faith under a previous law cannot be deprived of the benefit of that law by a subsequent statute.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948). Petitioner acted in good faith. He did not re-enter the country to evade the law; he re-entered to build a life. He did not know that a law would be enacted that would strip him of the right to seek adjustment of status. To apply such a law retroactively is to punish him for conduct that was lawful at the time.
