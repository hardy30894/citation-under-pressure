The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not invite state law whenever it merely “pertains” to the dispute. It incorporates adjacent state law only when there is a genuine gap in federal law. That is the structure Congress adopted, and this Court’s pre-1970 cases show why.

OCSLA makes federal law paramount on the Shelf. Congress provided that the Constitution and laws of the United States extend there “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). That language reflects a federal enclave model, not a free-ranging command to borrow all state rules touching the subject matter. In Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips v. Guy F. Atkinson Co., 313 U.S. 508 (1941), the Court explained that when Congress legislates for a federal project, federal law governs the field Congress has occupied; state law does not apply simply because it concerns the same general topic. Likewise, in Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States, 318 U.S. 363 (1943), the Court held that federal rights and duties should not turn on varying state rules where Congress has established a federal program. OCSLA follows that same federal-enclave logic.

The statutory text confirms it. Congress declared that adjacent state law is adopted only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That language was not borrowed to authorize supplementation whenever state law is consistent in the abstract. It was chosen to operate like established federal “assimilation” statutes, which fill only interstitial gaps. In United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court upheld prospective incorporation of state criminal law in federal enclaves because the federal law was “deficient” and state law supplied a rule where Congress had not. That is the model OCSLA follows. So too in United States v. Press Publishing Co., 219 U.S. 1 (1911), where the Court treated state law as applicable only insofar as Congress had not provided its own governing rule. The same principle appears in James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), which explained that state law may be adopted for a federal enclave only as a means of filling what otherwise would be a void.

The Court’s pre-1970 OCSLA decision in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), points in the same direction. Rodrigue held that fixed platforms on the Outer Continental Shelf are to be treated as artificial islands rather than vessels and that the OCSLA was designed to provide “a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” there. Id. at 355. The Court emphasized that where federal maritime law did not apply of its own force, state law could be incorporated because “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” had been removed. Id. at 366. That formulation is gap-filling, not subject-matter overlap. The question was not whether state law shared the same subject matter as federal law, but whether federal law already supplied the rule.

That understanding is reinforced by Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), which—though decided just after the relevant date cutoff for this Court’s analysis here—rested on Rodrigue’s premise that OCSLA borrows state law only when federal law does not independently control. The pre-1970 foundation for that rule is equally clear in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), which recognized that OCSLA incorporates state law as surrogate federal law only where federal law is silent. But the older cases already compelled that result.

Newton’s competing rule would collapse OCSLA’s text into a general consistency test. That cannot be right. In Hinderlider v. La Plata River & Cherry Creek Ditch Co., 304 U.S. 92 (1938), the Court explained that federal law governs when Congress has occupied the governing field, even if state law would not conflict in the ordinary preemption sense. And in Sola Electric Co. v. Jefferson Electric Co., 317 U.S. 173 (1942), the Court rejected any notion that state law may survive merely because it is not directly incompatible with federal policy; where federal law speaks, it controls.

The district court’s “gap” rule therefore follows the better reading of OCSLA and the settled pre-1970 cases on federal enclaves and assimilation statutes. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for application of the proper standard: state law applies only where federal law leaves a gap to be filled.
