ARGUMENT

A district court's omission to advise a defendant of the right to appeal does not furnish ground for collateral relief when the record establishes that the defendant already knew of that right. In Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), this Court held that a Rule 32 violation supplies no basis for vacating a sentence absent a showing that the defendant "was prejudiced by the error." The same principle governs here. Because the district court found that Peguero "knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred," the omission caused no prejudice.

Collateral attack is not a substitute for direct review. Sunal v. Large, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947), teaches that habeas corpus reaches only those errors that "render the proceeding itself irregular and invalid." An unadvised but known right to appeal does not render the sentencing proceeding irregular. See Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 275 (1942) (a defendant who "understandingly and competently" forgoes an appeal cannot later claim the proceeding was fundamentally unfair).

Harmless-error analysis applies to Rule 32 omissions. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), requires reversal only when the error "had substantial influence" on the outcome. Knowledge of the right to appeal eliminates any such influence. Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189, 202 (1943), likewise refused to disturb a judgment where the defendant suffered "no actual prejudice."

The requirement of prejudice is longstanding. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938), the Court conditioned relief on a showing that the petitioner "suffered a denial of fundamental fairness." Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963), reiterated that habeas petitioners must demonstrate "actual prejudice" flowing from the claimed irregularity. Peguero has made no such demonstration.

Nor does the mere existence of a procedural default by the court excuse the need to show prejudice. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 210 (1950), and Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 118 (1944), both emphasize that collateral relief is unavailable when the petitioner "has not been prejudiced." Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953), confirmed that federal courts may deny habeas when the state record demonstrates the absence of harm.

The court of appeals correctly applied these settled principles. Because Peguero knew of his appellate rights and elected not to pursue them, the Rule 32 omission was harmless. The judgment should be affirmed.
