**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATUTE IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION**

The State has a substantial interest in ensuring the safety and access of patients and clinic staff. *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 90 (1940). To protect this interest, the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech to prevent obstruction. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 571 (1941). The Massachusetts buffer zone satisfies this constitutional test.

**A. The Regulation Is Content-Neutral**

The statute regulates conduct—entering a designated area—rather than the content of speech. *United States v. O'Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 376 (1968). The regulation does not distinguish between pro-life and pro-choice messages; it applies equally to all individuals within the zone. Consequently, the law is content-neutral.

**B. The Regulation Serves a Significant Government Interest**

The State has a compelling interest in preventing harassment and ensuring the safe passage of individuals entering and leaving healthcare facilities. *Thornhill*, 310 U.S. at 90. The buffer zone addresses the practical reality that sidewalk counseling often involves crowding and obstruction, which can impede access and create safety hazards.

**C. The Regulation Is Narrowly Tailored**

The 35-foot buffer zone is narrowly tailored to serve the State’s interest in safety without banning speech entirely. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 571. The zone is the minimum spatial restriction necessary to prevent obstruction while still allowing petitioners to communicate from a distance. The First Circuit correctly applied the *Hill v. Colorado* framework, which is consistent with the principles established in *Cox*.

**D. The Regulation Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels of Communication**

Petitioners retain ample avenues for communication. *Martin v. City of Struthers*, 319 U.S. 141, 143 (1943). They may still distribute literature, display signs, and engage in prayer outside the buffer zone. The State has not shown that the zone eliminates all effective means of communication.

**II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE *HILL* FRAMEWORK**

The First Circuit properly applied the *Hill v. Colorado* standard. The principles of safety and access are consistent with the Court’s history of upholding regulations that prevent obstruction. *Shuttlesworth v. City of Birmingham*, 394 U.S. 147, 151 (1969). The Court has long recognized that the right to picket is not absolute and must yield to the State’s interest in public order. *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 234 (1963). The First Circuit’s decision is therefore sound.
