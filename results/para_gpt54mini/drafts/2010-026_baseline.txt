**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision cannot be reconciled with FOIA’s text, structure, or this Court’s precedents. FOIA’s “basic policy” is disclosure, and exemptions must be “narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976); *FBI v. Abramson*, 456 U.S. 615, 630 (1982). Exemption 2 covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit erred by transforming that language into a broad shield for safety and operational data simply because the agency uses the information internally and fears misuse.

1. **The ESQD documents are not “personnel rules and practices” within Exemption 2’s text.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA exemptions cannot be expanded beyond their statutory terms. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). Exemption 2 reaches matters relating to “personnel rules and practices,” not all internal operational information. *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. at 370-71 (holding that the exemption concerns “employment relationships” and internal agency matters). The ESQD arcs and related explosion-effect documents do not concern personnel rules, employee discipline, or routine internal workplace administration. They are technical safety and planning materials about blast effects, storage distances, and surrounding-area impacts. That is operational data, not personnel rules.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard cannot do the statutory work the court assigned to it. The statutory phrase “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” is not satisfied merely because information is generated by an agency and kept in-house. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. If “internal use” alone were enough, the word “personnel” would do no real limiting. Courts do not read FOIA exemptions that way. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

2. **The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on “risk of circumvention” improperly detached that phrase from the statute.**

The “risk of circumvention” formulation came from *Rose*, where the Court suggested that some internal rules may be withheld when disclosure would risk circumvention of agency regulation. 425 U.S. at 369. But *Rose* did not hold that any internal information causing a circumvention risk falls within Exemption 2. To the contrary, the Court made clear that the exemption still turns on the statutory requirement that the material concern “internal personnel rules and practices.” *Id.* at 360-61, 369-71.

That limitation is central. Other exemptions expressly protect materials when disclosure would create a risk of evasion, but Exemption 2 does not become a general “security” exemption simply because the government predicts harmful use. See *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 152-57 (1989) (FOIA exemptions are distinct and must be applied according to their terms). Here, the Navy’s concern is not with personnel administration but with the possibility that release could aid outsiders in understanding blast effects. Whatever the merits of that concern, it is not Exemption 2.

3. **The Ninth Circuit’s rule conflicts with this Court’s instruction that exemptions be narrowly construed and with FOIA’s structure.**

FOIA’s nine exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. The government may not borrow one exemption to do the work of another. If the Navy believed the ESQD documents were properly classifiable for national-defense reasons, Exemption 1 was the avenue Congress supplied. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1); *CIA v. Sims*, 471 U.S. 159, 167-73 (1985). If the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and disclosure could endanger life or safety, Exemption 7(F) could potentially apply. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F); *Dep’t of Justice v. Reporters Comm. for Freedom of the Press*, 489 U.S. 749, 757 (1989). But the Navy did not establish those exemptions here, and the Ninth Circuit’s Exemption 2 ruling effectively converted that provision into a catchall for sensitive technical material.

That approach is inconsistent with FOIA’s design. Congress chose narrow, enumerated exemptions; courts are not free to enlarge them because the government’s concerns seem practical or compelling. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

4. **This Court should reverse and hold that the requested documents are not exempt under Exemption 2.**

The proper inquiry is straightforward: do the requested documents relate solely to internal personnel rules and practices? *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The answer here is no. ESQD data describes the effects of explosions and the safe placement of ordnance. It is not personnel policy, and it does not become exempt merely because the Navy uses it internally and prefers secrecy. Because the Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test departs from the statute and from this Court’s narrow construction of FOIA exemptions, the judgment should be reversed.
