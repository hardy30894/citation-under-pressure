**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (“ESQD”) documents are exempt under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). That exemption covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The court’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard, drawn from Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051, 1074 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), expands the exemption beyond its text and the Supreme Court’s construction.

FOIA mandates disclosure unless material falls within one of nine “explicitly exclusive” exemptions that “are to be interpreted narrowly.” U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting Adm’r FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)); Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). Exemption 2 therefore reaches only rules and practices “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). In Rose, the Court described the exemption as protecting “matters . . . of no genuine public interest” such as “employee parking and lunch hour policies.” 425 U.S. at 363, 369.

The Ninth Circuit’s prior decision in Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), limited the exemption to “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” Id. at 656. Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997), treated the law-enforcement category as merely one sufficient, not necessary, route to exemption. Id. at 1085–86. The panel below nevertheless endorsed Crooker’s “predominantly internal” test, holding that any material “predominantly internal” and whose disclosure “would present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation” is exempt. This formulation finds no support in the statutory language or in Rose.

The ESQD documents do not qualify under either the Hardy or Crooker formulation. They consist of mathematical formulas and arc maps showing the effects of hypothetical explosions at ammunition storage sites. As the Navy’s own declarations establish, the arcs are “designed to be a long term planning tool” used “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” They are not personnel rules, do not govern employee conduct, and were not compiled for law-enforcement purposes. Their disclosure therefore cannot “risk circumvention of agency regulation” within the meaning of Rose or Hardy.

Because the documents fall outside the plain terms of Exemption 2, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
