# ARGUMENT

## I. FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME SATISFIES THE SIXTH AMENDMENT BECAUSE THE JURY, NOT THE JUDGE, FINDS THE AGGRAVATING CIRCUMSTANCE THAT EXPOSES THE DEFENDANT TO DEATH

This Court's decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that "[c]apital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants . . . are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment." *Id.* at 589. That holding rested on the recognition that Arizona's aggravating factors "operate as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense." *Id.* at 609 (quoting *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)). Florida's scheme differs in a constitutionally significant respect: the jury's guilt-phase verdict of first-degree murder already exposes the defendant to a maximum sentence of death under section 775.082, Florida Statutes, because the jury necessarily finds facts—such as premeditation or felony murder—that this Court has recognized function as aggravating circumstances in their own right. *See Hurst v. State*, 819 So. 2d 689 (Fla. 2002).

This Court has never held that *Ring* requires unanimity in the jury's ultimate sentencing recommendation. *Ring* addressed only "the manner in which a State establishes death eligibility," 536 U.S. at 608, not the procedure by which a jury, once that eligibility is established, recommends whether death is the appropriate penalty. The distinction between eligibility and selection has long animated this Court's capital jurisprudence. In *Tuilaepa v. California*, 512 U.S. 967 (1994), the Court explained that the eligibility decision "narrow[s] the class of persons eligible for the death penalty," while the selection decision requires only an "individualized determination" through consideration of relevant mitigating and aggravating circumstances. *Id.* at 971–72. Florida's advisory jury recommendation—reached by majority vote after the eligibility-establishing element has already been found by unanimous jury verdict at the guilt phase—falls within the selection function that this Court has consistently left to legislative judgment. *See Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862, 875 (1983) (once a State narrows the class of death-eligible defendants, the sentencer may consider additional factors "in whatever manner is deemed appropriate").

Moreover, this Court's post-*Ring* decisions confirm that *Ring* did not disturb traditional judicial sentencing discretion once eligibility is fixed. In *Harris v. United States*, 536 U.S. 545 (2002), decided the same day as *Ring*, the Court reaffirmed that judges may find facts that increase a sentence within an already-authorized range without offending the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 565–66. Florida's sentencing judge performs precisely this function: after the jury's guilt verdict authorizes the maximum penalty of death, the judge's weighing of aggravating and mitigating circumstances—informed by the jury's advisory recommendation—operates within, not beyond, the range the jury's verdict already permits. *Cf. Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984) (upholding Florida's judicial override of jury sentencing recommendations because "the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury").

Nor does unanimity in sentencing recommendations find footing in this Court's Sixth Amendment jurisprudence. In *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404 (1972), a plurality of this Court held that the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimous jury verdicts even in noncapital felony trials. *Id.* at 406. If unanimity is not constitutionally compelled for guilt determinations generally, it cannot be a freestanding Sixth Amendment requirement for a capital sentencing recommendation that, under Florida law, serves an advisory rather than final adjudicative function. *See Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 465 ("[T]he Sixth Amendment does not require jury sentencing, even where the sentence is death.").

## II. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE A JURY, RATHER THAN A JUDGE, TO DETERMINE MENTAL RETARDATION AS A BAR TO EXECUTION

This Court's decision in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of the mentally retarded, but expressly "le[ft] to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction." *Id.* at 317. Florida exercised that discretion by assigning the mental retardation determination to the trial judge under a clear-and-convincing evidence standard, a procedural choice squarely within the latitude *Atkins* preserved. *See Bobby v. Bies*, 556 U.S. 825, 831 (2009) (recognizing that *Atkins* "leaves to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce" the constitutional restriction, including procedural mechanisms for adjudicating the claim).

This Court has repeatedly approved judicial fact-finding on threshold constitutional questions that condition death-penalty eligibility without requiring jury resolution. In *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits execution of the incompetent but did not mandate jury adjudication of competency; rather, it required only that the State provide "an adequate means" for the prisoner to raise the claim before an impartial decisionmaker. *Id.* at 411. The mental retardation inquiry is analogous: it is a status determination bearing on eligibility for punishment, not a fact that increases the "statutory maximum" within the meaning of *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000). *Apprendi*'s rule concerns facts that expose a defendant to greater punishment than the jury's verdict alone would allow; the mental retardation inquiry does the opposite—it operates only to exempt an already death-eligible defendant from that punishment. *See Ring*, 536 U.S. at 602 (limiting the *Apprendi* rule to "facts that increase the prescribed range of penalties").

Respondent's proportionality argument likewise fails under this Court's precedent. Where, as here, the trial court found two weighty aggravating circumstances—that the murder was especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel, and was committed during a robbery—against mitigation the court expressly considered and weighed, the sentence comports with the individualized reliability the Eighth Amendment demands. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (approving guided discretion schemes that permit "particularized consideration of relevant aspects of the character and record of each convicted defendant").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
