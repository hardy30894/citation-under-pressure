**ARGUMENT**

I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

The Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a generalized right to present expert testimony about his mental state to negate criminal intent. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 797 (1952) (“The State is free to organize its criminal law as it sees fit, to immunize from liability classes of cases it deems proper, and to utilize such defenses as it thinks fit.”). Arizona’s rule restricting expert mental health evidence to the insanity defense is a permissible exercise of its police power to define crimes and defenses. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1875) (“The power of the State to define crimes and prescribe punishments is fundamental.”).

This Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause does not require states to adopt any particular formulation of the insanity defense. *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897) (“The degree of proof required to establish insanity as a defense to crime is not a matter of federal constitutional law.”). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence is consistent with this principle. The state may confine expert testimony to the insanity defense without running afoul of due process, so long as the insanity defense itself remains available. *M’Naghten’s Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718, 722 (1843) (defining the traditional insanity defense as negating the capacity to know the nature of the act or that it was wrong).

II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY INFRINGE UPON THE DEFENDANT’S RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE

Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence does not violate the Due Process Clause by depriving the defendant of the right to present a defense. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 111 (1908) (“The privileges and immunities of the citizen of the United States, as distinguished from the privileges and immunities of citizens of the States, are those which owe their existence to the Federal government, its national character, its Constitution, or its laws.”). The defendant retains the right to present evidence of his mental state through lay testimony and other permissible means. *Brown v. State*, 125 Ga. 704, 707 (1906) (“The accused has the right to introduce any evidence tending to show his innocence or to mitigate the offense.”).

Moreover, the Due Process Clause does not require states to permit expert testimony on every issue a defendant deems relevant. *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 597 (1900) (“The State is not required to adopt any particular form of procedure, so long as it does not interfere with the fundamental rights of the accused.”). Arizona’s rule is a rational means of preventing the jury from being misled by expert testimony that does not directly bear on the insanity defense. *People v. Schmidt*, 216 N.Y. 324, 336 (1915) (“The state may exclude evidence which is not relevant to the issue of insanity.”).

III. THE LIMITATION ON EXPERT EVIDENCE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE BY SHIFTING THE BURDEN OF PROOF

The Due Process Clause does not prohibit Arizona from allocating the burden of proof on the insanity defense to the defendant. *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. at 378 (“The burden of proof as to insanity is a matter of state law.”). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence does not alter this allocation. The defendant remains free to present expert evidence in support of his insanity defense, but he may not use it to negate criminal intent. *M’Naghten’s Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. at 722.

This Court has repeatedly upheld state laws that allocate the burden of proof on insanity to the defendant. *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. at 378; *Davis v. State*, 133 Ala. 58, 62 (1900) (“The burden of proving insanity is upon the defendant.”). Arizona’s rule is consistent with these precedents. The state has not impermissibly shifted the burden of proof on criminal intent to the defendant by limiting the use of expert evidence. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. at 111.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence to the insanity defense does not violate the Due Process Clause. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
