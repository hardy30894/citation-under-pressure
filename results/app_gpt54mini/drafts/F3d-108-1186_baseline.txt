### ARGUMENT

#### I. The district court erred in entering a zero-damages judgment from an ambiguous verdict that the jury never clarified.

A trial court may not speculate about a jury’s intent where the verdict forms are internally ambiguous and the ambiguity is brought to the court’s attention before discharge of the jury. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 49(a) and 49(b) authorize the court to seek clarification when the verdict is uncertain, and the Tenth Circuit has recognized that a court must avoid “guess[ing] at the jury’s intent” when the record permits more than one reasonable reading of the verdict. *Guidry v. Sheet Metal Workers Nat’l Pension Fund*, 10 F.3d 700, 716 (10th Cir. 1993); *United States v. Schiff*, 801 F.2d 108, 113 (2d Cir. 1986).

Here, the verdict was plainly susceptible to two interpretations. The jury awarded Unit $70,768.02 on Form 1 and awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30 on Form 3. Unit argued the jury had already netted Enron’s repair costs against the unpaid invoices and then separately found Enron entitled to an additional set-off, while the district court assumed the jury intended the damages and set-off awards to be mechanically offset against each other, producing a zero recovery. Because both readings were plausible on the face of the verdict, the verdict was ambiguous as a matter of law. See *United States v. Morris*, 612 F.2d 483, 489 (10th Cir. 1979) (where a verdict is uncertain, the court should seek clarification before the jury is discharged); *McKay v. Ashland Oil, Inc.*, 120 F.3d 74, 79 (7th Cir. 1997) (trial court should secure clarification of an ambiguous verdict before discharge).

Unit did exactly what the law requires: before the jury was discharged, it asked the court to question the jurors and clarify the damage awards. The court refused. That refusal mattered. Once the jury left, the court no longer had a reliable means of determining whether the Form 1 award represented the jury’s gross damages calculation or its net recovery after accounting for Enron’s repair costs. Courts are not permitted to convert uncertainty into judgment by judicial arithmetic. See *Tanner v. United States*, 483 U.S. 107, 117 (1987) (the finality of jury deliberations is protected, and post-discharge speculation is disfavored); *United States v. Dotson*, 817 F.2d 1127, 1130 (5th Cir. 1987) (a court may not reconcile an ambiguous verdict by conjecture).

The district court’s interpretation also conflicted with the instructions it gave the jury. The jurors were told that if they found Enron breached, they could award expectation damages up to the amount of the unpaid invoices, or, if Unit had only substantially performed, they could award the invoice amount less the cost to Enron of correcting defects. But the jury was also given a separate set-off form. Those instructions invited duplication unless the jury was told expressly that any repair costs included in Unit’s damages award could not be duplicated as an Enron set-off. When a verdict form structure creates a risk of double counting, the proper course is clarification, not post hoc judicial reconstruction. See *Brooke Group Ltd. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.*, 509 U.S. 209, 242 (1993) (courts must evaluate verdicts by the jury’s actual findings, not by after-the-fact conjecture); *Morris*, 612 F.2d at 489.

The consequence of the district court’s error was to deprive Unit of the jury’s apparent award. A verdict that awards damages to the plaintiff cannot be transformed into a defense judgment merely because the court later concludes the jury may have meant something else. Because the verdict was ambiguous and the ambiguity was timely presented, the court should have sought clarification from the jury. Its failure to do so was reversible error. See *Guidry*, 10 F.3d at 716; *Morris*, 612 F.2d at 489.

#### II. Any asserted instructional defect was waived, but the ambiguity in the verdict forms remains independently reversible.

Unit recognizes that it did not lodge a specific objection to every defect in the instructions under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 51. See *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977). But waiver of an instructional objection does not authorize entry of judgment on an ambiguous verdict. Even if the Court declines to reach the instruction issue, the separate question is whether the district court erred in refusing to clarify the jury’s actual findings before discharge. On that issue, Unit preserved its objection and the court’s refusal was error. See *Morris*, 612 F.2d at 489.

#### III. The case should be remanded for entry of judgment consistent with a clarified verdict, or for a new trial on damages.

Where a verdict is ambiguous and the ambiguity cannot be resolved because the court declined to ask the jury before discharge, the appropriate remedy is a new trial on the affected issue. See *McKay*, 120 F.3d at 79; *Morris*, 612 F.2d at 489. At minimum, the judgment below should be vacated and the case remanded so the district court may enter judgment only after resolving the inconsistency in a manner faithful to the jury’s findings, not the court’s speculation.
