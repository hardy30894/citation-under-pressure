Respondent Montana should prevail because title navigability is fixed as of the moment of statehood, not by later conditions. The Equal Footing Doctrine gives a State title to the beds of waters that were “navigable” when it entered the Union. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 801 (1931); United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935); United States v. Utah, 304 U.S. 64 (1938). This Court has repeatedly treated the title inquiry as historical and statehood-specific. In United States v. Utah, the Court instructed that navigability for title depends on whether the waters were navigable “at the time of statehood,” and the Court resolved title by examining conditions then existing. 283 U.S. 801 (1931). Likewise, in United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), the Court assessed whether the river segments were navigable when Oregon entered the Union, because that is when title passed if at all. And in United States v. Utah, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), the Court again emphasized that title turns on the condition of the waterway when the State was admitted.

The question presented is therefore answered by settled doctrine. “The rights of the state and of riparian owners in navigable waters within the state and to the soil beneath are matters of state law to be determined by the statutes and judicial decisions of the state,” but only after title has been fixed by federal law at statehood. Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Commission, 274 U.S. 651 (1927). The federal rule is that a State acquires title to the beds of waters then navigable, and later changes cannot enlarge or diminish that title. Barney v. Keokuk, 94 U.S. 324 (1877); Hardin v. Jordan, 140 U.S. 371 (1891); Packer v. Bird, 137 U.S. 661 (1891).

PPL’s contrary position cannot be squared with the Court’s navigability cases. This Court has distinguished navigability for title from other navigability doctrines and has required a statehood-based inquiry for title purposes. United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377 (1941), while expanding the concept of navigability for Congress’s regulatory power, did not alter the title rule. To the contrary, the Court recognized that “[t]he question of navigability, when raised in another context, may be a different one.” Id. at 406. The Court later made the same distinction in Federal Power Commission v. Union Electric Co., 381 U.S. 90 (1965), explaining that a stream may be relevant to federal licensing even when title navigability is separately determined. The same is true here: regulatory navigability and title navigability are not interchangeable.

The Equal Footing Doctrine also explains why statehood is the critical date. In United States v. Texas, 339 U.S. 707 (1950), the Court held that a State takes title to submerged lands when it enters the Union if the waters are then navigable. And in United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 801 (1931), the Court rejected after-the-fact arguments that later developments could alter the title outcome. Once title vests at statehood, it remains with the State unless expressly conveyed away by the United States. Cf. United States v. Grand River Dam Authority, 363 U.S. 229 (1960) (recognizing federal and state rights must be analyzed according to the governing title background). That principle would be eviscerated if courts could determine title based on later navigability, later damming, or later commercial use.

PPL’s position would also conflict with the settled protection of state title against federal claims. In United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222 (1956), the Court held that the Government could not be required to pay for the hydroelectric value attributable to the navigable character of the river itself. In United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121 (1967), the Court reaffirmed that the special value derived from navigable waters is part of the federal navigational servitude and not a private entitlement. Those cases underscore that navigability is a legal status fixed by history and sovereignty, not by later private improvements. But they do not suggest that title depends on present-day conditions; rather, they confirm the opposite.

Finally, the state-court record here properly focused on the historical condition of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers in 1889. That approach accords with the federal cases governing title navigability and should be affirmed. Green Bay & Mississippi Canal Co. v. Patten Paper Co., 172 U.S. 58 (1898); Fox River Paper Co., 274 U.S. 651 (1927); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 801 (1931); United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935); United States v. Utah, 304 U.S. 64 (1938); United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377 (1941); United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222 (1956); United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121 (1967).

The judgment should be affirmed.
