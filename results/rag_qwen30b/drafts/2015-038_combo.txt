**ARGUMENT**

The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit the National Park Service (NPS) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private land and waters physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System in Alaska. The plain text of ANILCA, interpreted in light of settled principles of federal authority over navigable waters and national parks, confirms that the NPS retains jurisdiction to enforce its regulations—including the ban on hovercrafts—within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, even where state-owned submerged lands lie beneath the river. This conclusion is compelled by the Supreme Court’s consistent recognition that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters and national parks is not diminished by concurrent state ownership of submerged lands.

First, the federal government’s authority to regulate navigable waters within national parks is rooted in the Constitution’s Commerce Clause and the federal navigational servitude. As the Court held in *United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co.*, 228 U.S. 87, 95 (1913), “the United States has the right to regulate commerce upon the navigable waters of the United States, and this right is not affected by the ownership of the lands beneath them.” The Court emphasized that “our laws cannot be extended so as to control or affect the foreign carriage,” but that “we certainly may control such citizens and corporations operating in our territory.” Id. at 96. The Nation River, a navigable waterway, falls within the federal domain under this principle. The fact that Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the river does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on the water surface, including the operation of hovercrafts, which are subject to federal safety and environmental concerns.

Second, the NPS’s regulatory authority over national parks is not limited by the ownership of underlying lands. In *Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co.*, 304 U.S. 518, 525 (1938), the Court upheld the federal government’s exclusive jurisdiction over Yosemite National Park, even though portions of the park contained privately owned land. The Court stated: “Exclusive jurisdiction shall be and the same is hereby ceded to the United States over and within all of the territory which is now or may hereafter be included in those several tracts of land in the State of California set aside and dedicated for park purposes.” Id. at 520. The Court further recognized that the federal government’s authority “shall not vest until the United States through the proper officer notifies the State of California that they assume police jurisdiction.” Id. at 521. This language confirms that federal jurisdiction over national parks is not contingent on the ownership of submerged or surface lands, but on the federal designation of the area as a park. The Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, like Yosemite, is a federally designated unit of the National Park System, and thus the NPS possesses full regulatory authority within its boundaries.

Third, the federal government’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within national parks is not abrogated by state ownership of submerged lands. In *Curtin v. Benson*, 222 U.S. 78, 85 (1911), the Court affirmed the Superintendent of Yosemite National Park’s authority to enforce regulations governing the movement of livestock across park lands, even where the land was patented and privately owned. The Court held that “the herding or grazing of loose stock or cattle of any kind on the Government lands in the park, as well as the driving of such stock or cattle over the same, is strictly forbidden, except in such cases where authority therefor is granted by the superintendent.” Id. at 86. This precedent establishes that federal regulatory authority over park lands—regardless of private or state ownership of underlying property—remains intact. The operation of a hovercraft on a navigable river within the preserve is no different in principle from the movement of livestock: both are activities that may affect the integrity of the park and are subject to federal regulation.

Fourth, the federal government’s authority to regulate fishing and other uses of navigable waters in Alaska is well established. In *Hynes, Regional Director, Fish & Wildlife Service v. Grimes Packing Co.*, 337 U.S. 86, 90 (1949), the Court upheld the Secretary of Commerce’s authority to regulate fishing in waters of Alaska, stating that “the Secretary may (a) fix the size and character of nets, boats, traps, or other gear and appliances to be used therein; (b) limit the catch of fish to be taken from any area; (c) make such regulations as to time, means, methods, and extent of fishing as he may deem advisable.” Id. The Court emphasized that these regulations “shall be of general application within the particular area to which it applies.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to the operation of hovercrafts: if the federal government may regulate fishing in navigable waters, it may also regulate other activities that may impact the environment, safety, or resource integrity of national parks.

Fifth, the federal government’s authority over navigable waters within national parks is not diminished by state ownership of submerged lands. In *Territory of Alaska v. Troy*, 258 U.S. 101, 105 (1922), the Court held that “the United States as a part thereof, and therefore Congress, in legislating concerning them, was subject only to the provisions of the Constitution applicable to territory occupying that relation.” Id. The Court recognized that Congress may regulate commerce and navigation in territorial waters, even where state or private interests exist. The Nation River, as a navigable waterway, falls within this federal domain. The fact that Alaska owns the submerged lands does not alter the federal government’s authority to regulate surface activities.

Sixth, the federal government’s authority to regulate activities in national parks is not limited by state sovereignty. In *Colorado v. Toll*, 268 U.S. 228, 235 (1925), the Court upheld the National Park Service’s authority to regulate automobiles in a national park, even though the park contained private property and state highways. The Court stated: “The park is open to automobiles operated for pleasure, but not to those carrying passengers who are paying, either directly or indirectly, for the use of machines.” Id. The Court further noted that the Director of the National Parks Service “is the sole judge” of such regulations. Id.
