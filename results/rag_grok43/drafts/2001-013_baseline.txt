The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1) divests OSHA of jurisdiction over working conditions aboard uninspected vessels. Section 4(b)(1) states that the OSH Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Preemption therefore requires an actual exercise of authority, not merely the existence of statutory power. See Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Assn., 505 U.S. 88, 96-97 (1992) (describing the Act’s preemption provisions as turning on whether federal standards have been promulgated on the issue).

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to discrete equipment requirements. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; see also 46 C.F.R. pts. 25, 26. No comprehensive occupational-safety standards governing the conditions that produced the explosion on Rig 52 have been prescribed or enforced. The OSH Act’s purpose is “to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b); see also Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall, 445 U.S. 1, 12 (1980); Industrial Union Dept., AFL-CIO v. American Petroleum Institute, 448 U.S. 607, 646 (1980) (plurality). Absent Coast Guard exercise of authority on these specific working conditions, that purpose remains operative.

Atlas Roofing Co. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Comm’n, 430 U.S. 442, 445-46 (1977), confirms that the Act supplies an independent federal remedy for unsafe working conditions even where other regulatory schemes exist. The Fifth Circuit’s extension of circuit precedent to uninspected vessels therefore conflicts with the statutory text and with the principle that preemption under the Act is issue-specific and requires affirmative federal action. OSHA retains jurisdiction to issue the citations.
