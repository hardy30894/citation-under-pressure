ARGUMENT

Punitive damages have never been available under the common law merely upon a showing of intentional wrongdoing or reckless indifference. Instead, the defendant’s conduct must independently be “outrageous” or “egregious.” This Court has long held that such awards “are given only in extreme cases, where the defendant’s conduct has been outrageous.” Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851). The same requirement appears in Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858) (“exemplary damages are recoverable only where the injury has been wanton and aggravated”).

The rule is not altered by the presence of malice or reckless disregard. Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), explained that even when “the act is done with a reckless disregard of the rights of others,” punitive damages still require “circumstances of aggravation.” Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), likewise insisted that the conduct must be “of a gross and wanton character” beyond the mental state that establishes liability.

This Court has repeatedly confirmed that the aggravating character of the act itself supplies the independent predicate. Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 563 (1886), stated that exemplary damages rest on “the degree of malice, oppression, or wantonness” manifested by the defendant’s behavior. Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58, 86 (1897), reiterated that the award turns on whether the wrong “was committed under circumstances of aggravation.” New York Central R.R. v. Johnson, 279 U.S. 310, 315 (1929), held that punitive damages are proper only when the defendant’s conduct “was so wanton or reckless as to imply a disregard of the rights of others.”

The same principle governed maritime and common-law trespass cases. The Amiable Nancy, 3 Wheat. 546, 558 (1818), permitted exemplary damages only upon a showing of “gross and wanton outrage.” These authorities establish that state of mind alone has never sufficed; the conduct must be independently outrageous.

Title VII’s incorporation of the phrase “malice or with reckless indifference,” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), therefore does not displace the settled common-law requirement. Because the record contains no evidence that the Association’s conduct was outrageous apart from any claimed mental state, the district court correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction.
