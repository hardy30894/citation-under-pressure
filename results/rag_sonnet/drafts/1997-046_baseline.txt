# ARGUMENT

## I. RESPONDENT HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

This Court's precedents foreclose any claim that clemency proceedings themselves trigger due process protections. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court rejected the precise argument Respondent now advances. The plaintiff in *Dumschat*, like Respondent here, sought judicial oversight of the clemency process itself, arguing that repeated favorable treatment of similarly situated inmates created a protected expectancy. This Court squarely held that no such liberty interest exists, explaining that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.* at 464.

The lower courts' evidence in *Dumschat* showed that "at least 75 percent of all lifers received some favorable action from the pardon board." *Id.* Even this striking statistical pattern did not create a constitutionally protected interest, because clemency—unlike parole—is an act of grace lying wholly within executive discretion, historically unconstrained by legal standards that could generate a legitimate expectation of a particular outcome.

That principle applies with full force here. The Sixth Circuit's own dissenting opinion below correctly recognized that "clemency review is not an integral part" of the state's system of finally adjudicating guilt, and that the Ohio Governor "enjoys almost unfettered discretion in determining whether to grant or deny clemency." Ohio's Constitution vests the Governor with sweeping authority "to grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons... upon such conditions as the Governor may think proper," and the Ohio Supreme Court has confirmed that the legislature cannot even require the Governor "to accept the recommendation of the [Ohio Adult Parole Authority] in the exercise of his clemency power." *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520 (1994). This unconstrained discretion is the antithesis of the kind of mandatory, standard-bound decisionmaking that generates a protected liberty interest under *Greenholtz v. Inmates of the Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), and *Board of Pardons v. Allen*, 482 U.S. 369 (1987).

Those cases are instructive by contrast. In *Greenholtz*, the parole statute's mandatory language—directing the Board to release inmates unless certain specified conditions were found—created a legitimate expectation of release that the Due Process Clause protected. Similarly, in *Allen*, this Court reaffirmed that a liberty interest in parole arises only where a state has "adopt[ed] a parole system that applies general standards of eligibility" such that "prisoners justifiably expect that parole will be granted fairly and according to law whenever those standards are met." 482 U.S. at 381. Ohio's clemency scheme contains no such standards. It commits the ultimate decision to the Governor's unreviewable grace.

This Court's decision in *Jago v. Van Curen*, 454 U.S. 14 (1981), confirms the point. There, this Court held that Ohio parole determinations create no protected liberty interest because Ohio law made clear that "no protectable interest in early release arises until actual release" and that "[p]arole for Ohio prisoners lies wholly within the discretion of the [Adult Parole Authority]." *Id.* at 14. Clemency, resting even more deeply in unreviewable executive grace than parole, cannot logically receive greater constitutional protection than the parole determinations this Court held unprotected in *Jago*.

The Sixth Circuit erred in relying on *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), and *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), to hold that Ohio's clemency procedure, once created, becomes "an integral part" of the adjudicatory system requiring due process scrutiny. *Evitts* addressed the right to counsel on a first appeal as of right—a proceeding fundamentally different from clemency, which by its nature exists as a mercy-based check *outside* the judicial process, available only after the judicial process has run its full course including any appeals. Indeed, as Judge Nelson observed in dissent below, if the logic the majority employed were correct, it would extend even further to habeas corpus proceedings, which "form an integral part of the adjudicative system much more clearly than executive clemency proceedings do"—yet this Court held in *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974), cited in *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394, that the right to counsel does not extend to habeas proceedings at all.

## II. OHIO'S VOLUNTARY CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

Respondent's Fifth Amendment claim independently fails because Ohio's procedure imposes no compulsion whatsoever. The regulation gives the inmate the *option* of a personal interview; it does not require him to answer any question, nor does it penalize silence. Because Respondent has not yet requested an interview, has not yet been asked any question, and has not yet suffered any use of a compelled statement, his claim rests entirely on speculation about events that "may never materialize at all."

This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, this Court held it "premature" for a federal court to grant relief against a hypothetical future use of testimony where no compulsion had yet occurred and no criminal prosecution was pending. 425 U.S. at 324. The same reasoning applies here: because Respondent himself represented that he "does not contemplate that this action will have any effect on his state convictions and/or sentences," any risk of self-incrimination is entirely prospective and abstract. Federal courts should not adjudicate such contingent, hypothetical constitutional claims. *Cf. United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. 181 (1977) (declining to impose elaborate prophylactic warnings requirements absent actual compulsion).

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
