**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption of Scienter Requires Construing §875(c) to Demand Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

Section 875(c) does not specify the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that the requirement of a culpable mental state "is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Where Congress is silent as to intent, this Court reads that silence as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement, not to dispense with it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here. Section 875(c) is not among the narrow "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter because the conduct regulated is inherently dangerous or subject to close regulatory oversight. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for words communicated in ordinary speech, carrying felony penalties and lasting stigma, is instead a "true crime" of the sort for which this Court has insisted that intent remain an essential element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260. And where a criminal statute is ambiguous, this Court has been unwilling to extend liability to conduct the defendant did not know was wrongful, construing an analogous federal statute's "willfully" requirement to demand that the defendant act with a specific intent to violate rights, rather than through mere carelessness or misjudgment. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101–107 (1945) (plurality opinion). The same interpretive caution requires reading a subjective-intent element into §875(c).

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not a Listener's Reaction.**

Apart from ordinary statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of the speaker's intent before expression may be punished as a "true threat." Only speech that is "no essential part of any exposition of ideas" falls outside the First Amendment altogether, and such categories must be defined with precision so as not to sweep in protected expression. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). This Court's treatment of an analogous threat prosecution in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), confirms that the inquiry cannot rest solely on how an alarmed listener might construe ambiguous words. Although the petitioner's statement that "the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J." could, read literally, have alarmed listeners, this Court held that "we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by petitioner fits within that statutory term." *Id.* at 708. The Court's focus was on the speaker's evident meaning, not merely on the fear his words produced in others.

An objective, listener-focused standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression—hyperbole, dark humor, venting, artistic lyrics—because a speaker cannot know in advance how an unseen and unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has held that First Amendment freedoms "need breathing space to survive," *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963), and that statutes lacking a scienter requirement impermissibly burden protected speech because they induce speakers to restrict themselves far more than the Constitution demands, out of fear of liability for meanings they never intended. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959). This danger is especially acute where the risk of misjudgment falls on the speaker after the fact: uncertainty about legal consequences causes individuals to "steer far wider of the unlawful zone" than the Constitution requires. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958). And where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise required proof that speech was intended to bring about the proscribed harm before criminal sanctions may be imposed. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective Standard Punishes Carelessness, Not Wrongdoing, and Offends Due Process.**

Dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense—punishing defendants not for what they meant to convey, but for failing to foresee how strangers online might interpret their words. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice of what is forbidden is essential to due process. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952). A defendant should not be convicted of a serious federal felony merely because a jury, applying hindsight, concludes that a reasonable person might have perceived his words as threatening; rather, courts must construe criminal statutes so that their reach is no broader than the text plainly requires, resolving doubtful questions of coverage against extending liability by inference. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952).

Because the jury here was instructed that it need only find that a reasonable person would view Elonis's statements as threatening—without any finding that Elonis himself intended them as threats—the conviction cannot stand. This Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten, and should reverse the judgment below.
