**Argument**

I. The district court abused its discretion by refusing to appoint a fingerprint expert and denying a continuance.

An indigent defendant is entitled to expert assistance when necessary for an adequate defense. In *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 83 (1985), the Court held that due process requires “the basic tools of an adequate defense or appeal” when the defendant demonstrates a sufficient need. The same principle applies to nonpsychiatric experts under 18 U.S.C. § 3006A(e): the defendant must show that the expert services are “necessary for adequate representation.” *United States v. Cravens*, 275 F.3d 637, 639-40 (7th Cir. 2001); *United States v. Alden*, 767 F.2d 314, 318-19 (7th Cir. 1984). Denial is reviewed for abuse of discretion, but discretion is not unlimited where the requested expert bears directly on the defense theory. *Cravens*, 275 F.3d at 639-40.

Winbush made the requisite showing. The government’s case tied him to the recovered drugs and firearm through circumstantial evidence: the police chase, the discarded jacket, the gun, and the narcotics found along the route. Yet the government also emphasized the absence of fingerprints on the recovered items, and its own notice identified expert fingerprint testimony. A defense expert was needed to test the government’s inference and to explain the forensic significance of an absence of usable prints. This is especially so because the government sought to use expert testimony about the difficulty of obtaining prints from different surfaces and materials. A defendant need not prove in advance that the expert will be decisive; it is enough that the services may materially assist the defense. *Ake*, 470 U.S. at 83; *Cravens*, 275 F.3d at 639-40.

The magistrate judge denied the request as “woefully late,” but timing alone cannot justify denial where the defendant’s need is concrete and trial fairness is at stake. Courts recognize that last-minute requests may be denied when they are purely dilatory, but they also require a meaningful assessment of necessity. *United States v. Greschner*, 802 F.2d 373, 376-77 (10th Cir. 1986); *United States v. Rodriguez-Lara*, 421 F.3d 932, 940-41 (9th Cir. 2005). Here, the request came after the government disclosed its fingerprint evidence and shortly before trial; Winbush sought expert assistance responsive to the government’s proof, not delay for its own sake. The denial prevented him from presenting a competent rebuttal to the government’s forensic theme and thus undermined the fairness of the adversary process. *Ake*, 470 U.S. at 77.

The related denial of a continuance compounded the error. A continuance should be granted when necessary to secure the defendant’s ability to present a defense; refusing one is reversible when it is arbitrary and substantially impairs the defense. *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964); *United States v. Sellers*, 645 F.3d 830, 837-38 (7th Cir. 2011). In evaluating a continuance denial, courts consider the diligence of the movant, the likely utility of the evidence, the inconvenience to the court, and the prejudice from denial. *United States v. Walters*, 913 F.2d 388, 393 (7th Cir. 1990). Those factors favor Winbush. He sought a fingerprint expert immediately after the government’s disclosures, the testimony would have been useful to his defense, and the prejudice was obvious: he was forced to proceed without forensic assistance against the government’s expert-driven narrative. Denial of additional time therefore was an abuse of discretion.

II. The evidence was insufficient to support the § 924(c) conviction.

Section 924(c) requires proof that the defendant possessed the firearm “in furtherance of” a drug trafficking crime. Mere possession, proximity, or presence during a drug offense is not enough. *United States v. Castillo*, 406 F.3d 806, 815-16 (7th Cir. 2005); *United States v. Brown*, 724 F.3d 801, 804-05 (7th Cir. 2013). The government must show a nexus between the gun and the drug trafficking, such as accessibility, strategic placement, or circumstances indicating the firearm advanced the offense. *Castillo*, 406 F.3d at 816.

That proof was lacking here. The gun was not recovered from Winbush’s person, nor was it shown that he used or displayed it to facilitate the controlled buy. The government proved, at most, that officers saw a gun in his hand during the chase after the transaction. But the statute requires evidence that the firearm furthered the drug crime itself. *Castillo*, 406 F.3d at 815-16. The later discovery of the gun in the discarded jacket, while corroborating possession, does not establish the required nexus to the earlier sale. On this record, the jury could infer possession, but not that the possession furthered the trafficking offense. The § 924(c) conviction should therefore be reversed.

III. At minimum, the drug counts and sentence must be vacated because the district court’s evidentiary rulings and the government’s theory infected the verdict and sentencing findings.

When evidentiary error affects the jury’s assessment of drug quantity, identity, or firearm use, remand for a new trial or resentencing is appropriate. *United States v. Nixon*, 930 F.3d 1096, 1101-02 (7th Cir. 2019). Here, the contested forensic evidence and the inability to obtain a defense expert likely affected the jury’s view of the recovered crack and the firearm. Because the sentence was driven by drug quantity and the § 924(c) count, the proper remedy is vacatur and remand for a new trial or, at minimum, resentencing. *United States v. White*, 406 F.3d 827, 832-33 (7th Cir. 2005).
