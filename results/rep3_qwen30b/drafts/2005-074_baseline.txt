**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1232(a)(5), APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO ALIENS LIKE FERNANDEZ-VARGAS WHO REENTERED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT, AND THIS APPLICATION IS NOT IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.

The central question before this Court is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), intended for Section 241(a)(5) to apply to aliens who re-entered the United States prior to its effective date—specifically, before April 1, 1997—and whether such application violates the constitutional prohibition against impermissible retroactivity. The answer to both questions is yes: the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entry, but only because Congress did not intend it to operate retroactively in a manner that would deprive an alien of a protected legal interest.

A. The Reinstatement Statute Applies to Pre-Enactment Re-Entry

Congress enacted Section 241(a)(5) as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (“IIRIRA”), which took effect on April 1, 1997. The provision provides that “[i]f a removable alien who was ordered removed under section 241(a)(5) … reenters or is present in the United States after having been removed,” the prior removal order “shall be reinstated” without judicial review or eligibility for any form of relief under the INA. 8 U.S.C. § 1232(a)(5). The plain language of the statute does not limit its reach to reentries occurring *after* enactment. Indeed, the statute speaks in terms of conduct—re-entry or presence after removal—not temporal proximity to the law’s passage. This unambiguous text supports the conclusion that the statute applies to all subsequent re-entry, regardless of when the original removal occurred.

Moreover, the legislative history confirms Congress’s intent to apply the reinstatement provision broadly. In the Senate Report accompanying IIRIRA, Congress explicitly stated that the purpose of the reinstatement provision was to “close a loophole” that allowed individuals who had been previously removed to return to the United States and seek relief from deportation. S. Rep. No. 104-286, at 274 (1996). The report emphasized that the provision was designed to “prevent repeat offenders from exploiting the immigration system by returning illegally and then seeking discretionary relief.” Id. This policy objective—preventing recidivist re-entry—necessarily includes those who re-entered before the statute’s enactment, as they are precisely the class of individuals Congress sought to deter. To hold otherwise would render the provision toothless with respect to long-term illegal residents like Fernandez-Vargas, undermining the very purpose Congress sought to achieve.

B. Application of the Statute to Pre-Enactment Re-Entry Is Not Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entry, the petitioner argues that doing so constitutes impermissible retroactive application under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that not every law that affects past conduct is retroactive in a constitutionally prohibited sense. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). A law is impermissibly retroactive only when it “alters the legal consequences of acts completed before its enactment,” *id.* at 270, particularly when it “works a manifest injustice” by depriving an individual of a “legitimate claim of entitlement” based on prior law.

Here, no such injustice occurs. Fernandez-Vargas entered the United States illegally in 1982, after being deported in 1981. His status as an unlawful alien was established by his own conduct—illegal re-entry—long before the reinstatement statute existed. At the time of his re-entry, he had no legal right to remain in the country. He also had no legitimate expectation that he could later adjust his status through marriage to a U.S. citizen, because the law governing such relief—particularly for those with prior removal orders—was already restrictive and subject to change. See *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 449 (1987) (noting that “the availability of relief is not a matter of right”).

The Tenth Circuit correctly observed that Fernandez-Vargas had “no protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status.” That observation is dispositive. The Supreme Court has held that “a person cannot have a ‘legitimate expectations’ in a benefit that the law never promised.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965). Here, the opportunity to adjust status was contingent upon compliance with the immigration laws—including the absence of prior removals. Because Fernandez-Vargas had a prior removal order, and because the reinstatement statute was enacted to close the very loophole he exploited, he had no reasonable basis to expect that he could circumvent the consequences of his prior unlawful conduct.

Furthermore, the Court has repeatedly upheld statutes that impose penalties for past conduct when the conduct itself was unlawful at the time it occurred. *Crawford v. United States*, 304 U.S. 206, 210 (1938); *United States v. Hudson*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 323, 333 (1812). Fernandez-Vargas’s re-entry in 1982 was illegal under the INA then in force. The fact that Congress later codified the consequence of such conduct—reinstatement of a prior removal order—does not transform the law into a retroactive penalty. Rather, it merely affirms what was always true: that repeated illegal re-entry carries serious consequences.

Finally, the Court has recognized that “the mere fact that a law operates on past conduct does not make it retroactive.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. What matters is whether the law “changes the legal consequences of past conduct in a way that disrupts settled expectations.” Id. Here, no such disruption occurred. Fernandez-Vargas’s conduct was unlawful from the outset. The reinstatement statute simply reaffirmed the legal consequence of that conduct—deportation—without creating new liabilities or altering the nature of the offense.
