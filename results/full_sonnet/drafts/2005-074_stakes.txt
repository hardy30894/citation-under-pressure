# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before the Statute's Effective Date

This Court's retroactivity jurisprudence begins with a settled principle: "the first rule of construction is that legislation must be considered as addressed to the future, not to the past." *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994) (internal quotation marks omitted). Accordingly, courts confronting a statute enacted after the conduct at issue must first ask "whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute's proper reach." *Id.* at 280. Only "a clear indication" that Congress intended a provision to reach preenactment conduct will suffice; a fair implication is not enough. *Id.*; *see also Lindh v. Murphy*, 521 U.S. 320, 328 n.4 (1997) (requiring an "unambiguous directive" before applying a statute retroactively).

Section 241(a)(5) contains no such clear statement. It speaks of an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally" without ever specifying whether Congress meant to reach reentries, like Petitioner's, that were completed fifteen years before the statute's 1997 effective date and that occurred, lived out, and matured entirely under a legal regime that permitted such aliens to seek adjustment of status. Where Congress has not "clearly manifested" its intent to reach the past, this Court does not supply that intent for it. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. The Tenth Circuit's conclusion that Congress "intended" § 241(a)(5) to reach preenactment reentries rests on inference from silence—precisely the kind of inference *Landgraf* forbids.

## II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Petitioner's 1982 Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statutory text could be read to encompass Petitioner's 1982 reentry, that application cannot stand because it would have an impermissible retroactive effect under *Landgraf*'s second inquiry. A statute has retroactive effect when it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. The proper focus is not the statute's date of application, but "whether it impairs rights a party possessed when he acted, increases a party's liability for past conduct, or imposes new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Id.* at 280. Where such retroactive effect exists, "the traditional presumption teaches that it does not govern absent clear congressional intent favoring such a result." *Id.*

This Court applied that very framework to the immigration laws in *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), and the result should be the same here. In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that IIRIRA's repeal of discretionary relief under former § 212(c) could not be applied to an alien whose guilty plea predated the repeal, because "the elimination of any possibility of § 212(c) relief" for people who had relied on the prior regime "attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Id.* at 321, 325 (internal quotation marks omitted). The Court emphasized that "preserving the possibility of such relief would have been one of the principal benefits sought by" aliens who structured their conduct around the law as it then stood. *Id.* at 323. It did not matter that the alien's removal proceedings, like Petitioner's, occurred after the new statute took effect; what mattered was that the conduct on which he relied—his decision to plead guilty—occurred while the old law was in force. *Id.* at 321.

The same logic controls here. Petitioner reentered the United States in 1982, fifteen years before § 241(a)(5)'s enactment, and thereafter built a life in this country: he married a United States citizen and sought to adjust his status through the very channels of relief Congress had made available to aliens in his position. Under the law as it existed when he reentered and when he acted, an illegally reentering alien was not automatically and permanently barred from adjustment of status. Section 241(a)(5) changes that calculus after the fact, "attach[ing] new legal consequences," *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270, to a reentry completed under the prior regime by permanently foreclosing any relief under the INA. That is retroactivity of the clearest kind: it "impairs rights a party possessed when he acted." *Id.* at 280.

Nor can the government escape this conclusion by characterizing Petitioner's continued presence, rather than his 1982 reentry, as the relevant "conduct." The event Congress made the trigger for reinstatement and the categorical bar on relief is the illegal reentry itself. As in *St. Cyr*, where the triggering event was the guilty plea rather than the subsequent removal proceeding, the retroactivity analysis must focus on the conduct that the alien could no longer undo—here, a reentry that occurred, and was long since completed, under the law as it stood in 1982. *See St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321; *see also Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 357–58 (1999) (retroactive effect assessed by reference to the conduct or transaction the new provision governs, not merely the date of the proceeding in which it is applied).

Because § 241(a)(5), so applied, imposes a severe new disability on conduct Petitioner completed under the law as it existed in 1982, and because Congress gave no clear indication that it intended this result, the presumption against retroactive application controls. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280; *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 320–21. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
