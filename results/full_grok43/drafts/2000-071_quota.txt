ARGUMENT

I. Milford operated a limited public forum and permissibly excluded religious instruction and prayer as a distinct subject matter.

A limited public forum exists when the government opens property “for use by certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics.” *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 n.7 (1983). Restrictions “need only be reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum and . . . viewpoint neutral.” *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s Community Use Policy opened facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings” while expressly barring “religious purposes.” The Club’s meetings consisted of prayer, memorization of scripture, and Bible lessons culminating in an invitation to accept Christ. These activities constitute religious instruction and worship, not the discussion of morals from a religious viewpoint. *See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 844 (1995) (distinguishing “religious viewpoints” from “religious instruction”); *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (recognizing that “religious worship” may be treated differently from other speech).

This Court has upheld similar exclusions. In *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993), after-school access was required only because the proposed use addressed child-rearing from a religious perspective on an otherwise permitted secular subject. Milford permitted the Boy Scouts and 4-H Club precisely because their programs addressed morals without crossing into worship or proselytization. The Club’s program, by contrast, was “the equivalent of religious worship.” *See Bronx Household of Faith v. Cmty. Sch. Dist. No. 10*, 127 F.3d 207, 215 (2d Cir. 1997). Subject-matter lines drawn at the boundary of worship remain viewpoint neutral. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 830-31.

II. Even if the exclusion implicated the Club’s speech rights, the Establishment Clause supplied a compelling justification.

Public schools may not “endorse or promote . . . prayer” or “religious instruction.” *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 586 (1992). Allowing the Club’s meetings would place Milford in the position of “appear[ing] to be . . . a sponsor of . . . prayer.” *Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 309-10 (2000). The resulting entanglement would fail the second and third prongs of *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971). Milford’s policy therefore served the compelling interest of avoiding an Establishment Clause violation. *See Board of Educ. of Westside Cmty. Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 253 (1990) (plurality) (recognizing that schools may exclude activity that would “give rise to . . . an Establishment Clause violation”).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
