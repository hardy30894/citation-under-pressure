**ARGUMENT**

The conviction cannot stand. Section 875(c) makes it a crime to transmit “any threat to injure the person of another.” The statute does not define “threat,” so Congress adopted the term’s established common-law meaning, including its mental-state requirement. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952) (“Mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent will not be read as dispensing with it.”). At common law, a threat is a statement made with the intent to intimidate or to place another in fear. The Third Circuit’s objective standard—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as a threat—reads that intent requirement out of the statute. It reduces the crime to negligence, punishing a speaker for failing to anticipate a listener’s reaction rather than for intentionally threatening. That contradicts the settled presumption that criminal liability requires a culpable mental state. *Id.* at 250 (“The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion.”). This is not a public-welfare offense where strict liability may be tolerated. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922). Section 875(c) reaches core expressive activity and carries severe penalties; the requirement of subjective intent is therefore essential.

The First Amendment independently demands a subjective intent standard. The “true threat” exception to speech protection is narrow. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (reversing conviction under an analogous threat statute because the words were “political hyperbole”). A true threat is a statement that the speaker intentionally communicates as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. The objective standard eliminates this inquiry. It punishes a speaker based on how a hypothetical reasonable listener reacts, not on what the speaker intended. That approach criminalizes artistic expression, sarcasm, and emotional venting—speech the First Amendment protects.

This Court has consistently required more than foreseeable reaction when speech is punished. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that a State may not “forbid or proscribe advocacy of the use of force” unless “such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940), reversed a conviction because there was no evidence that the defendant intended to incite a breach of the peace. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), held that speech may not be punished simply because it “stirs people to anger.” And even *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the source of the clear-and-present-danger test, focused on “the intent with which the act was done.” These cases apply a fortiori to threats. If incitement requires purpose to produce imminent lawless action, then threats—which are equally communicative—require at least the same subjective mental state.

The government’s interest in protecting listeners from fear does not justify a lesser standard. The criminal law already punishes intentional threats. But where a speaker did not subjectively intend to threaten, punishment adds nothing to public safety and only chills protected expression. *Watts* demonstrates that the mere possibility of fear is not enough; otherwise, the most controversial speakers could be silenced by the sensibilities of their audiences. The objective standard invites juries to convict based on the offensiveness of the words themselves rather than the speaker’s culpable state of mind.

Finally, the objective standard renders §875(c) unconstitutionally vague. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (striking down a criminal statute that “forbids no specific or definite act” and leaves to courts and juries the task of fixing its meaning). A speaker cannot know in advance whether a reasonable person will deem his words threatening; liability turns on a jury’s after-the-fact evaluation of context and tone. That is intolerable in the realm of speech, where clarity is essential to avoid self-censorship. The rule of lenity therefore requires that §875(c) be construed to require subjective intent. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1876) (“It would certainly be dangerous if the legislature could set a net large enough to catch all possible offenders, and leave it to the courts to step inside and say who could be rightfully detained.”).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
