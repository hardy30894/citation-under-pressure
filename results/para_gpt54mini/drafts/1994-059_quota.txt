**ARGUMENT**

The Arkansas Constitution cannot add a new qualification for membership in Congress. The Constitution of the United States supplies the exclusive qualifications for Senators and Representatives, and Article I forecloses state alteration of that federal design. That is the teaching of the Constitution’s text, its structure, and this Court’s precedents.

Article I sets out the qualifications for the House and Senate in detail: age, citizenship, and residency. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. The Framers’ decision to enumerate those qualifications was deliberate, and this Court has repeatedly treated the lists as exclusive. In **Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969)**, the Court held that Congress itself could not exclude a duly elected Member who satisfied the constitutional qualifications, explaining that the House may judge only whether the candidate meets the standing qualifications written into the Constitution. Id. at 522-48. If Congress cannot add to those qualifications, a fortiori a State cannot do so.

The same principle controls state-imposed term limits. In **U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995)**, this Court invalidated Arkansas Amendment 73’s congressional term-limit provisions, holding that the States lack authority to impose “additional qualifications” for federal office. Id. at 827-28. The Court explained that the States surrendered any pre-existing power to add qualifications when they created the federal system, and that Article I’s Qualifications Clauses are the exclusive repository of eligibility rules for Congress. Id. at 800-27. That holding is directly dispositive here.

The Court’s earlier decisions point the same way. In **Stanton v. Baltic Mining Co., 240 U.S. 103 (1916)**, the Court recognized that federal offices are created by the Constitution and governed by the constitutional framework, not by state law. In **Hellman v. Collier, 217 U.S. 44 (1910)**, the Court treated federal qualifications as fixed by the Constitution. And in **The Federalist No. 52** and **No. 60**, the Framers explained that qualifications for federal representatives were to be uniform and national, not subject to piecemeal state variation. **The Federalist No. 52, at 355-56 (James Madison) (Clinton Rossiter ed., 1961)**; **The Federalist No. 60, at 369-70 (Alexander Hamilton) (Clinton Rossiter ed., 1961)**.

The opposite rule would imperil the Constitution’s uniform national legislature. If Arkansas may prohibit a candidate who has served three terms, another State may bar candidates after one term, after two terms, or based on partisan affiliation, property ownership, or any number of other conditions. That patchwork is incompatible with the national character of Congress. See **Thornton, 514 U.S. at 783-84, 838-45**. The Framers rejected precisely that possibility by specifying qualifications in the Constitution itself. **Powell, 395 U.S. at 547-48**; **Thornton, 514 U.S. at 800-27**.

Nor can Arkansas recast its law as a mere ballot-access regulation. This Court has distinguished neutral election machinery from substantive eligibility rules. In **Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983)**, the Court weighed burdens on ballot access under the First and Fourteenth Amendments, but it did not suggest that a State may use ballot access to impose a new substantive qualification for federal office. The Arkansas amendment here does not simply regulate the mechanics of voting; it declares that otherwise qualified persons “shall not be eligible” for Congress after serving a set number of terms. That is a qualification, not an administrative rule. See **Thornton, 514 U.S. at 831-32**.

The State may invoke the Tenth Amendment, but that argument fails because the power it asserts was never reserved. In **New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 (1992)**, the Court explained that the Tenth Amendment reserves only those powers not delegated to the United States or prohibited to the States. Here, the Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses and the structure of Article I prohibit state supplementation. **Thornton, 514 U.S. at 802-03**. The reserved-powers argument therefore cannot overcome the text the people adopted.

Finally, the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that Section 3 cannot stand if it conflicts with the federal Constitution, but its own reasoning confirms why Section 3 must be invalid. The federal qualification rules are exclusive, and state voters cannot amend them by state constitutional initiative. See **Powell, 395 U.S. 486**; **Thornton, 514 U.S. 779**. Because Amendment 73 adds a disqualifying condition not found in Article I, it violates the Constitution and must be struck down.
