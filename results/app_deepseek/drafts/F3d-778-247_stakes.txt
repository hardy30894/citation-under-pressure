**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Search Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus to the Place Searched**

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched.” *Groh v. Ramirez*, 540 U.S. 551, 557 (2004). To establish probable cause, the application must supply a “nexus between the items to be seized and the place to be searched.” *United States v. Werra*, 638 F.3d 326, 334 (1st Cir. 2011). Here, the affidavit failed to link the alleged evidence to Joubert’s parents’ home. Although SJ reported that Joubert removed computer components and stacked them inside that residence, SJ did not see Joubert discard the components there, and the affidavit contains no other facts suggesting that incriminating materials—such as the VHS tape later seized—remained at that address. The mere fact that Joubert *lived* there does not establish probable cause that evidence of child exploitation would be found there months after the alleged abuse occurred. *See United States v. Feliz*, 467 F.3d 227, 235 (2d Cir. 2006) (nexus lacking where affidavit only showed defendant’s presence at location). The warrant was therefore invalid, and all evidence obtained under it should have been suppressed.

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Testimony of Uncharged Child Molestation**

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) prohibits evidence of prior bad acts “to prove a person’s character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character.” Such evidence is admissible only for a non-propensity purpose, such as motive or intent, and its probative value must outweigh unfair prejudice. *United States v. Mound*, 149 F.3d 799, 800 (8th Cir. 1998). Here, the government introduced testimony from other alleged victims that Joubert molested them years before the charged conduct. No proper purpose existed—Joubert’s intent was not in dispute given the VHS tape, and the testimony served solely to paint him as a pedophile. The prejudicial effect was immense, as it invited the jury to convict based on character rather than the charged crimes. *See United States v. Varoudakis*, 233 F.3d 113, 118 (1st Cir. 2000). The district court abused its discretion by admitting this evidence.

**III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert’s Purely Intrastate Conduct**

To regulate activity under the Commerce Clause, Congress must show a substantial effect on interstate commerce. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558-59 (1995). Here, Joubert’s production and possession of child pornography involved no interstate commerce: he filmed and kept the VHS tape entirely within Maine and New Hampshire. The government cannot rely on the “channels” or “instrumentalities” of commerce, as Joubert did not use any. *See United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 609 (2000) (requiring substantial economic effect). The statutes, as applied to Joubert, exceed Congress’s commerce power in violation of the Tenth Amendment.

**IV. Joubert’s 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable**

A sentence is substantively unreasonable if it is “greater than necessary” to achieve the purposes of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 51 (2007). The district court imposed a 480-month term—effectively a life sentence for a man over 50—without adequately considering his lack of criminal history, his age, or the advisory Guidelines range’s excessive length. *See United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 91 (1st Cir. 2008) (review for abuse of discretion). The court gave undue weight to the nature of the offense and ignored Joubert’s capacity for rehabilitation. A shorter sentence would have adequately protected the public and reflected the seriousness of the conduct.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse Joubert’s conviction and sentence, or remand for further proceedings.
