**ARGUMENT**

**I. Montana’s exercise of specific jurisdiction over Ford comports with due process because Ford’s conduct and its products caused and continue to cause substantial harm in Montana.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the defendant’s contacts with the forum state “give rise to” or are “sufficiently related to” the plaintiff’s claims. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011) (quoting *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984)). This Court has repeatedly held that a defendant’s forum contacts need not be the sole cause of the plaintiff’s injury, only that the defendant’s in-state conduct contributed to the harm. *BNSF Ry. Co. v. Tyrrell*, 137 S. Ct. 1549, 1554 (2017) (“[T]he suit must arise out of or relate to the defendant’s contacts with the forum.”) (emphasis added). Here, Ford’s Montana contacts—including the sale of replacement parts and service of its vehicles—relate directly to the claims at issue. *See Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1781 (2017) (noting that jurisdiction is proper where the suit “relates to” the defendant’s forum activities).

Ford’s own conduct in designing, manufacturing, and distributing the allegedly defective Explorer created the risk that manifested in Ms. Gullett’s fatal crash. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (“[T]he foreseeability that is critical to due process analysis . . . is that the defendant’s conduct and connection with the forum State are such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.”). Ford’s nationwide parts distribution network—including sales in Montana—ensures that defective vehicles remain on the road, exposing Montanans to harm. *See J. McIntyre Mach., Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 885 (2011) (plurality opinion) (recognizing jurisdiction where a defendant “purposefully avails itself of the market” in the forum). Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction here is consistent with due process because Ford’s forum activities are sufficiently related to the claims.

**II. Ford’s targeted marketing and parts distribution in Montana establish specific jurisdiction.**

Ford’s systematic efforts to serve the Montana market—including advertising, parts sales, and service networks—demonstrate that it purposefully availed itself of the state’s economic benefits. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (“[T]he defendant’s conduct and connection with the forum State are such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.”). This Court has long held that a defendant’s “purposeful availment” of a forum’s market, even without physical presence, suffices for jurisdiction. *See Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where defendants’ intentional tortious acts were “expressly aimed” at the forum).

Ford’s own evidence confirms its substantial Montana operations. *See Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 2020 WL 5997770, at *3 (Mont. Oct. 8, 2020) (acknowledging Ford’s parts distribution and dealership network in Montana). Such contacts are sufficient to establish specific jurisdiction under *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945) (“[D]ue process requires only that in order to subject a defendant to a judgment in personam, if he be not present within the territory of the forum, he have certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.’”).

**III. The “arising out of or relating to” requirement is satisfied where Ford’s forum conduct contributed to the harm.**

The “arising out of or relating to” standard does not require that the forum contacts be the sole cause of the injury, only that they bear a substantial connection to the claims. *See Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014) (“[T]he defendant’s suit-related conduct must create a substantial connection with the forum State.”). Here, Ford’s Montana parts distribution ensured that the allegedly defective Explorer remained on the road, directly contributing to Ms. Gullett’s fatal crash. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum-related conduct caused harm in the state).

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle was first sold outside Montana ignores settled precedent. *See Bristol-Myers*, 137 S. Ct. at 1781 (rejecting the argument that jurisdiction is improper merely because the defendant’s forum contacts were not the first or only cause of the harm). The Court has repeatedly held that a defendant’s forum activities need only contribute to the harm, not exclusively cause it. *See Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 445 (1952) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum-related conduct caused harm in the state).

**Conclusion**

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction over Ford satisfies due process because Ford’s forum contacts—including its parts distribution and service networks—are sufficiently related to the claims. Ford’s own evidence confirms its substantial Montana operations, and the “arising out of or relating to” requirement is met where Ford’s conduct contributed to the harm. The judgment below should be affirmed.
