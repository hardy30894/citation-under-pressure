**ARGUMENT**

**The District Court Erred in Denying the Motion to Suppress Because the Pat-Frisk of Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment.**

The Fourth Amendment guarantees the right of the people to be secure against unreasonable searches and seizures. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 9 (1968). A limited pat-frisk for weapons is permissible only where an officer has “reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous.” *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968). This standard requires “specific and articulable facts” that, taken together with rational inferences, warrant a reasonable belief that the suspect poses a threat to officer safety. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. The government bears the burden of proving that the frisk was justified. *Beck v. Ohio*, 379 U.S. 89, 96 (1964).

Here, the officers lacked any particularized basis to suspect that Soares was armed and dangerous. The initial stop—for driving without headlights—was concededly lawful. *See Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 149 (1925) (traffic stop permissible upon probable cause of civil infraction). But the stop’s validity does not automatically authorize a frisk. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 19. The frisk of Soares required independent justification, which the record does not supply.

**A. The Furtive Movements Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion of Danger.**

The officers observed a passenger make a movement “down and to his left,” toward the console, and the driver move toward his door. Such movements, without more, are insufficient to justify a frisk. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 62 (rejecting frisk where officer observed “furtive movements” but no specific indication of a weapon). The Supreme Court has long held that “a vague suspicion of criminal activity” is not enough. *Henry v. United States*, 361 U.S. 98, 104 (1959). Here, the movement was consistent with retrieving a wallet, phone, or simply adjusting position. No officer testified to seeing a bulge or any object that suggested a weapon. The district court’s reliance on the “high-crime area” designation cannot salvage the deficiency. *See id.* at 101 (noting that even in high-crime areas, officers must have more than “inarticulate hunches”). *Terry* itself required “specific reasonable inferences which he is entitled to draw from the facts in light of his experience.” 392 U.S. at 27. The officers here drew no such inferences; they merely observed generic movement.

**B. Soares’s Agitation and Verbal Abuse Did Not Provide Reasonable Suspicion.**

Soares became agitated, rocked back and forth, and used profane language. He accused the officers of racial profiling. While such behavior may be unsettling, it does not give rise to reasonable suspicion that he was armed. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 64 (holding that “nervousness” and “evasive” behavior, without more, cannot justify a frisk). The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that “a person’s reaction to the presence of police” is not a reliable indicator of criminality. *Ker v. California*, 374 U.S. 23, 34 (1963) (plurality opinion). Indeed, “the Fourth Amendment does not permit a policeman to stop and search a citizen merely because the citizen is nervous or belligerent.” *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 22. Soares’s verbal abuse, while perhaps disrespectful, was a constitutionally protected expression of dissatisfaction. *See Beck*, 379 U.S. at 96 (rejecting reliance on “uncooperative” behavior as a basis for search). The officers’ subjective unease does not satisfy the objective standard. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27.

**C. The Sequence of Events Undermines Any Claim of Imminent Danger.**

The officers had already secured the scene. Two additional officers arrived. The driver had stepped out voluntarily and consented to a pat-frisk. Soares remained in the car, under orders to keep his hands visible. He complied initially. His later hand movements, while agitated, did not suggest he was reaching for a weapon; he was “flailing” and “flipping” his hands, not reaching for his waistband or pockets. *Cf. United States v. Collins*, 349 F.2d 296, 298 (1st Cir. 1965) (frisk justified where defendant reached for pocket after officer ordered him to stop). Moreover, the officers did not observe any suspicious bulges or objects before the removal. The decision to remove Soares was based on Reid’s “uneasy” feeling—a classic hunch. But *Terry* demands “specific and articulable facts,” not mere intuition. 392 U.S. at 21. The officers could have maintained the status quo—Soares in the car, under observation—until the traffic citation was completed. *See Henry*, 361 U.S. at 104 (holding that police must “act with the utmost restraint” when no immediate danger exists).

**D. The Frisk of Soares Was Not a Lawful Terry Frisk.**

Even if the removal were justified, the subsequent pat-frisk was invalid because the officers lacked reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 64. Soares’s act of swatting away Lapolis’s hand during the attempted frisk cannot retroactively justify the intrusion. *See Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963) (exclusionary rule applies to evidence derived from unlawful search). The Fourth Amendment forbids using a suspect’s reaction to an unlawful police action as a basis for further search. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). The gun found during the scuffle was the fruit of an unconstitutional pat-frisk.

**Conclusion**

Because the officers lacked reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous, the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment. The district court’s denial of the motion to suppress must be reversed.
