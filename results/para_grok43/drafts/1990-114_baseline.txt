**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges.**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act excludes from its definition of “employee” any “appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan and retained by election exercise precisely that function. “Policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (quoting 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). Missouri Supreme Court decisions establish common-law rules binding throughout the state. See, e.g., Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc). Judges also promulgate rules of professional conduct and resolve intra-judicial disputes. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4.

Because the tenure of state judges “is a matter of considerable importance to a state, and one that traditionally has been left to each state to regulate,” any congressional intent to displace that authority must be “explicit and unequivocal.” United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The statutory text supplies that clarity by placing appointed judges within the policymaking exception. The legislative history confirms the exception reaches “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers, 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180. State judiciaries are among the most powerful agencies of state government; nothing in the statute or history suggests Congress intended to exempt them from the exception while simultaneously exempting elected judges.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Rational-Basis Review.**

Age is not a suspect classification. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). A classification that “neither burdens a suspect group nor impinges on a fundamental right” must be sustained if it is “rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979). Missouri’s rule advances several legitimate objectives: it “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); reduces delays caused by death or disability, Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); and permits “important decisions . . . [to] be made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens,” Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). A state “has a legitimate interest in maintaining a judiciary fully capable of performing the demanding tasks that judges must perform,” and it is not irrational to conclude that “physical and mental capacity do not remain constant.” Murgia, 427 U.S. at 314; Vance, 440 U.S. at 97. Because the classification rests on plausible empirical and administrative grounds, it does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
