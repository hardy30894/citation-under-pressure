# ARGUMENT

## I. THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien "may be detained beyond the removal period." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). The statute does not say "indefinitely," nor does it say "permanently." Where Congress intends to authorize potentially permanent civil detention, it must speak with clarity, particularly where, as here, the detained class includes lawful permanent residents whose only "crime" may be a decades-old conviction followed by good behavior. This Court has long required a "clear indication" that Congress intended a statute to push against constitutional limits. See *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 298-99 (2001) (canon of constitutional avoidance requires "clear indication" before construing a statute to raise "serious constitutional problems").

Under settled principles of statutory construction, "when an Act of Congress raises a serious doubt as to its constitutionality, this Court will first ascertain whether a construction of the statute is fairly possible by which the question may be avoided." *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). That canon controls here. Section 1231(a)(6) is fairly susceptible of a construction that avoids indefinite detention: it may be read to authorize detention only for the period reasonably necessary to accomplish the alien's removal. Nothing in the statute's text compels the Government's reading that detention may continue forever, without regard to whether removal will ever occur.

## II. INDEFINITE CIVIL DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN RAISES SERIOUS CONSTITUTIONAL PROBLEMS

### A. The Due Process Clause Protects All Persons Within the United States, Including Removable Aliens

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause applies to "all persons" within the United States, "including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent." *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 210 (1982). This Court has repeatedly confirmed that once an alien enters the country, that alien "is entitled to due process of law in deportation proceedings." *Reno v. Flores*, 507 U.S. 292, 306 (1993) (recognizing that aliens facing deportation retain constitutional protections). Freedom from imprisonment "lies at the heart of the liberty" protected by the Due Process Clause. *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Civil, non-punitive detention is permitted only in narrow circumstances involving a special justification outweighing the individual's liberty interest, such as a showing of mental illness combined with dangerousness, accompanied by strong procedural protections. *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 356-58 (1997). No such showing exists here. The petitioner is not alleged to be mentally ill; he is detained solely because the Government has been unable to accomplish his removal.

### B. Mezei Does Not Control Because Petitioner Was Lawfully Admitted

The Government's principal authority, *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), addressed a fundamentally different circumstance: an alien stopped at the border who was "treated" for constitutional purposes "as if stopped at the border." *Id.* at 215. Mezei had never effected entry into the United States. Petitioner, by contrast, was lawfully admitted as a permanent resident decades ago. This distinction is constitutionally significant. This Court has consistently held that the constitutional status of aliens turns on whether they have effected entry: "once an alien gains admission to our country and begins to develop the ties that go with permanent residence, his constitutional status changes accordingly." *Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982). Mezei's holding cannot be extended to strip admitted aliens of due process protections merely because removal proceedings have concluded against them.

### C. Indefinite Detention Is Not Reasonably Related to the Statute's Purpose

Civil detention must bear a reasonable relation to the purpose for which the individual is committed. *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972) (holding that "due process requires that the nature and duration of commitment bear some reasonable relation to the purpose for which the individual is committed"). The purpose of post-removal detention is to secure the alien's presence at the moment removal becomes possible. Once it is no longer reasonably foreseeable that removal will occur, continued detention no longer serves that purpose—it becomes indefinite, potentially permanent incarceration untethered to any legitimate governmental objective. Such detention resembles the preventive detention this Court has permitted only in narrowly cabined circumstances involving heightened procedural safeguards and demonstrated dangerousness. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747, 751-52 (1987) (upholding pretrial detention only where "limited to a specific category of extremely serious offenses" and accompanied by "extensive safeguards"). Section 1231(a)(6), by contrast, applies broadly to any alien ordered removed for myriad reasons—including routine visa violations—without the individualized, adversarial procedures Salerno requires.

## III. THE STATUTE SHOULD BE CONSTRUED TO CONTAIN AN IMPLICIT REASONABLE-TIME LIMITATION

Because the Government's construction of § 1231(a)(6) would require this Court to resolve serious constitutional questions, the statute should instead be read, consistent with *Crowell*, 285 U.S. at 62, to contain an implicit limitation: detention is authorized only for the period reasonably necessary to bring about the alien's removal. Once a habeas petitioner "provide[s] good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future," the burden shifts to the Government to rebut that showing. If it cannot, detention is no longer authorized, and release—subject to appropriate conditions of supervision—must follow.

This construction respects Congress's authority to detain aliens during the process of removal, to impose supervised release, and to reincarcerate those who violate release conditions. It does not require the release of dangerous individuals; it requires only that indefinite, potentially permanent detention be justified by a genuine prospect of removal, subject to meaningful judicial review under the writ of habeas corpus preserved by 28 U.S.C. § 2241.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
