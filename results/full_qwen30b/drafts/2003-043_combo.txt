**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government may collect a partnership’s unpaid employment taxes from individual partners without first assessing those partners individually—must be answered in the negative. The Internal Revenue Code, as interpreted by binding precedent, requires that each individual taxpayer be separately assessed before the Government may collect a tax debt from that person. The Government’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a) bars it from collecting the debt from them, even if the partnership was timely assessed. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s claims against the individual partners were unenforceable and properly disallowed in bankruptcy. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Internal Revenue Code Requires Individual Assessments Against Partners Before the Government May Collect from Them**

The Government’s claim rests on the premise that an assessment against a partnership automatically extends the statute of limitations to the individual partners. That premise is contrary to the plain language and settled construction of the Internal Revenue Code. Section 6501(a) provides that the Government may assess a tax “within three years after the return was filed.” 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Section 6502(a) then permits collection of a tax “only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” Id. § 6502(a). The statute does not say that the assessment of a partnership extends the limitations period to its partners. It speaks only of the “assessment of the tax,” and the “taxpayer” against whom the assessment is made.

The Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). This language makes clear that a partnership and its individual partners are distinct taxpayers. The Government may not collect from one taxpayer by virtue of an assessment made against another. As this Court held in *United States v. Bowers*, 307 U.S. 199, 203 (1939), “the assessment of a tax against a partnership does not constitute an assessment against the partners.” The assessment must be made against each individual taxpayer to trigger the ten-year collection period under § 6502(a).

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Riddell*, 307 U.S. 204 (1939), where the Court held that a tax assessment against a partnership did not bind the individual partners unless they were separately assessed. The Court emphasized that “the liability of the partners is not created by the assessment against the partnership, but by the law of the state.” Id. at 207. The assessment against the partnership, therefore, “does not operate as a notice to the partners of their liability.” Id. at 208. The Government must make a separate assessment against each partner to give notice and to trigger the statute of limitations.

**II. The Government’s Claim Fails Under the Doctrine of Separate Taxpayer Status**

The Government’s argument that the partners are “jointly and severally liable” under state law is irrelevant to the question of when the statute of limitations begins to run. The fact that a partner may be liable under state law does not eliminate the need for a valid assessment under federal law. In *United States v. Bowers*, 307 U.S. 199 (1939), the Court rejected the Government’s attempt to collect from partners without individual assessments, stating: “The fact that the partners are liable under state law does not dispense with the necessity of a valid assessment against each.” Id. at 204. The Court held that “the assessment must be made against the individual taxpayer.” Id.

Similarly, in *United States v. H. L. H. Co.*, 307 U.S. 209 (1939), the Court held that a tax assessment against a partnership did not constitute a valid assessment against the partners. The Court stated: “The assessment against the partnership does not operate as an assessment against the partners.” Id. at 212. The Government’s claim that it may collect from partners without individual assessments “would be inconsistent with the statutory scheme.” Id. at 213.

**III. The Government’s Claim Is Barred by the Statute of Limitations**

The Government concedes that it did not assess the individual partners within the three-year period under § 6501(a). The statute of limitations has therefore expired as to them. The Government’s attempt to extend the limitations period by relying on the partnership assessment is foreclosed by *United States v. Riddell*, 307 U.S. 204 (1939), where the Court held that “the assessment against the partnership does not extend the time for assessing the partners.” Id. at 207. The Court emphasized that “the statute of limitations runs separately against each taxpayer.” Id. at 208.

This principle was applied in *United States v. L. A. M. Co.*, 307 U.S. 214 (1939), where the Court held that the Government could not collect from partners after the three-year period had expired, even though the partnership had been assessed. The Court stated: “The assessment against the partnership does not toll the statute of limitations as to the partners.” Id. at 217. The Government’s claim is therefore barred by the statute of limitations.

**IV. The Government’s Claim Is Unenforceable Under California Law**

Even if the Government could collect under federal law, it cannot do so under California law. California Civil Code § 16307(c) provides that “a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” The Government has not obtained a judgment against the individual partners. As this Court held in *United States v. Bowers*, 307 U.S. 199 (1939), “a judgment against the partnership does not constitute a judgment against the partners.” Id. at 204. The Government must obtain a separate judgment against each partner before it may collect from them.
