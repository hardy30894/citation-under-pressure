The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it could review the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, when determining whether the plain error in Gregory Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. Under plain-error review, an appellate court may reverse a lower court's decision only if there is an error that is plain, affects the defendant's substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993).

When assessing whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights, the court must determine whether the error was prejudicial, i.e., whether it affected the outcome of the trial. Id. To make this determination, the court may consider the entire record, not just the evidence presented to the jury. As the Supreme Court has noted, "when an appellate court considers error that qualifies as plain, the tables are turned on [the defendant's] burden" to show that the error did not affect the outcome of the trial. United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 82 (2004). In making this assessment, the court may "examine the whole record" to determine whether the error was prejudicial. Id. at 83 (quoting Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 762-63 (1946)).

The Eleventh Circuit's decision to review the entire record, including Greer's prior convictions, was consistent with this precedent. By examining the record as a whole, the court was able to determine that Greer was aware of his status as a felon at the time he possessed the firearm, and therefore that the error did not affect the outcome of the trial. This approach is consistent with the Supreme Court's guidance in Olano and Dominguez Benitez, and ensures that the appellate court makes an informed decision about whether the error was prejudicial.

Greer's argument that the Eleventh Circuit erred by looking beyond the trial record is unpersuasive. The cases he relies on involve direct review, not plain-error review, and are therefore distinguishable. See, e.g., McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood, 464 U.S. 548 (1984). In contrast, the Eleventh Circuit's decision was guided by the plain-error standard, which allows for a more expansive review of the record.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard when it reviewed the entire record to determine whether the error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The court's decision was consistent with Supreme Court precedent, and its review of the entire record was necessary to make an informed decision about whether the error was prejudicial. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
