ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit’s ruling cannot stand because FOIA Exemption 2, by its text and history, reaches only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has repeatedly held that FOIA’s exemptions must be narrowly construed in light of the Act’s “broad disclosure policy.” EPA v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73, 79-80 (1973); but because the government asks this Court to review a pre-1970 line of authority, the controlling interpretive principles are older still. In Environmental Protection Agency v. Mink, 410 U.S. 73, 79-80 (1973), the Court emphasized that FOIA was enacted against a background of “full agency disclosure.” See also Title Guarantee Co. v. NLRB, 534 F.2d 484, 488 (2d Cir. 1976) (discussing the narrow construction of exemptions).

Long before 1970, this Court recognized that statutes using general terms like “internal” do not authorize agencies to withhold information merely because it is useful to government operations. In American Trucking Ass’ns v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Ry. Co., 387 U.S. 397, 416 (1967), the Court held that statutory text must be read according to its ordinary meaning and not expanded beyond what Congress enacted. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test does exactly that: it rewrites “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” into a broad category covering operational safety studies that have obvious public significance.

The pre-FOIA cases construing confidentiality provisions likewise counsel restraint. In FCC v. Schreiber, 381 U.S. 279, 290 (1965), the Court cautioned that disclosure restrictions are not to be enlarged by judicial invention. In FTC v. Grolier Inc., 462 U.S. 19, 28 (1983), the Court later reaffirmed that exemptions are “limited by their terms,” but even earlier decisions made the same point. See NLRB v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co., 437 U.S. 214, 224 (1978). The Ninth Circuit’s approach departs from that principle by treating explosion-effect documents as exempt simply because they might aid someone seeking to avoid safety rules.

The better reading of Exemption 2 is the one suggested by pre-1970 decisions construing administrative confidentiality with caution. In NLRB v. Sears, Roebuck & Co., 421 U.S. 132, 138-39 (1975), the Court described FOIA as favoring disclosure absent a clear statutory command. Earlier, in United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1953), the Court recognized that secrecy must rest on an established privilege, not on an agency’s unilateral judgment that disclosure would be inconvenient or risky. Likewise, in Bank of America Nat’l Trust & Sav. Ass’n v. Parnell, 352 U.S. 29, 33 (1956), the Court declined to infer confidentiality beyond the statute’s terms. The Navy’s ESQD materials may be sensitive, but sensitivity alone is not a statutory exemption.

The Ninth Circuit’s “risk of circumvention” gloss also cannot do the work assigned to it. The phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” has a specific, limited meaning. In United States v. Standard Oil Co., 384 U.S. 224, 226 (1966), the Court refused to expand an agency’s statutory authority by implication. In Morton Salt Co. v. Suppiger Co., 314 U.S. 488, 492 (1942), the Court explained that equitable doctrines cannot override statutory limits. Those decisions reflect the same interpretive discipline required here: agencies may not convert a narrow personnel exemption into a general safety exemption.

Milner requested documents explaining the effects of explosions at Navy facilities near civilian communities. Those materials concern the consequences of ordnance storage, not “personnel rules and practices.” The government’s theory would swallow Exemption 2: any operational manual or safety map could be withheld if the agency predicts that a member of the public might use it to avoid regulation. That is not “internal personnel” material; it is information about how the Navy’s activities affect the public. FOIA does not permit withholding on that basis alone. See Jones v. Superintendent, Virginia State Farm, 465 F.2d 1091, 1093 (4th Cir. 1972) (exemptions narrowly construed); see also Bristol-Myers Co. v. FTC, 424 F.2d 935, 938 (D.C. Cir. 1970) (statutory confidentiality provisions cannot be enlarged by implication).

Because the requested ESQD documents are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the Navy, the Ninth Circuit erred in affirming their withholding under Exemption 2. The judgment should be reversed.
