**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly held that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief for the trial court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2). A Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review, and the record establishes that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue one. *See generally United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 253–54 (1940) (holding that harmless-error analysis applies to procedural violations where the error did not affect the outcome); *Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82, 84 (1946) (finding no due process violation where the defendant was aware of his rights and failed to take advantage of them).

Petitioner’s reliance on *McConnell v. Rhay*, 393 U.S. 2 (1968), is misplaced. *McConnell* involved a complete denial of counsel at sentencing, a structural error that undermines the fundamental fairness of the proceeding. *Id.* at 4. Here, by contrast, the sentencing court’s failure to advise Petitioner of his right to appeal did not deprive him of a meaningful opportunity to challenge his conviction or sentence. *See Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948) (holding that a sentence imposed after a valid guilty plea is not subject to collateral attack absent a showing of constitutional magnitude).

The Third Circuit correctly applied harmless-error review to the Rule 32 violation. *See generally United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (establishing the harmless-error doctrine in federal appellate review); *Woods v. Nierstheimer*, 328 U.S. 211, 215 (1946) (applying harmless-error review to procedural defaults in state criminal proceedings). The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their right to appeal. *See Sigler, Warden v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969) (mem.) (recognizing that procedural rules are designed to protect defendants’ rights). Where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, the error is harmless. *See Smith v. O’GRADY*, 312 U.S. 329, 334 (1941) (holding that a defendant’s ignorance of procedural rights does not invalidate a conviction where the defendant was otherwise aware of his substantive rights).

Petitioner’s argument that any violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is per se reversible is foreclosed by this Court’s decision in *Mempa v. Rhay*, 389 U.S. 128 (1967). In *Mempa*, the Court held that the right to counsel at sentencing is a fundamental right, but it did not extend that holding to procedural rules governing post-conviction remedies. *Id.* at 134. The Court has consistently refused to treat procedural violations as structural errors unless they implicate the fundamental fairness of the trial itself. *See Hunter v. Martin*, 334 U.S. 302, 306 (1948) (holding that procedural defaults do not warrant collateral relief absent a showing of prejudice).

The District Court’s finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is supported by the record. *See Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 585 (1959) (deferring to the trial court’s factual findings in habeas proceedings). Petitioner does not dispute that he was informed of his right to appeal by his counsel or that he elected not to pursue an appeal. Under these circumstances, the trial court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal did not prejudice him. *See Canizio*, 327 U.S. at 84 (finding no prejudice where the defendant was aware of his rights but failed to act).

Finally, Petitioner’s reliance on *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), is misplaced. *Darr* involved the exhaustion of state remedies, not the harmlessness of a procedural violation. *Id.* at 204. The Court has never held that a defendant is entitled to habeas relief for a procedural violation where the defendant was aware of his rights and chose not to exercise them. *See Gryger*, 334 U.S. at 732 (rejecting collateral attack on a sentence where the defendant was not deprived of a fundamental right).

In sum, the District Court correctly applied harmless-error review and concluded that Petitioner was not entitled to relief. The judgment should be affirmed.
