# ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit's decision cannot be reconciled with the text Congress enacted or with this Court's longstanding rules for construing statutory exemptions. Exemption 2 shields only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court of appeals nonetheless upheld withholding of documents describing the blast radius of military explosives near a civilian population—information having nothing to do with agency personnel administration—by grafting onto the statute a "predominantly internal" gloss and a freestanding "risk of circumvention" test found nowhere in the statutory text. That approach violates three settled interpretive principles.

**I. The Statute's Plain Language Controls.**

"It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Congress did not write that Exemption 2 covers matters "related to" agency practices, or "predominantly" related to them; it wrote "solely." That word choice is not surplusage. As this Court has explained, "[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes. Often these words are sufficient in and of themselves to determine the purpose of the legislation." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The Ninth Circuit's substitution of "predominantly" for "solely" is not construction; it is amendment. Where a court concludes that Congress omitted a qualification the government wishes had been included, "[t]o supply omissions transcends the judicial function." *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926).

**II. The Words "Personnel Rules and Practices" Limit the Reach of "Internal."**

Even accepting that "internal" modifies something, the noun it modifies—"personnel rules and practices"—confines the exemption's subject matter to agency employment administration, not to the substantive contents of a Navy ordnance-safety program affecting the public. Under the settled canon of noscitur a sociis, general or ambiguous language is read in light of the more specific terms with which it is associated; a word "is known by its associates," and general terms following specific ones take their meaning from that company. *Jarecki v. G. D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961). Here the specific terms are "personnel rules and practices"—the classic subject matter of employee handbooks, leave policies, and workplace conduct rules. Explosive Safety Quantity Distance data describing how far a detonation will travel across Puget Sound is not a personnel rule in any sense the associated words can bear. The Ninth Circuit's rule allows the "internal" label to swallow the limiting words "personnel rules and practices" entirely, precisely the kind of construction that ignores the statutory neighbors Congress chose.

**III. Statutory Exemptions From Disclosure Must Be Narrowly Construed, and the Burden of Establishing Them Rests on the Agency.**

This Court has repeatedly held, in analogous remedial contexts, that exceptions to a statute's general command must be read narrowly against the party invoking them, and that the exception's proponent bears the burden of showing it applies plainly and unmistakably. "To extend an exemption to other than those plainly and unmistakably within its terms and spirit is to abuse the interpretative process and to frustrate the announced will of the [legislature]." *A. H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945). This Court has applied the same narrow-construction principle again and again where a statute's general remedial purpose is at stake. See *Mitchell v. Kentucky Finance Co.*, 359 U.S. 290, 295 (1959); *Arnold v. Ben Kanowsky, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 388, 392 (1960) (exemptions are to be "narrowly construed" and limited to matters coming "plainly and unmistakably within their terms"); *Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 607, 617 (1944). The same logic applies with equal, if not greater, force to a statute like FOIA whose exemptions carve out exceptions from a general rule of public access. Just as this Court has held that statutory exemptions from taxation "are not to be extended by implication beyond the clear import of the language used," *Commissioner v. Jacobson*, 336 U.S. 28, 49 (1949), Exemption 2 should not be extended by implication to reach categories of documents—technical explosive-effects data bearing directly on public safety—that Congress never mentioned and that bear no relationship to "personnel rules and practices."

**IV. Application.**

Measured against these principles, the ESQD documents at issue fall outside Exemption 2. They are not personnel rules; they are technical, scientific data about the physical effects of explosions, compiled to guide construction and storage decisions that directly affect surrounding civilian communities like Port Townsend and Port Hadlock. The Navy's own affidavits describe these materials as safety and engineering data, not employee-conduct rules. Under the narrow construction commanded by *Phillips*, *Arnold*, and *Jacobson*, and under the plain words Congress chose—"solely," "personnel," "internal"—the Navy cannot satisfy its burden of showing these records fall "plainly and unmistakably" within Exemption 2's terms. The Ninth Circuit's judicially invented "predominantly internal" and freestanding circumvention-risk tests cannot substitute for the statutory text, and this Court should reverse.
