## ARGUMENT

**I. The plain text of § 522 exempts only funds that presently constitute "retirement funds," not funds that once bore that character in someone else's hands.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds ... in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation." The starting point—and, where the text is clear, the ending point—of statutory interpretation is the language Congress actually enacted. This Court held long ago that "where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning, the duty of interpretation does not arise." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Likewise, in *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court explained that "there is no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes," and that those words are "often ... sufficient in and of themselves to determine the purpose of the legislation." *Id.* at 543.

The statute's present-tense formulation—funds that "are" retirement funds—asks a question about the asset's current function, not its provenance. An inherited IRA cannot be held until the beneficiary's own retirement; it must be liquidated within a statutorily fixed period regardless of the beneficiary's age or retirement plans. It therefore does not, at the time relevant to the bankruptcy exemption, hold the character the statute demands. Congress's chosen words control; this Court should not read into the statute an exemption for funds that were once, but no longer are, dedicated to retirement.

**II. Exemption status is determined by the character of the property as of the date the bankruptcy petition is filed, not by its history in a predecessor's hands.**

This Court has consistently held that the bankrupt's exemption rights are fixed by reference to the condition of the property at the time bankruptcy is commenced. In *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310 (1924), the Court held that "the point of time at which the status of the bankrupt as to exempt property is fixed is the date of the filing of the petition," and that exemptions must be assessed according to that status, not by reference to earlier or later events. The same temporal principle animated *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), where the Court looked to the character and availability of the exemption as it existed under law applicable at the commencement of the case, rather than to some antecedent condition of the property.

This principle forecloses the theory—adopted by the courts below in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and rejected by the Seventh Circuit—that an asset carries an exemption forward simply because it was "retirement funds" in a decedent's hands. Just as a homestead exemption depends on the debtor's own occupancy of the property, not a parent's prior occupancy, the retirement-fund exemption must depend on the debtor's own relationship to the funds. This Court's insurance-exemption decisions confirm the point: in *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459 (1913), the Court examined the actual character and cash value of a life-insurance policy *at the time of bankruptcy* to decide whether it qualified for exemption, not its character when the policy first issued. And in *Holden v. Stratton*, 191 U.S. 115 (1903), the Court likewise tied the exemption to the nature of the interest as it existed when the bankruptcy proceeding began. The inherited IRA here, evaluated as of the Clarks' petition date, was not a fund reserved for their retirement; it was a mandatory, time-limited payout vehicle available for current consumption.

**III. The purposes underlying the bankruptcy exemption—protecting a debtor's own provision for retirement—do not extend to inherited windfalls, and any doubt should be resolved by reference to the balance Congress struck between debtors and creditors.**

The retirement-funds exemption serves the bankruptcy system's broader purpose of giving debtors "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). But that purpose is aimed at debtors' own provision against the day they can no longer earn income—not at shielding inherited wealth that a debtor may spend immediately. Congress did not write an exemption for "any funds ever contributed to a retirement account by anyone"; it wrote an exemption limited to "retirement funds." Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would convert a narrow, purpose-specific protection into a general shelter for inherited assets, contrary to the balance Congress struck between debtor relief and creditors' legitimate claims.

This Court has cautioned against construing bankruptcy provisions in ways that upset that balance. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court construed the scope of the debtor's "property" for purposes of the bankruptcy estate by reference to the practical operation of the provision within the bankruptcy scheme as a whole, not by resort to the broadest imaginable purpose said to underlie it. The same discipline applies here: creditors are entitled to reach assets that do not fit the retirement-fund definition Congress actually wrote, even though a broader definition might further the abstract policy of encouraging savings.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
