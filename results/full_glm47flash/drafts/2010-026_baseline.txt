**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit erred by replacing the statutory “solely” requirement with the judicially created “predominantly internal” standard.**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision to adopt the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard for Exemption 2 contravenes the plain text of the statute and exceeds the scope of congressional intent. Section 552(b)(2) exempts from disclosure matters “related **solely** to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The word “solely” is a critical limitation on the scope of the exemption, ensuring that only information strictly concerning agency personnel management is protected. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” test effectively deletes this statutory qualifier, allowing agencies to withhold documents that are only tangentially related to internal personnel matters.

The Supreme Court has instructed that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed” and are “explicitly exclusive.” *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592 (1976); *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841 (1989). The “solely” requirement reflects Congress’s determination that the public interest in transparency outweighs the need to disclose information that is merely related to, but not exclusively for, internal agency use. By replacing “solely” with “predominantly,” the Ninth Circuit has rewritten the statute to create a new category of exempt information that Congress never intended to protect.

**II. The “predominantly internal” standard is a judicial invention that distorts the balance of FOIA.**

The “predominantly internal” standard is a judicial creation, not a statutory mandate. The D.C. Circuit developed this test in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981), to address a specific set of law enforcement manuals. However, the Ninth Circuit’s application of this standard to the present case improperly expands the reach of Exemption 2 beyond its intended limits. The Supreme Court has emphasized that FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The “predominantly internal” standard is not clearly delineated; it is a flexible, fact-intensive inquiry that allows agencies to hide information that has a genuine and significant public interest.

In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), this Court correctly held that Exemption 2 is not limited to “law enforcement” materials. However, *Hardy* did not endorse the “predominantly internal” standard. Instead, *Hardy* focused on whether the specific documents at issue were “concern[ed] internal personnel practices.” Id. at 656. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case conflates the subject matter of the document with the consequence of its disclosure. While the risk of circumvention is a relevant factor, it does not excuse the failure to meet the “solely” requirement. The Navy’s ESQD data is used for safety planning and risk mitigation, not solely for internal personnel management. Therefore, the “solely” requirement is not satisfied, and the documents are not exempt under Exemption 2.
