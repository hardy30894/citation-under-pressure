**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001), the Court held that the Immigration and Nationality Act’s post-removal-period detention provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention. Rather, the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas has been detained for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period, despite the fact that no country will accept him. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to repatriate him, and no other nation has offered to do so. The INS has made no meaningful effort to secure his removal in the years since the removal period expired. Under *Zadvydas*, this is not merely a matter of administrative delay—it is a constitutional violation.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that the statute’s purpose is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” 533 U.S. at 2504. That purpose ceases to be served when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. The Court emphasized that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. It further instructed that “the habeas court must ask whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” Id. The Court did not leave this inquiry to the discretion of the Executive; it placed the burden on the judiciary to determine whether the government has demonstrated a reasonable prospect of removal.

The Fifth Circuit, in its initial opinion, held that the government could detain Zadvydas so long as it made “good faith efforts” to deport him and so long as he failed to show that deportation was “impossible.” 185 F.3d at 297. But this standard, as the Supreme Court correctly observed, “would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—[which] demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” 533 U.S. at 2505. The Court rejected the notion that an alien must prove *absolute* impossibility of removal. Instead, the burden shifts to the government to demonstrate that removal remains reasonably foreseeable.

The Court in *Zadvydas* established a practical framework: after six months beyond the removal period, an alien may present “good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future,” and the government must then rebut that showing. Id. The Court noted that “as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink.” Id. Zadvydas has now been detained for more than three years beyond the removal period. At that point, the “reasonably foreseeable future” must be measured in months, not years. The government has not presented any evidence—nor has it even claimed—that removal is likely within the next six months, let alone the next year.

The government’s own actions confirm the absence of any realistic prospect of removal. It has not pursued any new avenues for repatriation. It has not engaged in diplomatic outreach to any country. It has not even sought to reapply for Lithuanian citizenship on Zadvydas’s behalf, despite the fact that such an application was “apparently still pending” at the time of the Supreme Court’s decision. 533 U.S. at 2496. The government’s silence is deafening. As the Court noted, “we nowhere deny the right of Congress to remove aliens, to subject them to supervision with conditions when released from detention, or to incarcerate them where appropriate for violation of those conditions.” Id. at 2501. But the government has not shown that such conditions are necessary here.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision to affirm the district court’s order of release, albeit with a modified stay, is consistent with *Zadvydas*. The court correctly recognized that Zadvydas had “provided good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future,” and that the government had failed to rebut that showing. The court’s conclusion—that continued detention is no longer authorized by statute—is sound and constitutionally required.

The government’s argument that Zadvydas remains a risk to the community is irrelevant. The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that “the choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large’... It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” Id. at 2502. The district court’s release conditions—bond, regular reporting, employment, residence, health insurance—are precisely the kind of supervision the Court envisioned. They are reasonable, enforceable, and sufficient to ensure Zadvydas’s presence at the moment of removal should that ever become possible.

The Constitution does not permit indefinite detention of a person who poses no threat and whose removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas* made that clear. The Fifth Circuit correctly applied the Court’s holding. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.

**For the foregoing reasons, the petition for writ of certiorari should be granted, and the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be affirmed.**
