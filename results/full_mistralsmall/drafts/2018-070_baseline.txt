**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A “GAP” IN FEDERAL COVERAGE BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ADOPTED AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State … are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. This Court has long recognized that the OCSLA’s incorporation of state law is not automatic; rather, state law applies only where federal law fails to address the subject matter at issue. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (state wrongful-death law applied because federal maritime remedies were inapplicable); *Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1126, 1135 (9th Cir. 2010) (state law applied only after determining that federal law did not address the claim).

The Fifth Circuit’s “gap-filling” test—articulated in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969)—correctly interprets the OCSLA’s structure. As that court explained, state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal coverage. *Id.* This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA’s purpose: to provide a comprehensive federal framework for activities on the outer Continental Shelf, supplemented by state law only where federal law is silent. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355 (federal law governs unless state law addresses an area not covered by federal statute).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law—ignores the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement. The Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that the absence of a “significant void or gap” is not a prerequisite to applying state law. *See Parker Drilling Mgmt. Servs., Ltd. v. Newton*, 929 F.3d 958, 965 (9th Cir. 2019). This misreading of the OCSLA conflicts with the Supreme Court’s admonition that federal law is the primary source of law on the outer Continental Shelf, with state law serving only as a supplementary remedy. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355.

II. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE STATUTORY SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO GAP FOR STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., is a comprehensive federal statute governing minimum wages, overtime, and related employment practices. The FLSA expressly preserves more protective state wage and hour laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218, but this savings clause does not create a gap in federal coverage. *See, e.g., Cipriano v. City of Houston*, 724 F.3d 627, 632 (5th Cir. 2013) (state law may supplement but not supplant federal wage standards).

The district court correctly held that the FLSA leaves no void for state law to fill. The FLSA’s detailed provisions—covering minimum wages, overtime, recordkeeping, and enforcement—demonstrate Congress’s intent to occupy the field of wage and hour regulation. *See Mitchell v. Chapman*, 343 U.S. 545, 554 (1952) (FLSA is a “comprehensive federal scheme”); *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys.*, 450 U.S. 728, 736 (1981) (FLSA provides “exclusive remedies” for covered workers).

Newton’s reliance on the FLSA’s savings clause is misplaced. The savings clause permits state laws to be *more protective* than federal standards, but it does not authorize state laws that impose additional obligations beyond those contemplated by the FLSA. *See, e.g., 29 C.F.R. § 778.205* (federal regulations interpreting the FLSA’s overtime provisions). California’s meal-period requirement, as applied to offshore workers, conflicts with the FLSA’s structure, which does not mandate meal periods for every five hours worked. *See 29 U.S.C. § 207; see also Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004) (federal law preempts state laws that frustrate the FLSA’s purposes).

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH SUPREME COURT PRECEDENT AND CREATES UNCERTAINTY FOR OFFSHORE EMPLOYERS.

The Ninth Circuit’s holding undermines the OCSLA’s federal framework by allowing state wage and hour laws to govern offshore employment. This creates a patchwork of regulations that frustrates Congress’s intent to provide uniform federal standards for activities on the outer Continental Shelf. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s approach conflicts with this Court’s decision in *Valladolid*, which reaffirmed that state law applies on the OCS only where federal law is silent. 604 F.3d at 1135. The Ninth Circuit’s departure from this precedent warrants this Court’s review to clarify the OCSLA’s choice-of-law framework.

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA requires a “gap” in federal coverage before state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law. The FLSA is a comprehensive statute that leaves no void for California’s wage and hour laws. The Ninth Circuit’s decision misreads the OCSLA and conflicts with Supreme Court precedent. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and reinstate the district court’s dismissal of Newton’s state-law claims.
