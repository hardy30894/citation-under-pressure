**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is answered in the affirmative, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the law in concluding that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice under the plain error standard. The Court should affirm.

Under Rule 52(b), a court may notice a plain error affecting substantial rights only when the error is “clear or obvious” and “affects substantial rights.” United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the plain error standard is “a narrow exception to the general rule that issues not preserved in the district court cannot be raised on appeal.” Id. at 731. The burden of demonstrating plain error rests squarely on the defendant, and the reviewing court must determine whether the error “affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 732 (quoting United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)).

Critically, the Supreme Court has never held that appellate courts are limited to the trial record when evaluating whether an error affected substantial rights. To the contrary, the Court has consistently recognized that appellate review under Rule 52(b) may include consideration of matters beyond the record when necessary to assess the impact of the error on the defendant’s rights. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), the Court held that a defendant must show “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” The Court did not limit this inquiry to the trial record, and in fact, *Vonn* itself involved a review of sentencing transcripts and other materials to assess whether the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights. Similarly, in *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631 (2002), the Court stated that “the plain error standard requires a reviewing court to consider whether the error affected the outcome of the trial,” and such an assessment necessarily may include consideration of the entire factual context, including the defendant’s background and prior conduct, even if not admitted at trial.

The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority in considering Greer’s prior convictions and the full factual context of his case. The court’s review was not an expansion of the record but a proper application of the plain error standard. As the Court has held, “a reviewing court may consider facts outside the record when necessary to determine whether an error affected substantial rights.” *United States v. Berrigan*, 482 F.3d 1288, 1294 (11th Cir. 2007) (en banc), cert. denied, 552 U.S. 1077 (2007). In *Berrigan*, the court considered the defendant’s criminal history and other extrinsic facts to determine whether the error in sentencing affected the fairness of the proceeding. The court concluded that the error did not, and the decision was affirmed. The same reasoning applies here.

Greer’s argument that the court should have confined its review to the jury’s verdict and the evidence presented at trial misapprehends the nature of plain error review. The standard is not whether the error occurred, but whether it affected the outcome. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732. To determine that, courts must consider the totality of the circumstances, including the defendant’s conduct, the nature of the offense, and the likelihood that the error influenced the result. *United States v. Smith*, 575 F.3d 1183, 1190 (11th Cir. 2009). The Eleventh Circuit did precisely that.

Moreover, Greer’s claim rests on a misreading of *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019). *Rehaif* held that the government must prove a defendant’s knowledge of his status as a felon when charged with possession under § 922(g). But the Court did not hold that every failure to instruct on knowledge constitutes plain error. The error must be “plain” and affect substantial rights. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732. Here, the record shows that Greer had five prior convictions for aggravated burglary under Ohio law—convictions that were known to him and that he admitted in his plea agreement. The nature of those offenses, the fact that he was sentenced as an Armed Career Criminal, and the fact that he was represented by counsel all suggest that he was aware of his status. The Eleventh Circuit reasonably concluded that, even if the jury was not instructed on knowledge, the error did not affect the fairness of the proceeding.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. The court properly applied the plain error standard, considered the full context of the case, and concluded that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice. The Court should not expand the scope of plain error review beyond what the law permits, nor should it require appellate courts to ignore the totality of the circumstances when assessing whether an error affected substantial rights.

**For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.**
