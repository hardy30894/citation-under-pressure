**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and §522(d)(12) protect only “retirement funds” held in a tax-exempt account. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). An inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands because the account may not receive new contributions, may not be rolled over, and must begin mandatory distributions shortly after the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A); 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B). As this Court explained below, an inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” In re Clark, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013).

1. **The statutory text protects retirement savings, not inherited assets available for current consumption.**

The phrase “retirement funds” carries its ordinary meaning: money set aside for retirement. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 562–63. The Clarks’ inherited IRA fails that test because it is not reserved for Heidi’s retirement; federal tax law requires distributions during her working years. Id. at 561–62. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that statutory interpretation must begin with the words Congress chose, not with an abstract policy preference. See Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A., 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000); Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004).

The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected the view adopted by the Fifth Circuit in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), that it is enough if the funds were once someone’s retirement funds. That reading detaches “retirement funds” from the debtor and from any retirement purpose at the time of bankruptcy. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 563–64. But Congress did not write “funds that once were retirement funds.” It wrote “retirement funds.” 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12).

2. **An inherited IRA is legally and functionally unlike an IRA owned by the debtor.**

Under the Internal Revenue Code, inherited IRAs are subject to special restrictions. The beneficiary may not contribute additional amounts, may not combine the account with another IRA, and must withdraw assets on an accelerated timetable. 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A); 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B). Those restrictions are incompatible with the ordinary function of retirement savings. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 562–63.

This Court has recognized that exemptions under §522 depend on the nature of the property as the debtor holds it. The same principle appears in cases addressing homestead and tenancy exemptions, which turn on the debtor’s own use and status at filing, not a predecessor’s. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 565–66; see also In re Barker, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985). Heidi did not use the inherited IRA as her own retirement account. It was, instead, a limited tax-deferral device available for present spending. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 564.

3. **Nondischargeability should not be expanded beyond Congress’s chosen exemption.**

Bankruptcy exemptions are construed according to statutory text, not sympathy for debtors or abstract incentives to save. The Supreme Court has warned that courts may not “rewrite” a statute to better serve perceived policy goals. Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987). The Clarks’ position would create a broad inherited-wealth exemption Congress never enacted. If Congress wished to protect inherited IRAs, it could have done so expressly. Instead, it limited the exemption to “retirement funds” in qualifying accounts. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12).

The better reading is the one adopted by the bankruptcy court and by the Seventh Circuit: because inherited IRAs are not funds set aside for the debtor’s retirement, they are not exempt under §522. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 567. This Court should affirm.
