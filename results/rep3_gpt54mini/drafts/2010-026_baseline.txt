**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit erred in extending FOIA Exemption 2 to Navy ESQD documents. FOIA permits withholding only for matters that fall within a “clearly delineated” exemption, and those exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61, 361 (1976); *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). The Ninth Circuit’s rule improperly broadens Exemption 2 beyond its text and this Court’s decisions.

Exemption 2 reaches matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). In *Rose*, this Court explained that the exemption covers mundane internal matters and recognized a separate concern where disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” 425 U.S. at 363, 369. But *Rose* did not authorize withholding every document that could aid an outsider in understanding how an agency operates. To the contrary, FOIA’s “general philosophy of full agency disclosure” requires that exemptions be confined to their statutory terms. *Id.* at 360-61.

The Ninth Circuit adopted the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” approach from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), and held that any document “predominantly internal” and creating a circumvention risk may be withheld under Exemption 2. That formulation is inconsistent with the statute. Congress did not say “predominantly internal”; it said “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of an agency. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). Reading “predominantly” into the statute dilutes the limiting word “solely,” something this Court has repeatedly refused to do in FOIA cases. See *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151; *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361.

This Court’s own cases confirm the error. In *Rose*, the Court applied Exemption 2 to internal cadet disciplinary materials only after emphasizing their routine internal character. 425 U.S. at 363-70. In *Department of Justice v. Julian*, the Court rejected a broad secrecy claim and reiterated that FOIA exemptions are to be applied only where Congress clearly so provided. *Dep’t of Justice v. Julian*, 486 U.S. 1, 8-9 (1988). And in *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 527-28 (1988), the Court recognized the Executive’s security interests, but did so in a context involving a distinct statutory and constitutional framework—not a judicial rewrite of FOIA exemption text.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision is especially unsound because the ESQD documents are not “personnel rules and practices” in any ordinary sense. They are technical safety and planning materials describing blast effects, storage distances, and risk mitigation for ammunition facilities. Documents of that kind may be operationally sensitive, but sensitivity is not the statutory test. Exemption 2 is not a catchall for information an agency would prefer not to disclose. See *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

Nor can the Navy justify withholding by invoking circumvention alone. *Rose* tied circumvention to “internal personnel rules and practices.” 425 U.S. at 369. The Ninth Circuit inverted that relationship, treating a speculative circumvention risk as enough to transform any operational document into exempt internal policy. That approach is incompatible with FOIA’s presumption of disclosure and with the burden Congress placed on the agency to justify withholding. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B); *U.S. Dep’t of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173 (1991).

Other decisions applying Exemption 2 likewise show the error of the Ninth Circuit’s rule. The D.C. Circuit in *Schwaner v. Department of the Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793, 794-95 (D.C. Cir. 1990), and *Morley v. CIA*, 508 F.3d 1108, 1124 (D.C. Cir. 2007), addressed information closely tied to internal agency practices and the avoidance of circumvention. Those cases do not support extending Exemption 2 to technical safety data of the sort requested here. Likewise, the Ninth Circuit’s own pre-*Milner* decision in *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085-87 (9th Cir. 1997), recognized that materials outside the core of internal personnel rules do not qualify merely because disclosure may affect agency operations.

The proper course is straightforward: the ESQD records are not exempt under Exemption 2 because they are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the Navy. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The judgment should be reversed.
