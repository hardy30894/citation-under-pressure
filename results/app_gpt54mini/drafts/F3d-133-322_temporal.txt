**Argument**

The convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) cannot stand because the indictment and proof treated Mrs. Parker’s conduct as “official” even though she lacked authority to approve or deny benefits. Section 201 reaches only a public official’s corrupt acceptance of something of value in exchange for an “official act,” and that term must be confined to a genuine act of office, not merely clerical assistance or unauthorized conduct. See 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3); United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 230-31, 34 S. Ct. 512, 514-15, 58 L. Ed. 930 (1914).

The Supreme Court has made clear that an “official act” is not any act performed by a government employee, but only a decision or action tied to the duties of office. Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 230-31, 34 S. Ct. at 514-15. Birdsall recognized that duties may be established by settled practice, but it did not dispense with the requirement that the conduct be a duty of the office. Id. at 231, 34 S. Ct. at 515. That limitation is critical here. Mrs. Parker was a clerk. Her duties were to assist ALJ Aronson before and during hearings, record proceedings, and type and mail decisions. She was not authorized to grant benefits, deny them, or sign Aronson’s name. A clerk’s ministerial tasks are not the kind of official judgment Congress criminalized in § 201(b)(2)(C).

The government’s theory impermissibly converts unauthorized deception into the equivalent of an official act. But bribery under § 201 requires a nexus between the thing of value and an official decision or action. The offense does not reach a government employee merely because she has access to files or a computer system, or because she can exploit her position to impersonate her superior. The statutory phrase “official act” presupposes action taken in an official capacity, not a private fraud committed while occupying government employment. See 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3); Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 230-31, 34 S. Ct. at 514-15.

That distinction matters even more because the indictment charged conspiracy as well as substantive bribery counts. A conspiracy conviction cannot stand where the object offense is unsupported by the statute’s elements. See Williamson v. United States, 207 U.S. 425, 447, 28 S. Ct. 163, 172, 52 L. Ed. 278 (1908). If Mrs. Parker’s conduct—changing letters, forging a signature, and demanding payment for that deception—was outside the scope of an “official act,” then the conspiracy count falls with the substantive § 201 counts.

Nor does the fact that Mrs. Parker occupied a position within the SSA office enlarge § 201 to cover every misuse of access. The Supreme Court has warned against expanding bribery statutes beyond their terms. See United States v. Gradwell, 243 U.S. 476, 485-86, 37 S. Ct. 407, 410, 61 L. Ed. 857 (1917) (criminal statutes must not be extended by construction beyond their fair meaning). Here, the government’s reading would erase the statutory line between official corruption and ordinary fraud. That is not what Congress enacted.

The district court’s approach also risks criminalizing conduct untethered to any lawful authority or duty. The accepted rule is that where Congress uses a term of art grounded in public duty, the term must be given a public-duty meaning. Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 231, 34 S. Ct. at 515. Mrs. Parker did not exercise official discretion; she counterfeited it. Her acts were wrongful, but they were not “official acts” within § 201(b)(2)(C). Because the indictment and the evidence failed to establish that essential element, the convictions should be reversed.

The same defect requires reversal as to Mr. Parker. His liability was derivative of the same bribery theory and the same supposed “official acts.” Without proof that the payments were made to influence a true official act within the statute, there is no basis for either the substantive counts or the conspiracy count against him. See Williamson, 207 U.S. at 447, 28 S. Ct. at 172.

For these reasons, appellants respectfully request that the Court reverse the convictions.
