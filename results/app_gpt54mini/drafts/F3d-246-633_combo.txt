**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below should be reversed because the district court resolved disputed issues of union representation and labor-conspiracy liability on summary judgment, and then imposed wiretap damages and fees on an expansive theory that cannot be squared with the governing statutes.

I. **The Railway Labor Act permits judicial review where a union’s conduct frustrates the employee’s statutory right to fair representation.**

Under the Railway Labor Act, a bargaining representative must serve the interests of all employees it represents without hostile discrimination or arbitrary conduct. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944); *Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen & Enginemen*, 323 U.S. 210, 213 (1944). The duty is not merely aspirational; it is enforceable in court when the union’s conduct deprives an employee of the fair and honest representation the Act requires. *Steele*, 323 U.S. at 207; *Graham v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen & Enginemen*, 338 U.S. 232, 239-40 (1949).

Smoot presented evidence that UTU first refused to pursue his claim, then shifted representation after internal pressure, and that the union’s handling of the stock dispute was infected by intra-union retaliation and inconsistent positions. That kind of evidentiary record does not permit summary judgment. Courts have long recognized that questions concerning a union’s statutory duty, motive, and treatment of a member are fact-intensive and ordinarily unsuitable for resolution as a matter of law. *Steele*, 323 U.S. at 207; *Ford Motor Co. v. Huffman*, 345 U.S. 330, 337-38 (1953). The district court therefore erred in treating UTU’s conduct as immune from review simply because the dispute arose in the arbitral context. The Railway Labor Act does not authorize a representative to abandon or undermine an employee’s claim and then invoke the arbitral process as a shield against judicial scrutiny. *Steele*, 323 U.S. at 198-99; *Tunstall*, 323 U.S. at 213.

II. **A conspiracy claim may be proved by circumstantial evidence, and summary judgment was improper where the record allowed competing inferences about coordinated conduct.**

A civil conspiracy may be established by concerted action and inference from surrounding facts; direct proof is not required. *American Tobacco Co. v. United States*, 328 U.S. 781, 809-10 (1946). Likewise, where the evidence permits competing inferences as to agreement and intent, the issue is for the trier of fact. *Theatre Enterprises, Inc. v. Paramount Film Distributing Corp.*, 346 U.S. 537, 540-41 (1954). Smoot’s claim that UTU and CSX coordinated to defeat his stock entitlement was supported by the sequence of representation decisions, the handling of the PLB proceedings, and the aftermath of the taped executive session. Those facts were enough to require a trial.

The district court’s approach effectively demanded direct proof of secret agreement. That is not the law. “Conspiracy” is often shown by a combination of acts that, taken together, support an inference of concerted action. *American Tobacco*, 328 U.S. at 809-10. And where the parties’ conduct is susceptible of more than one reasonable interpretation, summary judgment is inappropriate. *Theatre Enterprises*, 346 U.S. at 540-41. Smoot was entitled to have a jury decide whether the union and carrier acted independently or in combination to defeat his claim.

III. **The wiretap judgment and damages cannot stand because the Act was applied beyond its proper remedial limits and without a sufficient basis for punitive enhancement.**

The federal wiretap statute must be construed in light of its text and purpose, not as a general punitive device for collateral labor disputes. Statutory damages and exemplary damages are not automatic; they depend on the structure of the Act and the proof. Courts have cautioned that statutory remedies should not be enlarged beyond congressional design. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952); *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 430 (1943). Here, the district court awarded $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and over $86,000 in fees and costs based on Smoot’s recording of an executive session in a labor arbitration setting. That extraordinary award should be reviewed closely because the record shows a dispute over an internal labor proceeding, not a commercial interception scheme of the kind Congress plainly sought to deter. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263.

Punitive damages in federal litigation require a lawful basis and careful justification. *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851). On this record, the district court’s punitive award rested on findings that were neither necessary to the liability determination nor supported by the kind of clear factual record that punitive sanctions require. *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371.

IV. **At minimum, the case should be remanded for trial on disputed issues of fact.**

Summary judgment is improper where material facts remain genuinely disputed. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627-28 (1944). This case presents exactly that circumstance. The parties dispute the union’s motives, the significance of the representation decisions, the meaning of the executive-session recording, and the extent of any coordinated conduct between UTU and CSX. Under settled principles, those disputes belong to a factfinder, not to the court on summary judgment. *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627-28.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the summary judgments, vacate the damages award on the counterclaims, and remand for trial.
