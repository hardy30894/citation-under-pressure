ARGUMENT

I. Mrs. Parker’s Convictions Must Be Reversed Because the Indictment Failed to Allege an “Official Act” Within the Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).

Section 201(b)(2)(C) punishes a public official who “corruptly demands, seeks, receives, accepts, or agrees to receive or accept anything of value” in return for “being induced to do or omit to do any act in violation of the official duty of such official or person.” Mrs. Parker’s duties were limited to assisting ALJ Aronson before and during hearings, recording notes, typing decisions, and mailing opinions. She possessed no authority to approve benefits or sign the ALJ’s name. The indictment therefore charged conduct outside any “official duty.”

An “official act” is defined as “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official’s official capacity, or in such official’s place of trust or profit.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). This language requires that the act be one the official is empowered to perform in an official capacity. The legislative history confirms that Congress intended the statute to reach “any decision or action taken by a public official in his capacity as such.” S. Rep. No. 87-2213 (1962), reprinted in 1962 U.S.C.C.A.N. 3852, 3856.

The Supreme Court has long held that duties “not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice,” and only action taken “in the course of their performance” falls within the bribery statutes. United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231, 34 S. Ct. 512, 514-15, 58 L. Ed. 930 (1914). No settled practice authorized a clerk to forge an ALJ’s signature or unilaterally grant benefits. Because Mrs. Parker’s conduct lay outside both her written duties and any established usage, it cannot constitute an act “in violation of the official duty” under § 201(b)(2)(C).

The Sixth Circuit’s observation that official acts are “not limited to those within the official’s specific authority,” United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983), does not alter the result. That court still required the act to bear some nexus to the official’s lawful responsibilities. Here, forging decisions and selling benefits bore no such nexus. The same reasoning defeats the conspiracy count under 18 U.S.C. § 371, which required proof of an agreement to commit the substantive bribery offenses.

Mr. Parker’s convictions rest on the same defective theory and must fall with his wife’s.

II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Either Appellant Committed an Official Act.

For the reasons set forth above, the government’s proof that Mrs. Parker forged letters and accepted payments failed to establish any act within the scope of her official duty. Mr. Parker’s participation in the same scheme likewise cannot support liability under the statute. The convictions should be reversed.
