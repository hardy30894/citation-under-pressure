**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that Anglo-American criminal law has historically conditioned criminal liability on proof of a culpable mental state, a requirement so deeply rooted in the common law that congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement rather than to dispense with it. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–251, 263 (1952).

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter. This Court has recognized that class of offenses only where "the accused, if he does not will violation, usually is in a position to prevent it with no more care than society might reasonably expect and no more exertion than it might reasonably exact from one who assumed his responsibilities" — typically in the regulatory context of food, drugs, or other inherently hazardous commerce. *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277, 281 (1943). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from that regulatory context; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty. In construing the term "willfully" in a federal tax statute, the Court explained that the word "often denotes an act which is intentional, or knowing, or voluntary, as distinguished from accidental," and that its meaning must be drawn from the context and evident purpose of the law. *United States v. Murdock*, 290 U.S. 389, 394 (1933). Likewise, this Court has long insisted that penal statutes give fair warning of what conduct they proscribe, cautioning that "a fair warning should be given to the world in language that the common world will understand" of what conduct is forbidden. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." Only narrow, historically unprotected categories of speech fall outside the First Amendment's shelter, and this Court has cautioned that restrictions on speech must be defined with precision. In upholding a narrowly drawn obscenity law, the Court emphasized that "obscenity is not within the area of constitutionally protected speech or press," a holding premised on the historically narrow and carefully bounded nature of the category being excluded from protection. *Roth v. United States*, 354 U.S. 476, 485 (1957). The same care must attend any doctrine — such as the "true threat" exception — that purports to remove speech from the Amendment's coverage; an objective, listener-focused standard that punishes speech based on how it might be received, rather than on what the speaker meant to convey, sweeps far beyond the narrow and carefully defined categories this Court has recognized.

Consistent with that caution, this Court has held that speech may be restricted only where the danger is grave and unambiguous, explaining that "the question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent." *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). Free expression that provokes discomfort, anger, or alarm in listeners does not thereby lose its protected character; indeed, this Court has recognized that a central function of free speech is to invite dispute and unsettle prevailing sentiment. *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949). If speech may not be suppressed merely because it provokes anger or alarm in its audience, a fortiori a speaker's own words may not be transformed into an unprotected "true threat" solely because a listener, applying a reasonable-person standard, perceives them as threatening — without regard to whether the speaker intended any such meaning.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression — dark humor, hyperbole, rap lyrics, venting — because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has recognized that uncertainty about the legal consequences of expression casts a chilling shadow over protected speech, observing that "compelled disclosure of affiliation with groups engaged in advocacy may constitute as effective a restraint on freedom of association as" other forms of governmental action. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958). The same logic applies where speakers must guess, at their peril, how a jury applying an objective reasonable-listener standard might construe their words after the fact. Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before punishment may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense — punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium, holding that "no one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
