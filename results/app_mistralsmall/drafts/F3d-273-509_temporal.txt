**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Release Does Not Bar Plaintiff’s Claims Because It Does Not Expressly Cover Them.**

Plaintiff’s claims are not barred by the 1988 release because the release’s language is limited to claims arising from the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. The release states that Bancorp “releases the [FDIC] ... from any and all claims which Bancorp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” (Emphasis added.) This Court has consistently construed release language narrowly, holding that a release “is to be interpreted as it is written, and not as it might have been written.” *United States v. Seckinger*, 397 U.S. 203, 211 (1970) (quoting *United States v. Alaska S.S. Co.*, 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920)). Accord, *United States v. National City Bank of New York*, 83 F.Supp. 872, 875 (S.D.N.Y. 1949) (“A release is to be strictly construed against the party claiming its benefit.”).

The claims here—unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, corporate waste, and inadequate accounting—do not arise from the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. They arise from the FDIC’s post-closure management of the receivership estate, including its payment of post-insolvency interest, sale of assets, and charging of legal and office expenses. These actions occurred years after the closure and are distinct from the OCC’s decision. As this Court has recognized, “a release does not extend to matters which differ in kind or nature from those covered in the release.” *United States v. First Nat’l Bank & Trust Co. of Augusta*, 157 F.2d 597, 600 (2d Cir. 1946). The release here is no broader.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run Until the Termination of the Receivership on November 1, 1995.**

Plaintiff’s claims are not barred by New York’s six-year statute of limitations, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), because the limitations period did not begin to run until the FDIC terminated the receivership. Under New York law, a cause of action accrues “when the plaintiff can obtain relief.” *Board of Educ. v. City of New York*, 250 N.Y. 152, 155 (1929). A plaintiff cannot obtain relief from a receiver’s actions until the receivership is terminated. See *Matter of Marine Midland Bank, N.A.*, 21 N.Y.2d 91, 95 (1967) (“The authority of a receiver continues until the estate is fully administered and the receivership is terminated.”).

Here, the FDIC’s actions—charging legal and office expenses, paying itself post-insolvency interest, and selling assets—were taken in its capacity as receiver and could not be challenged until the receivership ended. The FDIC itself acknowledged this principle in its briefing below, stating that “the receiver’s acts are not subject to judicial review until the termination of the receivership.” (See FDIC Br. at 12.) This Court has long held that a plaintiff may not sue a receiver for actions taken in the course of the receivership until the receivership is concluded. See *In re Marine Midland Bank, N.A.*, 21 N.Y.2d at 95; *Matter of Niagara Fire Ins. Co.*, 159 N.Y. 627 (1899). Thus, the statute of limitations did not begin to run until November 1, 1995, when the receivership terminated, and plaintiff’s October 31, 1995 complaint was timely filed.

**III. The District Court Erred in Holding That Plaintiff’s Claims Were Barred by the Statute of Limitations.**

The District Court erred in concluding that plaintiff’s claims accrued at the time of the FDIC’s challenged actions, rather than at the termination of the receivership. The Court relied on *Matter of Marine Midland Bank, N.A.*, 21 N.Y.2d at 95, but misapplied it. In *Marine Midland*, the Court held that a plaintiff could not sue a receiver for actions taken during the receivership until the receivership ended. Id. The District Court, however, incorrectly assumed that the limitations period began to run at the time of the challenged acts, rather than at the termination of the receivership. This was error.

New York courts have consistently held that a plaintiff may not sue a receiver for actions taken in the course of the receivership until the receivership is concluded. See *Matter of Niagara Fire Ins. Co.*, 159 N.Y. at 627; *Matter of Manhattan Storage & Warehouse Co.*, 125 N.Y. 175 (1891). The District Court’s contrary holding misconstrues New York law and warrants reversal.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed. The release does not bar plaintiff’s claims, and the statute of limitations did not begin to run until the termination of the receivership. Plaintiff’s complaint was timely filed, and its claims should be adjudicated on the merits.
