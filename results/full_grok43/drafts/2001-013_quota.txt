The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1) divests the Occupational Safety and Health Administration of jurisdiction over working conditions aboard uninspected vessels. Section 4(b)(1) applies only when another federal agency has “exercise[d] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That threshold is not satisfied by the mere existence of statutory authority; the agency must actually exercise it. Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n, 499 U.S. 144, 152 (1991); see also Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984).

The Coast Guard has not exercised such authority over the working conditions at issue here. Its regulations governing uninspected vessels address only discrete equipment requirements, 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq., and do not extend to the blowout-prevention and well-control procedures that OSHA cited. Where an agency’s regulations leave a subject untouched, section 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Gileo, 351 U.S. 493, 500 (1956) (holding that limited federal regulation does not displace broader remedial statutes).

This Court’s decisions confirm that the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen’s working conditions is not exclusive when it remains unexercised. Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 426–27 (1985), recognized that the Longshore Act and the OSH Act may apply concurrently to maritime workers unless another agency has affirmatively regulated the precise hazard. Similarly, the primary-jurisdiction doctrine requires actual regulatory coverage before one agency’s jurisdiction yields to another. United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co., 352 U.S. 59, 63–64 (1956); Ricci v. Chicago Mercantile Exchange, 409 U.S. 289, 302–03 (1973).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule rests on an overreading of its own precedents. Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983), addressed inspected vessels subject to comprehensive Coast Guard standards. Neither decision examined whether the Coast Guard had exercised authority over uninspected vessels, nor did either purport to create an industry-wide exemption. Colomb v. Texaco, Inc., 736 F.2d 218, 220 (5th Cir. 1984), and Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray, 361 F.2d 432, 434 (5th Cir. 1966), likewise concerned seaman status under the Jones Act and did not address OSHA preemption.

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised regulatory authority over the cited working conditions on uninspected vessels, section 4(b)(1) does not bar OSHA jurisdiction. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
