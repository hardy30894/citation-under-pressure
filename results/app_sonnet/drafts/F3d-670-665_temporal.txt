ARGUMENT

I. A credible showing of actual innocence excuses AEDPA's limitations bar without imposing a separate diligence precondition.

Habeas corpus has never been a creature of rigid procedural forfeiture. This Court's predecessor rule—that a petitioner's substantive claim of innocence may open the courthouse door notwithstanding a procedural default—rests on a principle the Supreme Court articulated long before AEDPA existed: "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake and infringement of constitutional rights is alleged." Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1, 8 (1963). In Sanders, the Court held that even successive habeas applications, ordinarily barred, must be heard on the merits unless "the ends of justice" would not be served by doing so. Id. at 15. Nothing in that formulation asks whether the petitioner was diligent; it asks only whether a fundamental miscarriage of justice is at stake.

The same principle animates Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), where the Court held that a state prisoner's failure to pursue an available remedy did not bar federal habeas review absent a deliberate bypass of orderly procedure, because the "boundaries of the writ" are shaped by its historic office of protecting against unlawful detention, not by technical rules of forfeiture. Id. at 399, 438. Fay makes clear that procedural default doctrines exist to prevent deliberate manipulation of the system, not to punish prisoners who present a credible claim that they are actually innocent. A diligence requirement layered onto the actual-innocence inquiry would resurrect exactly the kind of rigid procedural bar that Fay and Sanders rejected.

This is consistent with the Court's earlier recognition that habeas corpus exists precisely to correct convictions obtained through the kind of manifest injustice Perkins alleges here—conviction procured on the testimony of the true perpetrator. In Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103 (1935), the Court held that a conviction resting on testimony known to be false offends due process, explaining that such a conviction is inconsistent with "the rudimentary demands of justice." Id. at 112. And in Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101 (1942), the Court reaffirmed that habeas corpus reaches beyond jurisdictional defects to correct fundamental unfairness in the fact-finding process itself. Id. at 104-05. These cases confirm that when a prisoner's evidence undermines confidence in the very determination of guilt, the writ's traditional function is to reach the merits—diligence or no diligence.

II. Diligence, if relevant at all, is an equitable factor for the district court to weigh, not a threshold that forecloses consideration of innocence evidence.

The Warden's position conflates two distinct equitable doctrines: the tolling of a limitations period because a petitioner could not discover his claim, and the miscarriage-of-justice exception that has always accompanied habeas review. Equitable doctrines of this kind have historically been applied flexibly, calibrated to the circumstances of the individual case rather than mechanical rules. In Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1875), the Court held that where discovery of a claim's factual predicate is delayed through no fault of the party, the limitations period does not begin to run until discovery is possible, because a contrary rule would make "the statute of limitations . . . a means of successfully perpetrating fraud." Id. at 349-50. The Court later confirmed that this equitable principle applies broadly to federal statutes of limitations. Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 396-97 (1946) ("This equitable doctrine is read into every federal statute of limitation."). Neither case treats diligence as an independent gatekeeping requirement standing apart from the equities of the case; diligence is simply one part of an overall equitable assessment of fairness.

That is precisely the approach this Court should take here. The district court's rule—that a prisoner's credible innocence evidence may be disregarded entirely unless he also satisfies a freestanding diligence test—inverts the equitable inquiry these cases describe. It converts a factor relevant to the overall fairness calculus into an absolute precondition that forecloses review of the merits altogether, contrary to the flexible, case-specific approach of Bailey v. Glover and Holmberg.

III. Perkins's presentation of the affidavits does not defeat relief even if diligence is considered.

Perkins came forward with three affidavits identifying an alternate perpetrator, obtained over a period of years from witnesses with no obligation to speak. Where, as in Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101 (1942), the underlying claim strikes at the integrity of the fact-finding process, courts have not treated delay in assembling corroborating proof as fatal, particularly where—as the district court itself observed—the theory of defense was not new but rather substantiation of a theory advanced, unsuccessfully, at trial. The equitable inquiry commanded by Bailey v. Glover and Holmberg v. Armbrecht asks whether the petitioner acted reasonably under the totality of circumstances, not whether he acted with maximum possible speed.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a credible claim of actual innocence permits equitable tolling of AEDPA's limitations period without imposing a freestanding diligence requirement, and should remand for the district court to assess Perkins's innocence claim on its merits.
