**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act coverage only to employees injured upon the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test conflates causation with geography and rewrites the statute Congress enacted.

**I. The Plain Text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) Imposes a Situs-of-Injury Requirement**

Section 1333(b) makes compensation payable for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “occurring as the result of” modifies “injury,” not “operations.” An injury “results from” operations, but the injury itself must “occur” in a particular place—namely, on the shelf where the operations are conducted. Congress could have written “any injury arising out of” or “any injury caused by,” but it chose language that ties the injury’s occurrence to the location of the operations.

The Longshore Act’s own coverage provision requires that the injury “occur[r] upon the navigable waters of the United States (including any dry dock).” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a) (emphasis added). This Court has consistently enforced that geographic limitation. In *Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson*, 396 U.S. 212, 214 (1969), the Court held that “injuries to longshoremen occurring on piers permanently affixed to shore” are not compensable because they do not occur “upon the navigable waters.” Likewise, in *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244, 246 (1942), the Court required that the “death occurred upon navigable waters” to trigger coverage. There is no reason to read the parallel geographic requirement in § 1333(b) out of the statute.

**II. The Overall Structure of OCSLA Confirms the Geographic Limitation**

OCSLA’s primary purpose is to extend federal jurisdiction over the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and “artificial islands and fixed structures which may be erected thereon.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1); see *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (quoting the statute). The Act’s operative provisions apply by “locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986) (citing *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365). Neither *Rodrigue* nor any other pre-1970 decision suggests that § 1333(b) abandons that principle. Indeed, *Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co.*, 375 U.S. 34, 35 (1963), treated OCSLA coverage as contingent on the worker’s location on a drilling barge on the shelf—not on a causal connection to shelf operations.

This Court has long recognized that the LHWCA’s situs requirement is strict. In *Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford R.R.*, 281 U.S. 128, 133 (1930), the Court held that “the injury occurring upon the navigable waters” is a prerequisite. Similarly, *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251, 255 (1940), required the injury to occur “upon the navigable waters.” And *Pennsylvania R.R. v. O’Rourke*, 344 U.S. 334, 336 (1953), reaffirmed that LHWCA coverage depends on the injury’s location. Congress incorporated that same geographic limitation into § 1333(b). See *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 423 (1985) (noting the “explicit geographic limitation” in OCSLA’s incorporation of the LHWCA).

**III. The Purpose of OCSLA Supports the Situs-of-Injury Test**

Congress enacted OCSLA to fill a gap in workers’ compensation coverage for employees working on the shelf—a place where state law could not apply. *United States v. Louisiana*, 363 U.S. 1, 35 (1960) (describing the shelf as beyond state boundaries). But Congress did not intend to federalize workers’ compensation for every onshore injury with a tenuous connection to offshore operations. The Third Circuit’s “but-for” test and the Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test would extend OCSLA coverage inland indefinitely, displacing state compensation regimes that properly govern onshore injuries like Mr. Valladolid’s death at an onshore processing facility.

Even under the LHWCA’s maritime-employment requirement, this Court has refused to stretch the statute beyond its geographic bounds. In *Northern Coal & Dock Co. v. Strand*, 278 U.S. 142, 145 (1928), the Court held that “the rights of the parties must be ascertained upon a consideration of the maritime law,” but only because the injury occurred on a vessel in navigable waters. Onshore work—even work connected to maritime commerce—does not bring an injury onto navigable waters. *Hahn v. Ross Island Sand & Gravel Co.*, 358 U.S. 272, 273 (1959) (applying the “twilight zone” only when the injury site is ambiguous). Here, the site is unambiguous: an onshore oil-processing facility. The OCSLA does not cover it.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Test Is Inconsistent with Precedent and Sound Administration**

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test lacks any limiting principle. It invites litigation over the degree of causal connection in every onshore accident, contrary to the bright-line rule this Court has applied under the LHWCA. *Nacirema*, 396 U.S. at 214. Only a situs-of-injury requirement provides the certainty Congress intended. The judgment should be reversed.
