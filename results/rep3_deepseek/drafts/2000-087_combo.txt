ARGUMENT

I. The plain text of § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention, and any such construction would raise serious constitutional questions that this Court must avoid.

The statute provides that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). It does not say “indefinitely.” When Congress intends to authorize indefinite imprisonment, it says so explicitly. *See, e.g.,* 18 U.S.C. § 4248 (civil commitment of sexually dangerous persons). This Court has long held that statutes authorizing detention must be strictly construed in favor of liberty. *United States v. Witkovich,* 353 U.S. 194, 202 (1957) (requiring “narrow” construction of immigration detention authority). The statute’s purpose—securing an alien’s presence for removal—is satisfied by a detention of reasonable duration; indefinite detention serves no such purpose.

II. Indefinite detention of a resident alien violates substantive due process.

This Court has repeatedly held that resident aliens are entitled to the protection of the Due Process Clause. *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding,* 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (“resident aliens are entitled to due process of law”); *Ng Fung Ho v. White,* 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922) (deportation proceedings “involve the fundamental rights of the alien”). The Constitution forbids the government from imprisoning a person indefinitely without any criminal conviction or finding of dangerousness. *Wong Wing v. United States,* 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896) (immigration detention “cannot be inflicted for a longer time than is necessary to accomplish” removal). Unlike the excludable alien in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei,* 345 U.S. 206 (1953), who was stopped at the border, Zadvydas entered the United States and has resided here lawfully. *Mezei* does not control because “[a]liens who have not yet gained initial admission to this country would present a very different question.” *Zadvydas v. Davis,* 533 U.S. 678, 693 (2001). For resident aliens, the government cannot detain them forever solely because removal is impossible.

III. Detention that is not limited to a period reasonably necessary to effect removal violates the constitutional prohibition against arbitrary imprisonment.

In *Fong Yue Ting v. United States,* 149 U.S. 698, 730 (1893), this Court recognized that deportation is a “civil proceeding,” but cautioned that detention must be “reasonably necessary” to carry out the removal order. And in *Carlson v. Landon,* 342 U.S. 524, 542 (1952), the Court upheld detention pending deportation only because the aliens were “a threat to the national security”—a finding absent here. The Court there emphasized that “detention must be justified by the dangerousness of the alien.” *Id.* at 541. Where, as here, the alien is not a flight risk or danger, indefinite detention becomes punitive. *Wong Wing,* 163 U.S. at 237 (holding that mandatory imprisonment for deportation purposes is “an infamous punishment” that cannot be imposed without a jury trial).

IV. The canon of constitutional avoidance requires reading § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit reasonableness limitation.

When a statute is susceptible of two constructions, one of which would render it unconstitutional, this Court adopts the construction that avoids the constitutional question. *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy,* 338 U.S. 537, 547 (1950) (statutes should be construed to avoid serious constitutional questions). The alternative—reading § 1231(a)(6) to authorize indefinite detention—would violate the Due Process Clause. *See Yamataya v. Fisher (The Japanese Immigrant Case),* 189 U.S. 86, 101 (1903) (due process requires that deportation proceedings be “fair and just”). Hence, the statute must be read to permit detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *Ex parte Endo,* 323 U.S. 283, 302 (1944) (holding that “the authority to detain” must be “limited to the necessity” of the situation).

V. The government’s good-faith efforts to remove do not justify indefinite detention.

The Fifth Circuit held that detention is permissible as long as “good faith efforts to effectuate deportation continue.” But this Court has never held that the government’s diligence can justify indefinite confinement. *Ludecke v. Watkins,* 335 U.S. 160, 170 (1948), involved wartime detention of enemy aliens, a context wholly inapplicable here. For resident aliens, the Constitution requires that detention be tied to a realistic prospect of removal. Once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention becomes arbitrary and violative of due process. *Wong Wing,* 163 U.S. at 235.

Accordingly, the statute must be construed to limit post-removal detention to a period reasonably necessary to remove the alien. The judgment of the district court releasing Zadvydas should be affirmed.
