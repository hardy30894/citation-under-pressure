**ARGUMENT**

**I. Bond Lacks Standing to Challenge the Statute on Tenth Amendment Grounds**

The United States respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. As the Supreme Court held in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S.Ct. 366, 83 L.Ed. 543 (1939), private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims absent the states or their officers. The Court stated that "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." *Id.* at 144. Bond is a private individual, not a state, and her grievance is generalized rather than concrete. Therefore, this Court should affirm the Third Circuit’s determination that she lacks standing to raise this claim.

**II. The Statute Is a Valid Exercise of the Treaty Power**

Even if Bond possessed standing, the statute is a valid exercise of federal power. The statute implements the Chemical Weapons Convention, a treaty ratified by the United States. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S.Ct. 382, 64 L.Ed. 641 (1920), the Supreme Court held that the treaty power is an independent power not dependent on the legislative power of the States. The Court stated that the power to make treaties is an independent power. Consequently, Congress may enact legislation necessary to execute this power, even if it intrudes upon areas traditionally regulated by the States.

**III. The Statute Is a Valid Exercise of the Commerce Power**

Furthermore, the statute is a valid exercise of the Commerce Power. In *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 61 S.Ct. 499, 85 L.Ed. 609 (1941), the Court upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act, holding that the power to regulate commerce is plenary. The Court noted that the power to regulate commerce... is plenary. The regulation of chemical weapons affects interstate commerce, and Congress has the authority to prohibit the use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes.

**IV. The Statute Is a Valid Exercise of the Necessary and Proper Clause**

The statute is also a valid exercise of the Necessary and Proper Clause. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 4 L.Ed. 579 (1819), the Court established that Congress may pass laws necessary and proper to execute its enumerated powers. The Court instructed that "Let the end be remembered, the means be confined." The statute is a means to enforce the Chemical Weapons Convention.

**V. The Tenth Amendment Does Not Limit the Treaty Power**

The Tenth Amendment does not limit the treaty power. In *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 56 S.Ct. 345, 80 L.Ed. 477 (1936), the Court noted that the Tenth Amendment is but a truism. The treaty power overrides state law.

**VI. The Statute Is Not Vague**

Finally, the statute is not vague. In *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 4 L.Ed. 487 (1824), the Court defined commerce as intercourse. The statute clearly prohibits the use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes, providing fair notice to citizens.

Accordingly, the United States requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit.
