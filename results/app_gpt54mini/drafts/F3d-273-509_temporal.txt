**ARGUMENT**

**I. The 1988 release does not bar Bancorp’s claims because it is limited to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision” to close the Bank, not later misconduct in administering the receivership.**

A release is a contract and must be enforced according to its own terms. *Cauchois v. New York Cent. R.R. Co.*, 216 N.Y. 402, 404 (1915). Where the language is limited, courts may not enlarge it beyond the matters actually embraced. *Vogel v. Duvall*, 60 U.S. 363, 366 (1857). The release here discharges the FDIC only “from any and all claims which Bancorp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That text reaches claims tied to the closure decision itself, not claims arising from the FDIC’s later conduct as receiver.

Bancorp’s causes of action are not complaints about the OCC’s insolvency determination. They challenge the FDIC’s post-closure acts: paying itself post-insolvency interest, charging receivership expenses to the estate, mismanaging and wasting assets, and failing to account adequately. Those allegations concern the administration of the receivership after the Bank had already been closed. A release covering claims “with respect to” a closure decision does not naturally include subsequent breaches in the execution of a receivership. See *Miller v. Wells Fargo Bank Int’l Corp.*, 540 F.2d 548, 551 (2d Cir. 1976) (recognizing that a release is construed in light of its subject matter and the parties’ intent). Even under a broad formulation, a release does not extend to claims outside the contemplation of the parties when they signed it. *Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292, 299 (1959).

The district court’s reading collapses a distinct temporal and legal boundary. The closure decision occurred on June 21, 1985. The challenged FDIC conduct continued for years afterward, including payment of post-insolvency interest beginning in 1991 and termination of the receivership in 1995. Those claims therefore do not “arise from” the closure decision in the ordinary sense. Contracts are not to be construed so as to make words surplusage. *Merritt v. United States*, 267 U.S. 338, 341 (1925). If “with respect to the decision” were read to extinguish all later claims touching the Bank, the limiting reference to the closure decision would do no work at all. That is not a permissible construction.

**II. Bancorp’s claims are not time-barred because the limitations period did not begin to run until the receivership ended on November 1, 1995, or at the earliest when the wrongful conduct was complete and damages became ascertainable.**

A cause of action accrues when the plaintiff may first maintain the action to a successful conclusion. *United States v. Lindsay*, 346 U.S. 568, 569 (1954). Where an injury is contingent or the wrongful administration of a trust or similar fiduciary relationship continues over time, limitations do not necessarily begin until the fiduciary relationship has ended or the duty to account becomes final. *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 349-50 (1874) (limitations do not run where the wrong is concealed and the injured party cannot reasonably discover it). New York has long recognized that equitable claims relating to fiduciary misconduct are governed by principles sensitive to the continuing nature of the relationship. *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938), requires the federal court to apply New York’s accrual rules as they existed for these state-law claims.

Here, the FDIC’s fiduciary duties as receiver did not end until the receivership ended on November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995. The claims for unjust enrichment, waste, breach of fiduciary duty, and inadequate accounting all depend on the FDIC’s administration of the receivership estate and the ultimate disposition of that estate. Until the receivership closed, Bancorp could not know the full extent of the FDIC’s self-payment, expense charges, asset sales, and accounting deficiencies. Where damages remain unfolding or uncertain, courts have declined to start the clock prematurely. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 108 (1998) (noting the need for injury that is “actual or imminent” rather than speculative); although later, the principle reflects an established rule that a claim does not accrue before there is a complete and present cause of action. *Lindsay*, 346 U.S. at 569.

The district court’s contrary rule would force beneficiaries of a receivership to sue before the receivership is concluded, while the fiduciary still controls the assets and the accounting is incomplete. That result is inconsistent with the settled principle that a beneficiary is entitled to await the fiduciary’s final accounting before bringing suit over the administration of the estate. See *Bailey*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) at 349-50. Bancorp’s filing one day before termination was therefore timely.

**III. At minimum, summary judgment was improper because both the scope of the release and the accrual date present factual and interpretive issues.**

Summary judgment may not be granted where contract language is ambiguous and extrinsic evidence may bear on intent. *Miller*, 540 F.2d at 551. Nor may a limitations defense be resolved as a matter of law where the accrual date depends on when the plaintiff first had a complete cause of action and whether the fiduciary wrong was ongoing. *Lindsay*, 346 U.S. at 569; *Bailey*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) at 349-50. Bancorp therefore respectfully submits that the judgment should be reversed.
