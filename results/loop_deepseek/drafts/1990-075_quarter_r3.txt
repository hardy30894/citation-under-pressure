**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle that satisfies the nondelegation doctrine. Congress may obtain assistance from coordinate branches when it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). This Court has upheld delegations “even where Congress has provided negligible standards for the delegatee to follow.” *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 373 (1989). The Temporary Scheduling Provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), easily meets this standard.

**I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Provides an Intelligible Principle Channeling the Attorney General’s Discretion.**

The Act requires the Attorney General to find that temporary scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is more specific than delegations this Court has repeatedly upheld. In *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 693 (1892), this Court sustained a delegation to the President to impose duties when he found that the duties imposed by another country were “reciprocally unequal and unreasonable.” In *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 497 (1904), this Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to fix standards of “purity, quality, and fitness for consumption” for imported tea. If “reciprocally unequal and unreasonable” and “purity, quality, and fitness for consumption” satisfy the intelligible principle test, the stricter “imminent hazard to the public safety” standard a *fortiori* satisfies it.

The Act further confines the Attorney General by mandating consideration of specified factors: “(4) Its history and current pattern of abuse; (5) The scope, duration, and significance of abuse; (6) What, if any, risk there is to the public health.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)–(6). This Court has held that requiring consideration of enumerated factors is a hallmark of a lawful delegation. *See Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 497 (1904) (sustaining delegation where Secretary must fix standards to secure the purity and quality of teas); *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (sustaining delegation where Secretary must act “for the protection of the consumer”).

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is also narrowly limited in duration—one year, with a possible extension of six months. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). This temporal limit is a “defining boundary,” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374, and far from the open-ended delegations this Court has cautioned against. As this Court has long recognized, “Congress cannot delegate legislative power to the President.” *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). But where Congress defines the hazard, prescribes factors, and imposes a sunset, no improper delegation exists.

**II. This Delegation Involves Criminal Sanctions, but the Intelligible Principle Standard Applies.**

The Toubys argue that a stricter test applies because temporary scheduling creates criminal prohibitions. But this Court has never held that delegating authority to define crimes requires greater specificity than delegating economic or regulatory authority. To the contrary, in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), this Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make regulations governing use of national forests, violations of which were criminal, because Congress had “laid down rules to guide the Secretary” and the statute “provided that his action, like that of other officers, must be reasonable.” So too here.

The Temporary Scheduling Provision is far more concrete than the delegation upheld in *Grimaud*. There, the Secretary had “general authority” to “make rules and regulations.” 220 U.S. at 517. Here, the Attorney General must make specific findings of “imminent hazard to the public safety” and consider three enumerated factors. This is not an uncontrolled grant to define crime.

**III. The Attorney General Properly Subdelegated to the DEA.**

The Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to “promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that officers to whom executive power is delegated may subdelegate that power unless Congress expressly prohibits it. *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887) (recognizing implied authority to subdelegate); *Caha v. United States*, 152 U.S. 211, 221 (1894) (upholding subdelegation by the Secretary of the Interior to the Commissioner of the General Land Office). Here, Congress vested the temporary scheduling power in the “Attorney General,” not in a specific subordinate or bureau. The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator, is a routine administrative arrangement.

The Toubys’ challenge would require this Court to hold that every executive officer must personally exercise every power Congress confers. That proposition has no support in precedent. *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557; *Caha*, 152 U.S. at 221. It would paralyze law enforcement.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Temporary Scheduling Provision provides an intelligible principle—imminent hazard to public safety—coupled with specific factors and a fixed duration. This easily satisfies the nondelegation doctrine as applied in *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. at 692; *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. at 497; *Shreveport Grain*, 287 U.S. at 85; and *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 521. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is well within the authority recognized in *Runkle*, 122 U.S. at 557, and *Caha*, 152 U.S. at 221. The judgment below should be affirmed.
