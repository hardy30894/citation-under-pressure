**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison a person without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L.Ed.2d 653 (2001), the Court held that the Immigration and Nationality Act’s post-removal-period detention provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention. Rather, the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas has been detained for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. No country—Germany, Lithuania, or any other—has accepted him. The INS has made no meaningful effort to secure his removal in the foreseeable future. Indeed, the government has conceded that Zadvydas’s Lithuanian citizenship application remains pending, and no definitive action has been taken. Under these circumstances, the continued detention of Zadvydas is not reasonably necessary to secure removal. It is, instead, a form of indefinite, punitive confinement that the Constitution forbids.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that the statute’s purpose is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” 533 U.S. at 2504. When removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the statute’s purpose is no longer served. The Court emphasized that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. This is not a mere procedural formality—it is a constitutional command. The Court rejected the notion that the government may detain an alien indefinitely so long as it claims to be “making good faith efforts” to remove him. Id. at 2505. Such a standard would render the statute’s constitutional limits meaningless.

The Fifth Circuit’s initial opinion, which upheld Zadvydas’s detention based on the government’s “good faith efforts,” was inconsistent with *Zadvydas*. The Court explicitly rejected that standard, noting that it “would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—[which] demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” Id. at 2505. The Court instead established a clear framework: after six months beyond the removal period, an alien may demonstrate that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. The burden then shifts to the government to rebut that showing with evidence of a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Id. As the period of detention grows, the “reasonably foreseeable future” must shrink. Id.

Zadvydas has satisfied his burden. He has been in custody for more than three years beyond the removal period. No country has accepted him. The INS has not presented any evidence that removal is imminent or even likely. The government’s own records show that Zadvydas’s Lithuanian citizenship application is still pending, and no country has agreed to accept him. The district court correctly found that “there is no place to send him,” and that his continued detention “is violative of his constitutional rights to substantive due process.” *Zadvydas v. Caplinger*, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997). That finding is not only supported by the record but is compelled by *Zadvydas*.

The Fifth Circuit’s subsequent reversal, based on the government’s assertion that deportation is not “impossible,” was legally erroneous. The Court in *Zadvydas* did not require proof of impossibility. It required proof of *reasonably foreseeable* removal. The government has not met that burden. The Court’s own observation that “there is no meaningful possibility of doing so has not been clearly established” was not a holding that detention is permissible, but a recognition that the issue must be decided on the facts. 185 F.3d at 291. Here, the facts show precisely the opposite: removal is not reasonably foreseeable.

The Court in *Zadvydas* also rejected the idea that detention is justified simply because the alien is removable. Id. at 2499. The statute applies broadly to aliens removed for “many and various reasons,” including tourist visa violations. Id. The fact that Zadvydas was ordered removed for a criminal conviction does not justify indefinite detention. The Court made clear that the choice is not between imprisonment and “living at large,” but between imprisonment and supervised release. Id. at 2502. The district court’s conditions—bond, reporting, employment, residence, health insurance—are reasonable and appropriate. They ensure Zadvydas’s presence and compliance without the need for incarceration.

The government’s argument that Zadvydas may be returned to custody if removal becomes likely is not a defense to the current detention. The issue is not whether the government may act in the future, but whether it may detain Zadvydas now. The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that “continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute” once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. Id. at 2503. That standard is met here.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court ordering Zadvydas’s release must be affirmed. The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite confinement. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the Constitution demands release—subject to appropriate conditions of supervision. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal was error. The judgment of the district court is correct and must be upheld.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the district court ordering the release of Kestutis Zadvydas, with appropriate conditions of supervision, and that the Court issue such further relief as justice may require.
