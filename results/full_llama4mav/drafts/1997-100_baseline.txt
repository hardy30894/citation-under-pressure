The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969)), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its decisions in Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), provide guidance.

In Bullington, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they share "the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" (Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439). The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding involved a trial-like process, with the prosecution presenting evidence and the jury making a determination based on that evidence. Similarly, in Rumsey, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a capital sentencing proceeding in Arizona, noting that the sentencing hearing "resembled and, indeed, in all relevant respects for double jeopardy purposes was the same as a trial" (Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209).

The same reasoning applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, such as the one in this case. Under California law, a defendant has a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations (Cal. Penal Code § 1025). The prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt (People v. Tenner, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993)), and the defendant has the right to confront witnesses and present evidence (People v. Reed, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224, 228 (1996)). These procedural protections are similar to those in a trial on guilt or innocence, and thus, the Double Jeopardy Clause should apply.

The California Supreme Court's attempt to distinguish Bullington and Rumsey is unpersuasive. The Court's suggestion that the Supreme Court has implied that Bullington does not apply to noncapital cases is based on dicta in Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), and Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer, 474 U.S. 28 (1985). However, these cases do not directly address the issue, and the Supreme Court has not held that Bullington is limited to capital cases.

Furthermore, the California Supreme Court's distinction between statutory and constitutional protections is unfounded. The fact that some procedural protections in a trial on prior conviction allegations are statutory does not preclude the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause. In Bullington, the sentencing proceeding was governed by statute, yet the Court still applied the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Double Jeopardy Clause should apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings like the one in this case, where the defendant is subject to a trial-like process with significant procedural protections. Retrial on the prior conviction allegation would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause, and the Court of Appeal's decision barring retrial should be affirmed.
