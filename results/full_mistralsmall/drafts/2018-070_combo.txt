**ARGUMENT**

I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) does not permit the application of state law unless federal law is silent on the subject.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State . . . are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). This Court has long held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only where federal law is silent. In *Sharpnack v. United States*, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court interpreted a similar assimilation statute—18 U.S.C. § 13—which extended state criminal laws to federal enclaves “to the extent that [they are] not inconsistent with the laws of the United States.” *Id.* at 288. The Court held that state law applied only where federal law was silent, explaining that the statute “does not purport to make state law controlling where federal law speaks.” *Id.* at 294. The same principle governs here. *See also United States v. Little Lake Misere Land Co.*, 412 U.S. 580, 593 (1973) (applying *Sharpnack*’s construction of assimilation statutes to a similar provision in the OCSLA).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this rule in OCSLA cases. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law applies under the OCSLA only where “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” exists in federal law. *Id.* at 1036. The court reasoned that the OCSLA’s assimilation provision “is not a grant of general authority to the states to legislate for the OCS,” but rather “a limited delegation of authority to fill interstices in the federal scheme.” *Id.* This interpretation comports with the OCSLA’s text and purpose. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (explaining that the OCSLA was enacted to define a “body of law applicable” to the OCS, not to cede regulatory authority to the states).

II. The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a comprehensive federal scheme that leaves no gap for state wage and hour laws.

The FLSA is a detailed, comprehensive statute that “establishes minimum wage, overtime pay, recordkeeping, and child labor standards affecting full-time and part-time workers.” 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized the FLSA as a “comprehensive federal statute” that occupies the field of wage and hour regulation. *See, e.g., Brooklyn Sav. Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 704 (1945) (“The [FLSA] is a federal statute of nationwide application and its provisions are controlling on state courts.”); *Wallace v. Lynn*, 332 F.2d 175, 177 (4th Cir. 1964) (holding that the FLSA is “a comprehensive federal statute” that preempts state wage laws). The FLSA’s savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218(a)—explicitly preserves state laws that are “more protective” than the FLSA, but only “to the extent that such laws are not inconsistent with” the FLSA. *Id.* This clause does not authorize the application of state wage and hour laws where federal law already provides a remedy. *See, e.g., Bay Ridge Operating Co. v. Aaron*, 334 U.S. 446, 462 (1948) (holding that the FLSA’s savings clause does not permit state laws to supplement federal wage standards where federal law is comprehensive).

The OCSLA does not alter this analysis. The Fifth Circuit has held that the OCSLA’s assimilation provision does not permit state law to apply where federal law is comprehensive. *See Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036 (holding that state law does not apply under the OCSLA where federal law is “complete and comprehensive”). This rule is consistent with the OCSLA’s purpose of creating a uniform federal regime for the OCS. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355 (explaining that the OCSLA was enacted to define a “body of law applicable” to the OCS, not to cede regulatory authority to the states).

III. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and therefore do not apply on the OCS.

Even if the OCSLA permitted the application of state law where federal law is silent, California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA. The FLSA’s savings clause preserves state laws that are “more protective” than the FLSA, but only “to the extent that such laws are not inconsistent with” the FLSA. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). California’s meal period requirement conflicts with the FLSA’s structure, which does not mandate meal periods but instead regulates overtime and minimum wage. *See, e.g., Mitchell v. Vollmer*, 349 U.S. 427, 430 (1955) (holding that the FLSA’s overtime provisions are the exclusive remedy for wage disputes). The FLSA’s comprehensive scheme preempts state laws that impose additional obligations on employers. *See Brooklyn Sav. Bank*, 324 U.S. at 704 (holding that the FLSA preempts state wage laws).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedent and the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of the OCSLA. The OCSLA does not permit the application of state law where federal law is comprehensive, and the FLSA is a comprehensive federal scheme that leaves no gap for state wage and hour laws. The judgment below should be reversed.
