## ARGUMENT

### I. The Convictions Under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) Cannot Stand Because Mrs. Parker's Forgeries Were Not Acts "in Violation of Official Duty" Within the Meaning the Statute Has Long Been Given.

Section 201(b)(2)(C) punishes a public official who is induced to "do or omit to do any act in violation of the lawful duty of such official." The word "duty" is not a blank check; it has always referred to something the official actually possessed, whether by written rule or by "established usage." *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). In *Birdsall*, this Court sustained a bribery conviction of Interior Department officials only after finding that the officials' recommendations on land-fraud matters, though "not completely defined by written rules," were nonetheless "established by settled practice," so that "action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the . . . statutes against bribery." *Id.* The touchstone the Court applied was not the breadth of the word "act," but the existence, by rule or by settled institutional practice, of a genuine duty that the official was empowered to perform and then betrayed.

Mrs. Parker had no such duty, defined or undefined. She was a clerk. Her recognized functions—assisting at hearings, taking notes, typing and mailing the ALJ's decisions—did not include, by rule, regulation, or "settled practice," the power to approve, deny, or reinstate benefits, much less to forge the signature of an Administrative Law Judge. Unlike the Interior Department officials in *Birdsall*, whose informal but real influence over land patents was the very "usage" that made their recommendations part of the machinery of government, Mrs. Parker's conduct was not an abuse of any function she was ever asked or allowed to perform. It was the wholesale invention of authority that never existed. A statute condemning betrayal of duty cannot fairly be read to condemn the fabrication of a duty out of whole cloth.

That is precisely the distinction penal statutes must respect. "The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself." *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). Where, as here, the government's theory requires stretching "official duty" to cover conduct outside any duty ever assigned, this Court's longstanding rule of lenity requires the opposite result: "when Congress leaves to the Judiciary the task of imputing to Congress an undeclared will, the ambiguity should be resolved in favor of lenity." *United States v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, 344 U.S. 218, 221–22 (1952). The government cannot invoke section 201(b)(2)(C) to reach conduct that a clerk of ordinary understanding would never have known fell within the "duty" the statute protects, for "it is reasonable that a fair warning should be given to the world in language that the common world will understand, of what the law intends to do if a certain line is passed." *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931).

The court below erred by treating *Birdsall*'s recognition that duties may arise from "settled practice" as license to dispense with any duty requirement at all. *Birdsall* enlarged the *source* of duty—rule or usage—but never abandoned the *requirement* of duty. Mrs. Parker's fabricated letters bearing a forged judicial signature find no analogue in *Birdsall*'s recognition of informal administrative practice; they are, instead, precisely the kind of "act[ion] . . . beyond the ordinary reach" of the statute that criminal statutes must be construed to exclude absent clear congressional intent to reach it. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 96 (1945) (plurality opinion) (courts should not extend criminal statutes "unless the language compels it," so as to avoid punishing conduct Congress never contemplated).

### II. Because the Substantive Bribery Counts Cannot Stand, the Conspiracy Conviction Premised on Those Same Counts Must Also Fall.

Both Mr. and Mrs. Parker were convicted of conspiracy to commit public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 371, the object offense of which was the very conduct described above. A conspiracy conviction cannot survive the collapse of its charged unlawful object. As this Court has explained, the essence of conspiracy is the agreement to accomplish an unlawful object; where the object act is not unlawful, the agreement to commit it cannot be punished as conspiracy to violate federal law. *United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205, 210–11 (1940) (conspiracy requires that the object of the agreement be criminal, not merely that the parties act in concert). Because Mrs. Parker's charged conduct did not violate section 201(b)(2)(C) for the reasons stated above, the conspiracy count resting on that same conduct cannot independently support a conviction for either appellant.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgments of conviction against Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker should be reversed.
