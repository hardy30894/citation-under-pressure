## ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing such a standard, which finds no support in this Court’s precedents and conflates the distinct inquiries of competency and voluntariness.

### I. The Dusky Standard Governs Competency for All Purposes

This Court has long held that the constitutional standard for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). That standard applies at all stages of a criminal proceeding. *See Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171–72 (1975) (noting that the competency standard “has been applied to all stages of a criminal trial”). Nothing in this Court’s decisions suggests that a different, more demanding standard governs competency to plead guilty or waive counsel.

In *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), this Court held that a defendant who is competent to stand trial is also competent to waive the right to counsel, provided the waiver is knowing and intelligent. *Id.* at 835. The Court did not require a separate inquiry into the defendant’s capacity for “reasoned choice.” Rather, it recognized that a competent defendant may choose to proceed without counsel, even if that choice seems unwise. *Id.* at 834. The same logic applies to guilty pleas: a defendant who is competent to stand trial is competent to plead guilty, so long as the plea is voluntary and intelligent. *See Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242–43 (1969) (requiring that guilty pleas be knowing and voluntary, but not imposing a heightened competency standard).

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard—requiring that a defendant have “the capacity for ‘reasoned choice’ among the alternatives available to him,” *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 266 (9th Cir. 1992)—is nowhere found in this Court’s jurisprudence. The *Dusky* standard already demands a *rational* understanding of the proceedings. A defendant who meets that standard is capable of making choices, even if those choices are not objectively reasonable. The Ninth Circuit’s standard improperly conflates competency with the wisdom of the defendant’s decision.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents

This Court has consistently treated competency and voluntariness as distinct inquiries. Competency concerns the defendant’s mental capacity; voluntariness concerns whether the waiver or plea was the product of free choice, free from coercion. *See Faretta*, 422 U.S. at 835 (distinguishing between competency to waive counsel and the knowing and intelligent nature of the waiver). The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard blurs this distinction by requiring that the defendant be capable of making a *reasoned* decision—a requirement that effectively imports a voluntariness inquiry into the competency determination.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s reliance on its own circuit precedent—*Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973); *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479 (9th Cir. 1983); *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853 (9th Cir. 1987)—is misplaced. Those cases created a heightened standard that has never been adopted by this Court. To the contrary, this Court has held that a defendant who is competent to stand trial is competent to waive counsel, *Faretta*, 422 U.S. at 835, and that a guilty plea by a competent defendant is valid if it is knowing and voluntary, *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 242–43. The Ninth Circuit’s standard would require trial courts to conduct a second, more searching competency inquiry whenever a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel—a requirement that finds no support in the Due Process Clause.

### III. The State Court Properly Applied the Correct Standard

The Nevada state court found Moran competent to stand trial based on psychiatric evaluations that concluded he understood the charges and could assist counsel. That finding is entitled to deference under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The Ninth Circuit erred by rejecting that finding and imposing its own “reasoned choice” standard. Because the state court applied the correct *Dusky* standard, its competency determination should have been upheld.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
