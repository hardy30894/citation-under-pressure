The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code (IRC) requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The IRC defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, both a partnership and its individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the IRC. Galletti v. United States (In re Galletti), 298 F.3d 1107, 1111 (9th Cir. 2002).

The government's assessment against the partnership does not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners. The IRC provision governing collection of tax after assessment, 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), presumes that "the taxpayer" against whom a deficiency has been assessed is the same taxpayer for whom the statute of limitations is extended. As the Ninth Circuit noted, the assessment against the partnership "left unaltered the limitations period applicable to" the individual partners. Galletti, 298 F.3d at 1112.

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D.Cal.1959). However, the court also acknowledged that the IRS would have had to either assess the tax against the partner or bring a court action to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960).

In United States v. Coson, the Ninth Circuit affirmed a district court decision holding that the IRS's failure to assess a tax against an individual partner rendered its lien on the partner's property invalid. 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir.1961). Although the Ninth Circuit relied on a different ground, the district court's holding that an assessment against the partnership was insufficient to create a lien on the partner's property is relevant here. Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Other circuits have also recognized that an assessment against a partnership is not sufficient to collect a tax from individual partners. In United States v. Wright, the Seventh Circuit held that the IRS could bring an action against individual partners to collect a tax debt, but only because the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir.1995). The Ninth Circuit correctly distinguished Wright on the grounds that, in this case, the IRS did not assess the individual partners. Galletti, 298 F.3d at 1113.

The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners under state law is also unavailing. While state law may make partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, it does not permit a creditor to collect a debt from a partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Under California law, for example, a creditor must obtain a judgment against a partner before collecting a partnership debt from the partner's assets. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c).

In Stallard v. United States, the Fifth Circuit held that the government was entitled to collect a tax liability from a general partner under Texas law. 12 F.3d 489 (5th Cir.1994). However, this case is distinguishable because the government had obtained a judgment against the partner. Similarly, in United States v. W. Prods., Ltd., the district court allowed the government to collect withholding taxes from a general partner under New York law, but this case also involved an assessment against the partner. 168 F.Supp.2d 84 (S.D.N.Y.2001).

The Bankruptcy Code permits a creditor to make a claim against a debtor's estate if the creditor has a "right to payment" against the debtor. 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). However, the Code also provides that a claim may be disallowed if it is "unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor" under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). In this case, the IRS's claim against the individual partners is unenforceable because the statute of limitations has expired and the IRS has not obtained a judgment against the partners.

In conclusion, the government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing those partners is unsupported by the IRC, case law, and state law. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS's assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax from the individual partners, and that the IRS's claim against the partners is unenforceable under applicable law.
