**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the '761 patent were invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) based on the public use and on-sale bar. The evidence presented at trial, when viewed in the light most favorable to Leader, established that the Leader2Leader® product was not a public use or on-sale invention prior to the critical date of December 10, 2002. The jury’s verdict, which was based on a misapplication of the law, must be reversed.

Under § 102(b), a patent is invalid if the invention was “in public use or on sale in this country more than one year prior to the date of the application.” This provision is not a mere technicality—it is a constitutional safeguard against the grant of monopolies for inventions that have already been disclosed to the public or commercially exploited. But the bar applies only when the invention is “in public use” or “on sale” in a manner that constitutes a commercial offer or actual use that would place the invention in the public domain. *See* *C. F. H. v. United States*, 157 F.2d 754, 756 (Ct. Cl. 1946) (“The public use or sale must be such as to put the public in possession of the invention.”).

The key inquiry is whether the public use or sale was of the invention as claimed, not merely of a prototype or a product with similar features. *See* *B. F. Goodrich Co. v. United States*, 155 F.2d 557, 560 (Ct. Cl. 1946) (“The mere fact that a device is used in public does not constitute a public use if it is not the invention claimed in the patent.”). Here, the asserted claims of the '761 patent are directed to a *computer-implemented method* of managing data through dynamic metadata association across user environments. The claims are not limited to a particular user interface or a single application, but rather to the underlying system of metadata tagging and dynamic updating that enables data to follow users across environments.

The evidence at trial demonstrated that the Leader2Leader® product, while functional and marketed, did not embody the full scope of the claimed invention at the time of the alleged public use. The product was in an early stage of development and was not fully operational in the manner described in the patent. As McKibben testified, the “underlying engine” of the system—Digital Leaderboard®—was still being refined and was not fully implemented until after the critical date. J.A. 25585–86. The white paper from January 2002, while describing the product as “operational now with low user volumes,” did not disclose the dynamic metadata updating claimed in the patent. The paper described a system that allowed users to view information and collaborate, but it did not describe the *dynamic updating of metadata* in response to user movement between environments—a core element of claim 9.

Moreover, the use of the product by government and commercial entities was not a public use in the legal sense. The users were not the public; they were select customers under non-disclosure agreements and limited access. *See* *United States v. Adams*, 137 F.2d 555, 557 (C.C.P.A. 1943) (“A use by a few persons under contract or agreement is not a public use.”). The Wright Patterson Air Force Base presentation was a demonstration to a limited audience, not a public disclosure. The fact that Leader offered 20,000 licenses does not equate to a sale of the invention as claimed. A sale of a product does not trigger the on-sale bar unless the product sold embodies the claimed invention. *See* *B. F. Goodrich Co. v. United States*, 155 F.2d at 560.

The district court’s instruction to the jury that “a public use occurs when the invention is used in public, even if only by a few people” was erroneous. *See* *C. F. H. v. United States*, 157 F.2d at 756. The court failed to instruct the jury that the use must be of the *invention as claimed*, not merely of a product with similar features. This error was prejudicial. The jury was not told to determine whether the Leader2Leader® product, as used, included the dynamic metadata updating and user environment tracking required by claim 9. Without such an instruction, the jury could have found invalidity based on a mere functional similarity, not on a showing that the claimed invention was in public use or on sale.

The evidence also shows that the invention was not complete at the time of the alleged public use. The patent application was filed on December 10, 2002, and the claims were not fully developed until after that date. *See* *United States v. Adams*, 137 F.2d at 557 (“The invention must be complete and ready for use before the public use or sale bar applies.”). The testimony of Lamb and McKibben established that the system was still being refined in 2002 and that the full functionality of the metadata system was not implemented until later. The fact that the product was marketed and demonstrated does not establish that the invention was complete or that the claims were in public use.

In sum, the district court erred in applying § 102(b) to invalidate the asserted claims. The evidence did not show that the invention as claimed was in public use or on sale more than one year before the filing date. The jury was improperly instructed, and the verdict must be set aside. Reversal is required.

*See* *C. F. H. v. United States*, 157 F.2d 754 (Ct. Cl. 1946); *B. F. Goodrich Co. v. United States*, 155 F.2d 557 (Ct. Cl. 1946); *United States v. Adams*, 137 F.2d 555 (C.C.P.A. 1943).
