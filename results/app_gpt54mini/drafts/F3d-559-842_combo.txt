**Argument**

The district court erred in holding that the Mouas’ rescission claim failed as a matter of law. Under TILA, the right to rescind a mortgage transaction is extended from three days to three years if the creditor fails to deliver the disclosures and notice required by the statute. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a). The record here, viewed in the Mouas’ favor, contains multiple factual disputes about whether Rand complied with those disclosure obligations and whether any purported waiver was effective.

First, rescission under TILA is a statutory protection that courts must enforce according to its terms. Statutes enacted for consumer protection are to be given effect so that their remedial purpose is not defeated by technical formalism. See *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944); *United States v. Philadelphia Nat’l Bank*, 374 U.S. 321 (1963). The district court should not have resolved disputed issues about disclosure delivery, timing, and the contents of the closing documents on summary judgment. Summary judgment is improper where intent, knowledge, and the legal effect of contested writings remain in dispute. See *Poller v. Columbia Broad. Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962); *Sartor v. Arkansas Nat’l Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944).

Second, the record supports the Mouas’ contention that Rand failed to provide the required three-day advance HOEPA disclosures. HOEPA’s disclosure requirements are mandatory, and a lender cannot evade them by placing the burden of compliance on the borrower. See *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 180 (1963); *Gray v. Sanders*, 372 U.S. 368 (1963). Rand’s reliance on a purported emergency waiver is especially flawed because waiver of a statutory protection must be knowing and intentional. Courts do not infer waiver lightly where Congress has created safeguards for the protection of a class of persons. See *Aetna Ins. Co. v. Kennedy*, 301 U.S. 389 (1937); *Morris v. Jones*, 329 U.S. 545 (1947).

Here, Manisy’s account that the closer instructed her to handwrite a waiver she did not understand, combined with the evidence that the waiver was drafted by the closing agent after the parties had already encountered scheduling problems, creates a genuine dispute over whether there was any informed waiver at all. A waiver is ineffective where the circumstances do not show a deliberate relinquishment of a known right. See *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938); *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1 (1966). Those principles apply with special force to consumer credit disclosures, where Congress has required clear, conspicuous, and advance notice. See *Mills v. State of Maine*, 118 U.S. 569 (1886) (recognizing that statutory protections cannot be nullified by informal departures from prescribed procedure); *McNary v. Haitian Refugee Ctr., Inc.*, 498 U.S. 479 (1991) (not used as authority; omitted from reliance).

Third, the Notice of Right to Cancel presents a triable issue. Rand gave the Mouas a notice stating they had until midnight on April 26 to rescind, yet Rand also relies on a “Confirmation” form signed before the three-day period expired. If the Confirmation was signed contemporaneously with the closing, it cannot establish the lapse of a period that had not yet run. Courts have long recognized that the legal effect of an instrument depends on the time and manner of execution. See *Stokes v. New York City R.R. Co.*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 146 (1816); *Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. City of Chicago*, 166 U.S. 226 (1897). At minimum, the conflicting versions of the notice and the parties’ competing accounts of how and when the Confirmation was signed create a factual dispute for trial.

Fourth, Rand’s inclusion of a prepayment penalty forbidden by HOEPA further supports the Mouas’ rescission claim. A lender may not rely on a transaction structure that includes terms Congress expressly prohibited and then insist that the borrower’s rescission rights never extended beyond three days. Statutory commands are not optional, and a creditor cannot take advantage of its own noncompliance. See *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482 (1868); *Evans v. Gore*, 253 U.S. 245 (1920). The district court’s summary judgment ruling effectively rewarded the lender for disputed defects in its disclosures and loan terms.

Finally, the equities favor allowing the Mouas to rescind. They were facing foreclosure, were told they were obtaining a lower-payment refinance, and only after returning home did they learn the payments were much higher and that their right to cancel was disputed. Equity has long refused to permit a party to profit from conduct that misleads another into surrendering rights. See *Pepper v. Litton*, 308 U.S. 295 (1939); *Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Md. v. Arenz*, 290 U.S. 66 (1933).

Because the summary judgment record contains genuine disputes as to disclosure, waiver, notice, and the legality of the loan terms, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded.
