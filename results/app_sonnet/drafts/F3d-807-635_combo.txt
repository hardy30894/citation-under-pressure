**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Judgment Is Final and Reviewable, and This Court Applies a De Novo Standard Drawing All Inferences in DISH’s Favor.**

The December 11 judgment is final and properly before this Court. A “final decision is generally one which ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.” *Catlin v. United States*, 324 U.S. 229, 233 (1945). Once the parties dismissed the remaining eight claims while expressly preserving DISH’s right to appeal the partial summary judgment, nothing remained but entry of judgment on the seven calls at issue. This Court reviews summary judgment de novo, and “the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts . . . must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion.” *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). DISH opposed judgment below, so all inferences must run in its favor.

**II. Summary Judgment for Ybarra on Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5 Was Error Because the Record Does Not Show, Without Genuine Dispute, That a Prerecorded Voice Message Was Ever Communicated.**

Summary judgment must be used with caution wherever the record permits more than one rational conclusion. “[S]ummary procedures should be used sparingly” where the facts and their significance remain contested. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). A court may not resolve a disputed factual inference in favor of the moving party simply because the opposing party has not offered directly contradictory testimony; credibility and inference-drawing remain for the trier of fact. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944).

DISH’s own call log establishes that four of the seven calls never resulted in delivery of a voice message to Ybarra. Calls 2, 3, and 4 registered as “Answering Machine,” and call 5 registered “Data Error–No Value” — meaning no connection capable of carrying a prerecorded voice was completed. The district court nevertheless treated all seven calls identically, reasoning only that DISH “admitted to using a prerecorded voice in calls from the 8047 number” and that liability attached “regardless of whether Ybarra triggered it with a positive voice.” That reasoning conflates a dialer’s *capacity* to play a message with the actual, completed *communication* of that message — a distinction the record leaves genuinely disputed as to calls 2 through 5. Because a rational factfinder could conclude that no prerecorded voice reached Ybarra on those calls, judgment as a matter of law for Ybarra on them was error. *Diebold*, 369 U.S. at 655; *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627.

**III. Any Doubt About the Scope of a Statutory-Damages Provision Should Be Resolved Against Extending Liability Beyond a Completed, Communicated Call.**

The TCPA’s automatic per-call damages provision must be measured by what the statute says, not by an expansive gloss on its terms. “It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed,” and where that language is plain, “the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The statute condemns a “call” made “using” a prerecorded voice — not merely a signal dispatched by a dialer that may or may not ripen into a received message. “There is . . . no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

Longstanding principle counsels restraint before extending a quasi-penal, automatic-damages provision beyond its plain terms. “The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself.” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). Where “choice has to be made between two readings of what conduct” a statute has singled out for sanction, it is appropriate, before choosing the harsher reading, “to require that Congress should have spoken in language that is clear and definite.” *United States v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, 344 U.S. 218, 221–22 (1952). Applying that restraint here, calls that produced no positive human connection and no completed voicemail delivery — calls 2 through 5 — cannot support the statutory damages the district court imposed.

**CONCLUSION**

DISH does not dispute that summary judgment for Ybarra was warranted on calls 1, 6, and 7. But the partial summary judgment as to calls 2, 3, 4, and 5 rested on an undifferentiated theory that ignored genuine, material disputes over whether a prerecorded voice message was ever communicated on those calls. This Court should reverse the judgment as to those four calls and remand for entry of judgment for DISH, or for further proceedings, consistent with this opinion.
