**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty without requiring the jury to find aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 592, 122 S.Ct. 2428, 153 L.Ed.2d 556 (2002), held that “the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find each fact necessary to impose a sentence of death.” But *Ring* did not mandate that juries unanimously find aggravating circumstances or that they determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. Rather, it held only that “any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.” Id. at 592, 122 S.Ct. 2428. Florida’s scheme satisfies this constitutional threshold.

**I. Florida’s Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Sixth Amendment as Clarified in *Ring***

*Ring* did not require a unanimous jury verdict on aggravating factors. The Court in *Ring* emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee “is not violated by a sentencing scheme that allows a judge to make the ultimate decision on the imposition of the death penalty so long as the jury has made a recommendation based on findings of aggravating circumstances.” Id. at 609, 122 S.Ct. 2428. The Court explicitly rejected the notion that “the jury’s recommendation must be unanimous.” Id. at 609 n.13. The Court reasoned that “the jury’s role in the sentencing process is to make a recommendation,” and that “the judge’s role is to make the final decision.” Id. at 609. This framework is consistent with Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death, followed by a judicial sentencing decision based on the jury’s findings.

This interpretation is further supported by *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 355, 136 S.Ct. 616, 193 L.Ed.2d 504 (2016), in which the United States Supreme Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because the jury did not find the aggravating circumstances. The Court did not hold that the jury must be unanimous in its recommendation. Rather, the Court held that “the Sixth Amendment requires that the jury, not the judge, find each fact necessary to impose the death penalty.” Id. at 362, 136 S.Ct. 616. The Court concluded that Florida’s scheme failed because the jury did not make the factual findings required by *Ring*—not because the jury’s recommendation was non-unanimous. Thus, *Hurst* confirms that the Sixth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in death penalty sentencing.

Moreover, the Court in *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490, 120 S.Ct. 2348, 147 L.Ed.2d 435 (2000), made clear that the jury trial guarantee applies only to facts that increase the penalty beyond the statutory maximum. The death penalty is the maximum penalty for first-degree murder under Florida law, and the jury’s recommendation of death is not a finding of an “element” of the crime but a recommendation on sentencing. See *Hurst*, 577 U.S. at 362, 136 S.Ct. 616. Therefore, the jury’s role is advisory, and the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimity in that advisory role.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Unanimity or a Determination of Mental Retardation by the Jury**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment does not require that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances or determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242, 153 L.Ed.2d 335 (2002), the Court held that executing mentally retarded individuals violates the Eighth Amendment. But the Court left to the states the “manner in which this constitutional restriction on execution of its sentences will be enforced.” Id. at 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242. Florida’s three-pronged test for mental retardation—requiring a full-scale IQ of 70 or below, adaptive deficits, and onset before age 18—is consistent with *Atkins* and is applied by the trial court, not the jury. The Court has never held that the jury must determine mental retardation as a bar to execution.

In *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994, 111 S.Ct. 2680, 115 L.Ed.2d 836 (1991), the Court reaffirmed that “death is different” and that “the Eighth Amendment demands heightened reliability in the determination whether the death penalty is appropriate.” But the Court did not require jury unanimity or jury findings on mental retardation. Rather, the Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not require that a State’s capital sentencing scheme be perfect, only that it be constitutional.” Id. at 994, 111 S.Ct. 2680. Florida’s scheme, which includes a detailed statutory framework, judicial review, and a requirement that aggravating circumstances be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, satisfies this standard.

The dissent’s concern about Florida’s “outlier status” is misplaced. In *Woodward v.
