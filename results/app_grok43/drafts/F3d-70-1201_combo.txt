The district court erred in directing the jury to disregard evidence of subsequent design changes that had been properly admitted for impeachment. At common law, proof of post-accident alterations was admissible when offered to contradict a defendant's claim that the original design remained unchanged and safe. Columbia & Puget Sound R.R. Co. v. Hawthorne, 144 U.S. 202 (1892); Morse v. Minneapolis & St. Louis Ry. Co., 30 Minn. 365, 15 N.W. 104 (1883); Hodges v. Percival, 132 Ill. 82, 23 N.E. 423 (1890); Terre Haute & Indianapolis R.R. Co. v. Clem, 123 Ind. 15, 23 N.E. 965 (1890); Shinners v. Proprietors of Locks & Canals, 154 Mass. 168, 28 N.E. 10 (1891); Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Pearson, 97 Ala. 211, 12 So. 176 (1892).

Morbark's opening statement and cross-examination of Infinger asserted that the seventeen-inch chute remained in daily use by the City of DeFuniak Springs and other agencies, thereby placing the absence of change in issue. Once a party opens the door by such representations, opposing counsel may introduce rebuttal evidence of subsequent modifications. Pennsylvania Co. v. Roy, 102 U.S. 451 (1880); Hopt v. Utah, 120 U.S. 430 (1887). The trial court correctly overruled the objection during Infinger's testimony, recognizing that Morbark had forfeited the benefit of the pretrial exclusion.

The subsequent instruction to disregard that testimony was therefore erroneous. An admonition that withdraws properly received impeachment evidence deprives the jury of relevant proof and constitutes prejudicial error requiring a new trial. Minneapolis, St. Paul & Sault Ste. Marie Ry. Co. v. Moquin, 283 U.S. 520 (1931); United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 310 U.S. 150 (1940). Because the verdict may have rested on the false impression that the original design had never been altered, the denial of Wood's motion for a new trial cannot stand.
