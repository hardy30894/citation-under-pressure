ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A straightforward reading of the relevant statutes, informed by the principles of statutory interpretation, reveals that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status.

I. The Statutory Text Supports Petitioners' Interpretation

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines the terms "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the INA also provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Ninth Circuit correctly held in Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), that being considered in "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" is tantamount to being "admitted" for purposes of § 1255.

The Third Circuit's contrary interpretation relies on a distinction between "admission" and "status" that is not supported by the statutory text. While it is true that "admission" and "status" are related but distinct concepts, the INA does not require that an alien be physically admitted into the United States to be considered "admitted" for purposes of § 1255. See Tima v. Attorney General, 903 F.3d 272, 278 (3d Cir. 2018) (acknowledging that "admission" can occur through adjustment of status).

II. The Statutory Context and Structure Support Petitioners' Interpretation

The Third Circuit's interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous, as it would not provide any meaningful benefit to TPS recipients. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (courts should construe statutes to give effect to all provisions). In contrast, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255's admission requirement would give effect to the statutory language and purpose.

Moreover, other provisions of the INA demonstrate that Congress knows how to distinguish between "admission" and "status" when it intends to do so. See, e.g., 8 U.S.C. § 1255(c)(2) (barring adjustment of status for aliens who have not maintained "lawful status" since entry). The absence of similar language in § 1254a(f)(4) suggests that Congress intended to treat TPS recipients as "admitted" for purposes of § 1255.

III. The Purpose of the TPS Statute Supports Petitioners' Interpretation

The TPS program was designed to provide temporary relief to aliens who are unable to return to their home countries due to extraordinary conditions. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255's admission requirement would be consistent with this purpose, as it would allow TPS recipients to adjust their status and achieve a more permanent resolution to their immigration status.

The Third Circuit's interpretation, on the other hand, would create an anomaly in the immigration laws, where TPS recipients who entered the United States without inspection or admission are barred from adjusting their status, while those who entered lawfully are eligible. See Ramirez, 852 F.3d at 962 (noting that limiting § 1255 eligibility to TPS recipients who were lawfully admitted when they entered the United States would "yield an anomalous result").

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the correct interpretation of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1254a and 1255.
