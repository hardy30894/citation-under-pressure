The question presented should be answered yes. Where the government seeks to convict a defendant of conspiracy within the limitations period, and the defendant has produced evidence of withdrawal before that period, due process requires the government to prove the defendant’s participation during the actionable time frame beyond a reasonable doubt. The burden is not a mere production burden; it is the ultimate burden of persuasion. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Because conspiracy is a continuing offense, the government must prove that the defendant remained a member during the period charged or limitations is not satisfied. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401-02 (1912).

This Court has long recognized that withdrawal is not presumed from the mere passage of time or dissociation in fact; rather, it is an affirmative event that must be established by proof. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910). But once the defendant comes forward with evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, the government must still prove the continuing offense element that keeps the prosecution alive. In Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957), the Court explained that the statute of limitations “is designed to limit exposure to criminal prosecution to a certain fixed period of time.” Grunewald thus confirms that continued membership within the limitations period is a fact the prosecution must establish to avoid the bar.

The government’s contrary position would improperly convert limitations into a defense the accused must negate. That is inconsistent with the structure of the criminal law and with the constitutional allocation of proof. In Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 274-75 (1952), the Court held that the prosecution bears the burden of proving every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. And in Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469 (1943), the Court rejected statutory presumptions that relieve the government of proving the facts that establish guilt. A defendant’s evidence of withdrawal is not an admission of ongoing guilt; it is evidence that the government must overcome if it wishes to place the defendant inside the limitations period. See Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 334-35 (1957) (recognizing that a conspirator who withdraws “ceases to be a party to it”). The same principle is reflected in United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532, 542-43 (1947), which emphasizes that conspiracy liability does not continue indefinitely after a defendant’s active participation ends.

This Court should therefore hold that, once the accused offers evidence of withdrawal before the limitations window, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a conspirator during the relevant period. That rule follows from Winship, from the continuing-offense nature of conspiracy recognized in Hyde, Kissel, and Grunewald, and from the settled principle that the prosecution must prove every fact essential to criminal liability. The judgment below should be reversed.
