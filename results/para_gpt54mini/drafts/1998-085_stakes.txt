**ARGUMENT**

**The court of appeals correctly held that Title VII punitive damages do not require proof that the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous,” beyond the statutory showing of malice or reckless indifference.**

Title VII’s punitive-damages provision permits such damages when the plaintiff proves that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Congress chose those words deliberately. Nothing in the text adds a separate requirement that the defendant’s conduct also be “egregious” or “outrageous” in some free-standing sense. When Congress supplies the governing standard, courts should not graft on an extra element. See *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994); *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993).

This Court’s punitive-damages jurisprudence confirms that the relevant inquiry is the defendant’s state of mind. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded upon a showing of “reckless or callous indifference” to federally protected rights. Id. at 56. The Court rejected any rule requiring conduct that is independently outrageous in all cases. Rather, punitive damages turn on whether the defendant acted with the requisite culpable mental state. See id. at 48-56. Congress used substantially similar language in § 1981a(b)(1), and the court of appeals correctly followed that statutory choice.

The text and structure of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 reinforce that conclusion. Congress enacted § 1981a to provide “additional remedies” for intentional discrimination. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 252. It also imposed a graduated cap on damages based on employer size, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), a safeguard that would be unnecessary if punitive damages were already limited to some undefined class of “extraordinarily egregious” cases. The better reading is that Congress set the standard in the statute itself—malice or reckless indifference—and then separately cabined the remedy through the statutory caps. See *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 252-55.

Petitioner’s contrary position would improperly collapse liability and punishment. This Court has repeatedly recognized that a plaintiff may prove intentional discrimination by showing that the employer’s stated reasons were pretextual, and the factfinder may infer discrimination from that showing. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr.*, 509 U.S. at 511; *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 147-49 (2000). But punitive damages require an additional inquiry into the employer’s state of mind with respect to federally protected rights. The court of appeals preserved that distinction. It did not treat every Title VII violation as punishable; it held only that punitive damages are available where the plaintiff proves the statutory mental state, without an independent “egregiousness” overlay.

That approach is also consistent with common-law punitive-damages principles. Punitive damages have long served to punish and deter conduct undertaken with conscious disregard of another’s rights. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 54-56. Courts do not ordinarily require a separate, free-floating finding that conduct was “outrageous” when the governing statute already specifies the culpable mental state. See *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 527 U.S. 526, 535-46 (1999). The Court in *Kolstad* recognized that “malice” and “reckless indifference” refer to the employer’s knowledge that it may be acting in violation of federal law. Id. at 536-40. That is the standard Congress adopted.

Nor does the legislative history support petitioner’s extra-textual gloss. The court of appeals correctly observed that the materials on which petitioner relies are conflicting and do not justify overriding the enacted text. Where Congress has spoken in the statute, isolated snippets of legislative history cannot add a requirement that the statute itself omits. See *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 263; *Kolstad*, 527 U.S. at 536-46.

Finally, petitioner’s rule would frustrate Title VII’s remedial purpose. Congress added punitive damages to deter intentional discrimination, especially where employers act with conscious disregard of employees’ federally protected rights. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 252-55. Imposing a separate “egregiousness” threshold would narrow that remedy beyond the statute’s text and undercut deterrence precisely in cases where discriminatory intent is proved and punishment is warranted.

The judgment should be affirmed.
