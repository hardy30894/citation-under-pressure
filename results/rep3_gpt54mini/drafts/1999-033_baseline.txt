**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not create a general federal tort remedy for every allegedly arbitrary state decision. It protects against intentional discrimination, and the Court has long required that a plaintiff plead that government treated him differently because of membership in an identifiable class or because the challenged classification burdened a protected interest. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239-42 (1976); *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 274-80 (1979). The court of appeals erased that settled principle by allowing respondent to proceed as a “class of one” based solely on an assertedly irrational, spiteful demand for a larger easement. That ruling should be reversed.

1. **Equal protection doctrine requires a cognizable basis for comparison, not merely alleged unfairness.**  
The Clause is violated when the State intentionally treats similarly situated persons differently without a constitutionally sufficient justification. *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439-47 (1985); *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265-66 (1977). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the Constitution does not require things which are different in fact or opinion to be treated in law as though they were the same.” *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940). Respondent’s complaint, by contrast, alleges only that the Village asked her for a 33-foot easement when it allegedly asked others for 15 feet. That is a bare claim of differential treatment, but not a constitutional violation absent some recognized doctrinal hook.

2. **This Court’s “class of one” cases do not support respondent’s theory.**  
The only decision on which respondent relies is *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000) (per curiam). There, the Court stated that respondent had “alleged that she has been intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that there is no rational basis for the difference in treatment.” *Id.* at 564. But the Court’s holding was narrowly framed around a pleaded comparison to “others similarly situated.” *Id.* at 564. The Court did not hold that any disappointed property owner may convert a discretionary municipal decision into a constitutional claim by alleging spite. To the contrary, the Court’s equal protection cases require an “extremely high degree of similarity” in the comparators when no protected class is involved. *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1, 10 (1992); *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 602-04 (2008).

3. **The court of appeals’ spite-based rule is inconsistent with the rational-basis cases.**  
Under rational-basis review, a law or decision will be sustained if any reasonably conceivable state of facts could provide a rational basis for it. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313-15 (1993); *Heller v. Doe*, 509 U.S. 312, 320-21 (1993). The court below replaced that deferential inquiry with a search for improper motive, holding that equal protection is violated if state action was motivated by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’” the plaintiff. But this Court has rejected constitutional claims that turn on subjective motivation untethered to a suspect classification or a protected interest. *Washington*, 426 U.S. at 239-42; *Personnel Administrator*, 442 U.S. at 274-80. If municipal land-use or utility decisions may be challenged whenever a plaintiff can allege ill will, federal courts will be turned into super zoning boards.

4. **The Due Process Clause, not Equal Protection, is the proper limit on arbitrary government action.**  
This Court has cautioned that constitutional review of governmental arbitrariness proceeds through the specific constitutional provision that fits the claim, not a free-floating guarantee of fairness. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 840-44 (1998). Where the complaint is simply that officials acted unfairly or vindictively in an ordinary property dispute, substantive due process, not equal protection, is the more natural doctrinal fit. *Id.* at 846-47. Respondent, however, did not plead a protected-class discrimination claim, and her effort to recast a local easement dispute as equal protection should be rejected.

The judgment below should be reversed. Respondent’s allegations of a larger easement demand, standing alone, do not state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. At minimum, a plaintiff must plead intentional differential treatment of similarly situated comparators without a rational basis. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564; *Nordlinger*, 505 U.S. at 10. Because respondent did not allege membership in any class or group, and because the court of appeals’ spite-based rule conflicts with this Court’s equal-protection precedents, the complaint was properly dismissed.
