ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because a Reasonable Jury Could Find That Ottobre’s Conduct Constituted Harassment Because of Sex.

Title VII prohibits an employer from discriminating against an individual “because of such individual’s . . . sex.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The same prohibition appears in the Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act. Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2102(1). Same-sex harassment is actionable when the plaintiff demonstrates that the conduct occurred because of sex. Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 79-80 (1998). One recognized route is proof that the harasser acted from sexual desire. Id. at 80. Another is evidence that the harassment was motivated by the victim’s failure to conform to gender stereotypes. Smith v. City of Salem, 378 F.3d 566, 574-75 (6th Cir. 2004).

Wasek presented evidence sufficient to reach a jury on both theories. Ottobre repeatedly grabbed Wasek’s buttocks, poked him with tools, and declared that Wasek had “a pretty mouth” and “pretty lips.” These acts and statements, coupled with Wasek’s testimony that Ottobre was bisexual and directed the conduct exclusively at him, permit the inference that Ottobre was motivated by sexual desire. See Oncale, 523 U.S. at 80. The same evidence supports a finding that Ottobre targeted Wasek for perceived nonconformity with masculine norms. See Smith, 378 F.3d at 575.

The district court’s conclusion that no gender-based motivation existed therefore cannot be sustained. When the record contains evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer that the harassment occurred because of sex, summary judgment is improper. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).

II. Arrow Energy Is Not Entitled to Summary Judgment on Its Affirmative Defense.

An employer may avoid liability for a supervisor’s harassment only if it proves both that it exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct harassing behavior and that the employee unreasonably failed to take advantage of corrective opportunities. Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 807 (1998); Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998). Wasek reported Ottobre’s conduct to his immediate supervisor, Tripp, who threatened retaliation and took no action. When Wasek escalated the complaint to Smillie and Strong, both responded by suggesting physical confrontation rather than investigation. A jury could find that Arrow Energy failed to exercise reasonable care and that Wasek’s eventual departure was not unreasonable under the circumstances. See Faragher, 524 U.S. at 807.

Because genuine issues of material fact exist on whether the harassment occurred because of sex and on the availability of the Faragher-Ellerth defense, the judgment must be reversed.
