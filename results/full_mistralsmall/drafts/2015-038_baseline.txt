**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft ban is valid as applied to non-federal lands within the boundaries of the National Park System in Alaska.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA), 16 U.S.C. § 3192(c), does not strip the National Park Service (NPS) of regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters physically located within the boundaries of conservation system units (CSUs) in Alaska. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 841 F.3d 1041, 1048-50 (9th Cir. 2016). This conclusion is compelled by the plain text of § 103(c), which provides:

> Only those lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] which are owned by the United States ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit. The Secretary shall not acquire by purchase or exchange any lands within the State which are owned by the State, a Native corporation, or a private party unless the State, corporation, or private party voluntarily offers to convey such lands.

16 U.S.C. § 3192(c) (emphasis added). As the Ninth Circuit recognized, the statute’s operative language—“Only those lands ... owned by the United States ... shall be deemed to be included”—does not purport to limit the NPS’s regulatory jurisdiction over non-federal lands within CSU boundaries. *Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1048 (quoting § 3192(c)). Rather, it merely clarifies which lands are *deemed* part of a CSU for purposes of statutory inclusion. *Id.* at 1049. The statute does not address regulatory jurisdiction at all. *Id.*

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding recognition that Congress may validly regulate activities occurring on non-federal lands within federal enclaves when those activities have a sufficient nexus to federal interests. *Silverburn v. United States*, 431 U.S. 195, 200 (1977) (“[T]he Federal Government may regulate activities occurring on non-federal lands within a federal enclave if those activities have a sufficient nexus to federal interests.”); *see also Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 535, 545 (1976) (federal authority over public lands includes power to regulate activities on non-federal lands that “affect the public lands and their intended use”).

Here, the NPS’s hovercraft ban plainly serves the federal interests underlying the creation of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. The ban protects wildlife, preserves the wilderness character of the preserve, and ensures visitor safety—interests that Congress expressly identified in ANILCA. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3101 et seq. (declaring that the purpose of the preserve is to maintain “the natural diversity and integrity of the environment” and “the natural scenic and wildlife values” of the area). The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the ban is not a regulation that “applies solely to public lands” within CSUs, but rather one that applies to all lands and waters within the preserve’s boundaries, regardless of ownership. *Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1050.

**II. The NPS’s interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984).**

The NPS’s interpretation of § 103(c) is entitled to deference under *Chevron* because the statute is ambiguous as to whether it limits the NPS’s regulatory jurisdiction over non-federal lands within CSU boundaries. *See Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 842-43. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied *Chevron* deference to uphold the NPS’s interpretation. *Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1049-50.

The statute’s text does not clearly address whether the NPS may regulate activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. The operative phrase—“Only those lands ... owned by the United States ... shall be deemed to be included”—does not speak to regulatory jurisdiction. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3192(c). The Ninth Circuit properly found that this language is ambiguous when read in the context of ANILCA’s broader structure and purpose. *Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1049. The NPS’s interpretation—that § 103(c) does not divest it of regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSU boundaries—is a permissible construction of the statute. *Id.* at 1050.

**III. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s hovercraft ban as applied to state-owned waters.**

Sturgeon’s claim that the NPS lacks jurisdiction over state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is foreclosed by the Supreme Court’s decision in *California Coastal Comm’n v. Granite Rock Co.*, 480 U.S. 572 (1987). In *Granite Rock*, the Court held that the federal government may regulate activities on non-federal lands within federal enclaves when those activities have a sufficient nexus to federal interests. *Id.* at 581. The Court rejected the argument that state ownership of the underlying land divested the federal government of regulatory authority. *Id.* at 582-83. The same principle applies here: the NPS’s hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of federal authority over activities occurring on state-owned waters within the preserve, as those activities implicate federal interests in wildlife protection, wilderness preservation, and visitor safety.

Moreover, Sturgeon’s reliance on *Katie John* is misplaced. The Ninth Circuit’s decisions in *Katie John v. United States*, 720 F.3d 1214 (9th Cir. 2013), *Katie John v. United States*, 247 F.3d 1032 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc), and *Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), do not support his claim. Those cases addressed subsistence fishing rights under ANILCA and the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act Amendments of 1986, not the NPS’s regulatory jurisdiction over non-federal lands. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3111 et seq. The Ninth Circuit correctly declined to extend *Katie John* to Sturgeon’s claim. *Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1051 n.7.

**IV. The NPS’s hovercraft ban does not violate the Submerged Lands Act or the Alaska Statehood Act.**

Sturgeon’s argument that the NPS’s hovercraft ban violates the Submerged Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1301 et seq., or the Alaska Statehood Act, ch. 339, 72 Stat. 339 (1958), is meritless. The Submerged Lands Act grants the State of Alaska title to submerged lands beneath navigable waters, but it does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities occurring on those waters when those activities implicate federal interests. *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 58 (1997) (“The Submerged Lands Act did not divest the United States of its paramount authority to regulate commerce on navigable waters.”). Similarly, the Alaska Statehood Act does not limit the federal government’s authority to regulate activities within federal enclaves. *See Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 545.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The NPS’s hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of federal authority over activities occurring on non-federal lands and waters within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the ban as applied to state-owned waters, and his claim is foreclosed by *Granite Rock* and *Chevron* deference. The NPS’s interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is entitled to deference, and the statute does not divest the NPS of regulatory jurisdiction over non-federal lands within CSU boundaries.
