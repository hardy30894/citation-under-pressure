Petitioner has no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings themselves, and Ohio’s offer of a voluntary interview does not compel self-incrimination.

First, this Court has long treated executive clemency as a matter of grace, not a judicially enforceable entitlement. In Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that a prisoner has no liberty interest in the “unfettered” clemency decision because “pardon and commutation decisions” are not “traditionally the business of courts.” That principle did not begin with Dumschat. In Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9 (1950), the Court rejected a due process challenge to a Georgia procedure under which the Governor could obtain medical information before deciding whether to postpone an execution, holding that a condemned prisoner had no constitutional right to a judicially supervised proceeding on insanity. Likewise, in United States v. Murray, 275 U.S. 347 (1928), the Court recognized clemency as a separate, post-judgment function distinct from the judicial process. See also Affronti v. United States, 350 U.S. 79 (1955) (relying on “parole and executive clemency” as distinct alternatives to judicial sentencing powers).

Those cases foreclose any claim that the Due Process Clause itself creates a protected entitlement to clemency procedures. A state may regulate the manner in which clemency applications are received, but that does not transform the Governor’s discretion into a liberty interest. Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464-67; Solesbee, 339 U.S. at 11-12. Petitioner therefore cannot show a cognizable deprivation merely because Ohio has chosen to structure the way it gathers information before exercising mercy.

Second, petitioner’s Fifth Amendment claim fails because Ohio does not compel a waiver of the privilege against self-incrimination. The challenged procedure offers a voluntary interview; it does not require a prisoner to speak, and it does not authorize punishment for silence. The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled testimony, not the mere availability of a forum in which a prisoner may choose to speak. See Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316-20 (1976) (adverse inference from silence in prison disciplinary proceeding did not violate the privilege where there was no compulsion to testify). The same principle is reflected in Johnson v. Massachusetts, 390 U.S. 511 (1968), where the Court treated a defendant’s own appeal for “clemency” as part of the penalty phase strategy, not as compelled self-incrimination. Ohio’s procedure is even less coercive: the inmate may decline the interview altogether.

This Court’s pre-1970 cases also confirm that the government may condition discretionary leniency on voluntary cooperation without converting the process into unconstitutional compulsion. In United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), the Court recognized that officials may make recommendations affecting leniency in the exercise of executive authority. In James L. Bradford, Appt. v. United States, 228 U.S. 446 (1913), the Court upheld conditions attached to a pardon, emphasizing that the petitioner obtained clemency through “voluntary reparation.” And in United States v. Page, 137 U.S. 673 (1891), and United States v. Fletcher, 148 U.S. 84 (1893), the Court treated executive approval and clemency as matters within the President’s prerogative, not judicially enforceable rights. Those decisions show that the sovereign may invite information and cooperation as part of a mercy process without imposing a penalty for silence.

Nor does petitioner’s claim resemble true compulsion under the cases addressing custodial restraints. In Zerbst v. Kidwell, 304 U.S. 359 (1938), the Court described parole status as forfeitable by the prisoner’s own conduct; the state’s ability to enforce conditions on a discretionary benefit does not itself violate the Constitution. So too here, Ohio may offer an opportunity to speak in aid of clemency without forcing the prisoner to surrender the privilege against self-incrimination. If the prisoner chooses silence, the State has not compelled anything.

At bottom, petitioner asks the Court to constitutionalize mercy. But the Court has repeatedly declined to turn clemency into a judicially managed entitlement. Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464-67; Solesbee, 339 U.S. at 11-12; Murray, 275 U.S. at 357-58. And because Ohio’s interview is voluntary, petitioner cannot establish the coercion necessary for a Fifth Amendment violation. Baxter, 425 U.S. at 317-18. The judgment below should be reversed.
