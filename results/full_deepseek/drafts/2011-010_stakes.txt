**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends workers’ compensation coverage to employees injured onshore. Properly construed, OCSLA’s compensation provision contains an implicit situs-of-injury requirement: the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf itself. This interpretation is compelled by the statute’s text, structure, and governing precedent.

**I. The Plain Text of OCSLA’s Compensation Provision Requires Coverage Only for Injuries Occurring on the Outer Continental Shelf**

OCSLA provides that the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act “shall apply with respect to disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The critical phrase linking coverage to “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” imposes a geographic nexus that cannot be severed. As the Supreme Court observed, “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). The Court specifically noted that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement,” *id.* at 219 n.2, confirming that a situs requirement is inherent in the provision.

The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, correctly recognized that “the plain language of the statute suggests a situs requirement.” *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 361 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). The statute does not say “resulting from operations relating to” the shelf, which would eliminate any geographic limitation. Rather, it requires that operations themselves occur “on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This language imposes a dual geographic hook: the operations and the injury must both bear a connection to the shelf.

**II. The “Substantial Nexus” Test Adopted by the Ninth Circuit Lacks Textual Support**

The Ninth Circuit’s holding—that coverage extends to any injury with “a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf”—reads the geographic limitation out of the statute. *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP*, 623 F.3d 6933, 6937 (9th Cir. 2008). Nothing in § 1333(b) mentions a “substantial nexus.” Congress knows how to draft broad "arising out of" language when it wishes. *See, e.g.*, 33 U.S.C. § 902(2) (defining “injury” under LHWCA to include “accidental injury . . . arising out of and in the course of employment”). The more specific phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” reflects a deliberate choice to cabin coverage to injuries with a direct geographic connection to the shelf. *See Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) (“Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely.”).

The “substantial nexus” test also produces absurd results. Under the Ninth Circuit’s standard, an employee injured in a car accident while commuting to a helicopter pickup could recover OCSLA benefits, as the Third Circuit held in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 811 (3d Cir. 1988). But OCSLA was enacted to provide “a comprehensive system of workers’ compensation for workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf.” *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 419 n.6 (1985). It was not intended to displace state workers’ compensation schemes for land-based injuries that happen to have a tangential relationship to offshore operations.

**III. The Text, Structure, and Purpose of OCSLA Confirm That Section 1333(b) Requires an On-Shelf Injury**

OCSLA’s broader structure reinforces a situs requirement. The Act’s choice-of-law provision applies federal law to “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). In *Tallentire*, the Court declined to extend that provision to an accident “occurring on the waters above” the shelf, holding that “the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale.” 477 U.S. at 219. Although the Court noted that § 1333(b) superimposes a status requirement, it did not suggest that the provision *eliminates* the situs requirement entirely. *Id.* at 219 n.2.

To the contrary, the Court’s statement that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement” indicates that a situs requirement remains. *Id.* The word “otherwise” modifies “determinative,” meaning that, but for the status addition, location alone would decide coverage. *Cf. District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570, 605 n.25 (2008) (explaining that “the question is not whether the particular words used in the opening clause are ordinarily used as terms of art, but whether they are used as terms of art anywhere in the legal tradition”). The *Tallentire* footnote confirms that the “plain meaning” of § 1333(b) includes a situs requirement. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361.

The legislative purpose of OCSLA was to provide coverage for workers “who are injured while performing work related to the exploration, development, and production of oil and gas on the Outer Continental Shelf.” *Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 419 n.6. This purpose is served by covering injuries occurring on the shelf itself—on platforms, vessels, and the waters immediately adjacent. Expanding coverage to onshore injuries would duplicate state workers’ compensation systems and undermine the careful federal-state balance Congress struck. *See Beaumont v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 275 F.3d 1083, 1087 (5th Cir. 2002) (per curiam).

**IV. The Situs-of-Injury Test Is the Only Rule That Preserves the Statute’s Coherence**

The Fifth Circuit’s approach in *Mills* provides a workable rule: an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on the outer Continental Shelf itself. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 362. This rule avoids line-drawing problems inherent in any “substantial nexus” test, which would invite litigation over how close a connection suffices. *See Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc.*, 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998) (reaffirming *Mills*). It also respects the principle that “when Congress creates a federal cause of action, it usually does so with an eye toward federal courts’ well-established authority to develop and apply federal common law.” *Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 367 (5th Cir. 2009). But that authority does not license courts to rewrite the geographic scope Congress chose.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Section 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends workers’ compensation coverage only to employees who suffer injury on the outer Continental Shelf itself.
