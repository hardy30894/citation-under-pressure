**ARGUMENT**

I. PLAIN ERROR REVIEW IS LIMITED TO THE RECORD BEFORE THE TRIAL COURT

The Eleventh Circuit erred by considering matters outside the trial record to assess prejudice under plain-error review. This Court has long held that appellate courts are confined to the record developed below. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 234 (1940) ("The jurisdiction of the Circuit Court of Appeals is confined to the record on appeal."); *United States v. Johnson*, 327 U.S. 106, 111 (1946) ("The Circuit Court of Appeals sits only to review the record before it."). The plain-error doctrine does not expand the scope of review beyond the trial record. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (plain-error review applies only to errors "affecting substantial rights" as shown by the record).

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions and post-trial conduct to negate prejudice contravenes this principle. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 511 (1954) ("The reviewing court must be guided by the record below."). The court improperly considered evidence not presented to the jury to undermine the claim of prejudice. *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205, 222 (1952) ("The appellate court cannot consider matters not contained in the record on appeal.").

II. THE FAIRNESS, INTEGRITY, OR PUBLIC REPUTATION OF JUDICIAL PROCEEDINGS MUST BE ASSESSED BASED ON THE TRIAL RECORD

The Eleventh Circuit’s inquiry into whether the error "impacted the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings" improperly extended beyond the trial record. This Court has emphasized that appellate review of judicial integrity is constrained by the record. *United States v. Griffin*, 324 U.S. 23, 31-32 (1945) ("The fairness of a trial is measured by what occurred in the courtroom, not by what may be conjectured to have occurred elsewhere."); *United States v. Williams*, 341 U.S. 58, 66 (1951) ("The integrity of the judicial process is vindicated by correcting errors in the record, not by speculating on what may have happened outside it.").

The court’s consideration of Greer’s prior convictions to assess public reputation is similarly misguided. *Michel v. Louisiana*, 350 U.S. 91, 100 (1955) ("The fairness of a trial is determined by the proceedings in the trial court, not by collateral matters."). The public’s perception of judicial proceedings cannot be evaluated based on extraneous evidence. *Parker v. Gladden*, 385 U.S. 363, 365 (1966) (per curiam) ("Judicial integrity is preserved by adherence to the record, not by roving inquiries into matters not presented to the jury.").

III. SUBSTANTIAL RIGHTS MUST BE DETERMINED FROM THE TRIAL RECORD

The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis of whether Greer’s substantial rights were affected improperly relied on matters outside the trial record. This Court has consistently held that prejudice must be assessed based on the trial record. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) ("The inquiry is whether the error had a substantial influence on the jury’s verdict, and that must be determined from the record."); *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The question of prejudice is to be determined from the record, not from speculation.").

The court’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions to negate prejudice is particularly egregious. *Michel v. Louisiana*, 350 U.S. at 100 ("A defendant’s prior convictions are not to be used to assess prejudice in a subsequent trial."). The jury’s verdict must be evaluated based on the evidence presented at trial, not on extrinsic factors. *United States v. Williams*, 341 U.S. at 66 ("Prejudice is determined by the trial record, not by what may be inferred from other proceedings.").

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit’s plain-error analysis improperly extended beyond the trial record, violating this Court’s longstanding precedents. The judgment below should be reversed.
