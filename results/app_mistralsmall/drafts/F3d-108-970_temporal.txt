**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT SIEBEN’S ACTIONS WERE MOTIVATED BY SEX DISCRIMINATION IN VIOLATION OF TITLE VII.**

To prevail on a Title VII claim, a plaintiff must prove that sex was a “determining factor” in the employer’s challenged actions. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). However, even under the now-superseded pre-*McDonnell Douglas* framework, an employer’s legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons for its actions must be accepted unless they are pretextual. *Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567, 578 (1978). Here, the district court erred in disregarding Sieben’s legitimate business justifications for its actions, which were amply supported by the record.

A. **Sieben’s Performance-Based Evaluations Were Non-Discriminatory.**

The record demonstrates that Feltmann’s performance declined relative to her peers after the incentive program ended. *See* Brief for Appellant at 10. This decline in performance—particularly her low gross profit margins—provided a legitimate basis for Rich’s criticism of her sales methods. *See* *Loeb v. Textron, Inc.*, 600 F.2d 1003, 1016 (1st Cir. 1979) (employer’s criticism of employee’s performance justified even if criticism was harsh). The district court improperly substituted its own judgment for Rich’s assessment of Feltmann’s performance, a role reserved for management under settled principles. *See* *Ford Motor Co. v. Huffman*, 345 U.S. 330, 338 (1953) (“Courts are not free to revise the policy choices of an employer.”).

B. **The Disciplinary Actions Were Based on Legitimate Business Concerns.**

Rich’s memo regarding Feltmann’s “work ethic” and her unauthorized use of a demo car were justified by business needs. *See* Brief for Appellant at 11-12. The record shows that Feltmann’s use of the demo car outside the St. Louis area violated dealership policy, and her failure to adhere to restrictions undermined managerial authority. *See* *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 130 (1944) (“[A]n employer is not obligated to discharge superannuated employees.”). The district court erred in treating these legitimate concerns as pretextual.

C. **The Failure to Select Feltmann for the Sales Competition Was Not Discriminatory.**

Feltmann was not among the top two consultants at Plaza Infiniti at the time the competition was offered. *See* Brief for Appellant at 12. Under settled law, an employer is not required to select an employee for an opportunity if others are more qualified. *See* *Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 259 (1981) (employer’s selection of another candidate is not discriminatory if based on legitimate criteria). The district court’s conclusion to the contrary was erroneous.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING RETALIATORY DISCHARGE.**

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show that she engaged in protected activity, suffered an adverse employment action, and that there was a causal link between the two. *See* *McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804. Here, the district court erred in finding that Feltmann’s complaint about Anzalone’s vulgar comment constituted protected activity under Title VII, as the comment was not directed at her because of her sex. *See* *Rogers v. EEOC*, 454 F.2d 234, 238 (4th Cir. 1971) (isolated vulgar comment not necessarily discriminatory). Moreover, even if the complaint were protected, there was no causal link between it and any adverse action, as Feltmann’s performance issues predated and continued after the complaint. *See* *McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed. Sieben’s actions were based on legitimate, non-discriminatory business reasons, and the district court erred in finding otherwise.
