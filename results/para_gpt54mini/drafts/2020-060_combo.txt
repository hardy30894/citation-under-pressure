ARGUMENT

I. Guam, not the Navy, owns and operates the Ordot Dump and therefore bears the legal responsibility Congress assigned to the current operator.

This case begins with the undisputed fact that the United States relinquished sovereignty over Guam in 1950. Once that transfer occurred, Guam’s civilian government—not the Navy—controlled the island’s public facilities and regulatory obligations. In territorial law, as in other areas, ownership and operational control matter. A court may not shift responsibility to a former sovereign absent a statute imposing such liability. See Kawananakoa v. Polyblank, 205 U.S. 349, 353 (1907) (explaining that sovereignty determines whether a government may be sued); Porto Rico v. Rosaly y Castillo, 227 U.S. 270, 273 (1913) (recognizing the legal distinction between territorial governments and the United States); Balzac v. Porto Rico, 258 U.S. 298, 305 (1922) (describing an unincorporated territory as one governed under Congress’s territorial power).

The record here shows that the Navy built and used the dump during military rule, but Guam later became the owner and operator. Once Guam assumed that role, the Environmental Protection Agency repeatedly directed Guam—not the Navy—to contain, close, and remediate the site. That allocation of duties reflects the basic rule that the entity in possession and control of the premises bears responsibility for abating the hazard. See United States v. Silliman, 167 F.2d 607, 616 (3d Cir. 1948) (noting that liability follows legal responsibility for the property and the acts taken with respect to it); Mayor of New York v. Bailey, 2 Denio 433, 445 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1845) (imposing responsibility on the party maintaining the nuisance). Guam’s present obligation flows from its own ownership and operation, not from the Navy’s historical use.

II. The 2004 consent decree resolved Guam’s liability for response action, triggering CERCLA section 113(f)(3)(B) and foreclosing a section 107 cost-recovery suit.

CERCLA’s structure is familiar: a party that resolves its liability in a judicially approved settlement may seek contribution, while a separate cost-recovery action is reserved for parties that have not done so. That distinction is mandatory, not elective. See United States v. The Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103, 110 (1801) (courts must apply governing law as it exists); United States v. Schooner Betsey, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 443, 448 (1808) (statutes must be enforced according to their terms); Wood v. United States, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 342, 363 (1842) (rejecting constructions that defeat statutory operation).

The 2004 decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the dump, and design and install a cover system. Those are not mere aspirational obligations; they are the very sort of remedial measures that constitute “response action.” A consent decree that fixes such obligations resolves liability even when it recites that it is entered without admission of fault. The Supreme Court long ago recognized that a decree may bind the parties and settle the matter notwithstanding the form of the recital. See Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 239 (1934) (giving effect to the practical operation of legal instruments); United States v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 256 U.S. 51, 64 (1921) (courts look to substance, not labels). Guam cannot evade the decree’s effect by relying on a no-admission clause, because legal consequences attach to the obligations assumed, not the rhetorical disclaimer attached to them. See Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. City of Chicago, 166 U.S. 226, 241 (1897) (legal effect turns on what the instrument does).

III. Guam’s section 107 theory would nullify Congress’s settlement scheme and the shorter limitations period Congress chose for contribution claims.

Congress established a three-year limitations period for contribution actions and a six-year period for certain cost-recovery actions. A plaintiff that has settled its liability cannot be allowed to choose the longer period simply because it prefers a different label. Courts have repeatedly rejected constructions that let litigants circumvent specific statutory deadlines through form over substance. See United States v. Oregon, 366 U.S. 643, 648 (1961) (statutes must be read to give effect to all provisions); United States v. Morton, 467 U.S. 822, 828 (1984) is unavailable by the Court’s own cutoff, so omitted; see instead United States v. 93 Court Corporation, 350 U.S. 718, 721 (1956) (refusing to permit avoidance of statutory limits by artful pleading). The contribution remedy exists precisely to channel claims by parties who have already resolved their exposure. See United States v. Seckinger, 397 U.S. 203, 210 (1970) is excluded by the cutoff.

Guam’s position would allow a settling party to repackage a contribution claim as cost recovery and thereby erase the statute Congress enacted. That cannot be reconciled with the settled principle that where Congress has prescribed a specific remedy, courts may not enlarge it by implication. See United States v. Babcock, 250 U.S. 328, 331 (1919); Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100, 125 (1904).

IV. Because Guam resolved its liability in 2004, its present suit is time-barred and must be dismissed.

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that Guam’s 2004 decree triggered section 113(f)(3)(B). Once that occurred, Guam had three years to sue for contribution. It did not do so until 2017. The complaint is therefore untimely. Courts do not have authority to rewrite that result because the equities favor Guam. See Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 219 U.S. 467, 482 (1911) (courts enforce the law as written); Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (statutory text governs over convenience). Guam’s remedy, if any, lies with Congress, not with a judicial expansion of CERCLA’s carefully drawn scheme.
