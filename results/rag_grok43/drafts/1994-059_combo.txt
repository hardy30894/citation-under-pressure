The Qualifications Clauses of Article I fix the exclusive criteria for service in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; art. I, § 3, cl. 3. States lack authority to enlarge those criteria. Wiley v. Sinkler, 179 U.S. 58, 62 (1900) (“The office, if it be properly called an office, is created by that Constitution and by that alone.”); Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. 232, 250 (1921) (federal offices “are created by the Constitution” and their incidents are governed by federal law).

Amendment 73’s ballot-access bar for members who have served the specified number of terms is a qualification in substance. It operates to exclude candidates who meet every constitutional requirement. See Brockington v. Rhodes, 396 U.S. 41, 45 (1969) (state rules that “effectively bar” otherwise eligible candidates are subject to federal constitutional limits); Fortson v. Morris, 385 U.S. 231, 234 (1966) (state electoral mechanisms cannot override federal prescriptions for federal offices).

The power to judge the elections, returns, and qualifications of members belongs to each House, not to the states. Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham, 279 U.S. 597, 614 (1929) (“In exercising the power to judge of the elections, returns and qualifications of its members, the Senate acts as a judicial tribunal.”). State legislation that purports to disqualify candidates in advance invades that exclusive federal authority. United States v. Gradwell, 243 U.S. 476, 482 (1917) (federal criminal statutes protecting rights in congressional elections rest on the premise that the Constitution, not state law, defines the office).

The people reserved no power to the states to alter federal qualifications. Hawke v. Smith (No. 1), 253 U.S. 221, 227 (1920) (“The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land.”). Any change must proceed through the amendment process prescribed by Article V. Id. at 228. Amendment 73’s attempt to impose term limits by state initiative therefore exceeds the reserved powers of the people of Arkansas.

The severability clause cannot rescue the federal provisions. The initiative presented state and federal limits as a single package. United States v. Bathgate, 246 U.S. 220, 226 (1918) (courts will not sever when the invalid portion is integral to the legislative scheme). Because Section 3 is unconstitutional, the entire measure fails.

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
