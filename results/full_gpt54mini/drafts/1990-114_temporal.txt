Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule for state judges should be upheld because the ADEA, properly construed, does not reach appointed members of the state judiciary, and because age-based judicial retirement easily satisfies rational-basis review under the Fourteenth Amendment. When Congress legislates in a field that touches the States’ core sovereign prerogatives, a clear statement is required before federal law is read to displace state authority. See Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 460-61 (1991); United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The selection and tenure of state judges are among the most sensitive of those prerogatives. See In re Wade, 694 F.2d 1328, 1330 (D.C. Cir. 1982).

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office … or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). That exclusion should be read to encompass appointed state judges. Judges do not merely apply fixed rules; they necessarily resolve unsettled questions of law, create precedents, and exercise judgment in matters of public significance. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 466-67; EEOC v. Massachusetts, 680 F. Supp. 455, 462-63 (D. Mass. 1988), aff’d, 858 F.2d 52 (1st Cir. 1988). Appointed judges also exercise administrative and supervisory authority over court operations, rules, and personnel. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 467-68. For that reason, courts have repeatedly recognized that judges occupy a policymaking role. See EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988); Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 435-36, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145-46 (1988); In re Stout, 521 Pa. 571, 579-80, 559 A.2d 489, 493-94 (1989).

The structure and history of § 630(f) reinforce that reading. Congress used the broad term “appointee on the policymaking level,” not a narrow phrase limited to executive-branch officials. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 467-69. The statutory exceptions show that Congress intended to distinguish those occupying positions of public trust and discretionary authority from ordinary rank-and-file employees. See id.; cf. Civil Service Comm’n v. Letter Carriers, 413 U.S. 548, 564-65 (1973). Appointed judges fit that description. To exclude them would create the anomalous result that elected judges are plainly outside the ADEA, while appointed judges performing identical duties would be covered. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 468-69. Congress did not silently upset that long-settled state allocation of judicial authority. See Bass, 404 U.S. at 349.

The Equal Protection Clause does not invalidate Missouri’s age-70 retirement rule either. Age is not a suspect classification, and no fundamental right to public employment exists. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1976) (per curiam). Under rational-basis review, a state classification must be upheld if any reasonably conceivable state of facts could provide a rational basis. Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co., 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911); Williamson v. Lee Optical of Okla., Inc., 348 U.S. 483, 488-89 (1955). Missouri’s rule readily meets that standard.

The State could rationally conclude that mandatory retirement promotes the competence, vigor, and administrative efficiency of the judiciary. Judges perform demanding work requiring sustained judgment, stamina, and quick legal analysis. See Murgia, 427 U.S. at 314-15; Goesaert v. Cleary, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948). A fixed retirement age also avoids the difficult and embarrassing process of individualized competency hearings and the disruption caused by judicial decline. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 472-73; cf. Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm’rs, 330 U.S. 552, 564-65 (1947). Missouri could further conclude that a mandatory cutoff promotes orderly succession and public confidence in the courts. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 472-73; see also Hirabayashi v. United States, 320 U.S. 81, 100-01 (1943).

Petitioners’ contrary policy objections do not matter. Under rational-basis review, a state “has no obligation to produce evidence to sustain the rationality of a statutory classification.” Heller v. Doe, 509 U.S. 312, 320 (1993). The question is only whether the legislature could rationally have believed the classification would serve legitimate ends. Williamson, 348 U.S. at 488-89. Missouri plainly could. Its Constitution’s age-70 retirement rule therefore violates neither the ADEA nor the Equal Protection Clause.
