**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Class Without Adequate Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis.**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this standard, a plaintiff must demonstrate that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis using common evidence. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011) (“[A] class cannot be certified on the premise that [the defendant] will not be entitled to litigate its statutory defenses to individual claims.”); *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 311 (3d Cir. 2008) (requiring a “rigorous analysis” of whether common evidence can prove class-wide damages).

Here, the District Court erred by certifying a class despite the absence of admissible evidence demonstrating a feasible, class-wide damages model. The plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. James McClave, proposed a regression analysis to isolate the effect of Comcast’s alleged anticompetitive conduct on prices. However, as the Third Circuit has recognized, a regression model must be “tied to the theory of liability” and must not rely on “speculative assumptions.” *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. Dr. McClave’s model failed this test. It assumed that Comcast’s clustering strategy caused supracompetitive prices for *all* non-basic programming, despite the fact that many class members never paid supracompetitive prices. *See* App. 00030 (District Court’s narrowing of the class to a single theory of impact). The model also relied on an arbitrary geographic market definition—individual households—rather than the relevant antitrust market. *See Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 336 (1962) (requiring a “relevant geographic market” that is “commercially significant”).

The District Court’s certification order rested on a flawed legal premise: that Rule 23(b)(3) permits certification where damages are merely *capable* of class-wide proof, without requiring a feasible methodology. This misreads *Dukes*, which requires that damages be measurable on a class-wide basis using common evidence. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350. The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* confirms this standard: “[T]he district court must conduct a ‘rigorous analysis’ of whether the plaintiff has proposed a method to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. The plaintiffs’ methodology here falls short.

**II. The District Court’s Certification Order Violated the Seventh Amendment by Usurping the Jury’s Role in Determining Individual Damages.**

The Seventh Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in civil cases. U.S. Const. amend. VII. Certification of a class that includes members with no damages or individualized proof violates this right by permitting a jury to award damages to absent class members without determining their individual entitlement. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 360 (“[A] class action, like other representative actions, may not ‘abridge, enlarge or modify’ any substantive right.” (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 23)).

Here, the District Court certified a class that included members who never paid supracompetitive prices, as well as those who did. The plaintiffs’ damages model attempted to estimate class-wide overcharges, but it could not determine which class members were actually injured. This impermissibly shifts the burden to Comcast to disprove damages on an individual basis—a burden that belongs to the plaintiffs. *See id.* at 350. The District Court’s certification order thus violated the Seventh Amendment by permitting a jury to award damages without resolving individual issues.

**III. The District Court’s Predominance Finding Was Clearly Erroneous.**

A district court’s predominance finding under Rule 23(b)(3) is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312. The court must determine whether common questions of fact or law predominate over individual issues. *Id.* Here, the District Court’s predominance finding was clearly erroneous because it rested on an unsupported damages methodology and an arbitrary geographic market definition.

First, the plaintiffs’ damages model was speculative. Dr. McClave’s regression analysis assumed that Comcast’s clustering strategy caused supracompetitive prices for *all* non-basic programming, despite the lack of evidence that this was true for many class members. *See* App. 00030. This assumption rendered the model unreliable for class-wide proof. *See Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) (requiring that damages be measurable on a class-wide basis).

Second, the District Court’s geographic market definition was arbitrary. It defined the market as individual households, rather than the relevant antitrust market. *See Brown Shoe*, 370 U.S. at 336. This definition ignored the commercial reality of the cable market and rendered the plaintiffs’ damages model unworkable. *See id.*

The District Court’s predominance finding was thus clearly erroneous, and its certification order must be reversed.
