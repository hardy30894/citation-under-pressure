**ARGUMENT**

**THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF THE PRIOR-CONVICTION ALLEGATION FOLLOWING THE COURT OF APPEAL'S REVERSAL FOR INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause, applicable to the States, forbids a second attempt to prove an allegation once a court has held the evidence insufficient to sustain it.**

The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has held that guarantee "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" and therefore binding on the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Among the core protections the Clause affords is the rule, articulated in *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904), that "former jeopardy includes one who has been acquitted by a verdict duly rendered." Applying that settled principle, Justice Douglas explained in *Sapir v. United States*, 348 U.S. 373, 374 (1955) (dissenting opinion), that "[t]he granting of a new trial after a judgment of acquittal for lack of evidence violates the command of the Fifth Amendment." As that opinion observed, "[i]f the jury had acquitted, there plainly would be double jeopardy to give the Government another go at this citizen. . . . I see no difference when the appellate court orders a judgment of acquittal for lack of evidence." *Id.* The same principle bars a second trial where the first factfinder found the evidence insufficient to convict on a particular allegation, even if the defendant was validly convicted of a lesser or different charge at the same proceeding. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 190 (1957) (retrial on a charge as to which the defendant was, in substance, not found guilty "placed Green in jeopardy twice for the same offense in violation of the Constitution").

The Court of Appeal here held, after full adversarial litigation, that the prosecution's evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioner personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury—the facts necessary to sustain the "serious felony" enhancement. That determination is indistinguishable in substance from the appellate acquittal condemned in *Sapir*. Having lost on the merits once, the State is not entitled, consistent with *Sapir* and *Kepner*, to a second opportunity to marshal better evidence of the same allegation.

**II. Because California elected to try the prior-conviction allegation as an adversarial proceeding bearing the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence, the proceeding must be treated as a trial for double jeopardy purposes.**

This Court has distinguished between informal sentencing determinations, where a judge may rely on hearsay, probation reports, and personal knowledge without offending due process, *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246, 251 (1949) ("The due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure"), and proceedings that the State itself structures with the formal trappings of a criminal trial. Where the State chooses the latter course, this Court has required core trial-type protections, including the right to confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967).

California did not leave petitioner's prior-conviction allegation to informal, *Williams*-style determination. It required proof beyond a reasonable doubt, application of the rules of evidence, and confrontation of witnesses—precisely the procedural apparatus of a criminal trial. Having voluntarily clothed the proceeding in the hallmarks of a trial, California cannot now strip away the double jeopardy consequences that attach when such a trial ends in a finding that the evidence was insufficient. A State may not obtain the practical benefits of a criminal trial—use of the reasonable-doubt standard to secure a greater sentence—while disclaiming the constitutional burdens, including finality, that accompany that choice.

**III. The Double Jeopardy Clause's protection against multiple punishments independently bars retrial to increase petitioner's sentence.**

The Clause also protects against being "punished twice" for the same conduct. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This Court's long solicitude for defendants facing enhanced punishment on retrial—evident even in the capital context of *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919), where the Court scrutinized a retrial that produced a harsher sentence—confirms that jeopardy concerns intensify, not diminish, when the State seeks a second chance to secure additional years of imprisonment. Permitting the State to retry the prior-conviction allegation here would allow exactly the kind of second bite at enhanced punishment that the Clause, as elaborated in *Pearce* and *Stroud*, was designed to prevent. See also *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671–672 (1896) (recognizing the settled but narrow circumstances in which retrial is permitted only where the defendant's own conviction, not an acquittal, has been set aside).

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed, and the bar against retrial of the prior-conviction allegation, as originally ordered by the Court of Appeal, should be reinstated.
