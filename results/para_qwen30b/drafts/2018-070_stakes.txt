**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires a faithful reading of the statute’s text, structure, and precedent. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California’s wage and hour laws may be applied on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) as “surrogate federal law” whenever they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, without requiring a showing of a gap or void in federal coverage. That interpretation disregards the foundational principle that federal law governs the OCS, and it misreads the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision as a broad invitation to import state law, rather than a narrow exception for cases where federal law is silent.

**I. The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision does not permit the automatic application of state law; it requires a gap in federal coverage.**

The OCSLA provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States for that portion of the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf ... to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation—reading “applicable and not inconsistent” as a standalone trigger for state law—ignores the long-standing judicial understanding that this provision operates only to fill gaps in federal law. As the Fifth Circuit held in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), “the OCSLA does not permit the application of state law unless there is a significant void or gap in federal law.” This principle has been consistently applied in cases involving tort, contract, and employment claims on the OCS. See *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835, 23 L.Ed.2d 360 (1969) (holding that state law applies only where federal law does not provide a remedy); *Huson v. Shell Offshore, Inc.*, 404 U.S. 95, 99, 92 S.Ct. 349, 30 L.Ed.2d 287 (1971) (reaffirming that state law supplements federal law only where federal law is silent).

The Ninth Circuit’s departure from this settled precedent is not only unsupported by the text of the OCSLA but also undermines Congress’s intent. When Congress enacted the OCSLA in 1953, it recognized that federal law alone would be inadequate to govern the complex activities on the OCS. See *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 27, 109 S.Ct. 278, 102 L.Ed.2d 186 (1988). But Congress did not intend to supplant federal law with state law; rather, it intended to allow state law to “fill the gaps” where federal law was silent. See *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835 (“[T]he application of state law is not automatic; it is limited to those situations in which federal law does not provide a remedy.”). The 1975 amendment to the OCSLA, which expanded the scope of “state law” to include laws “hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed,” did not alter this fundamental limitation. The amendment was designed to ensure consistency between the OCS and deepwater ports, not to eliminate the gap requirement. See *Pub. L. No. 93-627*, § 19(f), 88 Stat. 2176 (1975).

**II. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to supplement it.**

The FLSA is not a mere floor; it is a comprehensive federal regime governing minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping. See *29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq.* The FLSA contains a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that explicitly permits states to impose more protective wage and hour standards. But this clause does not mean that state law applies automatically on the OCS. Rather, it confirms that federal law is the baseline, and state law may supplement it only where federal law is silent or inadequate. The FLSA’s regulatory framework, including Department of Labor (DOL) regulations, provides detailed guidance on when employees are entitled to meal periods, how hours are counted, and what constitutes compensable work. See *Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004) (relying on DOL regulations to interpret FLSA obligations).

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that California’s meal period law is “not inconsistent” with the FLSA and therefore applicable on the OCS is legally flawed. Inconsistency is not merely a matter of conflict in outcome; it is a matter of whether state law imposes obligations that are incompatible with the structure or purpose of federal law. See *Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 517, 112 S.Ct. 2608, 120 L.Ed.2d 407 (1992) (state law is preempted if it “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress”). California’s requirement of a 30-minute meal period for every five hours worked is not merely more protective—it is a distinct legal obligation that the FLSA does not require.
