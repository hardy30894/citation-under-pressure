The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. This principle is a cornerstone of conspiracy law, as established by the Supreme Court in a series of decisions that have consistently emphasized the importance of proving a defendant's participation in a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt.

In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), the Court held that "the burden is on the Government to prove that the conspiracy... continued into the period covered by the statute of limitations." (Id. at 216). This requirement is fundamental to ensuring that defendants are not held liable for conspiracies that may have terminated before the limitations period. The Court reaffirmed this principle in Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391 (1957), where it held that the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, and that the defendant's participation in it was not limited to a period outside the limitations period. (Id. at 396-397).

The government's burden to prove a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period is not alleviated by the defendant's withdrawal from the conspiracy. As the Court noted in Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), a defendant's withdrawal from a conspiracy does not necessarily mean that they are not liable for the conspiracy, but the government must still prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Id. at 369-370). This principle is consistent with the Court's earlier holding in United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205 (1940), that mere knowledge of a conspiracy is not enough to prove membership.

The Court has also established that a defendant's participation in a conspiracy must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, and that the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Blumenthal v. United States, 332 U.S. 539, 556-557 (1947)). Furthermore, the Court has held that a defendant's liability for a conspiracy is not limited to the period during which they were actively participating, but the government must still prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640, 646-647 (1946)).

In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court emphasized the importance of proving a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period, stating that "the proof must show that [the defendant] was a member of the conspiracy... during the period covered by the indictment." (Id. at 752-753). This requirement is essential to ensuring that defendants are held accountable only for conspiracies in which they were actively involved.

In this case, the government presented sufficient evidence to prove that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period, and that the defendants were members of it during that time. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial. The Supreme Court's precedents, including Fiswick, Grunewald, Kotteakos, Falcone, Blumenthal, Hyde, and Pinkerton, establish that the government bears the burden of proving a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period, and that this burden is not alleviated by a defendant's withdrawal from the conspiracy.
