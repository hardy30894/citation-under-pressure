**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s Denial of Access to the Good News Club Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because the Club Was Seeking to Engage in Religious Worship, Not Speech.**

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club did not violate the First Amendment because the Club sought to engage in religious worship, not speech. *See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 512 (2d Cir. 2000). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Establishment Clause permits a public school to exclude religious worship from its facilities, even when those facilities are otherwise open to community groups. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (noting that a school may “preserve the building for its intended purpose” and avoid “the appearance of favoring religion” by excluding religious worship). The Good News Club’s activities—singing hymns, reciting scripture, and praying—are quintessentially religious worship, not secular speech. *See id.* at 395-96 (distinguishing between religious worship and secular speech).

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), is misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university’s refusal to fund a student religious publication violated the First Amendment because the university had created a limited public forum for student speech and could not discriminate based on viewpoint. *Id.* at 829-30. Unlike *Rosenberger*, Milford did not exclude the Club based on viewpoint; it excluded the Club because its activities constituted religious worship, which is not speech at all. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. The Court in *Rosenberger* expressly distinguished between speech and worship, noting that a university may “exclude from its forum speech that is tantamount to worship.” *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 839 n.7.

**II. Milford’s Policy Excluding Religious Worship from Its Facilities Is Constitutional Under the Establishment Clause.**

Milford’s policy excluding religious worship from its facilities is constitutional under the Establishment Clause. The Supreme Court has held that a public school may exclude religious worship from its facilities to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court upheld a school’s refusal to allow a religious group to show films on school premises, reasoning that the school could “preserve the building for its intended purpose” and avoid the appearance of favoring religion. *Id.* Similarly, Milford’s policy preserves its facilities for secular community use and avoids the appearance of endorsing religion.

The Club’s reliance on *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), is unavailing. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university’s refusal to allow a religious group to meet on campus violated the First Amendment because the university had created a limited public forum for student groups. *Id.* at 276-77. Unlike *Widmar*, Milford did not create a limited public forum for religious worship. Instead, Milford’s policy explicitly excludes religious worship from its facilities to comply with the Establishment Clause. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512.

**III. The Second Circuit Correctly Held That Milford’s Denial of Access Did Not Constitute Viewpoint Discrimination.**

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club did not constitute viewpoint discrimination. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. The Club argues that Milford engaged in viewpoint discrimination by excluding its religious activities while allowing other groups to discuss morals and character. However, the Supreme Court has recognized that a public school may exclude religious worship from its facilities without engaging in viewpoint discrimination. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395. Milford’s policy is neutral with respect to viewpoint; it excludes all religious worship, not just the Club’s viewpoint. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512.

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is again misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, the Court held that the university’s refusal to fund a student religious publication constituted viewpoint discrimination because the university had created a limited public forum for student speech. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829-30. Unlike *Rosenberger*, Milford did not exclude the Club based on viewpoint; it excluded the Club because its activities constituted religious worship, which is not speech at all. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club did not violate the First Amendment, and its policy excluding religious worship from its facilities is constitutional under the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
