**Argument**

The district court committed plain error by admitting Special Agent Jones’s testimony that Stanley’s statements were deceptive, that his conduct was typical of “the criminal element,” and that his invocation of faith showed deception. Although Stanley did not object contemporaneously, this Court may correct a plain error that affects substantial rights and seriously impugns the fairness and integrity of the proceedings. *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189 (1943); *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936). The challenged testimony violated long-settled limits on opinion evidence and usurped the jury’s exclusive role in determining credibility. *Gulf, C. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Shane*, 157 U.S. 348 (1895); *United States v. Spaulding*, 293 U.S. 498 (1935).

First, a witness may not tell the jury, in substance, that another witness or defendant is lying. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that opinion testimony must not invade the province of the jury on ultimate issues of credibility. *United States v. Buchanan*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 116 (1870); *Ohio & Mississippi Ry. Co. v. McCarthy*, 96 U.S. 258 (1877); *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944). That principle is especially strict where the witness is not an ordinary lay observer but a government agent cloaked with special expertise. *United States v. Spaulding*, 293 U.S. 498 (1935). Agent Jones did not merely describe Stanley’s demeanor; he repeatedly opined that Stanley’s answers “were not worthy of credence,” “did not make sense,” and were “deceptive.” That was credibility-determination testimony in its purest form.

Second, the government may not use expert status as a vehicle for bolstering an inference of guilt from claimed “training” in detecting deception. Expert testimony is admissible only when it concerns matters beyond the ken of the average juror and when it is sufficiently reliable to aid, rather than replace, the jury’s own judgment. *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923); *Wigmore, Evidence* principles were adopted in the federal cases limiting opinion evidence to help, not supplant, fact-finding. *Alison v. United States*, 362 U.S. 528 (1960), though later than the classic common-law cases, confirms that federal procedure does not relax the traditional requirement that evidence assist the trier of fact rather than dictate the result. Jones’s claimed ability to detect deception in faith-based statements or “common” criminal behavior was not a scientific or technical matter beyond juror competence; it was merely a personal judgment dressed up as expertise. *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503 (1943).

Third, the testimony was improper character evidence. By saying Stanley exhibited evasive behaviors “common among the criminal element,” Jones invited the jury to infer guilt from criminal propensity. The common law has long rejected conviction by bad character or disposition rather than proof of the charged offense. *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469 (1948); *Griffin v. United States*, 336 U.S. 704 (1949). A witness may not smuggle in a conclusion that a defendant is “the kind of person” who lies or acts like a criminal and therefore likely committed the crime. *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76.

Fourth, the admission of Jones’s testimony seriously prejudiced Stanley. The government’s case was circumstantial and depended substantially on the jury’s assessment of Stanley’s conduct and identity after arrest. A federal appellate court may reverse when improperly admitted evidence “had substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the verdict. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946). Here, Jones’s testimony came from an FBI agent, was presented as expert opinion, and directly attacked Stanley’s truthfulness on the very issues the jury had to decide. That kind of government-backed credibility assault is precisely the sort of evidence most likely to tip the balance. *Shepard v. United States*, 290 U.S. 96 (1933).

Nor can the error be dismissed as harmless. Plain-error review asks whether the error is obvious and affects substantial rights. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936); *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189 (1943). Where a witness’s opinion testimony reaches the central issue of whether the defendant is lying, appellate courts have long recognized the danger that the jury will abdicate its duty. *Gulf, C. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Shane*, 157 U.S. 348 (1895); *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944). That danger was acute here because the testimony was not isolated; Jones repeatedly vouched for his own deception-detection skills and repeatedly branded Stanley’s answers as deceptive.

Finally, because the testimony was admitted without objection, the Court should exercise its power to prevent a miscarriage of justice. The Supreme Court has long recognized that appellate courts have authority to notice plain errors affecting the fairness of criminal trials even when not preserved below. *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632 (1896); *Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States*, 251 U.S. 385 (1920). Allowing an FBI agent to tell the jury, over no meaningful constraint, that Stanley was deceptive, criminal, and faith-manipulating undermines confidence in the verdict and the integrity of the process.

For these reasons, Stanley’s conviction should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
