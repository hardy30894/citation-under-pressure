**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY AND HEALTH ACT (“OSH ACT”) APPLIES TO THE MR. BELDON BECAUSE IT IS A “WORKPLACE” UNDER SECTION 4(A) OF THE ACT.**

The Fifth Circuit erred in failing to address whether the MR. BELDON qualifies as a “workplace” under the OSH Act. Section 4(a) of the Act expressly applies to “employment performed in a workplace.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is defined broadly to include “any establishment, job site, or premises where work is performed by an employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). The legislative history confirms that Congress intended this definition to encompass all places where employees work, including vessels operating on navigable waters. *See* S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 12 (1970) (“The definition of ‘workplace’ is intended to be broad enough to cover all places where employees perform work.”).

The MR. BELDON was a drilling barge engaged in oil and gas exploration, where Mallard’s employees performed their duties. It was not a vessel in navigation transporting passengers or cargo but a stationary worksite where drilling operations occurred. *See Chao v. Smith Packing Co.,* 226 F.3d 316, 320 (6th Cir. 2000) (holding that a meatpacking plant was a “workplace” under the Act). The Fifth Circuit’s prior decisions likewise recognize that fixed worksites on navigable waters fall within the Act’s coverage. *See Donovan v. Red Star Marine Servs., Inc.,* 739 F.2d 771, 774 (D.C. Cir. 1984) (finding that a dockside facility was a “workplace” under the Act).

The court below improperly conflated the issue of whether the MR. BELDON was a “workplace” with the question of whether OSHA’s jurisdiction was preempted by the Coast Guard. These are distinct inquiries. *See Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n,* 499 U.S. 144, 156 (1991) (noting that the Act’s coverage is broad and that exemptions must be narrowly construed). The Fifth Circuit’s failure to address the “workplace” issue was error, and this Court should remand for consideration of that question.

**II. SECTION 4(B)(1) OF THE OSH ACT DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen’s working conditions preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that it does not apply to “working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, this provision does not grant the Coast Guard exclusive jurisdiction over all maritime workplaces. *See Martin,* 499 U.S. at 157 (holding that the Coast Guard’s authority over inspected vessels does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over longshoremen working on those vessels).

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited. While the Coast Guard regulates certain safety equipment on uninspected vessels, such as fire extinguishers and life preservers, it does not prescribe comprehensive occupational safety and health standards for workers aboard those vessels. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. The Fifth Circuit’s prior decisions confirm that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is not exclusive where it has not exercised its authority to prescribe occupational safety and health standards. *See Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.,* 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980) (holding that OSHA applies where the Coast Guard has not prescribed standards for a particular hazard).

The MR. BELDON was not subject to comprehensive Coast Guard regulation. The Coast Guard’s investigation did not cite Mallard for any violations, and its regulations do not address the specific hazards at issue in this case, such as blowout prevention or drilling safety procedures. *See* 46 C.F.R. Part 109 (applicable to drilling vessels but not uninspected vessels like the MR. BELDON). Because the Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to prescribe occupational safety and health standards for uninspected vessels, OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted under Section 4(b)(1). *See Martin,* 499 U.S. at 157.

**III. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT AND SHOULD BE REVERSED.**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s holding in *Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n,* 499 U.S. 144 (1991). In *Martin,* this Court rejected the argument that the Coast Guard’s authority over inspected vessels preempted OSHA’s jurisdiction over longshoremen working on those vessels. The Court held that the Act’s coverage is broad and that exemptions must be narrowly construed. *Id.* at 156–57. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in this case extends *Martin*’s reasoning to uninspected vessels, but it misapplies the preemption analysis by conflating the Coast Guard’s limited authority over uninspected vessels with its comprehensive authority over inspected vessels.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Donovan v. Red Star Marine Servs., Inc.,* 739 F.2d 771 (D.C. Cir. 1984), which held that OSHA applies to dockside facilities where the Coast Guard has not exercised its authority to prescribe occupational safety and health standards. *Id.* at 774. The Fifth Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split and should be reversed to ensure uniformity in the application of the OSH Act.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the decision of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed, and this case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
