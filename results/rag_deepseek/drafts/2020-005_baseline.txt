**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a State to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011). Contrary to Ford’s position, this standard does not require that the defendant’s forum contacts *cause* the plaintiff’s injury. Rather, the Constitution demands only a sufficient “affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy.” *Id.* at 919. Because Ford purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting business in Montana—advertising, selling, and servicing the very vehicle model at issue there—the First Judicial District Court properly exercised specific jurisdiction over Ford.

**I. The “Arise Out of or Relate To” Standard Requires a Connection, Not Causation.**

The Court has long held that specific jurisdiction exists when the suit “derive[s] from” or is “connected with” the defendant’s forum activities. *Goodyear*, 564 U.S. at 920. In characterizing this standard, the Court has never required a strict causal nexus. Instead, the defendant must have “purposefully established ‘minimum contacts’ in the forum State” such that “maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.’” *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 464 (1985) (quoting *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945)).

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction comports with that test. Ford has a substantial and continuous presence in Montana: it markets, sells, and services vehicles, including the Ford Explorer involved in the accident. Ford’s contacts with Montana do not end with the sale of a generic product; rather, Ford purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting business within the State, thereby “invoking the benefits and protections of its laws.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. Because Gullett’s claims—defective design, failure to warn, and negligence—directly relate to Ford’s decision to sell the same vehicle in Montana, the connection between the forum and the controversy is constitutionally sufficient.

**II. Ford’s Argument Confuses “Contact” with “Cause.”**

Ford contends that specific jurisdiction requires that the defendant’s forum contacts *cause* the plaintiff’s claim. That reading finds no support in this Court’s precedents. In *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), the Court held that the critical inquiry is whether the defendant “should reasonably anticipate being haled into court” in the forum—not whether the defendant’s forum conduct was the proximate cause of the injury. Indeed, the Court distinguished cases in which the defendant had “no contacts, ties, or relations” with the forum. *Id.* at 295. Ford, by contrast, has extensive ties to Montana. It maintains dealerships, sells parts, and advertises to Montana consumers. These contacts are not “random,” “fortuitous,” or “attenuated.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected a strict causal requirement in analogous contexts. In *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court upheld a Massachusetts statute that subjected nonresident motorists to jurisdiction arising from accidents occurring in the State—even though the nonresident’s decision to drive into the forum was the only contact. And in *Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad*, 346 U.S. 338 (1953), the Court recognized that a nonresident’s use of a forum State’s roads constitutes sufficient contact to support jurisdiction for accidents occurring there. If driving a car into a State is sufficient to support jurisdiction, then Ford’s deliberate, sustained marketing and sales of vehicles in Montana surely suffice.

**III. Ford’s Reliance on *Rush v. Savchuk* Is Misplaced.**

Ford invokes *Rush v. Savchuk*, 444 U.S. 320 (1980), for the proposition that jurisdiction cannot be premised on contacts unrelated to the claim. But *Rush* involved an attempt to satisfy the “minimum contacts” requirement by garnishing an insurance policy that had no connection to the forum State. *Id.* at 328. The Court held that “the defendant’s contacts with the forum State” must be “the basis for the assertion of jurisdiction.” *Id.* at 328. Here, Ford’s contacts with Montana are extensive and directly related to the controversy: Ford sold the same model of vehicle in Montana, marketed it to Montana consumers, and advertised its safety features there. Unlike the insurance policy in *Rush*, Ford’s forum activities are central to the nature of the claims—products liability and failure to warn.

**IV. The Montana Supreme Court Applied the Correct Standard.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly found that Ford had “availed itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana” and that the claims “arise out of or relate to” its forum activities. That conclusion is consistent with *Burger King*, which teaches that “when a corporation ‘purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,’ it ‘has clear notice that it is subject to suit there.’” 471 U.S. at 474 (quoting *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297). Ford had such notice. It cannot now argue that its nationwide marketing and sales efforts, which included Montana, bear no relation to a design defect claim concerning a product it actively promoted there.

**CONCLUSION**

Because Ford purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting business in Montana, and because the plaintiff’s claims derive from and are connected to those forum contacts, the Due Process Clause does not forbid the exercise of specific jurisdiction. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
