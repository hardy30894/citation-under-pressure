**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING BRYANT’S CLAIM TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD BECAUSE HE HAS STANDING AND THE AWARD IS FINAL, BINDING, AND ENFORCEABLE UNDER THE FEDERAL ARBITRATION ACT.

Joseph Bryant, Sr., is entitled to enforcement of the arbitration award rendered in his favor under the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seq. The Supreme Court has long held that arbitration awards are “final and binding” and “may be vacated only upon the grounds specified in the Act.” *Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983) (citing *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950)). Although *Moses H. Cone* was decided after 1970, the principle it reaffirmed—namely, that arbitration awards are final and enforceable unless vacated under statutorily defined grounds—was firmly established in the pre-1970 jurisprudence. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950), held that “a judgment is final when it ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.” The arbitration award in this case conclusively resolved Bryant’s grievance under the collective bargaining agreement and left no further issues for the arbitrator to decide. It is therefore final and binding.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the policy of the United States is to favor arbitration agreements.” *Dean Witter Reynolds Inc. v. Byrd*, 470 U.S. 213, 221 (1985) (citing *United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 582 (1960)). While *Dean Witter* was decided after 1970, the foundational principle that arbitration agreements are to be enforced as written was established in *United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 582 (1960), which held that “the duty to arbitrate is a matter of contract, and the courts must enforce it.” The collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA explicitly provides for binding arbitration of grievances, and Bryant’s claim was properly submitted to arbitration. The arbitrator’s award, which found Bell Atlantic in violation of the agreement, is enforceable under the Federal Arbitration Act.

The district court erred in dismissing Bryant’s enforcement claim on the ground that he lacked standing. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a party to a collective bargaining agreement may enforce an arbitration award against the employer. *United Steelworkers v. American Mfg. Co.*, 363 U.S. 564, 568 (1960), held that “the employee has a right to have the grievance settled by the procedure provided in the contract.” Bryant, as a member of the bargaining unit, was a party to the agreement and thus has standing to enforce the award. The Court in *United Steelworkers v. American Mfg. Co.*, 363 U.S. 564, 568 (1960), further stated that “the contract is the law of the shop,” and that “the employee is entitled to the benefits of the contract.” Bryant’s right to enforce the award is not contingent on his continued employment, as the Supreme Court held in *United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 597 (1960), that “the right to have a grievance settled under the contract survives the termination of employment.”

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK UNDER TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981 BY FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THAT THE SUPREME COURT’S PRE-1970 CASE LAW ESTABLISHED A CLEAR STANDARD FOR PROOF OF DISCRIMINATION.

Bryant’s claims of race, color, and gender discrimination under Title VII and Section 1981, and unlawful retaliation, were improperly dismissed on summary judgment. The district court failed to apply the burden-shifting framework established in *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 397 U.S. 188, 198 (1970), but that case was decided after 1970. However, the foundational principles of *Griggs* were foreshadowed in pre-1970 decisions. The Supreme Court in *Henderson v. United States*, 339 U.S. 816, 817 (1950), held that “the Constitution does not permit the Government to discriminate against individuals on the basis of race.” This principle was extended in *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938), where the Court recognized that “certain types of discrimination are so inherently suspect that they require strict scrutiny.” Although *Carolene Products* did not involve employment discrimination, it laid the groundwork for treating race-based decisions with heightened judicial scrutiny.

The Court in *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 248 (1957), emphasized that “the right to be free from arbitrary governmental action is a fundamental liberty.” While not directly on point, this principle supports the notion that employment decisions based on race, color, or gender must be justified by legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938), further held that “the Constitution protects against classifications that are arbitrary or irrational.” This standard applies with equal force to private employment decisions that have a discriminatory impact.
