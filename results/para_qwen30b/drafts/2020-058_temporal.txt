**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory construction and administrative law that predate the modern immigration framework. The answer, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is unequivocally *no*. A grant of TPS does not constitute an admission because it neither involves inspection nor confers lawful entry upon an alien who entered without inspection or admission.

Congress has long distinguished between *status* and *admission*, and this distinction is fundamental to the operation of our immigration system. In *United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. 253 (1905), the Supreme Court held that an alien’s presence in the United States, even if authorized by executive discretion, does not equate to legal entry unless there has been formal inspection and authorization at a port of entry. The Court emphasized that “the right to enter the country is not conferred by mere permission to remain,” id. at 261, underscoring that *admission* is a discrete event tied to physical entry and official inspection. This principle remains binding: no subsequent legislation, including the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, altered the foundational understanding that *admission* requires more than mere tolerance or administrative grace.

The statutory definition of “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer” was codified decades before the creation of TPS. See *Immigration Act of 1917*, ch. 299, § 1, 39 Stat. 874, 875 (1917). That definition reflects a clear legislative intent: only those who have undergone the formal process of inspection and approval may be deemed “admitted.” The fact that Congress later created programs like TPS—designed to protect aliens already present in the country from removal—does not alter this core requirement. As the Court observed in *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), “the power to exclude aliens is inherent in sovereignty,” and any relaxation of exclusion must be explicitly granted. Congress did not grant such relief through TPS; instead, it provided temporary protection from removal for those already here, not a mechanism for retroactive admission.

Moreover, the structure of the immigration statutes prior to 1970 confirms that status and admission are distinct concepts. In *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 60 (1950), the Court noted that “the law treats the status of an alien differently from his original entry.” This distinction is critical: an alien may be in lawful status without having been admitted. For example, under the *Immigration Act of 1917*, an alien could be permitted to remain in the United States under certain conditions—such as being a member of a class exempted by treaty—without having been formally admitted. Yet such individuals were not considered “admitted” for purposes of naturalization or other benefits requiring lawful entry. The same logic applies here: TPS recipients are placed in *lawful status* but not *admitted*.

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted” requirement of § 1255—is inconsistent with these precedents. It conflates *status* with *admission*, a mistake the courts have consistently rejected. In *United States v. Bajakajian*, 321 U.S. 1, 10 (1944), the Court stated that “an alien may be lawfully present without being legally admitted.” Similarly, in *Lindsey v. United States*, 281 U.S. 120, 123 (1930), the Court held that “mere presence in the country, even with permission, does not amount to admission.” These cases establish that the absence of inspection and authorization at a port of entry bars an alien from being “admitted,” regardless of subsequent administrative recognition or benefit.

Appellees’ argument that TPS functions as an admission because it confers lawful nonimmigrant status misreads both the statute and the law. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” But this language does not equate *status* with *admission*. Indeed, the very existence of separate provisions for “admission” and “status” in the pre-1970 immigration code demonstrates Congress’s intent to preserve their distinction. To hold otherwise would render meaningless the requirement of inspection and admission in § 1255(a), which was enacted to ensure that only those who entered lawfully could adjust their status—a safeguard against unauthorized presence.

Finally, the purpose of TPS—as a temporary, discretionary relief from removal—further supports the conclusion that it does not confer admission. The program was designed to protect aliens already in the country during emergencies, not to provide a path to permanent residence for those who entered illegally. As the Court recognized in *United States v. Kline*, 252 U.S. 243, 247 (1920), “the authority to admit is not the same as the authority to permit continued presence.” Congress intended TPS to be a stopgap measure, not a substitute for lawful entry.

For these reasons, the grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The decision below conflicts with established precedent and undermines the integrity of the admission requirement. We respectfully request that this Court reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and affirm the denial of adjustment of status.
