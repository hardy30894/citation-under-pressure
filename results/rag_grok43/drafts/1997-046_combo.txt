**ARGUMENT**

I. An inmate possesses no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings.

Clemency is an executive function distinct from judicial adjudication. In *Solesbee v. Balkcom*, 339 U.S. 9 (1950), this Court observed that a person legally convicted and sentenced to death had no statutory or constitutional right to a judicially conducted or supervised proceeding on post-conviction claims. *Id.* at 11. The same principle governs clemency. *See United States v. Murray*, 275 U.S. 347 (1928) (noting provisions for parole and executive clemency as separate from judicial power); *Affronti v. United States*, 350 U.S. 79 (1955) (same). 

The Due Process Clause does not transform executive clemency into a protected interest. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103 (1935), confirms that state courts share the obligation to guard rights secured by the Constitution, yet it imposes no requirement that clemency itself create a liberty interest. *Id.* at 112-13. Historical practice reinforces this limit. *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), treated official recommendations concerning leniency as matters of executive discretion, not judicially enforceable entitlements. *Id.* at 234. *James L. Bradford, Appt. v. United States*, 228 U.S. 446 (1913), likewise described a pardon as a voluntary act of clemency granted upon conditions the executive deems proper. *Id.* at 453-54.

*Zerbst v. Kidwell*, 304 U.S. 359 (1938), underscores that parole and clemency authorities exercise powers separate from the original sentence. *Id.* at 362-63. *United States v. Page*, 137 U.S. 673 (1891), and *United States v. Fletcher*, 148 U.S. 84 (1893), confirm that presidential or gubernatorial action on clemency is presumed to rest on executive authority alone. No precedent recognizes a life or liberty interest arising from the clemency process itself.

II. Voluntary participation in a clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

The challenged procedure affords an inmate the option, not the obligation, to request an interview. *Johnson v. Massachusetts*, 390 U.S. 511 (1968), illustrates that statements made without compulsion raise no self-incrimination concern. *Id.* at 512. Because the regulation permits the inmate to remain silent or to speak only as desired, no unconstitutional condition is imposed. The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled testimony; it does not bar an inmate from voluntarily providing information in pursuit of executive mercy.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
