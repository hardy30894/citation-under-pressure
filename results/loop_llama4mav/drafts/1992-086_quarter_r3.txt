The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's facilities, although not traditional public forums, were opened for use by the general public for certain purposes, thereby creating a limited public forum.

As established in Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939), and reaffirmed in Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983), the government's power to regulate expression in a public forum is limited. When the government creates a limited public forum, it is bound by the same standards as those applicable to traditional public forums, as noted in Perry, 460 U.S. at 46. The exclusion of speech from a limited public forum based on content is permissible only if it serves a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that interest, as required by Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940).

The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series, which was described as "Family oriented movie from a Christian perspective," was based on the religious content of the speech. The District's policy, as stated in Local Rule No. 7, prohibited the use of school premises "by any group for religious purposes." This rule, as applied to Lamb's Chapel, constitutes a content-based exclusion.

The Supreme Court has held that a state may not exclude speech from a public forum based on its content unless the exclusion is necessary to serve a compelling state interest, as seen in Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939). In this case, the District has not demonstrated a compelling state interest in excluding Lamb's Chapel's film series. The Court noted in Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), that the First Amendment does not require a complete separation of church and state, and that the government may accommodate religious practices. The Zorach Court stated that "we are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being," 343 U.S. at 313. This principle suggests that the District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not justified by a compelling state interest.

Furthermore, the District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral. As the Court held in Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951), a regulation that grants or denies a permit based on the content of the speech is not viewpoint-neutral.

The lower court's reliance on the fact that the District had not previously allowed religious groups to use the facilities is misplaced. The principle that a prior restraint on speech is subject to strict scrutiny is well-established, as seen in Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 716 (1931). The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not justified by a history of prior religious uses, as there is no evidence that the District had previously allowed such uses.

In conclusion, the District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral and does not serve a compelling state interest.
