**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit’s holding that an employee need not be injured on the Outer Continental Shelf to receive OCSLA compensation benefits cannot be squared with the statute’s plain language. Section 1333(b) extends LHWCA coverage to “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The key phrase—“occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf”—modifies “injury.” It tells us where the injury must happen: on the shelf. Because Juan Valladolid was killed on land, his injury did not occur on the shelf. The situs-of-injury test is therefore compelled by the statutory text.

**I. The Plain Text of § 1333(b) Requires the Injury to Occur on the Shelf**

This Court has repeatedly held that when a statute’s language is clear, courts must enforce it as written. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *United States v. Fisher*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 396 (1805). The word “occurring” in § 1333(b) is a present participle describing the injury. It answers the question: where does the injury take place? The phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” further specifies that the injury must happen as a consequence of operations that are themselves located on the shelf. But the modifying clause does not sever the connection between the injury’s location and the shelf; it reinforces that connection by requiring that the injury result from shelf operations. An onshore injury, by definition, does not occur on the shelf—even if it results from shelf operations.

Congress’s word choice is instructive. In other parts of OCSLA, Congress demonstrated that it knows how to extend coverage beyond the shelf when it wishes. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) applies state law to “artificial islands and fixed structures” on the shelf and to “devices” attached to the seabed. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Section 1333(b) contains no such expansive language; it is limited to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The contrast shows that Congress deliberately confined the compensation provision to the shelf itself. *Cf. FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941) (a statute’s jurisdictional language must be given its plain meaning).

**II. OCSLA’s Territorial Purpose Confirms That Coverage Is Limited to the Shelf**

OCSLA was enacted to assert United States sovereignty over the submerged lands beyond state boundaries. *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 29 (1947); *United States v. Texas*, 339 U.S. 707, 718 (1950). The Act’s workers’ compensation provision is part of that territorial scheme; it fills the gap left by the absence of state workers’ compensation laws on the shelf. *See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 423 (1985) (noting OCSLA’s “explicit geographic limitation”). The purpose of the provision is to provide coverage for injuries occurring where no state law applies—on the shelf itself. That purpose is not served by extending coverage to onshore facilities, where state compensation systems already apply.

The LHWCA, which OCSLA incorporates, has always required a maritime situs. *Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 4 (1946). OCSLA acts as a situs substitute: the shelf serves as the statutory situs for workers injured there. To read OCSLA as covering onshore injuries would eliminate the situs requirement entirely for offshore workers—a result Congress did not intend. *See Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (statutory jurisdiction must be construed in light of territorial limits).

**III. The “Substantial Nexus” Test Impermissibly Rewrites the Statute**

The Ninth Circuit adopted a test requiring only a “substantial nexus” between the injury and shelf operations. That test reads the word “occurring” out of the statute. If Congress had intended to cover all injuries with any connection to shelf operations, it could have said so. It did not. Instead, it specified that the injury must “occur” as a result of shelf operations. A “nexus” test would cover injuries that merely relate to shelf operations, even if they occur far from the shelf. This Court has warned against adding words to a statute. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924). The Ninth Circuit’s approach adds the concept of “nexus” while removing the geographic anchor of “occurring.”

**IV. Valladolid’s Onshore Death Does Not Satisfy the Statute**

Valladolid was killed at La Conchita, an onshore processing facility separated from the coast by a highway and railroad tracks. His injury did not occur on the Outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. Therefore, his death is not covered by § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit erred in remanding for application of its “substantial nexus” test. The judgment should be reversed.
