**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT FOR BORA CORP. BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP OWED SERBIN A DUTY UNDER THE "ACTIVE OPERATIONS" DOCTRINE.

A. The "Active Operations" Duty Requires the Vessel to Exercise Reasonable Care to Prevent Injury from Hazards the Stevedore Could Not Reasonably Have Anticipated.

In *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), the Supreme Court recognized that a vessel owes a longshoreman a duty of reasonable care under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act ("LHWCA"), 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), only when the vessel is engaged in "active operations." *Id.* at 167. This duty arises from the vessel’s "control over the cargo operations and the concomitant ability to prevent accidents." *Id.* The Court further explained that the duty is limited to hazards that the stevedore could not reasonably have anticipated and guarded against. *Id.* at 167-68.

The Third Circuit has consistently applied this principle. In *Gutierrez v. Waterman Steamship Corp.*, 373 U.S. 206 (1963), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s duty to warn of hidden dangers is triggered only when the vessel has actual or constructive knowledge of the danger and the stevedore could not reasonably have discovered it. *Id.* at 210. Similarly, in *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), the Court recognized that a vessel’s duty arises from its superior knowledge and control over the work environment. *Id.* at 95.

Here, Serbin presented evidence that the snatch block was improperly left in the down position, obstructing the cargo unit and creating a hazard. He further testified that the block’s position prevented the safe removal of the cargo and posed a risk to workers in the wings. The ship’s failure to secure the block in the up position—despite its control over the block and pulley system—created a hazard that Serbin could not reasonably have anticipated. Thus, a genuine issue of material fact exists as to whether the ship breached its active operations duty.

B. The District Court Improperly Concluded That Serbin’s Decision to Move the Block Precluded Liability.

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on the ground that Serbin’s decision to move the block himself severed any causal connection between the ship’s negligence and his injury. This reasoning conflates causation with duty and overlooks the foreseeability of the injury.

In *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539 (1960), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s negligence is not absolved merely because the stevedore attempted to remedy the hazard. *Id.* at 550. The Court explained that the vessel’s duty is to avoid creating or exacerbating hazards, and its breach may still be the proximate cause of injury even if the stevedore takes corrective action. *Id.*

Here, Serbin’s decision to move the block was a foreseeable response to the ship’s failure to secure it properly. The ship’s negligence in leaving the block in the down position created the very hazard that led to Serbin’s injury. Thus, the district court erred in concluding that Serbin’s actions precluded liability as a matter of law.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP’S NEGLIGENCE WAS THE PROXIMATE CAUSE OF SERBIN’S INJURY.

A. The Ship’s Failure to Secure the Snatch Block Properly Was a Substantial Factor in Causing Serbin’s Injury.

In *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s negligence need not be the sole cause of injury to establish liability under the LHWCA. *Id.* at 409. The Court explained that a defendant’s conduct is a proximate cause if it is a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. *Id.*

Here, the ship’s failure to secure the snatch block in the up position was a substantial factor in causing Serbin’s injury. The block’s improper position forced Serbin to move it, and the block’s weight and instability caused him to fall. Thus, a genuine issue of material fact exists as to whether the ship’s negligence was a proximate cause of Serbin’s injury.

B. The District Court Improperly Disregarded Evidence of the Ship’s Control Over the Hazard.

The district court erred in disregarding evidence that the ship controlled the block and pulley system. In *Halcyon Lines v. Haenn Ship Ceiling & Refitting Corp.*, 342 U.S. 282 (1952), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s control over the work environment is a critical factor in determining liability. *Id.* at 286.

Here, the ship had exclusive control over the block and pulley system, including the responsibility to secure the snatch blocks in the up position. The ship’s failure to exercise this control properly created the hazard that led to Serbin’s injury. Thus, the district court erred in granting summary judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether the ship owed Serbin a duty under the active operations doctrine and whether its negligence was the proximate cause of his injury. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
