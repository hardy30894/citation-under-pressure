The constitutional test for determining whether a river segment passes to a state under the equal-footing doctrine turns on navigability at the precise moment of statehood. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845). Title to the beds of navigable waters vests in the new state "in the same manner and to the same extent" as in the original states. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 27 (1894). That title is fixed by the condition of the stream when the state entered the Union. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931).

The test itself is whether the waters "are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). Susceptibility is measured by the river's capacity at statehood, not by later improvements or present-day use. Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 118 (1921). Evidence of actual navigation before or at statehood, together with the river's physical characteristics at that time, establishes the fact. United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926).

Segments must be examined individually. Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 585 (1922). A finding that one reach is navigable does not control another. Packer v. Bird, 137 U.S. 661, 667 (1891). The inquiry remains historical: the court must determine the river's condition "at the time of the admission of the State." Barney v. Keokuk, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876).

This Court has repeatedly required trial courts to anchor the navigability determination in contemporaneous evidence. St. Anthony Falls Water-Power Co. v. St. Paul Water Comm'rs, 168 U.S. 349, 359 (1897). Later changes in the stream or in commercial practices cannot enlarge or diminish the title that vested at statehood. United States v. Cress, 243 U.S. 316, 321 (1917). The constitutional rule therefore compels the trial court to decide whether the relevant stretch was navigable when Montana entered the Union.
