**ARGUMENT**

**I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings Protected by the Due Process Clause.**

From the founding era forward, this Court has treated executive clemency as an act of sovereign grace standing outside the machinery of adjudication—not as a further stage of the trial or appellate process to which due process attaches. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall described a pardon as "an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That description forecloses any suggestion that clemency redetermines guilt or continues the adjudicatory process; it instead mitigates a judgment that the adjudicatory process has already validly produced.

This Court reaffirmed the singular, non-judicial character of the clemency power in *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), holding that the power "is unlimited, with the exception stated," and may be exercised "at any time after [the offense's] commission, either before legal proceedings are taken, or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment." Clemency thus operates on an entirely different plane than trial or appeal, unconstrained by the procedural sequence that due process ordinarily governs. The same principle underlies *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120–21 (1925), where the Court explained that the pardoning power was deliberately vested in the Executive, separate from and unreviewable by the judicial branch, precisely because clemency serves interests distinct from those vindicated at trial.

Justice Holmes captured the point in *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927): a modern pardon "is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power," but "a part of the Constitutional scheme" reflecting "the determination of the ultimate authority that the public welfare will be better served" by mercy than by strict enforcement of the sentence. Nothing in that description casts clemency as an extension of the adjudicatory process. Due process protections arise only when government action threatens an interest to which the individual is already legally entitled. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (the process due turns on "the precise nature of the governmental function involved" and "the private interest that has been affected"). A capital prisoner's hope for clemency is not such an entitlement—it depends entirely on executive grace, as *Wilson*, *Garland*, and *Biddle* establish. The Sixth Circuit therefore erred in extending due process scrutiny, however minimal, to a proceeding this Court's precedents have consistently placed outside the adjudicatory system.

**II. Because Participation in the Clemency Interview Is Voluntary, It Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination in Violation of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment forbids only *compelled* self-incrimination. In *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964), this Court held that the privilege "is fulfilled only when the person is guaranteed the right 'to remain silent unless he chooses to speak in the unfettered exercise of his own will, and to suffer no penalty . . . for such silence.'" Ohio's clemency regulation satisfies that standard precisely. An inmate may request a personal interview or decline one; if he participates, he remains free to answer as much or as little as he wishes, and no penalty attaches to silence.

Where disclosure is voluntary, no constitutional violation occurs. *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (the privilege protects against compulsion, not voluntary testimony); *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (the privilege shields only answers that would themselves support a conviction or "furnish a link in the chain of evidence" needed to prosecute, when such answers are compelled). The privilege belongs to the individual, who may invoke or waive it as to particular questions; it does not operate automatically to bar all participation. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 594–97 (1896). Indeed, in *United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263–64 (1927), Justice Holmes explained that a person confronted with a request for information generally must decline to answer particular questions rather than refuse to appear at all—the very choice Ohio's regulation preserves for every clemency applicant.

Nor can Woodard transform speculation about hypothetical future prosecutions into a present constitutional violation. This Court has repeatedly declined to extend the privilege to remote, contingent risks divorced from any real threat of prosecution. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 430–31 (1956) (privilege does not reach speculative future harm absent a realistic prosecutorial threat). Woodard's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing conviction or sentence, and the clemency regulation imposes no penalty for silence or partial answers at the interview. Any risk of self-incrimination is therefore wholly theoretical.

Because the clemency interview is optional and carries no adverse consequence for declining it or limiting one's answers, Ohio's procedure does not compel Woodard to be a witness against himself. The judgment below should be reversed.
