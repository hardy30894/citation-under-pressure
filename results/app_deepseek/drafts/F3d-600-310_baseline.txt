**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Exercising Jurisdiction Over Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs After the Chapter 13 Case Was Closed and Discharge Entered.**

The standard of review for subject-matter jurisdiction is de novo. *See In re Bulldog Trucking, Inc.*, 66 F.3d 1390, 1395 (4th Cir. 1995). A bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction is “purely statutory” and limited to matters “arising under title 11” or “arising in or related to a case under title 11.” 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b). Once a bankruptcy case is closed, the court’s jurisdiction over post-confirmation, post-discharge disputes is sharply circumscribed. *See In re Walker*, 51 F.3d 562, 569 (4th Cir. 1995) (holding that “after a bankruptcy case is closed, the bankruptcy court retains only limited jurisdiction to enforce its orders and to address matters that are ‘related to’ the closed case”). A proceeding is “related to” a bankruptcy case only if the outcome “could conceivably have any effect on the estate being administered.” *In re A.H. Robins Co.*, 86 F.3d 364, 371 (4th Cir. 1996) (quoting *Pacor, Inc. v. Higgins*, 743 F.2d 984, 994 (3d Cir. 1984)). Here, the Chapter 13 estate had been fully administered, the case closed, and the discharge entered. No estate existed to be affected.

The bankruptcy court grounded its jurisdiction in 28 U.S.C. § 1334(a) and § 157(b)(2)(A), which covers “core proceedings” concerning “the administration of the estate.” But the estate was no longer being administered. The dispute over post-petition interest and collection costs did not arise from the administration of the estate; it arose from a post-discharge contractual obligation. The bankruptcy court’s only remaining connection to the loan was Kirkland’s complaint to determine dischargeability under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8). That complaint, however, disclaimed any dispute regarding post-petition interest, as Kirkland specifically acknowledged “that interest that accumulated during the term of the Chapter 13 would be non-dischargeable.” J.A. 5. The only dischargeability issue was whether the principal had been paid. The bankruptcy court resolved that issue—finding the principal not discharged—and then exceeded its jurisdiction by sua sponte adjudicating the amount of post-petition interest and collection costs.

Once the bankruptcy court determined that the loan was not discharged, its jurisdiction over the loan ended. The remaining issues—whether ECMC was entitled to post-petition interest and collection costs, and in what amounts—were state-law contract questions that could be resolved in a non-bankruptcy forum. *See In re Biondo*, 180 F.3d 126, 131 (4th Cir. 1999) (holding that bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to resolve a post-discharge state-law claim that was not “related to” the closed case). The bankruptcy court’s award of $184.40 in post-petition interest and its denial of collection costs were therefore void for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. *See In re Carraher*, 971 F.2d 874, 878 (1st Cir. 1992) (“A judgment rendered without subject-matter jurisdiction is void ab initio.”).

Moreover, even if the bankruptcy court had jurisdiction, it erred in the exercise of that jurisdiction. The bankruptcy court stated that Kirkland and ECMC “agree that some amount of interest has accrued to date since the filing of the bankruptcy petition.” J.A. 87. But ECMC never agreed to litigate the amount of post-petition interest in the bankruptcy court; ECMC explicitly argued that the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine that issue. The bankruptcy court’s reliance on a purported “agreement” is unsupported by the record. Similarly, the court’s denial of collection costs because ECMC “has not provided any statutory or factual basis for the accrual of such costs” was not a proper ruling on the merits—it was a refusal to exercise jurisdiction that the court did not have.

Because the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs, the district court’s affirmance of that portion of the bankruptcy court’s order must be reversed. This Court should vacate the awards of post-petition interest and denial of collection costs, and remand with instructions to dismiss those claims for lack of jurisdiction. ECMC may pursue its state-law remedies in an appropriate forum.
