**Argument**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on appellants’ retaliation claims under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (“FRSA”), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The FRSA prohibits a railroad from disciplining an employee for “reporting, in good faith, a hazardous safety or security condition.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Congress enacted that protection to encourage railroad employees to report safety hazards without fear of retaliation. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109; *Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 788–89 (8th Cir. 2014). Summary judgment was improper because a reasonable jury could find that appellants engaged in protected activity, BNSF knew of that activity, and the ensuing discipline was retaliatory. See *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789; *Armstrong v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 880 F.3d 377, 381–82 (8th Cir. 2018).

Under the FRSA, an employee need only show by a preponderance of the evidence that protected conduct was a contributing factor in the adverse action. 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A)(i); *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789. A “contributing factor” is “any factor, which alone or in connection with other factors, tends to affect in any way the outcome of the decision.” *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789 (internal quotation marks omitted). That standard is “broad and forgiving.” *Armstrong*, 880 F.3d at 382. The employee need not prove that the protected activity was the sole or primary reason for discipline. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789.

Appellants satisfied that standard. Immediately after John Moore’s fall, they told BNSF that the bridge was unsafe because it lacked side rails and lighting, and Foster specifically stated that employees had previously reported the bridge as dangerous. Those reports fall squarely within § 20109(a)(1)(C). See *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789–90. BNSF then began investigating appellants, issued notices of a disciplinary hearing, and later imposed discipline on each appellant after hearing testimony about the bridge conditions. A jury could infer causation from that sequence. See *Armstrong*, 880 F.3d at 382–83 (temporal proximity and decisionmaker knowledge support causation).

The district court also erred by minimizing evidence of retaliatory animus. BNSF subjected appellants to increased operations testing while the disciplinary proceeding was pending, and Kline and Snyder testified that they altered their work choices because they feared being blamed for others’ mistakes. Evidence that a railroad increased scrutiny or imposed adverse working conditions after protected reporting can support a retaliatory inference. See *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790; *Armstrong*, 880 F.3d at 382. A reasonable jury could view the testing and heightened scrutiny as part of a pattern of adverse treatment following appellants’ safety complaints.

BNSF cannot avoid trial by pointing to its asserted rule violations. Even if appellants committed some infractions, the FRSA asks whether protected reporting contributed to the discipline. 49 U.S.C. § 20109(d)(2)(A)(i); *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789. Mixed motives do not defeat liability. *Armstrong*, 880 F.3d at 382. The record would permit a finding that BNSF disciplined appellants not merely for safety-rule violations, but because they reported the unsafe bridge conditions and contradicted management’s account of the crew-change location. That is enough to create a jury question.

BNSF’s reliance on the Public Law Board proceedings also does not resolve the FRSA claim. Arbitration under the Railway Labor Act does not extinguish a separate statutory retaliation claim unless Congress has clearly so provided. See *Hawaiian Airlines, Inc. v. Norris*, 512 U.S. 246, 256–58 (1994). The Board’s later adjustments to the discipline do not eliminate appellants’ showing that BNSF initiated and imposed discipline after their protected safety reporting. The FRSA claim therefore survives independent of the arbitration outcome. See *Armstrong*, 880 F.3d at 382–83.

Nor may BNSF obtain summary judgment by recasting the bridge reports as merely a complaint about where the crew was instructed to change. The record shows more. Kline and Snyder expressly identified unsafe conditions at the bridge, including the lack of side rails and lighting. Foster likewise reported that the location had previously been identified as dangerous. Reporting a hazardous condition to management is the core conduct protected by § 20109(a)(1)(C). See *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789–90. At minimum, whether appellants’ statements were protected and whether they contributed to the discipline are disputed factual issues for a jury.

Because the record, viewed in the light most favorable to appellants, supports a finding that they reported a hazardous safety condition and that BNSF disciplined them at least in part because of those reports, the judgment should be reversed. See *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986); *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789–90.
