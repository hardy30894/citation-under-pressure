# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

Because this appeal arises from summary judgment, this Court must view the record in the light most favorable to Wasek. Summary judgment is a disfavored device where, as here, a case turns on questions of motive and intent. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment should be used sparingly "where motive and intent play leading roles"). The district court's contrary disposition should be reversed.

## II. Title VII's Prohibition on Discrimination "Because of Sex" Does Not Depend on the Harasser's Gender

Title VII makes it unlawful for an employer "to discriminate against any individual ... because of such individual's ... sex." 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). Nothing in that text limits protection to harassment inflicted by a member of the opposite sex. Courts construing Title VII in the years immediately following its enactment have consistently refused to graft limitations onto its unqualified language. In *Weeks v. Southern Bell Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 408 F.2d 228 (5th Cir. 1969), the Fifth Circuit rejected a cramped reading of the statute's bona fide occupational qualification exception, holding that an employer bears "the burden of proving that he had reasonable cause to believe ... that all or substantially all women would be unable to perform safely and efficiently the duties of the job involved," *id.* at 235, precisely because Congress intended Title VII's protections to be construed broadly rather than narrowly. Likewise, in *Bowe v. Colgate-Palmolive Co.*, 416 F.2d 711 (7th Cir. 1969), the Seventh Circuit struck down facially neutral weight-lifting restrictions because they operated to the disadvantage of women, reasoning that the statute must be read to reach the practical effect of discriminatory treatment, not merely its most obvious form. And in *Quarles v. Philip Morris, Inc.*, 279 F. Supp. 505 (E.D. Va. 1968), the district court explained that Title VII was designed to reach the full range of discriminatory practices Congress identified, not a narrow subset chosen by the courts.

This same principle of liberal construction has long applied to civil rights legislation generally. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Supreme Court recognized that civil rights statutes are remedial measures entitled to a construction that gives full effect to their protective purpose. *See also Hurd v. Hodge*, 334 U.S. 24 (1948) (construing civil rights statute broadly according to its terms). Applying that same interpretive approach here, the statute's plain command—that an employer may not discriminate "because of sex"—draws no distinction based on whether the harasser and the victim share the same sex. Ottobre's conduct toward Wasek was explicitly and repeatedly sexual in nature: sexually explicit stories, comments about Wasek's "pretty lips," and physical touching of an unmistakably sexual character. A jury, not the court, should decide whether this conduct occurred "because of" Wasek's sex within the meaning later cases would call a hostile environment, but which the statute's own words already reach.

## III. Arrow Energy Is Liable Because Its Supervisors Had Actual Knowledge of Ottobre's Conduct and Failed to Act

An employer is answerable for the tortious conduct of those in its employ where the risk of such conduct is one fairly attributable to the enterprise itself and where those with authority to prevent it fail to do so. *Ira S. Bushey & Sons, Inc. v. United States*, 398 F.2d 167 (2d Cir. 1968), instructs that liability attaches when the conduct in question arises out of and is connected to the very activity the employer has undertaken, such that it is not unfair to charge the enterprise with responsibility for it. Here, the connection is even more direct than in *Bushey*, because Wasek's own supervisors—Tripp, Smillie, and Strong—were told of Ottobre's sexual touching and comments and did nothing. Tripp watched Ottobre poke Wasek with a hammer handle and laughed. Smillie, informed of the sexual touching, responded only "Why don't you just kick his ass?" Strong told Wasek he was "whining" and should "duke it out" with Ottobre. Each supervisor had actual knowledge and each declined to exercise the authority his position carried to stop the conduct. Under settled principles of respondeat superior, that failure is chargeable to Arrow Energy.

## IV. Wasek's Departure Was a Constructive Discharge

An employee who resigns because his employer has rendered his working conditions intolerable has not voluntarily quit; he has been discharged. *NLRB v. Tennessee Packers, Inc., Frosty Morn Division*, 339 F.2d 203 (6th Cir. 1964), recognizes that an employer's deliberate creation of conditions an employee cannot reasonably be expected to endure is the functional equivalent of discharge. Wasek complained repeatedly, through the chain of command, of continuing sexual harassment; he was met with threats of a "starvation schedule," accusations of "whining," and suggestions that he physically fight his harasser. Left with no recourse, and after his property was stolen and his truck access denied, Wasek had no reasonable alternative but to leave. That departure was not abandonment—it was discharge.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
