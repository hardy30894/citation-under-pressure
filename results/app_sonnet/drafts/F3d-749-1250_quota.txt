                                    ARGUMENT

I.  THE DISTRICT COURT COMMITTED PLAIN ERROR BY ADMITTING AGENT JONES’S TESTIMONY THAT HILL WAS LYING

Because Hill did not object at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. Under that standard, Hill must show (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732, 736 (1993). Each factor is satisfied here.

A.  Admitting Agent Jones’s opinion that Hill was being deceptive was error.

It is a bedrock evidentiary principle that a witness—expert or lay—may not tell the jury that another witness is lying or telling the truth, because credibility determinations belong exclusively to the jury. This Court has repeatedly enforced that rule. In United States v. Toledo, 985 F.2d 1462 (10th Cir. 1993), this Court held that an expert may not offer an opinion that directly comments on the credibility of another witness’s statements, because doing so invades the jury’s province. See id. at 1470–71. Likewise, in United States v. Charley, 189 F.3d 1251 (10th Cir. 1999), this Court reaffirmed that expert testimony vouching for the truthfulness of a witness usurps the jury’s exclusive function of weighing credibility. Id. at 1266–67. Other circuits agree. In United States v. Azure, 801 F.2d 336 (8th Cir. 1986), the Eighth Circuit reversed a conviction because an expert’s testimony effectively told the jury the complaining witness was truthful, reasoning that such testimony “tak[es] over” the jury’s essential role. Id. at 340. And in United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135 (2d Cir. 1988), the Second Circuit held that expert testimony that certain witnesses had lied “usurp[ed] a critical function of the jury” and was “not the sort of assistance” contemplated by the Federal Rules of Evidence. Id. at 142.

Agent Jones’s testimony was precisely the kind of credibility-vouching these decisions forbid, and it was worse for having been dressed up as expertise in “deception detection.” Jones testified that he was trained to “identify [] deception in statements and truths in statements,” and he applied that supposed expertise directly to Hill, opining that Hill’s answers “[did] not make sense,” that Hill exhibited behaviors “common among the criminal element,” that “[n]ever in [his] career” had he seen an innocent person say he would rather die than face charges, and that invoking God is characteristic of “deceptive statements.” Each of these statements told the jury, in the voice of a federal law-enforcement expert, that Hill was lying. That is exactly what Toledo, Charley, Azure, and Scop condemn. There is no meaningful science underlying a claimed ability to detect deception from a suspect’s references to faith or willingness to die rather than be charged; the testimony was merely opinion testimony masquerading as expertise, offered to do what only the jury may do—decide whom to believe.

B.  The error was plain.

The impropriety of a government witness testifying that a defendant is lying is not a novel or unsettled proposition; it has been the law of this Circuit since Toledo and Charley, decided well before Hill’s trial. An error is “plain” when it is “clear or obvious” under current law. Olano, 507 U.S. at 734. Given the clarity of Toledo and Charley, the district court’s failure to exclude Jones’s testimony sua sponte, or the government’s elicitation of it, was plainly erroneous.

C.  The error affected Hill’s substantial rights.

To satisfy the third Olano prong, Hill must show a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the result of the proceeding would have been different. United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004). That showing is easily made here. This was Hill’s second trial; the first ended in a mistrial when the jury deadlocked on identical charges based on largely the same evidence. The government’s case rested on circumstantial proof—GPS tracking to a residence, Hill’s presence there, and his initial false identification to police—but no witness identified Hill as one of the men who entered the bank. In a case this closely balanced, an FBI agent’s repeated, direct assertions that Hill was lying were uniquely likely to tip the scales, particularly because jurors naturally give weight to an expert’s specialized claim to be able to detect deception. There is a reasonable probability the jury would have reached a different verdict absent this testimony.

D.  The error seriously affected the fairness and integrity of the proceedings.

Finally, this Court should exercise its discretion to correct the error because allowing a federal agent to tell a jury, in the guise of expertise, that the defendant is a liar “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736; see United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 632–33 (2002) (applying the same standard). This Court has cautioned that reversal under the fourth prong is warranted where the error implicates core trial safeguards rather than mere technicalities. United States v. Gonzalez-Huerta, 403 F.3d 727, 736–37 (10th Cir. 2005) (en banc). The right to have a jury—not a government witness cloaked in scientific authority—decide credibility is such a safeguard. Permitting this conviction to stand would signal that prosecutors may substitute an agent’s ipse dixit for the jury’s considered judgment.

                                    CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Hill’s conviction should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
