**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed. The Massachusetts Act regulates only speech in the most traditional public forum—the public sidewalk adjacent to reproductive-health facilities—and it does so to protect access, safety, and unimpeded passage. The First Amendment, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, does not disable a State from imposing reasonable place restrictions on speech in a public way. See **Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652 (1925)**; **Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496 (1939)**; **Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569 (1941)**; **Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158 (1944)**; **Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77 (1949)**.

I. **The Act is a permissible time, place, and manner regulation of speech in a public forum.**

This Court has long recognized that public streets and sidewalks are protected forums, but not forums in which all regulation is forbidden. **Hague, 307 U.S. at 515–16**; **Cox, 312 U.S. at 574**. In **Cox**, the Court upheld a licensing scheme for parade-like use of streets, explaining that the State may regulate the use of its streets in the interest of public convenience and order. **312 U.S. at 574–76**. In **Kovacs**, the Court sustained restrictions on loud sound trucks because the government may protect the public’s use of the streets from disruptive modes of expression. **336 U.S. at 86–89**. And in **Prince**, the Court recognized that the liberty of speech may yield when the State acts to protect the welfare and safety of others. **321 U.S. at 166–67**.

The Massachusetts statute fits comfortably within those principles. It does not prohibit petitioners from speaking, leafletting, praying, or counseling. It regulates only where they may stand, leaving open ample means of communication. The record shows that petitioners continue to speak, distribute literature, display signs, and converse outside the clinics. A regulation that leaves open alternative channels is the classic time, place, and manner restriction. See **Cox, 312 U.S. at 574**; **Kovacs, 336 U.S. at 89**.

II. **The law is content-neutral because it regulates location, not viewpoint.**

The statute applies to all persons within the buffer zone, regardless of message, and exempts only those categories necessary to ensure access and safety. That is a permissible distinction drawn by reference to conduct and location, not opinion. The Court has upheld neutral regulations that incidentally burden expression so long as they are aimed at conduct and are not a disguise for censorship. See **Grosjean v. American Press Co., 297 U.S. 233 (1936)**; **Lovell v. City of Griffin, 303 U.S. 444 (1938)**; **Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson, 283 U.S. 697 (1931)**.

Unlike the prior restraints condemned in **Near** and the discriminatory burdens in **Grosjean**, the Act does not target a disfavored idea or suppress publication. It simply reserves a narrow band of sidewalk for ingress and egress. The State’s interest in protecting access to medical facilities is well within the police power recognized in **Prince**, **321 U.S. at 166–67**, and the use of neutral place restrictions has long been sustained where public order and convenience are at stake. **Cox, 312 U.S. at 574–76**; **Kovacs, 336 U.S. at 86–89**.

III. **Hill should not be overruled, and there is no basis to narrow it here.**

The petitioners ask this Court to revisit **Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000)**. But even if the Court were inclined to limit its reasoning, this case does not require overruling it. Hill rested on the principle that the government may regulate targeted speech-related conduct in a public forum by content-neutral means to protect unwilling listeners and ensure access. **530 U.S. at 715–16, 728–30**. That principle is consistent with older precedents recognizing broad authority to regulate the use of streets and sidewalks to secure public order and safety. **Hague, 307 U.S. at 515–16**; **Cox, 312 U.S. at 574–76**; **Kovacs, 336 U.S. at 86–89**; **Prince, 321 U.S. at 166–67**.

The petitioners’ effort to distinguish Hill because Massachusetts uses a fixed buffer zone instead of a floating one goes to degree, not constitutional kind. The State may draw a narrow line around clinic entrances to prevent obstruction and intimidation without forbidding speech altogether. **Cox, 312 U.S. at 574–76**. The Constitution does not require Massachusetts to wait until access is actually blocked before acting to preserve it. **Prince, 321 U.S. at 166–67**.

IV. **The First Circuit’s judgment is consistent with the settled line of public-forum cases.**

The court below correctly recognized that the sidewalks remain open to expression, while the State has drawn a modest boundary to protect passage. That is exactly the sort of accommodation the Court approved when it sustained regulation of expressive use of streets in **Cox**, **312 U.S. 569**, and disruptive street speech in **Kovacs**, **336 U.S. 77**. The First Amendment protects petitioners’ message, but it does not give them an unqualified right to deliver it at a particular spot in a manner that impairs access to a lawful medical facility. **Hague, 307 U.S. at 515–16**; **Cox, 312 U.S. at 574–76**.

For those reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed, and **Hill** should remain intact.
