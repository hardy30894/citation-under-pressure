# ARGUMENT

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that in applying plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), a reviewing court may examine the entire record—including undisputed facts outside the trial evidence—to determine whether an error affected substantial rights or the fairness of judicial proceedings. This Court's plain-error jurisprudence, the structure of Rule 52(b), and sound principles of judicial economy all compel this conclusion.

## I. Plain-Error Review Under Rule 52(b) Requires Examination of the Whole Record, Not Merely the Evidence Presented to the Jury

Rule 52(b) provides that "[a] plain error that affects substantial rights may be considered even though it was not brought to the court's attention." This Court has consistently interpreted the Rule to demand a holistic assessment of the proceedings below. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), the Court explained that the defendant bears the burden of demonstrating that a plain error "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." *Id.* at 734. That inquiry, by its nature, is not artificially cabined to the evidence a jury heard—it asks what actually happened and what would have happened absent the error.

This Court reaffirmed and sharpened that standard in *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), holding that a defendant seeking reversal for plain error must show "a reasonable probability that, but for the error," the outcome would have been different. *Id.* at 83. Critically, this Court in *Dominguez Benitez* looked beyond the four corners of the guilty-plea colloquy to the "record as a whole" in assessing prejudice, including the defendant's failure to dispute the underlying facts establishing his guilt. *Id.* at 85. That approach is irreconcilable with a rule confining appellate courts to only the evidence formally admitted before the fact-finder.

## II. The Rehaif Context Confirms That Courts Must Look to Undisputed Record Facts

The error at issue here—the failure to instruct on and prove knowledge-of-status under *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019)—is precisely the kind of error for which the entire record is probative. In *Rehaif*, this Court held that the Government "must prove both that the defendant knew he possessed a firearm and that he knew he belonged to the relevant category of persons barred from possessing a firearm." *Id.* at 2200. But *Rehaif* said nothing about how courts must evaluate the effect of an omitted knowledge element when a defendant fails to object at trial. Left to fill that gap, courts must apply ordinary plain-error principles—principles that have never restricted reviewing courts to a hermetically sealed trial record.

Where, as here, a defendant's own record of prior felony convictions unambiguously establishes that he knew he was a felon, that undisputed fact is directly relevant to whether the *Rehaif* error affected his "substantial rights" or the "fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 469-70 (1997) (quoting *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732). In *Johnson*, this Court itself looked to matters outside the specific trial evidence—including the overwhelming and essentially uncontroverted nature of the omitted element—to conclude that reversal was unwarranted, explaining it would be "extraordinary" to require reversal where "the evidence supporting materiality was so overwhelming" that the error could not have affected the outcome. *Id.* at 470.

## III. Limiting Review to the Trial Record Would Conflict with the Purposes of Plain-Error Doctrine

The plain-error standard exists to respect principles of finality and to encourage timely objections, while still permitting correction of errors that undermine confidence in the judicial process. See *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129, 134 (2009) (plain-error review encourages "timely objections" and respects "the systemic costs of remedying the error"). This purpose is best served by permitting courts to consider whether an unpreserved error actually mattered in light of everything the record reveals—not by blinding courts to readily available, undisputed facts.

Petitioner's proposed rule—confining review to only what the jury heard—would produce perverse results. A defendant who stipulated to his felon status, or who has an unchallengeable record of multiple felony convictions, could obtain automatic reversal though he suffered no conceivable prejudice, simply because the jury was not formally shown what everyone, including the defendant, already knew. This Court rejected exactly this kind of formalism in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), holding that in conducting plain-error review of a Rule 11 violation, "a reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights." *Id.* at 59. *Vonn*'s holding applies with full force here: there is no principled basis for treating *Rehaif* errors differently from other unpreserved errors subject to Rule 52(b).

## IV. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied This Framework

The court below did precisely what *Vonn*, *Dominguez Benitez*, and *Johnson* require: it examined the entire record, including Petitioner's undisputed history of multiple prior felony convictions, and concluded that no reasonable probability existed that a properly instructed jury would have found Petitioner lacked knowledge of his felon status. That determination was consistent with, indeed compelled by, this Court's precedents. Petitioner's contrary rule finds no support in the text of Rule 52(b) or in this Court's decisions construing it.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
