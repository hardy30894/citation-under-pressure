## ARGUMENT

### I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require States to Admit Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea When a Separate Insanity Defense Is Provided.

Arizona’s rule channeling mental-disease evidence exclusively to the insanity defense is a constitutionally permissible exercise of state authority. The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant the right to introduce all relevant evidence. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42 (1996) (plurality). States have “wide latitude” to define the elements of criminal offenses and to structure the presentation of evidence. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–02 (1977). Arizona has chosen to treat mental disease as a matter of criminal capacity—addressed through the insanity defense—rather than as a factor negating specific intent. This choice is consistent with historical practice. *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 536 (1968) (plurality) (noting that the criminal law has “traditionally” treated mental condition as a question of responsibility, not of mens rea).

In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), this Court upheld a state’s requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, recognizing that states may define the contours of the insanity defense without violating due process. Similarly, in *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877 (1976) (summary affirmance), the Court affirmed a state rule that limited mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense and excluded it from consideration of mens rea. That summary affirmance is binding precedent that Arizona’s rule is constitutional. *See Hicks v. Miranda*, 422 U.S. 332, 344–45 (1975).

### II. The Distinction Between Insanity and Mens Rea Is Constitutionally Sound.

Arizona’s rule does not deprive Clark of a fair opportunity to present a defense. The state has provided a full and fair insanity defense, and Clark was permitted to introduce all his expert evidence in support of that claim. Due process requires only that a defendant have a meaningful opportunity to present a defense, not that he may choose the legal theory under which his evidence is considered. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (right to present defense is not absolute; states may impose reasonable procedural rules).

The rule is analogous to the exclusion of voluntary intoxication evidence to negate mens rea, which this Court upheld in *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 56. There, the plurality reasoned that a state may “define the mental state required for a crime” and may “decide that intoxication is not a defense to a crime.” *Id.* at 49. So too, Arizona may decide that mental disease is not a defense to the mens rea element, but rather a separate affirmative defense. This approach respects the state’s “sovereign power to define the elements of a crime.” *McMillan v. Pennsylvania*, 477 U.S. 79, 85 (1986).

### III. The Rule Does Not Violate Fundamental Fairness.

Clark’s argument that due process requires admission of mental-disease evidence to negate mens rea would effectively convert every insanity defense into a failure-of-proof claim, collapsing the distinction between capacity and intent. This Court has consistently rejected such a result. *See Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 82–83 (1985) (recognizing a right to psychiatric assistance for insanity defense, but not for mens rea); *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 233–34 (1987) (states may place burden of proving affirmative defense on defendant). The rule is a reasonable procedural limitation that does not “offend[] some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445 (1992) (internal quotation marks omitted).

Arizona’s approach is also consistent with the traditional view that mental disease affects criminal responsibility, not the existence of a particular mental state. *See United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 998 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (en banc) (distinguishing between evidence of mental disease for insanity and for mens rea). The Due Process Clause does not require a state to adopt a particular model of criminal culpability. *Powell*, 392 U.S. at 536.

### IV. Conclusion

Because Arizona’s rule is a permissible exercise of state authority, does not deprive Clark of a fair opportunity to present his defense, and is consistent with historical practice and this Court’s precedents, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
