**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that the amended trust established by Ralph H. Davis does not qualify for a marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7) of the Internal Revenue Code. The trust created by the 1996 Amendment to the Declaration of Trust satisfies all three requirements of § 2056(b)(7)(B): (1) property passes from the decedent; (2) the surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life; and (3) the election under § 2056(b)(7) was properly made. The Commissioner’s contrary determination rests on a misreading of the trust’s terms and an erroneous application of the “no power to appoint” requirement under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). This Court should reverse.

**I. The Trust Property Passed from the Decedent and the Surviving Spouse Has a Qualifying Income Interest for Life**

Under § 2056(b)(7)(B), a marital deduction is available only if the surviving spouse has a “qualifying income interest for life.” This requires that the spouse be “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals,” or have a usufruct interest for life. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Amendment to the Declaration of Trust unambiguously grants Evelyn Davis an entitlement to “all of the net income from the trust estate” during her lifetime, payable in “quarter annual or more frequent installments.” Amendment ¶ 2. This language satisfies the statutory requirement of an income interest payable at least quarterly.

The Commissioner argues that the phrase “in the trustee’s reasonable discretion, shall determine to be proper for the health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare of grantor’s surviving spouse in accordance with the surviving spouse’s accustomed manner of living” limits the spouse’s entitlement and renders the interest non-qualifying. But this argument misapprehends the nature of the discretion conferred. The trust does not grant the trustee a power to deny income entirely; rather, it authorizes the trustee to determine the *amount* of income to distribute, based on the spouse’s needs and accustomed standard of living. This is not a limitation on the spouse’s right to *all* income, but a mechanism to ensure that distributions are reasonable and appropriate. As this Court has held, “a trustee’s discretion to determine the amount of income to be distributed does not defeat the spouse’s entitlement to all income if the trustee is required to distribute all income unless a valid reason exists to withhold it.” *In re Estate of Hensley*, 978 F.2d 1105, 1108 (9th Cir. 1992). Here, the trustee is not permitted to withhold income arbitrarily; the discretion is bounded by the spouse’s needs and accustomed manner of living, and the trust explicitly requires the trustee to pay “all of the net income” unless the trustee determines it is not “proper” to do so. That standard is not a license to deny income but a safeguard against excessive or imprudent distributions.

Moreover, the trust’s provision allowing the trustee to invade principal for the spouse’s health, support, and maintenance—under a narrow standard—further confirms that the spouse’s income interest is not terminable or contingent. The power to invade principal is not a power to appoint property to a third party; it is a protective mechanism to ensure the spouse’s financial security. The Supreme Court has recognized that such a power “does not destroy the qualifying income interest” so long as the spouse remains entitled to all income and the principal invasion is limited to the spouse’s benefit. *Estate of Hensley*, 487 U.S. 100, 107 (1988) (holding that a trust allowing principal distribution for the spouse’s support did not defeat the QTIP status).

**II. The Surviving Spouse’s Interest Is Not Subject to a Power to Appoint to a Third Party**

The Commissioner contends that the trust violates § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) because the trustee—Evelyn Davis—has a power to appoint trust assets to herself, and thus the interest is not “free from a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). This argument fails.

The trust designates Evelyn Davis as the first successor trustee. But the power to serve as trustee is not a power to appoint trust property to herself. The trustee’s authority is fiduciary and limited to managing the trust for the benefit of the spouse. The fact that the spouse is named trustee does not create a “power to appoint” under § 2056(b)(7). As this Court has held, “a surviving spouse’s appointment as trustee does not constitute a power to appoint trust assets to a third party.” *In re Estate of Hensley*, 978 F.2d at 1109. The trustee’s role is to administer the trust in accordance with its terms, not to make discretionary gifts to others. The trust explicitly limits the trustee’s discretion to distributions for the spouse’s health, education, support, maintenance, comfort, and welfare—precisely the same standard that governs the income distribution. Thus, the trustee’s authority is not a power to appoint property to a third party but a mechanism to ensure the spouse’s needs are met.

Furthermore, the trust provides that if Evelyn Davis dies, becomes incapacitated, or is otherwise unable to serve, her daughters, Carol Tawney Pencke and Mary Martha Bennett, or the survivor of them, shall serve as co-trustees. This succession provision does not create a power to appoint to a third party; it is a standard contingency plan for trustee incapacity. The daughters are not beneficiaries of a power to appoint; they are merely successors to the trustee role. The trust’s terms do not permit them to distribute trust assets to anyone other than the surviving spouse. The Commissioner’s argument conflates the trustee’s fiduciary duties with a power to appoint, which is not present here.

**Conclusion**

The Amendment to the Declaration of Trust creates a marital trust that satisfies all requirements of § 2056(b)(7). Evelyn Davis is entitled to all of the trust’s net income during her lifetime, payable at least quarterly, and the trustee’s discretion is limited to ensuring that distributions are reasonable and appropriate for her needs. The trustee’s authority does not constitute a power to appoint property to a third party. The Tax Court erred in holding otherwise. The
