**ARGUMENT**

I. THE COURT HAS JURISDICTION TO REVIEW BOND’S CHALLENGE TO THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 18 U.S.C. § 229, AND BOND HAS STANDING TO RAISE A TENTH AMENDMENT CLAIM.

The Third Circuit correctly concluded that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. This Court has long held that “a party seeking to invoke federal judicial power must demonstrate a personal stake in the outcome of the controversy.” *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998). Standing requires not only injury-in-fact but also a causal connection between the injury and the challenged conduct, and a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the harm. Id. at 96–97.

Here, Bond’s claim is not that she was prosecuted under an unconstitutional law in violation of her own rights—she pled guilty and reserved her right to appeal—but that Congress exceeded its constitutional authority by enacting a statute that intrudes upon state sovereignty. Yet, as this Court made clear in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), private parties lack standing to assert a Tenth Amendment violation when no state or its officers are involved. The Court explained: “There is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. Similarly, in *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005), the court held that a private citizen cannot bring a Tenth Amendment challenge where the state has acquiesced in the federal action. The state, not the individual, represents the interests of its citizens in matters of federalism.

Bond does not allege that Pennsylvania refused to prosecute her conduct or that the state declined to enforce its own laws. Indeed, her criminal acts—attempted poisoning with toxic chemicals—would have been subject to prosecution under Pennsylvania’s criminal code. But she chose instead to challenge the federal statute, asserting a generalized grievance about the expansion of federal power. That is insufficient. As this Court stated in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992), “a plaintiff must show that he personally has suffered some actual or threatened injury as a result of the putatively illegal conduct.” A mere assertion that a federal law violates the Tenth Amendment because it regulates conduct traditionally within state control does not satisfy this requirement when the state itself has not objected.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty, not individual rights. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559 (1995); *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 154 (1992). It is the state, not the individual, that bears the burden of protecting its own autonomy. When a state chooses not to intervene, the individual cannot step into the breach. See *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35 (“the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state’s policy of acquiescence”).

Thus, Bond’s claim fails at the threshold. She has no standing to challenge § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds. The Third Circuit properly dismissed the claim for lack of jurisdiction.

II. EVEN IF BOND HAD STANDING, § 229 IS VALIDLY ENACTED UNDER THE TREATY POWER AND THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE.

Assuming arguendo that Bond had standing, the statute remains constitutional. The Constitution grants Congress the power to “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18. This includes the authority to implement treaties ratified by the President and Senate. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). In *Holland*, the Court upheld a federal statute regulating migratory birds, even though such regulation would otherwise fall within the domain of state police powers, because it was enacted pursuant to a treaty. The Court reasoned: “It is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” Id. at 433.

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a binding international agreement ratified by the United States. Congress enacted § 229 to fulfill the nation’s obligations under the treaty. The statute prohibits the use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes—exactly the conduct targeted by the treaty. It does not regulate ordinary commercial activity or local crime; rather, it addresses a global threat to human life and security. The fact that Bond used a chemical weapon against a private individual does not render the statute invalid. The treaty’s purpose is to prevent the proliferation and use of chemical weapons regardless of the actor—state or non-state. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (“Congress may legislate to protect the integrity of the treaty-making process itself.”).

Furthermore, the statute contains sufficient specificity to satisfy due process. It defines “chemical weapon” and “toxic chemical” with precision. *18 U.S.C. § 229F*. The term “knowingly” provides fair notice of culpable conduct. *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). Bond, a trained laboratory technician, knew the dangers of potassium dichromate and deliberately used it to harm another person. Her actions fall squarely within the statute’s reach.
