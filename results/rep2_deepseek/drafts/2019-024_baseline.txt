**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit Erroneously Required Proof That the Plaintiff Subjectively Understood the Legal Significance of Disclosed Facts, Thereby Reading “Actual Knowledge” Out of § 1113(2).**

The three-year ERISA limitations period begins to run when a plaintiff has “actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has held that “actual knowledge” means knowledge of the “underlying conduct” that forms the basis of the claim, not knowledge that the conduct violates ERISA. *See Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633, 648 (2010) (interpreting analogous “actual knowledge” provision in the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act) (“[T]he limitations period begins to run once the plaintiff *actually knows* of the facts giving rise to the claim.”). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach—requiring proof that the plaintiff subjectively understood that the disclosed investments were *imprudent*—conflicts with the text of § 1113(2) and with this Court’s precedent.

**A. The Statute’s Plain Language Focuses on Knowledge of the Breach, Not Legal Characterization.**

Section 1113(2) triggers the limitations period when the plaintiff has “actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” Knowledge of the *breach*—the fiduciary’s conduct—is sufficient; the statute does not require knowledge that the conduct is *actionable*. *See Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985) (“The statute of limitations is triggered by [the plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.”). The Ninth Circuit departed from this settled rule by requiring proof that Sulyma knew the investments were “imprudent.” Pet. App. 22a–23a. That added requirement conflates knowledge of the facts with knowledge of their legal significance, which § 1113(2) does not demand. *See Merck*, 559 U.S. at 648.

**B. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Frustrates Congress’s Intent to Provide a Short Limitations Period for ERISA Claims.**

Congress repealed ERISA’s “constructive knowledge” provision in 1987 to simplify the limitations inquiry. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b). But the Ninth Circuit’s standard effectively resurrects a constructive-knowledge analysis by requiring courts to assess whether the plaintiff *actually appreciated* the import of disclosed information. This standard undermines the statute’s purpose of ensuring “prompt resolution of ERISA claims.” *See Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523, 529 (2015) (acknowledging the importance of limitations periods in ERISA cases). When a defendant has disclosed all relevant information—as Intel did here—the plaintiff cannot avoid the limitations period by ignoring or failing to recall the disclosures. *See Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520–21 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that a plaintiff’s actual knowledge of one breach starts the limitations period for all similar breaches).

**II. Intel’s Extensive Disclosures Provided Sulyma with Actual Knowledge of the Alleged Breach as a Matter of Law.**

Intel disclosed the Funds’ asset allocation, investment strategy, and risks through Fund Fact Sheets, annual disclosures, and website materials. Pet. App. 17a–18a. Sulyma accessed some of this information, and the district court found no genuine dispute that he had actual knowledge of the investments. Pet. App. 3a–4a. The Ninth Circuit disregarded this evidence, holding that Sulyma’s testimony—that he did not “recall” seeing the disclosures and was “unaware” of the investments—created a triable issue. Pet. App. 22a–23a. That holding conflicts with *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007), which instructs courts to “view the facts in the light depicted by the videotape” rather than the nonmoving party’s subjective account where documentary evidence irrefutably shows the opposite. Here, the undisputed record shows that Sulyma had access to and reviewed documents disclosing the alleged breach. His self-serving testimony to the contrary cannot defeat summary judgment. *See Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 586–87 (1986).

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Ruling Undermines the Finality of ERISA Fiduciary Claims and Encourages Gamesmanship.**

The Ninth Circuit’s approach incentivizes plaintiffs to avoid reading disclosures and then claim ignorance to preserve claims indefinitely. This result is antithetical to ERISA’s goal of protecting plan participants through “uniform and consistent regulation.” *Aetna Health Inc. v. Davila*, 542 U.S. 200, 208 (2004). If a plaintiff can reset the limitations clock simply by disregarding or forgetting information, fiduciaries can never achieve finality. This Court should reject a standard that erodes the “actual knowledge” requirement and permits stale claims to proceed. *See CTS Corp. v. Waldburger*, 573 U.S. 1, 12 (2014) (explaining that statutes of repose, like § 1113(2), provide “a substantive right to be free from liability after a certain time”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
