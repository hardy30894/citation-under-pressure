**Argument**

Section 1407(a) is unequivocal: the Judicial Panel may transfer civil actions for “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and “shall” remand each action “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The statute thus cabins the transferee court’s role to pretrial management and reserves trial for the originating district absent a lawful remand. In **Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach, 523 U.S. 26 (1998)**, the Supreme Court held that a transferee court lacks authority to invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial. The Court explained that the command that each transferred action “shall be remanded” is mandatory and leaves no room for a transferee court to keep the case for trial. Id. at 35-40.

Milberg’s contrary position collapses the distinction Congress drew between §§ 1407 and 1404. Section 1404(a) permits a district court to transfer a civil action “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but that general venue statute cannot override § 1407(a)’s specific remand directive. **Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 40-41**. The Court rejected the notion that “pretrial proceedings” include a transferee court’s decision to retain and try the case under § 1404(a), holding that such a construction would nullify the remand command. Id. at 40. Statutes are to be read so that each has effect, and the specific governs the general when Congress has spoken with precision. See **RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank, 566 U.S. 639, 645 (2012)**; **Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 550-51 (1974)**.

The Ninth Circuit’s own pre-Lexecon decision correctly recognized that petitioners raised a serious statutory objection, but it treated the problem as one of expedience rather than authority. The Supreme Court later resolved that question against the transferee court. **Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 34-35**. The Court rejected reliance on local multidistrict rules and judicial practice, holding that “the Panel’s Rule 14(b)” could not authorize what the statute forbids. Id. at 28, 40-41. Agency- or court-made rules cannot expand statutory power. See **United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 695-96 (1974)**; **Sibbach v. Wilson & Co., 312 U.S. 1, 10-11 (1941)**.

Nor can waiver or consent supply authority to retain a transferred case for trial absent the statutory mechanism Congress created. The Supreme Court in **Lexecon** held that the remand obligation is not displaced by a transferee court’s convenience-based desire to finish the case, because “the statutory command is clear” and “shall be remanded” means what it says. 523 U.S. at 34-35. While parties may seek transfer under § 1404(a), that request must be decided by a court with proper statutory authority and cannot be used by the transferee court to convert its limited pretrial assignment into plenary trial jurisdiction. Id. at 40-41.

The government cannot justify this result on efficiency grounds. The Supreme Court acknowledged that multidistrict litigation presents obvious managerial benefits, but held that policy concerns do not permit courts to depart from the text Congress enacted. **Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 42**; see also **Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 538 (2004)** (“It is well established that ‘when the statute’s language is plain, the sole function of the courts—at least where the disposition required by the text is not absurd—is to enforce it according to its terms.’”). Congress chose a scheme that centralizes pretrial proceedings while preserving trial in the transferor district. **Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 40-41**. That choice must be enforced, not revised by judicial convenience.

Because the district court lacked authority to assign the case to itself for trial under § 1404(a), its order must be vacated, and the case remanded to the transferor court as § 1407(a) requires. **Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 42-43**.
