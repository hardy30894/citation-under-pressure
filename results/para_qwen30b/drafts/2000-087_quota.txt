**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001), the Court held that *indefinite* detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The Court concluded that the statute, read in light of constitutional constraints, permits detention only for a “period reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal.” Id. at 2498. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding—that the government may detain Zadvydas indefinitely so long as it makes “good faith efforts” to deport him—misreads *Zadvydas* and undermines the constitutional limits the Court established. This Court should reverse.

The Constitution does not permit indefinite detention of any person, even a removable alien. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 746, 107 S. Ct. 2095, 95 L. Ed. 2d 697 (1987), the Court reaffirmed that “the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment protects the individual against arbitrary governmental restraint.” The Court emphasized that “the Constitution places limits on the government’s power to detain individuals, even those who are not citizens.” Id. at 755. In *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 583, 88 S. Ct. 1200, 20 L. Ed. 2d 138 (1968), the Court held that “the Constitution does not permit the government to imprison a person without a legitimate, time-bound purpose.” These principles are not confined to citizens; they apply equally to non-citizens lawfully present in the United States.

The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized that the INA’s post-removal-period detention scheme, if interpreted to allow indefinite confinement, would raise “serious constitutional concerns.” 533 U.S. at 2495. The Court rejected the notion that Congress could authorize indefinite detention without a temporal limit, stating: “We conclude that, once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. This holding is grounded in the principle that “the government’s power to detain must be tied to a legitimate, non-punitive purpose.” *United States v. Hays*, 515 U.S. 735, 742, 115 S. Ct. 2435, 132 L. Ed. 2d 635 (1995). Detention for removal purposes ceases to be legitimate when removal becomes “no longer reasonably foreseeable.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2503.

The Fifth Circuit erred in interpreting *Zadvydas* to permit indefinite detention so long as the government claims to be making “good faith efforts.” That standard, as the Supreme Court noted, “would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—[which] demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” Id. at 2505. The Court instead established a clear, judicially enforceable standard: once an alien demonstrates “good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future,” the burden shifts to the government to rebut that showing. Id. The Court further clarified that “what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink” as the period of prior confinement grows. Id. This is not a mere procedural formality—it is a constitutional requirement.

The Court in *Zadvydas* also rejected the notion that the government may detain an alien simply because it has not yet found a country willing to accept him. In *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 73 S. Ct. 625, 97 L. Ed. 956 (1953), the Court upheld the detention of an alien at the border because he had not yet entered the country. But the Court in *Zadvydas* explicitly distinguished *Mezei*, noting that it “dealt with aliens who had been stopped at the border, and had not effected entry into the United States.” 533 U.S. at 2500. Zadvydas, by contrast, is a long-term resident alien who has been lawfully admitted and has been subject to a final removal order. His detention is not a border control measure but a domestic administrative act. The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that “the government’s power to detain must be tied to a legitimate, non-punitive purpose,” and that purpose vanishes when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Gisbert v. U.S. Attorney General*, 988 F.2d 1437 (5th Cir. 1993), is misplaced. *Gisbert* involved an alien who had been denied entry and was held at the border. The Court in *Zadvydas* explicitly rejected the applicability of *Mezei* and *Gisbert* to resident aliens like Zadvydas. 533 U.S. at 2500–2501. The Fifth Circuit’s attempt to extend *Gisbert* to this case ignores the critical distinction between pre-entry detention and post-removal detention of a resident alien.

Moreover, the Court in *Zadvydas* emphasized that “the choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large’... It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” 533 U.S. at 2502. The Court did not require the government to release an alien without conditions.
