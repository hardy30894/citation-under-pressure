**ARGUMENT**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. The Pressls’ complaint raises only a state-law dispute over the scope of a flowage easement. Neither 28 U.S.C. § 1331 nor 16 U.S.C. § 825p confers jurisdiction, and the district court erred in denying remand and dismissing the complaint.

**I. The well-pleaded complaint rule requires remand.**

Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, and removal is proper only if the plaintiff’s complaint, as it stands at the time of removal, states a claim arising under federal law. *Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987). The plaintiff is master of the complaint; a case may not be removed on the basis of a federal defense. *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust*, 463 U.S. 1, 10 (1983). Here, the Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaratory judgment that APCO’s demands exceed the rights granted by the flowage easement. The easement is a private contract between the Pressls’ predecessors and APCO. The complaint does not mention the Federal Power Act, FERC, or any federal statute. It raises only state property law. Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, no federal question appears on the face of the complaint. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908).

The district court erred by looking beyond the complaint to APCO’s FERC license. A federal defense—that the FERC license or the Federal Power Act preempts or otherwise affects the Pressls’ rights—cannot support removal. *Caterpillar*, 482 U.S. at 393. Nor can the Declaratory Judgment Act create jurisdiction where none exists. *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 671–72 (1950). The Pressls’ request for a declaration of their rights under the easement is not a federal claim.

**II. Section 825p does not confer jurisdiction.**

16 U.S.C. § 825p grants district courts jurisdiction over “suits brought to enforce any liability or duty created by” the Federal Power Act. The Pressls’ suit does not seek to enforce any duty created by the FPA. It seeks to enforce the terms of a private easement. The FPA does not create a private right of action for landowners to challenge a licensee’s demands regarding dock construction. *See California ex rel. Lockyer v. FERC*, 383 F.3d 1006, 1013 (9th Cir. 2004) (FERC’s jurisdiction is limited to matters arising under the FPA). The mere fact that the property lies within a FERC project boundary does not transform a state contract dispute into a federal case. The district court’s reliance on § 825p was therefore misplaced.

**III. Any federal issue is not substantial enough for jurisdiction.**

In limited circumstances, a state-law claim may arise under federal law if the complaint necessarily raises a disputed and substantial federal issue that a federal forum may entertain without disturbing the federal-state balance. *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). But the federal issue must be “substantial” and “actually disputed.” *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 258 (2013). Here, the central dispute is the meaning of the flowage easement under Virginia law. The FERC license is at most background. The Pressls do not challenge any FERC order, and their rights under the easement do not depend on the meaning of any federal statute. This case is far more like *Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 817 (1986), where a state tort claim that incorporated a federal standard was held not to arise under federal law, than like *Grable*, where the federal issue was the essential element of the claim. *See also Empire HealthChoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006) (no federal jurisdiction where the dispute is fundamentally one of state law). The interpretation of a flowage easement is a matter of state property law, and the federal interest, if any, is too attenuated to support jurisdiction.

**IV. The district court lacked authority to dismiss on the merits.**

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, it had no authority to dismiss the Pressls’ complaint on the merits. A court must first determine its jurisdiction. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998). The district court’s analysis of the easement under Virginia law was an exercise of jurisdiction the court did not possess. The proper course was to remand the case to state court. 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the district court’s order denying remand and dismissing the complaint should be vacated, and the case remanded with instructions to remand to the state court.
