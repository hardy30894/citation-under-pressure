I. The District Court Erred in Designating Furlow an Armed Career Criminal and Career Offender.

A defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal only upon three prior convictions for a "violent felony" or "serious drug offense." 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). A defendant qualifies as a career offender only upon two prior convictions for a "crime of violence" or "controlled substance offense." USSG § 4B1.1(a). Courts determine whether a prior conviction meets either definition by applying the categorical approach, which examines the elements of the offense of conviction rather than the facts underlying it. Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). If the elements of the state offense are broader than the generic federal definition, the conviction cannot serve as a predicate. Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013).

Furlow's 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine does not satisfy the ACCA's definition of a "serious drug offense," which requires an offense "involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance" carrying a maximum term of ten years or more. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). Nor does it satisfy the Guidelines definition of a "controlled substance offense," which requires an offense "punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year" that prohibits the manufacture, distribution, or possession with intent to distribute a controlled substance. USSG § 4B1.2(b). South Carolina law criminalizes distribution of a controlled substance without regard to the quantity or the defendant's knowledge of the substance's identity, rendering its elements broader than the federal definitions. Because the statute is indivisible, the modified categorical approach does not apply. Mathis v. United States, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2253 (2016). The conviction therefore cannot count as a predicate under either provision.

Furlow's 2008 Georgia convictions for first-degree arson likewise fail to qualify as an enumerated "violent felony" under the ACCA, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), or "crime of violence" under the Guidelines, USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). Both provisions enumerate "arson" as a predicate. The generic definition of arson requires the malicious burning of the property of another. The Georgia statute, however, reaches the burning of one's own property when done with intent to defraud an insurer. Because the elements of the Georgia offense sweep more broadly than the generic offense, the convictions do not constitute predicates. See Taylor, 495 U.S. at 599.

Without these convictions, Furlow lacks the requisite number of predicates. The district court's designations were therefore erroneous.
