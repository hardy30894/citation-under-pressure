# ARGUMENT

**An Uncounseled Tribal-Court Conviction, Valid When Entered, May Be Used to Satisfy the Predicate-Offense Element of 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) Without Offending the Sixth Amendment**

## I. Nichols Establishes That a Valid Uncounseled Misdemeanor Conviction May Be Used in Later Proceedings

This Court's decision in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), forecloses the Ninth Circuit's holding below. *Nichols* held that "an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott* because no prison term was imposed," may later be used "to enhance punishment." *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47 (citing *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). Under *Scott*, "the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to uncounseled state or federal proceedings in which a sentence of imprisonment is not imposed." *Scott*, 440 U.S. at 372-73. Because the conviction is constitutionally valid the moment it is entered, nothing in the Sixth Amendment prevents its subsequent use.

*Nichols* expressly rejected the contrary approach this Court had taken in *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980) (per curiam)—an opinion this Court characterized as having "baffled and divided the lower courts," *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746, because it rested on no coherent rationale, but only on "reasons stated in [its] [three] concurring opinions," *id.* at 743-44 (quoting *Baldasar*, 446 U.S. at 224). *Nichols* abandoned *Baldasar*'s approach and adopted the bright-line rule that validity, not subsequent use, is what matters under the Sixth Amendment. This rule tracks the Court's earlier recognition, in *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), that only a conviction obtained in violation of the right to counsel—there, an uncounseled *felony* conviction subject to mandatory appointment under *Gideon v. Wainwright*—is void and therefore unusable in later proceedings, including under a recidivist statute. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115. Where, as here, no Sixth Amendment violation occurred in the first instance, *Burgett*'s bar does not apply.

## II. Tribal-Court Convictions Are Categorically Valid Because the Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply in Tribal Court

The Ninth Circuit's error is compounded by the unique posture of tribal-court proceedings. The Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, simply does not govern tribal courts. As this Court has explained, it is "beyond dispute that the 'Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.'" *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Tribal courts instead operate under the procedural protections Congress itself has prescribed in the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303. A tribal-court conviction obtained in full compliance with the ICRA is not merely "valid" in the *Nichols*/*Scott* sense—it could never have violated the Sixth Amendment at all, because that Amendment has no application to the proceeding. It follows a fortiori from *Nichols* that such a conviction may be used to satisfy a predicate-offense element: if a state-court misdemeanor conviction untainted by any Sixth Amendment violation may be used under *Nichols*, then a tribal-court conviction, to which the Sixth Amendment never even attaches, stands on at least as strong footing.

Respect for tribal adjudications as a matter of comity confirms this conclusion. Federal courts have long recognized that "[c]omity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts," and that federal courts "must also be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems." *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir. 1997); see also *Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (extending comity to tribal courts). This Court has likewise cautioned against federal-court intrusion "needlessly on tribal self-government." *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978). By treating tribal convictions as constitutionally suspect unless they mirror federal procedure, the decision below inverts these principles and imposes exactly the kind of "judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance" that *Wilson* forbids. 127 F.3d at 811.

## III. Section 117(a) Punishes Only the Present Offense, Consistent With Recidivist-Statute Doctrine

Nor does the use of prior tribal convictions offend *Winship*'s beyond-a-reasonable-doubt requirement in the way the panel feared. As this Court's recidivism jurisprudence recognizes, a recidivist enhancement or element "penaliz[es] only the last offense committed by the defendant." *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747. The predicate-offense element of § 117(a) requires the government to prove only the historical fact that a final conviction was entered—not to relitigate the fairness of the tribal proceeding. That fact is reliably established by the tribal-court judgment itself, just as a certified record of conviction sufficed in *Burgett* and *Parke v. Raley*, 506 U.S. 20 (1993), which held that state courts may presume the regularity of final judgments absent affirmative evidence of invalidity. *Id.* at 29-30.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
