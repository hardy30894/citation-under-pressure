# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses, and Excluding Religious Worship Is a Permissible Definition of That Forum's Boundaries, Not Viewpoint Discrimination.

A school building is not a street or park held "immemorially . . . for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is a facility of limited and specific dedication—to the education of children during the day and, after hours, to social, civic and recreational activities benefiting the community, as its Community Use Policy provides. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains broad authority to define the boundaries of the access it grants. As this Court has explained in upholding the exclusion of demonstrators from the grounds surrounding a jailhouse, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). A government need not open every corner of its property to every use citizens might wish to make of it merely because it has opened the property for some limited purposes. *Id.* Just as the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held—in an opinion this Court adopted—that a legislature may regulate the use of public grounds no more an infringement of the rights of a member of the public than for the owner of a private house to forbid a particular use of his own property, *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897), Milford may exclude religious worship and instruction from a forum it opened only for secular civic and recreational purposes.

That line—between teaching morals and character generally, which Milford permits, and religious worship, instruction, and proselytizing, which it does not—is a reasonable, content-based limitation on the forum's subject matter, not an act of viewpoint suppression. This Court has long recognized that reasonable, evenhanded regulation of the use of public property is consistent with the First Amendment so long as it is not a subterfuge for suppressing particular ideas. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576 (1941), this Court upheld a licensing scheme that afforded the licensing authority discretion to define the rights of applicants "with regard only to considerations of time, place and manner so as to conserve the public convenience." Milford's Community Use Policy operates on the same principle: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith or sect might wish to conduct it. The Good News Club's proposed meeting, which the record shows opens and closes in prayer, includes a "challenge and invitation" inviting children to accept Christ as their personal savior, and culminates in a recited sinner's prayer, is not the teaching of morals from a religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of the very kind the Policy reserves against. Excluding it from a limited forum dedicated to civic and recreational activity is a permissible definition of the forum's boundaries, not discrimination against a viewpoint on a subject otherwise open.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Involving School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that permitting the Club's activities in its facility would create Establishment Clause difficulties was reasonable and sufficient justification. This Court has repeatedly cautioned that public schools occupy a uniquely sensitive position in Establishment Clause doctrine because they involve compulsory, impressionable, school-age children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962), and the state may not sponsor devotional religious exercises within the school setting, *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963). The concern is heightened, not diminished, when the activity takes place in the school building immediately after the close of the school day, using the same classrooms, and directed at the same young children who attend that school. As this Court held in striking down the use of public school classrooms for religious instruction conducted by outside instructors during released time on school premises, use of "tax-supported public school buildings" for such purposes offends the Establishment Clause even absent formal compulsion. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948).

To be sure, this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not require the state to be actively hostile to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But *Zorach* permitted released-time programs precisely because they occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and the Establishment Clause commands governmental neutrality in matters of religion. *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 103–04 (1968). Given the Club's own description of its activities as prayer, scripture memorization, and religious conversion directed at grade-school children on school premises, Milford could reasonably conclude that hosting the Club would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, and *McCollum* forbid. The exclusion was therefore justified on this independent ground as well.
