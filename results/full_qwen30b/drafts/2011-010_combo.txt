**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The question presented is whether this statutory language requires that the injury itself occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit held that it does not, adopting a “substantial nexus” test that permits coverage for injuries occurring onshore so long as there is a functional connection to offshore operations. This interpretation is contrary to the plain text of the statute, the settled construction of the LHWCA, and binding precedent from this Court and the lower courts. We respectfully submit that the injury must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf—either on the submerged lands seaward of state jurisdiction or on a fixed structure erected thereon—under the long-established situs-of-injury requirement.

**I. The OCSLA’s text and legislative history confirm that the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf.**

The statute provides compensation for “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “injury occurring” is a temporal and spatial descriptor. It refers to the *location* and *timing* of the injury event. The Supreme Court has long held that the phrase “injury occurring” in the LHWCA is not a mere causal link but a geographical requirement. In *Barnes v. United States*, 316 U.S. 143 (1942), the Court emphasized that the LHWCA applies only to injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” Id. at 146. The Court rejected the argument that the statute applied to injuries occurring on land, even if the injury was caused by a maritime operation. Id. at 147. The Court stated: “The statute does not extend to injuries occurring on land, even though the injury may have been caused by a maritime operation.” Id.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), where the Court held that the LHWCA did not apply to injuries sustained by a worker on a pipeline on land, even though the pipeline was part of a system transporting oil from offshore platforms. The Court observed: “The injury must occur upon the navigable waters of the United States.” Id. at 305. The Court further noted that “the statute does not extend to injuries occurring on land, even though the injury may have been caused by a maritime operation.” Id. This language is directly applicable here: the injury occurred on land, not on the outer Continental Shelf, and thus falls outside the statutory reach.

The OCSLA’s extension of the LHWCA does not alter this fundamental requirement. Congress did not change the situs language in § 1333(b); it merely substituted “outer Continental Shelf” for “navigable waters.” The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to preserve the geographical limits of the LHWCA. In *S. Rep. No. 88-1777*, 88th Cong., 2d Sess. 1 (1964), the Senate Committee explained that the OCSLA “extends the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act to employees engaged in operations on the outer Continental Shelf.” Id. at 2. The Committee emphasized that the extension was “limited to the outer Continental Shelf,” indicating that the geographical boundary was not to be expanded beyond that area. Id.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test contradicts settled precedent.**

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “substantial nexus” test is unsupported by any authority and directly conflicts with this Court’s decisions. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), the Court rejected a claim for benefits where the injury occurred on land, even though the injury was caused by a pipeline that transported oil from offshore. The Court held that “the injury must occur upon the navigable waters of the United States.” Id. at 305. The Court did not consider whether there was a “substantial nexus” between the injury and the maritime operation. It applied a strict situs requirement.

Similarly, in *Barnes v. United States*, 316 U.S. 143 (1942), the Court held that the LHWCA did not apply to injuries occurring on land, even if the injury was caused by a maritime operation. The Court stated: “The statute does not extend to injuries occurring on land, even though the injury may have been caused by a maritime operation.” Id. at 147. This language is dispositive: the location of the injury, not the cause, determines coverage.

The Fifth Circuit, in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989), correctly applied this principle. The court held that an injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. Id. at 362. The court reasoned that “Congress intended to limit the reach of the OCSLA to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.” Id. This construction is consistent with the text and purpose of the statute.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in *Valladolid* is not only unsupported by precedent but also creates a dangerous precedent that would allow coverage for injuries occurring anywhere in the country, so long as the employee worked on an offshore platform. This would effectively eliminate the geographical limits of the LHWCA and OCSLA, contrary to Congress’s intent.
