ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” This language limits assimilation to circumstances in which federal law leaves a void. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court explained that Congress enacted the statute because “federal law, because of its limited application to the structures, would be inadequate,” id. at 357, and therefore “state law is adopted as surrogate federal law,” id. at 357. The Court emphasized that the Act treats fixed platforms “as though they were federal enclaves in an upland State,” id. at 355, and that state law fills only those areas “where federal law does not apply,” id. at 366.

This gap-filling rule follows from the statute’s text and from the established treatment of federal enclaves. In United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court confirmed that assimilation statutes borrow state law solely where Congress has not otherwise provided a complete body of federal rules. The same principle governs here: the Fair Labor Standards Act supplies comprehensive wage-and-hour regulation on the outer Continental Shelf. Because that federal scheme is not silent, California law has no role to play as surrogate federal law.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—would convert the statutory phrase “to the extent” into surplusage and would permit state law to operate even where Congress has already legislated. Rodrigue rejected that approach, holding instead that state law is borrowed only when federal law leaves the subject “inadequately” addressed. 395 U.S. at 357. The district court therefore correctly dismissed the complaint.
