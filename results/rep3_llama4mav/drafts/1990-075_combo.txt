The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The Constitution grants Congress the power to make laws, but it also allows Congress to seek assistance from other branches of government. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, Congress may delegate authority to another branch when it lays down "an intelligible principle" to guide the delegatee's actions (J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). 

The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General when temporarily scheduling substances. The statute lists specific factors to be considered, such as the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health (21 U.S.C. § 811(c)). This is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Fahey v. Mallonee, where the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board because the statute provided "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the field being regulated (332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947)).

The Attorney General's authority to temporarily schedule substances is also limited by the requirement that the substance pose "an imminent hazard to the public safety" (21 U.S.C. § 811(h)). This limitation is similar to the one upheld in Yakus v. United States, where the Court sustained a delegation of authority to the Price Administrator to regulate prices during wartime because the statute provided a clear standard for the Administrator to follow (321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944)).

Furthermore, the CSA's provision precluding judicial review of the Attorney General's temporary scheduling orders does not render the delegation unconstitutional. As the Supreme Court held in Carlson v. Landon, the availability of judicial review is not a determining factor in assessing the constitutionality of a delegation (342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952)). The Court has also upheld delegations where judicial review was limited or unavailable, as in United States v. Rock Royal Co-operative, where the Court sustained a delegation of authority to the Secretary of Agriculture despite limited judicial review (307 U.S. 533, 577 (1939)).

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the DEA is also authorized. The CSA grants the Attorney General broad authority to delegate his functions to other officials (21 U.S.C. § 871(a)). This is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, where the Court recognized that an agency head may delegate authority to subordinates unless Congress has explicitly prohibited it (315 U.S. 357, 361 (1942)). 

In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, the Supreme Court invalidated delegations of power due to a lack of clear standards (293 U.S. 388 (1935); 295 U.S. 495 (1935)). However, the CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, and the delegation is therefore constitutional. The Court has also upheld delegations in other cases, such as Field v. Clark, where the Court sustained a delegation of authority to the President to impose tariffs (143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892)), and Buttfield v. Stranahan, where the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the Secretary of the Treasury to regulate tea imports (192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904)).

In conclusion, the CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General is constitutional, and the Attorney General's sub-delegation to the DEA is authorized. The statute provides sufficient guidance and limitations to ensure that the delegated authority is exercised in a constitutional manner.
