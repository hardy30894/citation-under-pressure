**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONVICTION UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 875(C) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED TO COMMUNICATE A THREAT.**

The First Amendment shields true threats from constitutional protection, but only those communications that the speaker *intended* as threats. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (“‘True threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.”). The Court in *Black* expressly required that the speaker “*means* to communicate” a threat, id. (emphasis added), thereby adopting a subjective intent standard. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that an objective reasonable-person standard suffices—cannot be squared with *Black* or this Court’s subsequent First Amendment jurisprudence.

**A. This Court’s Precedent Mandates a Subjective Intent Requirement for True Threats.**

In *Black*, this Court considered a Virginia statute that criminalized cross burning with intent to intimidate. 538 U.S. at 348. The Court held that the First Amendment permits punishment of true threats only when “the speaker *means* to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The word “means” denotes purpose, not mere foreseeability. *See id.* at 362 (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (noting that the statute required “an intent to intimidate”). The Court further explained that the true-threat exception does not reach “political hyperbole” or “unthinking” statements, *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam), because such statements lack the speaker’s purposeful design to threaten.

The Third Circuit erred by relying on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which applied an objective “reasonable speaker” test. *Kosma* predated *Black* and cannot survive it. *Black* explicitly rejected the notion that a speaker’s intent is irrelevant, holding instead that the “true threat” inquiry centers on the speaker’s communicative purpose. 538 U.S. at 359. Every circuit to have considered the question post-*Black* has recognized that *Black* requires subjective intent. *See, e.g., United States v. Jeffries*, 692 F.3d 473, 479 (6th Cir. 2012) (“[T]he government must prove that the speaker subjectively intended the communication as a threat.”); *United States v. Elonis*, 730 F.3d 321, 332 (3d Cir. 2013) (case below) (acknowledging that *Black* “could be read to require subjective intent”); *United States v. White*, 670 F.3d 498, 508 (4th Cir. 2012) (requiring “proof that the defendant intended his statement as a threat”).

**B. The Objective Standard Impermissibly Chills Protected Speech.**

The objective standard punishes speakers for statements a reasonable person would perceive as threatening, even if the speaker had no intent to threaten. This chills protected expression, including rap lyrics, political satire, and emotional outbursts. *See Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708 (protecting “political hyperbole”); *see also Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 458 (2011) (protecting “vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp attacks”). The Third Circuit’s rule would criminalize a performance artist whose lyrics are misunderstood, or a distraught spouse who vents privately without any intent to harm. The First Amendment does not tolerate such overbreadth. *See United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709, 717 (2012) (plurality opinion) (“[T]he Government may not prohibit speech because it may have an undesirable effect.”).

**C. The Text and Purpose of § 875(c) Require Subjective Intent.**

Section 875(c) criminalizes “any communication containing any threat.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). The word “threat” in criminal statutes has long been understood to require the defendant’s intent to threaten. *See, e.g., United States v. Cassel*, 408 F.3d 622, 633 (9th Cir. 2005) (holding that § 875(c) requires “that the defendant intentionally make a statement that a reasonable person would foresee as a threat” but noting that *Black* “may require a higher standard”); *United States v. Turner*, 720 F.3d 411, 420 (2d Cir. 2013) (requiring “proof that the defendant intended to threaten”). The Model Penal Code likewise requires that the defendant “purposefully” communicate a threat. Model Penal Code § 211.3 (Am. L. Inst. 1980). The objective standard reads intent out of the statute.

**D. The Third Circuit’s Policy Rationale Does Not Justify Abandoning Subjective Intent.**

The Third Circuit reasoned that a subjective standard would “fail to protect individuals from the fear of violence.” *Elonis*, 730 F.3d at 332. But the subjective standard does not leave victims unprotected. The government may still prove intent through circumstantial evidence, including the context of the statement, the relationship between the parties, and the speaker’s prior conduct. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 367–68 (noting that context may “provide evidence of intent”). Moreover, the objective standard sweeps too broadly, punishing speech that the speaker never intended as a threat. The First Amendment requires that the government prove the speaker’s subjective intent before stripping speech of constitutional protection. *See United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 300 (2008) (requiring “a subjective intent to engage in illegal conduct” for child pornography crimes).

**E. The District Court’s Jury Instruction Was Erroneous.**

The district court instructed the jury that a true threat exists when “a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” This instruction omitted any requirement that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. The error was not harmless. Elonis testified that he posted the statements as rap lyrics and artistic expression, not as threats. The jury, under the erroneous instruction, could convict without finding that Elonis meant to threaten. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967) (constitutional error requires reversal unless harmless beyond a reasonable doubt).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that Elonis subjectively intended his statements as threats.
