The District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. Under Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a class action may be maintained only if "the court finds that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating the controversy." The predominance requirement is not met here because the plaintiffs' damages model fails to measure damages attributable to the alleged antitrust violation.

In Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), this Court held that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence and arguments in making the class certification decision. The Court emphasized that "the requirements set out in Rule 23 are not mere pleading rules" and that "an overlap between a class certification requirement and the merits of a claim is no reason to decline to resolve relevant disputes when necessary to determine whether a class certification requirement is met." Id. at 317.

The plaintiffs' damages expert, Dr. James McClave, presented a model that purported to measure damages on a class-wide basis. However, the model failed to isolate damages attributable to the alleged antitrust violation, namely Comcast's clustering scheme. Instead, the model measured damages based on four theories, only one of which was related to the clustering scheme. The District Court erred in accepting this model, as it did not provide a reliable method for measuring damages attributable to the alleged antitrust violation.

The Supreme Court has held that a class cannot be certified without a reliable method for measuring damages. In Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, the Court is likely to follow its previous decisions, such as Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156 (1974), which emphasized the importance of a rigorous analysis in class certification decisions. The Court has also noted that a district court's decision to certify a class is subject to review for abuse of discretion, as seen in Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 131 S. Ct. 2541 (2011).

Here, the District Court's decision to certify the class without adequate evidence of a reliable damages model was an abuse of discretion. The Court should reverse the certification decision because the plaintiffs failed to provide a damages model that measures damages attributable to the alleged antitrust violation. As the Supreme Court stated in Anderson v. Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573-574 (1985), a reviewing court may not reverse a district court's finding of fact unless it is "clearly erroneous." However, in this case, the District Court's finding was clearly erroneous because it was based on an unreliable damages model.

Therefore, the Court should reverse the District Court's certification decision and remand the case for further proceedings. The plaintiffs must provide a reliable method for measuring damages attributable to the alleged antitrust violation before the class can be certified.
