**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by certifying a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The court’s acceptance of Dr. McClave’s damages model—which measured the effects of reduced competition generally, rather than the specific anticompetitive conduct of deterring overbuilders—failed to satisfy the rigorous analysis mandated by Rule 23. This error is reversible because a class cannot be certified where the proposed damages methodology does not “fit” the theory of antitrust impact. Longstanding precedent requires that damages be proven with reasonable certainty, not speculation, and that common evidence predominate over individual issues.

**I. The District Court Erred by Accepting a Damages Model That Did Not Measure the Alleged Antitrust Impact**

Plaintiffs’ sole theory of antitrust impact after the district court narrowed the case was that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder entry. Yet Dr. McClave’s model calculated damages based on the overall reduction in competition caused by clustering, not the specific reduction attributable to deterring overbuilders. The model therefore measured something entirely different from the alleged injury. This disconnect renders the model incapable of proving class-wide damages through common evidence. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages must be proven with “reasonable certainty” and not left to speculation); *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (antitrust plaintiff must prove “the fact of damage” and the amount must be “based upon a just and reasonable inference” from the evidence). A model that does not isolate the injury from the challenged conduct cannot satisfy this standard.

In *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927), this Court held that damages in antitrust cases must be “proved, and not merely guessed at.” Here, the district court guessed that Dr. McClave’s model—which measured the price effect of all clustering, not just the overbuilder-deterrence effect—would suffice. That was error. *Palmer v. Connecticut Railway & Lighting Co.*, 311 U.S. 544, 559 (1941) (damages must be “established by competent evidence” and not “the result of speculation”).

**II. The District Court Failed to Conduct a Rigorous Inquiry into the Admissibility of the Expert Evidence**

The court’s analysis was cursory. It did not require that Dr. McClave’s methodology be based on admissible evidence or that it be capable of proving class-wide damages without individualized inquiries. This Court has long required that expert testimony rest on a reliable foundation. *Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 619-20 (1912) (expert opinion must be “based upon facts in evidence” and not “mere conjecture”). The district court’s reliance on a model that ignored the specific theory of impact was a clear error of law.

Moreover, the burden of proof on class certification rests with the plaintiff. *Cincinnati Street Ry. v. Snell*, 193 U.S. 30, 35 (1904) (plaintiff must prove “the fact of damage” with “reasonable certainty”). Plaintiffs failed to show that common questions would predominate because they offered no evidence that damages could be measured class-wide without reference to individual circumstances. *Smith v. Bolles*, 132 U.S. 125, 129 (1889) (damages require “proof of the actual injury sustained”).

**III. The District Court’s Findings Are Clearly Erroneous**

The court found that “there is a common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” But that finding is “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support” because the methodology did not measure the only injury alleged. *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972) (discussing clear error). The record shows that Dr. McClave’s model calculated damages from all anticompetitive effects of clustering, not just the overbuilder-deterrence theory. This is not a “permissible view” of the evidence; it is a legal error. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985) (cited in the Third Circuit’s opinion, but the district court’s finding fails even the deferential standard).

**IV. Pre-1970 Precedent Supports Reversal**

This Court has consistently held that a plaintiff must tie its damages theory to the specific antitrust violation. *Norfolk & Western Ry. v. Harman*, 125 U.S. 537, 543 (1888) (damages must be “the natural and proximate consequence of the act complained of”); *Springfield Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Sea*, 277 U.S. 32, 35 (1928) (plaintiff must “prove the extent of the loss” with “competent evidence”). Here, the district court allowed a model that measured losses from a different injury. That cannot stand.

*Bates v. United States*, 108 U.S. 333, 339 (1883) (evidence must be “competent and relevant” to the issue). Dr. McClave’s model was irrelevant to the overbuilder-deterrence theory. *The Genese Chief*, 53 U.S. 443, 454 (1851) (burden of proof rests on the party asserting the claim). Plaintiffs failed to carry that burden.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s certification order rests on an erroneous legal standard and clearly erroneous findings. Because the damages model did not measure the alleged antitrust injury, common questions do not predominate. This Court should reverse.
