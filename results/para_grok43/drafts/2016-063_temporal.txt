The Merit Systems Protection Board’s dismissal of a case on jurisdictional grounds does not alter the forum for judicial review when the employee has alleged that an adverse action rested on discrimination prohibited by federal statute. Such a claim arises under the laws of the United States and therefore falls within the original jurisdiction of the district courts. Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 681 (1946) (a suit “arises under” federal law whenever the complaint is drawn to seek recovery under the Constitution or laws of the United States).

The distinction between a ruling on the merits and a threshold determination that the Board lacks power to proceed is immaterial to the choice of reviewing court. Where the employee has invoked a federal right, the district court possesses authority to examine the entire controversy, including any antecedent question of administrative jurisdiction. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54 (1932) (the judicial power extends to “all matters arising under the Constitution and laws of the United States,” including determinations preliminary to the merits). A contrary rule would fragment federal-question litigation and force piecemeal adjudication in a specialized appellate tribunal never intended to serve as the sole forum for statutory discrimination claims.

The same principle governs review of administrative orders that rest on jurisdictional findings. When an agency concludes it has no power to act, the reviewing court must still apply the standards supplied by the governing federal statute; that inquiry belongs in the court of original jurisdiction over the federal claim. St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States, 298 U.S. 38, 51 (1936) (judicial review encompasses “the question whether the administrative body has acted within its statutory authority”). Because petitioner’s complaint asserts that the suspension and retirement were the product of race, age, and disability discrimination, the case remains one “arising under” federal law even after the Board’s jurisdictional ruling. Gully v. First Nat’l Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936).

Consequently, the petition for review must be heard in the district court.
