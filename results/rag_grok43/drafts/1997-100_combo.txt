The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). It applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). An appellate reversal for evidentiary insufficiency operates as an acquittal barring retrial. Sapir v. United States, 348 U.S. 373, 374 (1955) (per curiam); Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957). The same rule governs when a jury’s silence on one count constitutes an acquittal. Cichos v. Indiana, 385 U.S. 76, 78 (1966).

These protections extend to determinations of prior convictions used for sentence enhancement. McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311, 313 (1901). A finding that evidence fails to establish the requisite prior conviction is therefore equivalent to an acquittal on that allegation. When the prosecution fails to prove the enhancement beyond a reasonable doubt in the initial proceeding, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes a second attempt. See United States v. Ewell, 383 U.S. 116, 121 (1966) (recognizing that reversal for legal error ordinarily permits retrial, but distinguishing insufficiency of proof).

The Clause likewise bars successive prosecutions that would subject a defendant to multiple punishments for the same underlying conduct. Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155, 158 (1900). Allowing retrial of the prior-conviction allegation after an appellate determination of evidentiary insufficiency would expose the defendant to precisely that risk. Sigler v. Losieau, 396 U.S. 988 (1969); Walker v. Wainwright, 390 U.S. 335, 336 (1968) (per curiam). Because the initial proceeding ended in a determination tantamount to acquittal on the enhancement, the State may not relitigate the allegation.
