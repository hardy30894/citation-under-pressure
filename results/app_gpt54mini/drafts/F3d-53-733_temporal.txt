**Argument**

The judgment should be vacated because the trial court committed multiple errors that affected the verdict and the comparative-negligence reduction. This Court reviews the admissibility of opinion evidence and other evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion, but legal errors affecting the scope of expert testimony and the sufficiency of the evidence to go to the jury must be corrected. *Salem v. United States Lines Co.*, 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962); *Rogers v. Missouri Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506–07 (1957).

**I. The district court erred in permitting American Steamship’s witness to testify as an expert on the cause of the line’s failure.**

Expert testimony is admissible only when it concerns “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” that will assist the trier of fact. *Salem*, 370 U.S. at 35. A witness may not be cloaked with expert status merely because he has practical experience where the subject matter is within the ordinary understanding of jurors or where the testimony is only a conclusion about what happened. *Salem*, 370 U.S. at 35–36. Here, the defense witness’s opinion on why the line parted was not the product of specialized analysis beyond the ken of lay jurors; it was instead an impermissible narrative conclusion about the ultimate cause of the accident. Because the issue was whether the line had been weakened or otherwise rendered unsafe, the jury could assess the facts itself from the physical evidence and the testimony of the crew. See *Texas & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Behymer*, 189 U.S. 468, 470 (1903) (questions of negligence ordinarily belong to the jury when reasonable minds may differ).

Allowing this opinion was prejudicial because it went to the central liability question. In a seamen’s injury case, the trier of fact must decide whether the vessel was reasonably fit for its intended use. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100–01 (1944). A witness’s unsupported “expert” conclusion that the rope failed for a particular reason usurped that function and improperly bolstered the defense theory. The error requires a new trial. *Salem*, 370 U.S. at 35–36.

**II. The court should have granted judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness, although it properly left comparative negligence to the jury.**

The warranty of seaworthiness is absolute and imposes liability when the vessel or its appurtenances are not reasonably fit for their intended service. *Mahnich*, 321 U.S. at 100–01; *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). The duty extends to gear used in the ship’s operations, and temporary or operational conditions do not excuse an unsafe appliance. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550; *Crumady v. Joachim Hendrik Fisser*, 358 U.S. 423, 428 (1959). On this record, the bos’n’s chair line failed while being used for its intended purpose to lower Cook to the dock. A line that parts under ordinary operational use is not reasonably fit for service. *Mahnich*, 321 U.S. at 100–01. The evidence therefore compelled a finding of unseaworthiness as a matter of law.

By contrast, the district court properly denied judgment as a matter of law on comparative negligence. Under the Jones Act, the standard is whether employer negligence played “any part, even the slightest,” in producing the injury. *Rogers*, 352 U.S. at 506. But a seaman’s own negligence may still reduce damages under comparative fault principles. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). Here, the evidence permitted the jury to conclude that Cook’s conduct in applying a deicing torch near the boom and rigging area may have contributed to the accident. Because reasonable jurors could differ on that issue, the court did not err in submitting comparative negligence to the jury. *Rogers*, 352 U.S. at 506–07.

**III. The district court did not err in admitting evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments.**

Evidence is admissible if it is relevant to a material issue. *Reed v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 326 U.S. 367, 374 (1945). In an action where the defendant asserted comparative negligence and where Cook’s overall physical condition bore on credibility, working capacity, and damages, evidence of alcohol-related ailments was relevant to the extent of injury and to the defense theory of fault. A plaintiff who places his physical condition and damages in issue cannot exclude otherwise probative evidence merely because it may be unfavorable. *Chicago, M. & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Coogan*, 271 U.S. 472, 478 (1926). The trial court therefore acted within its discretion in admitting that evidence.

Because the admission of improper expert opinion was prejudicial, and because unseaworthiness should have been decided as a matter of law in Cook’s favor, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550; *Salem*, 370 U.S. at 35–36.
