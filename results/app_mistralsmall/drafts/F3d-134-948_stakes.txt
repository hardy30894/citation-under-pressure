**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TRIBAL COURT DENIED SELAM HIS RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS IN VIOLATION OF THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT.**

The Sixth Amendment’s compulsory process guarantee, made applicable to tribal courts through the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), ensures that a defendant may compel the attendance of witnesses in his favor. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 18 (1967). This right is violated when a defendant is prevented from presenting witnesses material to his defense. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). Here, the tribal court failed to vindicate Selam’s compulsory process rights when it permitted his appointed counsel to abandon efforts to secure favorable witnesses and failed to act sua sponte to ensure Selam’s right to present a defense.

The record shows that Selam provided his counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of five potential witnesses, two of whom she located but who refused to testify favorably. *See* *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 656 n.19 (1984) (noting that ineffective assistance may arise when counsel fails to investigate or call witnesses). Despite this, neither counsel nor the court took further steps to secure witnesses or subpoenas. The tribal court’s inaction deprived Selam of the opportunity to present a defense, a violation of his rights under the ICRA. *See* *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (due process requires the opportunity to present a defense).

The tribal court’s failure to ensure Selam’s compulsory process rights was not cured by his counsel’s shortcomings. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), does not apply here because the Sixth Amendment right to compulsory process is not dependent on counsel’s effectiveness but on the court’s obligation to vindicate the right. *See* *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858, 873 (1982) (compulsory process is a structural right). The tribal court’s refusal to act violated Selam’s rights under the ICRA.

**II. SELAM DID NOT WAIVE HIS CONFRONTATION CLAIM UNDER THE ICRA.**

The district court erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in tribal court. The ICRA’s confrontation guarantee, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), is not subject to state or tribal procedural default rules. *See* *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (federal habeas review is not barred by state procedural default unless the state rule is firmly established and consistently applied). Tribal courts are not courts of general jurisdiction and do not have the same procedural rules as federal or state courts. *See* *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 65 (1978) (tribal courts retain inherent sovereignty subject to federal limitations).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that federal habeas review is not barred by a failure to raise a claim in tribal court when the claim implicates federal rights under the ICRA. *See* *National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe*, 471 U.S. 845, 855 (1985) (federal courts may review tribal court decisions when federal rights are at issue). Selam’s confrontation claim arises under federal law and was not fairly presented to the tribal court. Thus, it was error for the district court to deem it waived.
