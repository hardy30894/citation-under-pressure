**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA EXEMPTION FOR APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL APPLIES TO STATE JUDGES.**

The ADEA defines "employee" to exclude "any person... an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The judicial branch performs functions indistinguishable from those of the executive and legislative branches in terms of policy creation. In *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304 (1936), the Supreme Court described the executive branch as the "sole organ of the federal government in the field of international relations," a characterization that underscores the high level of policy discretion inherent in such roles. Similarly, in *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), the Court recognized the President's power to "persuade" and shape policy. Judges exercise this same discretion; they interpret the law and, in doing so, fill the "interstices" of authority to create binding precedent. This lawmaking function places them squarely within the "policymaking level" exception. To exclude them would be to ignore the reality that judges, like executive officers, are charged with the formulation and execution of public policy.

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The State has a legitimate interest in ensuring the competence and efficiency of its judiciary. Under the deferential standard of review established in *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), a law is presumed valid if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. The State argues that mandatory retirement prevents the decline of faculties that age brings, thereby reducing the risk of error and ensuring the efficient administration of justice. This interest is well within the "wide latitude" afforded to state legislatures in social and economic legislation, as noted in *Baldwin v. Fish & Game Comm'n of Montana*, 436 U.S. 371 (1968). Furthermore, the public interest in public employment, as discussed in *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105 (1943), supports the State's right to set conditions of service, including age limits, to ensure the workforce remains capable. Therefore, the mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to these legitimate goals and withstands equal protection scrutiny.
