**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Recognize a "Class of One" Claim Absent Allegations of Membership in a Discriminatory Class**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment commands that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has consistently interpreted the Clause to prohibit discrimination that is based on membership in a protected class or group. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976) (holding that the Equal Protection Clause is concerned with "racial classifications"). The Clause does not create a cause of action for every alleged arbitrary or irrational government action. *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 272 (1979) (requiring proof of discriminatory purpose, not merely disparate impact).

The "class of one" theory, as recognized by the court below, would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general tort remedy for any perceived governmental unfairness. This Court has never endorsed such a sweeping interpretation. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938), the Court identified "discrete and insular minorities" as the primary beneficiaries of heightened equal protection scrutiny. The "class of one" theory, by contrast, requires no such group-based discrimination.

**II. The Seventh Circuit's "Spiteful Effort" Standard Lacks Support in This Court's Precedent**

The court below held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was "motivated solely by a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." This standard finds no support in this Court's decisions. In *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961), the Court applied rational basis review to economic regulations, requiring only that the classification be "rationally related to a legitimate state interest." The Court has never required that a government action be free from any improper motive to survive rational basis review. *New Orleans v. Dukes*, 427 U.S. 297, 303 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding economic regulation under rational basis review despite alleged animus).

The "spiteful effort" standard is also inconsistent with *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955), where the Court held that "the law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional." A government action that is rationally related to a legitimate purpose satisfies the Equal Protection Clause, even if the decisionmaker harbors some subjective ill will. *See also Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979) (rational basis review is "a paradigm of judicial restraint").

**III. The "Class of One" Theory Would Undermine the Traditional Limits of Equal Protection Jurisprudence**

This Court has consistently required that equal protection plaintiffs demonstrate that they were treated differently from others similarly situated. *See, e.g., Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923) (tax assessment case requiring showing of intentional discrimination). But the "class of one" theory would allow any plaintiff who can identify a single comparator to state a claim, regardless of whether the differential treatment was based on a protected characteristic. This would flood the federal courts with litigation challenging routine administrative decisions.

In *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336, 345 (1989), the Court held that a tax assessment system that systematically undervalued some properties while overvaluing others violated equal protection. But the Court emphasized that the violation arose from "intentional systematic undervaluation by state officials," not from mere administrative error or isolated animus. The "class of one" theory, by contrast, would constitutionalize every instance of alleged favoritism or bias.

**IV. The Village's Action Was Rationally Related to a Legitimate Government Purpose**

Even if the "class of one" theory were cognizable, the Village's demand for a 33-foot easement was rationally related to legitimate municipal objectives. The Village sought to ensure adequate water supply and drainage for the entire community. *See Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 416 U.S. 1, 9 (1974) (upholding zoning ordinance as rationally related to "the quality of life"). The fact that the Village may have required a smaller easement from other property owners does not, standing alone, establish an equal protection violation. *See Lehnhausen v. Lake Shore Auto Parts Co.*, 410 U.S. 356, 364 (1973) (upholding tax classification that treated corporations differently from individuals).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
