# ARGUMENT

## THE CONFERRAL OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS CONSTITUTES AN "ADMISSION" UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255

### A. The Plain Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Establishes Admission

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The statutory definition of "admitted" is "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). By deeming TPS recipients to be in "lawful status as a nonimmigrant," Congress necessarily deemed them to have satisfied the predicate of lawful entry after inspection—because no alien can lawfully hold nonimmigrant status without having been admitted. As the Ninth Circuit recognized in adopting this reading, "[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted." *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (quotation marks omitted).

This Court has long instructed that statutes must be read to avoid rendering their operative terms meaningless. *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) ("A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous."). If § 1254a(f)(4)'s grant of "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" does not satisfy § 1255(a)'s admission requirement, the provision accomplishes nothing for the very class of aliens Congress most needed to protect—those who entered without inspection and now face humanitarian crises preventing their return.

### B. Congressional Purpose Confirms That TPS Recipients Who Entered Unlawfully Must Not Be Categorically Barred From Adjustment

Congress created TPS to shield vulnerable aliens from removal during extraordinary conditions abroad. The Third Circuit itself acknowledged that "TPS shields foreign nationals present in the United States from removal during armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions in their homelands." Sanchez, slip op. at 4 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)). Many aliens who qualify for TPS—including Petitioners, who fled El Salvador after devastating earthquakes—entered without inspection precisely because emergency conditions made lawful entry impossible or impractical.

Reading § 1255(a)'s admission requirement to permanently disqualify these individuals contradicts the remedial purpose of the TPS program. It would force TPS recipients to leave the United States to obtain a lawful admission—stripping them of the very protection TPS was designed to provide—merely to become eligible for the adjustment process Congress made available to nonimmigrants generally. This Court has cautioned against interpretations that undermine a statute's evident purpose. See *Dolan v. U.S. Postal Serv.*, 546 U.S. 481, 486 (2006) (statutory interpretation must consider "the specific context in which that language is used, and the broader context of the statute as a whole").

### C. The BIA's Own Precedent Confirms That Grants of Status Can Constitute Admission

The Board of Immigration Appeals has recognized that "[a]n adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission." *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). If adjustment of status—itself a change in status occurring while the alien is physically present in the United States—can constitute an admission, there is no principled textual reason why Congress's express statutory command that TPS recipients be considered as being in "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" should not likewise satisfy § 1255(a)'s threshold requirement.

### D. Section 1254a(f)(4) Functions as a Statutory Fiction Deeming Admission, Consistent With Analogous INA Provisions

Congress routinely uses statutory fictions to grant admitted status to aliens who did not undergo actual inspection at a port of entry. For example, § 1255(i) and § 1255(h) except certain aliens from the admission requirement altogether. That Congress did not use identical exception language for TPS recipients does not defeat Petitioners' reading; rather, Congress chose a different mechanism—deeming TPS recipients to hold lawful nonimmigrant status, a status that by definition presupposes admission.

The Sixth Circuit's decision in *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548 (6th Cir. 2013), correctly recognized that Congress's use of the term "nonimmigrant" in § 1254a(f)(4) was not accidental. Nonimmigrant status is a term of art throughout the INA that presupposes lawful admission. See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) (defining categories of "nonimmigrant" aliens by reference to their purpose for entry). Congress's choice to use this specific term, rather than merely "lawful status," reflects its intent to bring TPS recipients within the ambit of admitted aliens.

### E. This Court Should Resolve the Circuit Split in Favor of the Ninth and Sixth Circuits' Approach

The courts of appeals are divided on this question. The Sixth and Ninth Circuits have held that TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255(a). See *Flores*, 718 F.3d at 553-55; *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960-64. The Third, Fifth, and Eleventh Circuits have reached the contrary conclusion. This Court's guidance is necessary to ensure uniform application of the immigration laws, particularly where, as here, an alien's eligibility for lawful permanent residence turns entirely on this threshold statutory question.

The Third Circuit's decision below, by treating "admission" and "status" as wholly discrete concepts incapable of overlap, ignored that Congress can and does confer admitted status through fictions like § 1254a(f)(4). This Court should reverse and hold that a grant of TPS satisfies § 1255(a)'s requirement that an alien be "inspected and admitted."
