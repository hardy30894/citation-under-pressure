ARGUMENT

I. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement for State Court Judges Does Not Violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)

The ADEA exempts from its coverage "any person elected to public office ... or an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question is whether Missouri's appointed state court judges fall within the exemption as "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." The Court should hold that they do.

Judges, by the very nature of their office, exercise significant discretion and judgment in deciding cases, which often involves creating law and shaping public policy. As the Court has recognized, judges are not simply mechanical appliers of settled law, but rather play a crucial role in shaping the law through their decisions. See, e.g., Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (noting that judges "make" law in the absence of statutory guidance).

Moreover, Missouri's appointed judges hold significant policymaking responsibilities, particularly in their supervisory roles within the state court system. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, is responsible for adopting rules governing the professional conduct of the Missouri judiciary and the Missouri Bar. This level of responsibility is characteristic of "appointee[s] on the policymaking level."

The statutory context and legislative history of the ADEA exemption support this interpretation. The exemption is not limited to appointees within the executive or legislative branches, but rather applies to "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" generally. See United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543-44 (1940) (emphasizing the importance of considering the statutory context in interpreting exemptions).

II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Requirement for State Court Judges Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment

The Equal Protection Clause requires that state laws be rationally related to a legitimate government interest. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is rationally related to the state's interest in ensuring the fitness and competence of its judiciary.

The state's interest in maintaining a judiciary that is capable of performing its duties effectively is a legitimate one, and the mandatory retirement requirement is a reasonable means of achieving that interest. See, e.g., Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483, 488-89 (1955) (upholding a state law regulating the practice of optometry as rationally related to the state's interest in protecting public health).

The Court should defer to the state's judgment in this matter, as the Constitution does not prohibit the state from making reasonable distinctions between different classes of individuals. See, e.g., Railway Express Agency v. New York, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (noting that the Equal Protection Clause does not require "mathematical nicety" in making classifications).

For these reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the District Court, upholding Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges against challenges under the ADEA and the Equal Protection Clause.
