SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The district court granted Ybarra summary judgment on all seven calls placed from DISH's 8047 number without requiring Ybarra to prove that DISH actually used a prerecorded voice on each call. DISH does not dispute that three of the seven calls—those coded "Positive Voice"—resulted in playback of a prerecorded message and that liability was therefore properly entered as to those three calls. But the remaining four calls—three coded "Answering Machine" and one coded "Data Error-No Value"—present no evidence that any prerecorded voice was ever used. The district court's judgment as to those four calls must be reversed, and judgment entered for DISH.

ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). Summary judgment is proper only "against a party who fails to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party's case, and on which that party will bear the burden of proof at trial." Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). A court may not enter judgment for a plaintiff where "the mere existence of a scintilla of evidence" supports his position; "there must be evidence on which the jury could reasonably find" for him. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986). Because Ybarra, not DISH, bore the burden of proving every element of his TCPA claim, the absence of proof that a prerecorded voice was used on four of the seven calls required judgment for DISH, not for Ybarra.

II. Ybarra Failed to Prove that DISH "Used" a Prerecorded Voice on the Four Calls Coded "Answering Machine" and "Data Error."

The TCPA makes it unlawful "to make any call ... using any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice ... to any telephone number assigned to a ... cellular telephone service." 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). The statute's operative verb is "using"—liability attaches to a call made using a prerecorded voice, not merely to a call attempted by equipment capable of delivering one. The Act was enacted to redress the "nuisance and invasion of privacy" caused by the receipt of unwanted automated messages, Mims v. Arrow Fin. Servs., LLC, 565 U.S. 368, 372 (2012); it does not impose liability where, as here, no such message was ever delivered.

The undisputed record establishes that DISH's Cisco Dialer played its prerecorded message only when a call was met by a "positive voice"—an actual human voice or a recorded voicemail greeting. Of the seven calls at issue, only three were logged as "Positive Voice." The remaining four were logged in categories distinct from, and mutually exclusive with, "Positive Voice": three as "Answering Machine" and one as "Data Error-No Value." Nothing in the record shows that the prerecorded message was played, or even could have been played, on a call that the dialer itself classified outside the "Positive Voice" category. As to the "Data Error-No Value" call in particular, the record affirmatively shows that no connection producing a discernible voice was ever registered at all—meaning there is no evidence that any call, in the sense contemplated by § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii), was completed to Ybarra using an artificial or prerecorded voice.

The district court nonetheless treated DISH's admission that it used a prerecorded voice on some calls from the 8047 number as conclusive proof that a prerecorded voice was used on all seven calls "regardless of whether Ybarra triggered it with a positive voice." That reasoning inverts the summary-judgment standard. A plaintiff must adduce evidence sufficient to permit a reasonable factfinder to conclude that each challenged call satisfies every element of the statute. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 252; Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322. Ybarra offered no evidence—expert, testimonial, or otherwise—that the Cisco Dialer deployed its prerecorded message on a call coded "Answering Machine" or "Data Error." He rested instead on DISH's general concession that the dialer was capable of playing a prerecorded voice and had done so on other calls. That showing establishes, at most, that DISH's system used a prerecorded voice on the three "Positive Voice" calls; it is not evidence—let alone undisputed evidence entitling Ybarra to judgment as a matter of law—that the same occurred on the four calls at issue here.

Because Ybarra failed to carry his burden on an essential element of his claim as to those four calls, DISH was entitled to summary judgment. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322 ("a complete failure of proof concerning an essential element of the nonmoving party's case necessarily renders all other facts immaterial"). At minimum, the conflicting record—showing DISH's dialer distinguished "Positive Voice" outcomes from "Answering Machine" and "Data Error" outcomes precisely because the prerecorded message was not triggered in the latter categories—precluded summary judgment for Ybarra. See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 248 (summary judgment improper where "a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party").

III. DISH Concedes Liability on the Three "Positive Voice" Calls.

DISH does not seek reversal of the judgment as to Calls 1, 6, and 7, each coded "Positive Voice," on which the prerecorded message was in fact delivered. DISH concedes liability and the corresponding $1,500 in statutory damages for those three calls.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment to Ybarra on Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5, render judgment for DISH on those four calls, and affirm the judgment only as to Calls 1, 6, and 7.
