## ARGUMENT

### I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent With the National Labor Relations Act.

Nothing in the National Labor Relations Act authorizes the Board to require an employer to possess sufficient evidence to *withdraw recognition* before it may conduct a secret-ballot poll to *test* employee support for an incumbent union. The Act protects employer speech and communication with employees about union representation. *NLRB v. Virginia Electric & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 477 (1941). An employer has a legitimate interest in ascertaining the truth of a union’s claim of majority status without risking an unfair-labor-practice finding. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 617–18 (1969) (recognizing employer’s right to “communicate . . . to his employees” about union matters).

The Board’s rule—that an employer may poll only when it already has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about majority status—renders polling a practical nullity. If the employer already possesses enough evidence to withdraw recognition, polling serves no legitimate purpose; the employer may simply withdraw. The Board’s own earlier precedent recognized that polling could serve a useful role when the employer has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support” short of justifying withdrawal. *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). The Act does not compel such a restrictive standard. The Board’s current standard, adopted without statutory warrant, discourages the use of the “potentially . . . less disruptive” tool of a supervised poll, undermining the Act’s goal of stable collective bargaining.

Moreover, the Board’s standard creates an anomaly: an employer may withdraw recognition (with no procedural safeguards for employees) on the same quantum of evidence required to conduct a poll (which includes secrecy, notice, and safeguards). This illogical scheme neither protects employee free choice nor furthers the Act’s purposes. A rational interpretation of the Act would allow polling on less evidence than withdrawal, giving the employer a “useful and legitimate tool” to test union support without prematurely severing the bargaining relationship.

### II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Mack Lacked Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.

Even under the Board’s own standard, its conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked reasonable doubt on January 25, 1991, is not supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board improperly discounted directly probative employee statements while ignoring the totality of objective evidence.

The record shows that at least seven employees—nearly one-fourth of the 32-person unit—expressed clear opposition to the union. The Board refused to credit the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, relying on a presumption that interview statements are insincere when the employer mentioned a non-union shop. But there was no evidence of coercion, and the Board’s *ipse dixit* that such statements “are unlikely to be sincere” is not supported by the record. *NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 408 (1962) (Board may not base findings on “suspicion and conjecture”).

The Board also rejected the statement of union steward Ron Mohr—an admitted union supporter—that “if a vote was taken, the union would lose.” The Board called this an “off-the-cuff” remark, yet Mohr was uniquely positioned to know employee sentiment. No evidence contradicted his assessment. The Board likewise dismissed Kermit Bloch’s report that the entire night shift of five or six employees opposed the union, simply because Bloch did not testify. But the company reasonably relied on this information at the time; the Board’s after-the-fact rejection does not bear on whether Allentown Mack possessed objective doubt on January 25.

The Board’s counting errors further undermine its conclusion. It excluded Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack because they were hired or left after the start of the disputed period. But objective evidence from close in time is probative. By arbitrarily cutting off the evidence on an exact date, the Board ignored the company’s reasonable pre-poll and post-hiring communications. The presumption of continuing majority status is weaker here, where the bargaining unit was drawn from a larger predecessor unit and had never been directly sampled for majority support. The Board’s rigid approach disregards the realities of a successor employer.

Finally, the poll itself—conducted under the strict procedural safeguards of *Struksnes*—resulted in a 19-to-13 vote against the union. That outcome corroborates the company’s pre-poll doubt. The Board cannot simultaneously require objective evidence of doubt and then ignore the most reliable evidence of all: an unbiased secret-ballot election.

Because the Board’s standard is arbitrary and its factual finding lacks substantial evidence, this Court should reverse the decision below and deny enforcement of the bargaining order.
