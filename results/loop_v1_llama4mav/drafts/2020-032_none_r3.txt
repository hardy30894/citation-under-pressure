The petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy is rendered moot by the revision of that policy during litigation, and their claim for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims. The mootness doctrine, as established by the Court, dictates that a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. See Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). The revision of the "Freedom of Expression Policy" and the removal of the challenged portion of the "Student Code of Conduct" eliminated the live controversy regarding the constitutionality of the superseded policies, rendering the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot.

The claim for nominal damages does not revive the moot claims. The Court has consistently held that a claim for damages is not sufficient to save a case from mootness when the underlying controversy has been resolved. Barker v. Twp. of West Norriton, 302 U.S. 644 (1937) (per curiam); Atherton Mills v. Johnston, 259 U.S. 13, 15-16 (1922). The petitioners' personal stake in the outcome was eliminated when the school revised its policies, and their claim for nominal damages does not provide a sufficient basis to continue litigating the constitutionality of the superseded policies.

Furthermore, the petitioners' complaint failed to adequately plead a claim for compensatory damages. The Court has emphasized the importance of proper pleading, requiring that a complaint provide sufficient notice of the claims being asserted, including any claims for damages. Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. Wallace, 288 U.S. 249, 262 (1933); Cummings v. Missouri, 71 U.S. 277, 318 (1866). The petitioners' attempt to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages was not properly presented to the district court, as it was embedded in a response to a motion to dismiss, rather than being presented in a properly filed motion, as required by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. See California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R., 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893).

The district court's dismissal of the petitioners' complaint as moot was proper. The Court has consistently held that a district court's decision to dismiss a case as moot is reviewed de novo. United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). The district court correctly applied the relevant law, including the principles set forth in Mills and Atherton Mills, and properly concluded that the petitioners' claims were moot.

The petitioners' attempt to distinguish their case from the relevant precedents is unpersuasive. The Court has made clear that a case is moot when the underlying controversy has been resolved, regardless of any potential distinctions between the facts of the case and those of prior precedents. San Pablo & Tulare R.R., 149 U.S. at 314. Therefore, the district court's dismissal of the petitioners' complaint should be affirmed. Commercial Cable Co. v. Burleson, 250 U.S. 360, 362-63 (1919).
