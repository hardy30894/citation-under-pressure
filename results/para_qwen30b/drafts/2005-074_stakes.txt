**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), APPLIES TO PETITIONER’S PRIOR DEPORTATION ORDER, AND ITS APPLICATION IS NOT IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE**

Petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas challenges the application of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5)—the so-called “reinstatement statute”—to his case, arguing that the statute, enacted in 1996 as part of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act (IIRIRA), cannot be applied retroactively to reinstate a deportation order issued in 1981. The Tenth Circuit held otherwise, concluding that Congress intended the statute to apply to all aliens who re-entered the United States illegally after a prior deportation, regardless of when the original order was issued. That conclusion is correct in law but fails to account for the constitutional limits on retroactive application of penal statutes, particularly when such application deprives an alien of a protected liberty interest without fair warning.

The reinstatement statute provides that “[i]f an alien who has been ordered removed under this chapter reenters the United States illegally, the prior order of removal shall be reinstated without further hearing.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). The statute explicitly denies the alien any form of relief under the INA, including adjustment of status, asylum, or cancellation of removal. This is not a procedural rule; it is a substantive bar to relief that operates as a permanent, automatic consequence of re-entry. When applied to an alien like Petitioner, who re-entered the United States in 1982—before the statute’s enactment—its application constitutes a retroactive application of a penal-like provision that significantly alters the legal consequences of past conduct.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a statute that alters the legal consequences of acts completed before its enactment” is retroactive and must be scrutinized under the Due Process Clause. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 274 (1994). In *Landgraf*, the Court held that “a court must determine whether Congress has made its intention clear” regarding retroactivity, and that “if Congress has not clearly indicated its intent, the court must presume that the statute does not apply retroactively.” Id. at 280. The Court emphasized that “the presumption against retroactivity is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence,” and that “the danger of unfair surprise is greatest when the statute imposes new obligations or increases penalties.” Id. at 270.

Here, Congress did not clearly indicate an intent to apply § 1231(a)(5) to pre-enactment deportation orders. The statute’s text contains no express retroactivity provision, and its legislative history is silent on whether it was meant to revive orders issued decades earlier. The absence of such language is fatal. As the Court stated in *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965), “where Congress has not spoken with clarity, we must assume it did not intend to alter the legal consequences of past conduct.” The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *Matter of L-*, 23 I&N Dec. 557 (BIA 2002), is misplaced. That decision, which predates *Landgraf*, did not address the constitutional limits on retroactivity and cannot override the Supreme Court’s clear directive that retroactive application of penal statutes is impermissible absent clear congressional intent.

Moreover, Petitioner had a “protectable expectation” that he could seek relief under the immigration laws after re-entering the United States. In *United States v. Brown*, the Court held that “a person may have a legitimate expectation of freedom from punishment for past conduct,” and that “the imposition of punishment after the fact, without fair warning, violates due process.” 381 U.S. at 442. Petitioner re-entered the United States in 1982, when the law provided for discretionary relief, including adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. He married a U.S. citizen in 2001 and, in good faith, applied for permanent residence. The government’s subsequent invocation of a statute enacted fourteen years later to deny him that relief—without any opportunity for a hearing or consideration of his circumstances—constitutes a deprivation of a liberty interest without due process.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that Petitioner “had no protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status” is factually and legally flawed. The law in 1982 permitted adjustment of status for certain aliens, and the government itself has long recognized that marriage to a U.S. citizen creates a legitimate basis for relief. To deny such relief based on a statute enacted after the fact, without notice or opportunity to respond, violates the principle that “a person should not be punished for conduct that was lawful when committed.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. at 442.

The Court has repeatedly held that “the Constitution forbids the application of a law to conduct that occurred before the law was enacted, unless Congress has clearly expressed its intent to do so.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. Here, Congress did not do so. The reinstatement statute was not designed to address pre-1997 conduct. It was enacted to close a loophole in the immigration system by preventing aliens from “restarting the clock” after re-entry. But it was not intended to strip aliens of rights they reasonably expected to have under the law at the time of their re-entry.

The Tenth Circuit’s decision, therefore, contravenes settled constitutional principles. The application of § 1231(a)(5) to Petitioner’s case is impermissibly retroactive. The statute cannot be applied to reinstate a deportation order issued in 1981 when it was not in effect at the time of Petitioner’s re-entry in 1982. To do so would violate the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.
