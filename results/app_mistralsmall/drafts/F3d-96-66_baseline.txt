**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT TO BORA CORP. BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST REGARDING WHETHER THE SHIP OWED SERBIN A DUTY UNDER THE "ACTIVE OPERATIONS" STANDARD OF *SCINDIA STEAM NAVIGATION CO. V. DE LOS SANTOS*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981).**

Under *Scindia*, a vessel owner owes a longshoreman a duty of reasonable care only when it is engaged in "active operations" at the time of the injury. *Id.* at 167. The Supreme Court explained that this duty arises "only when the vessel’s crew is engaged in operations that the vessel owner has authorized or directed." *Id.* (emphasis added). The Third Circuit has repeatedly applied this standard, holding that the vessel’s duty is "limited to those areas where the vessel’s crew is actively engaged in cargo operations." *Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 372 F.3d 195, 201 (3d Cir. 2004) (quoting *Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 286 F.3d 286, 292 (3d Cir. 2002)).

Here, the district court erred by concluding that the ship’s crew was not engaged in "active operations" at the time of Serbin’s injury. The record shows that the crew had been actively operating the hatch cover system—including the snatch blocks—throughout the unloading process. The snatch blocks were part of the vessel’s cargo-handling apparatus, and their proper positioning was essential to the crew’s ongoing unloading operations. The crew had just finished opening the hatch covers to expose the next deck of cargo, and the snatch blocks had to be returned to the vertical position to allow the crane to remove the remaining cargo units. The crew’s failure to secure the snatch block in the proper position—thus creating a hazard—was directly tied to their active role in the cargo-handling process.

The district court’s reliance on *Gutierrez* is misplaced. In *Gutierrez*, the vessel’s crew was not engaged in any operations at the time of the injury; the ship was merely "moored and inactive." 372 F.3d at 198. Here, by contrast, the crew was actively managing the hatch cover system, and the snatch blocks were integral to that process. The court below improperly conflated the "active operations" duty with the vessel’s broader duty to maintain a reasonably safe workplace, which is not the standard under *Scindia*. *See Chandris*, 286 F.3d at 292 ("The vessel’s duty to maintain a reasonably safe workplace is distinct from its duty during active operations.").

Because the ship’s crew was actively engaged in cargo operations at the time of Serbin’s injury, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. Genuine disputes of fact remain regarding whether the ship breached its duty of reasonable care under *Scindia*.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT SERBIN WAS A "SEAWARD OF THE GANGWAY" AND THEREFORE BARRED FROM RECOVERY UNDER THE "OPEN AND OBVIOUS" DOCTRINE.**

The district court further erred by holding that Serbin’s injury occurred "seaward of the gangway," thus barring his claim under the "open and obvious" danger doctrine. *See Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167 (noting that a vessel owes no duty to warn of "open and obvious" dangers). However, the record does not support this conclusion.

The "open and obvious" doctrine applies only where the danger is "so obvious that the plaintiff should have reasonably appreciated the risk." *Chandris*, 286 F.3d at 294. Here, the snatch block’s improper position was not open and obvious; it was hidden beneath a cargo unit, and its misalignment was not readily apparent to a longshoreman performing his duties. Moreover, the danger posed by the block—its potential to fall or obstruct the workspace—was not so apparent that Serbin could reasonably be expected to avoid it. *See id.* (holding that a danger is not open and obvious if it is "latent" or "hidden").

The district court’s conclusion that Serbin was "seaward of the gangway" is also unsupported by the record. The gangway’s location is not clearly defined in the record, and the parties dispute whether Serbin was within the vessel’s workspace at the time of the injury. Because this is a factual dispute, summary judgment was inappropriate. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (holding that summary judgment is improper where there is a genuine dispute of material fact).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and this matter remanded for further proceedings consistent with *Scindia* and its progeny.
