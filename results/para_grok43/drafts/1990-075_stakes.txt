**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies the constitutional requirement that Congress “lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Congress identified the precise evil—imminent hazards to public safety created by rapidly emerging designer drugs—and supplied the Attorney General with enumerated factors drawn from 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4), (5) and (6), a mandatory consultation requirement with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, publication in the Federal Register, and strict temporal limits of one year (extendable by six months). 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1)–(2), (4). These constraints are at least as specific as those upheld in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372–79 (1989), where Congress directed the Sentencing Commission to consider multiple statutory factors, pursue stated goals, and remain within defined boundaries.

The same intelligible principle governs the Attorney General’s subdelegation. Section 871(a) expressly authorizes the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter.” Exercising that authority, the Attorney General promulgated 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), transferring performance of the temporary scheduling power to the DEA Administrator. Such administrative subdelegation has long been recognized as permissible when the statute itself contemplates delegation and the subdelegate remains subject to the same statutory standards. See, e.g., United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512–14 (1974) (approving Attorney General subdelegation under analogous drug-enforcement statutes).

Petitioners’ reliance on the two 1935 decisions striking down delegations is misplaced. Unlike the National Industrial Recovery Act provisions invalidated in Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), § 811(h) contains an explicit policy, defined criteria, procedural safeguards, and judicially enforceable limits. The nondelegation doctrine therefore poses no constitutional obstacle, and the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
