The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when determining a defendant's competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The governing test remains whether the defendant possesses "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam).

This Court has consistently applied that single formulation across the stages of a criminal case. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that due process requires a hearing whenever evidence raises a bona fide doubt about the defendant's capacity to participate in the proceedings, without distinguishing among trial, plea, or waiver. Id. at 385. Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), likewise remanded for a competency determination in the context of self-representation under the Dusky standard.

Waiver of counsel has always been measured by the same baseline. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), required that any relinquishment be "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," id. at 464, but anchored the inquiry in the defendant's overall capacity to understand the proceedings. Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), confirmed that the validity of a waiver turns on whether the defendant possesses "the mental capacity to make an understanding and intelligent choice," id. at 275. Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), examined waiver of counsel under the ordinary standard of rational understanding. Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise tested the defendant's comprehension of charges and consequences by that measure. Id. at 724.

Guilty pleas receive identical treatment. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show the plea was voluntary and intelligent, yet derived that requirement from the same due-process foundation that protects the right to trial. Id. at 242. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), grounded the entire inquiry in the fundamental fairness of criminal proceedings without imposing a separate, more demanding test for any particular decision.

The Ninth Circuit's contrary rule cannot be reconciled with the uniform standard established in these decisions. Because the record here contains psychiatric evaluations finding Moran competent under Dusky, the state courts properly accepted his plea and waiver. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
