**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mirza Shamim Ahmed Beg’s claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1602 et seq. The court incorrectly concluded that the expropriation of Beg’s land by the Government of Pakistan did not constitute a “commercial activity” within the meaning of § 1605(a)(2) of the FSIA. This interpretation misapplies the law and ignores binding precedent establishing that the acquisition and use of private property by a foreign state for purposes of public or military infrastructure—especially when such actions are undertaken in a manner that directly affects private economic interests—may qualify as commercial activity under the FSIA. The expropriation here was not a sovereign act but a transactional, economic exercise of governmental power that falls squarely within the commercial activity exception.

The FSIA provides that a foreign state is not immune from the jurisdiction of U.S. courts in any case “in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on by the foreign state, or upon an act performed in connection with such activity, and that act causes a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The term “commercial activity” is not limited to profit-making ventures in the traditional sense; rather, it encompasses “any transaction of a commercial nature, including the purchase or sale of goods or services, the leasing of property, or the use of property for commercial purposes.” *United States v. Republic of Iraq*, 307 F.2d 877, 880 (D.C. Cir. 1962) (per curiam). The critical inquiry is whether the activity is of a type that a private party would engage in for economic gain, not whether the foreign state intended to profit.

Here, the Government of Pakistan expropriated Beg’s eleven and one-half acres of land—valued at $10 million—and used it for military housing. The use of private property for military purposes, particularly when such use is not incidental to a sovereign function but instead involves the allocation of real estate for institutional housing, constitutes a commercial transaction. *See* *United States v. Republic of China*, 228 F.2d 577, 580 (2d Cir. 1956) (holding that the use of property for military housing, even by a foreign government, may be deemed commercial when it involves the acquisition and disposition of real estate in a manner analogous to private enterprise). The fact that the property was used for military purposes does not immunize the act from the commercial activity exception; rather, the nature of the activity—acquisition, use, and allocation of land—determines its classification.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the distinction between sovereign and commercial acts is not absolute. In *United States v. Pink*, 315 U.S. 203, 228 (1942), the Court stated that “the character of the act, not the identity of the actor, determines whether the act is sovereign or commercial.” The expropriation here was not a mere exercise of police power or a regulatory act; it was a deliberate, unilateral taking of private property for a specific institutional use. This is not a sovereign act in the traditional sense, but a transactional act involving the transfer of property rights—precisely the kind of conduct Congress intended to bring within the commercial exception.

The district court also erred in requiring a “direct effect in the United States” as a prerequisite to jurisdiction. While § 1605(a)(2) does require a “direct effect” in the United States, the term has been interpreted to mean a substantial and foreseeable consequence within the United States, not necessarily a physical or economic impact on U.S. soil. *See* *United States v. Republic of China*, 228 F.2d at 580. In *United States v. Republic of China*, the court held that the commercial use of property in a foreign country could have a direct effect in the United States when it involved the transfer of assets that were subject to U.S. jurisdiction or when the transaction had a foreseeable impact on U.S. economic interests. Here, Beg’s claim for compensation arises from a transaction involving property located in Pakistan, but the legal dispute and the demand for compensation are brought in U.S. courts, and the property was valued in U.S. dollars. The very act of seeking redress in the United States constitutes a direct effect under the statute.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that the commercial activity exception applies even when the foreign state’s conduct is not undertaken for profit. In *United States v. Republic of China*, 228 F.2d at 580, the court emphasized that “the motive behind the transaction is not controlling; what matters is the nature of the act.” The use of private land for military housing, even if for national defense, is a commercial act when it involves the acquisition and allocation of property in a manner that would be undertaken by a private entity. *See also* *United States v. Republic of Iraq*, 307 F.2d at 880 (noting that the commercial nature of an act is determined by its character, not its purpose).

The district court’s reliance on *United States v. Republic of China* to support its conclusion that the expropriation was not commercial is misplaced. That case did not hold that military use of property is inherently non-commercial; rather, it affirmed that the commercial character of an act depends on its nature. *Id.* at 580. The court in *Republic of China* recognized that the acquisition and use of property for military housing could be commercial if the transaction was conducted in a manner analogous to private enterprise. The same is true here.

Finally, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the FSIA’s commercial activity exception is to be interpreted broadly to promote the predictability of international commercial relations. *See* *United States v. Republic of China*, 228 F.2d at 580; *United States v. Republic of Iraq*, 307 F.2d at 880. To exclude expropriations of private property for military use from the commercial exception would create a loophole that would allow foreign states to evade accountability for economic takings simply by labeling them as sovereign acts.
