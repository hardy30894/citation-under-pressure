I. The District Court Erred by Refusing to Resubmit the Verdict for Clarification.

A general verdict that cannot be reconciled with the evidence or the instructions is ambiguous and requires resubmission to the jury before discharge. Fed. R. Civ. P. 49; see also Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd., 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962) (“[I]t is the duty of the court to attempt to harmonize the answers, if it is possible under a fair reading of them.”). When the verdict forms and damage calculations are irreconcilable on their face, the trial court must permit the jury to resolve the inconsistency rather than impose its own construction. See Pierce v. Underwood, 487 U.S. 552, 560 (1988) (recognizing that factual ambiguities in verdicts are for the jury, not the court).

Here the jury returned a Form 1 award of $70,768.02 to Unit while simultaneously returning a Form 3 set-off of $86,494.30 to Enron. The district court subtracted the latter from the former and entered judgment for neither party. Unit timely requested clarification before the jury was discharged. The court’s refusal left an irreconcilable verdict that the court itself could not lawfully interpret. See Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963) (courts may not “search the record for a basis on which to sustain” an ambiguous verdict).

II. The District Court Misconstrued the Jury’s Damage Award.

A verdict must be read to give effect to the jury’s evident intent when that intent is discernible from the forms and instructions. See United States v. Powell, 469 U.S. 57, 64-65 (1984). The instructions permitted the jury to reduce Unit’s recovery on Form 1 by the cost of repair if it found only substantial performance. The jury’s $70,768.02 award is precisely the difference between the unpaid invoices ($157,262.36) and the set-off claimed on Form 3 ($86,494.30). That arithmetic demonstrates the jury had already netted the set-off against Unit’s damages. The district court’s further subtraction therefore double-counted the same deduction, contrary to the rule that verdicts are to be upheld if any reasonable construction supports them. See Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, 369 U.S. at 364.

Because the verdict was ambiguous and the court declined the only permissible means of clarification, the judgment must be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial on damages.
