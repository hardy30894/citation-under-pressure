# ARGUMENT

## I. The Eighth Amendment Forbids the State From Maintaining Conditions of Confinement That Inflict Needless Suffering, Whatever the Administrative Cost of a Remedy.

The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause has never been static. This Court has long recognized that the Amendment "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). The Clause condemns not only the barbarities of an earlier age but also conditions that, "in the light of contemporary human knowledge," would be judged an infliction of needless suffering. *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962). Confinement that subjects prisoners to conditions incompatible with the "dignity of man," *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 100, violates the Amendment no less than does an excessive sentence disproportionate to the offense. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910) (punishment must be "graduated and proportioned to [the] offense" and free of needless cruelty). Overcrowding that produces the substandard medical care, sanitation, and safety documented below the three-judge court's order is precisely the kind of "unnecessary cruelty," *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878), the Amendment forbids. A State may not excuse continuing violations of this character by pointing to the expense of compliance; the Constitution's guarantees do not yield to a State's fiscal convenience. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (constitutional rights may not be sacrificed to administrative considerations).

## II. A Population-Reduction Order Is a Traditional Exercise of Equitable Power and Therefore Satisfies the PLRA's Requirement That Relief Be Narrowly Drawn, Necessary, and No Broader Than the Violation.

The Prison Litigation Reform Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), requires that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn," "extend no further than necessary," and represent "the least intrusive means" of correcting the violation. These are the same principles that have always governed courts sitting in equity. This Court has explained that "[t]he essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case. Flexibility rather than rigidity has distinguished it." *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321, 329-30 (1944). That flexibility permits — indeed requires — a court to escalate a remedy when a defendant's noncompliance has rendered lesser measures futile. As Justice Cardozo explained for the Court, "[a] continuing decree of injunction directed to events to come is subject always to adaptation as events may shape the need," particularly where the decree "involve[s] the supervision of changing conduct or conditions and [is] thus provisional and tentative." *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932).

That is precisely the posture of this litigation. The State stipulated to remedial orders in 2002 and 2004, admitted in 2005 that it could not comply, and by its own admission remained unable years later to fund even a portion of the court-supervised remedial construction program. A district court is not required to stand by while a defendant's repeated failures perpetuate the very constitutional injury the decree was meant to cure. Equity's "practical flexibility in shaping its remedies and . . . facility for adjusting and reconciling public and private needs," *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955), authorizes exactly the kind of structural, population-based order entered here once the record establishes — as it did after years of failed half-measures — that no less drastic remedy will suffice. A population cap tailored to the level necessary to permit adequate medical and mental-health staffing is not broader than the violation; it is the minimum necessary to eliminate the overcrowding that the trial court found to be the "primary cause" of the ongoing constitutional deprivation.

## III. The Three-Judge Court's Findings Are Entitled to Deference and Do Not Reveal Any Abuse of the Court's Equitable Discretion.

Finally, the factual findings underlying the order — that overcrowding was the primary cause of the constitutional violations and that no remedy short of population reduction could cure them — are reviewable only for clear error. "A finding is 'clearly erroneous' when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *accord Graver Tank & Mfg. Co. v. Linde Air Products Co.*, 336 U.S. 271, 275 (1949). After extensive evidentiary hearings, the three-judge panel's findings rest on a substantial record built over nearly a decade of failed remediation efforts. Nothing in that record suggests the kind of manifest error that would justify displacing the equitable judgment of the court closest to the facts.

For these reasons, the order reducing California's prison population is a lawful and necessary exercise of the district court's remedial authority, consistent with both the Eighth Amendment and the governing statute.
