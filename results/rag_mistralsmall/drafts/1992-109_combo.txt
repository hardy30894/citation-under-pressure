**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard of competency—one of "reasoned choice"—before accepting a guilty plea or waiver of counsel.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that competency to waive constitutional rights demands more than the minimal capacity to stand trial. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987) (citing *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981)). This principle is rooted in the longstanding recognition that waiver of fundamental rights must be knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) ("[W]e cannot presume a waiver of these three important federal rights from a silent record."). The Court has long distinguished between the functional capacities required for trial and those required for waiver. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (competency to stand trial requires "a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings" and "capacity to consult with counsel"); *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1983) (competency to waive counsel requires "a higher level of mental functioning" than competency to stand trial).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that due process protections extend beyond mere procedural formality when fundamental rights are at stake. *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966) ("[T]he record must show, or there must be an allegation and evidence which show, that an accused was offered counsel but intelligently and understandingly rejected the offer."). The voluntariness of a waiver cannot be presumed from silence or cursory inquiry. *McCarthy v. United States*, 394 U.S. 459, 466 (1969) ("[A] plea of guilty entered by one fully aware of the direct consequences . . . must be attended by safeguards to insure that the plea is voluntary.").

II. The trial court failed to conduct any inquiry into Moran’s capacity for reasoned choice before accepting his waiver of counsel and guilty pleas.

The record reveals no meaningful assessment of Moran’s ability to understand the alternatives before him or to make a deliberate choice. The court’s perfunctory colloquy—conducted while Moran was heavily medicated—falls far short of the "utmost solicitude" required for waiver of constitutional rights. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973) (citing *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243). The Court has long condemned such superficial inquiries. *Arsenault v. Massachusetts*, 393 U.S. 5, 5 (1968) (per curiam) (highlighting the dangers of unassisted pleas in open court).

Moreover, the state’s post-conviction reliance on competency-to-stand-trial evaluations is misplaced. *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 568 (1953) ("[T]he question of sanity at the time of the commission of the crime, at the time of the plea, and at the time of the sentence are separate and distinct issues."). The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that competency to waive rights requires a higher degree of cognitive functioning than competency to stand trial. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. The state court’s erroneous application of the lower standard undermines its conclusion.

III. Due process requires the trial court to initiate a competency hearing sua sponte whenever substantial doubt exists.

This Court has long held that trial courts bear an affirmative duty to protect constitutional rights. *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173, 174 (1946) ("[T]he right to counsel being an important constitutional safeguard . . . the court must be alert to see that it is not forfeited."). The Ninth Circuit properly applied this principle, noting that a "good faith doubt" arises whenever there is "substantial evidence of incompetence." *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1982)). Here, Moran’s recent suicide attempt, heavy medication, and monosyllabic responses to leading questions provided ample grounds for such doubt. *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 313 (1966) (per curiam) (recognizing the need for psychiatric evaluation when competency is questioned).

The state’s argument that a delayed post-conviction hearing cured the error is unavailing. *Palmer v. Ashe*, 342 U.S. 134, 134 (1951) ("[T]he right to counsel being an important constitutional safeguard . . . must be protected at every stage."). A retrospective competency determination cannot retroactively validate a flawed waiver. *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. at 568.

**Conclusion**
The Due Process Clause demands a heightened standard of competency—one of reasoned choice—before accepting a waiver of counsel or guilty plea. The trial court’s failure to conduct any meaningful inquiry, coupled with the state’s reliance on an incorrect legal standard, violated Moran’s constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be affirmed.
