**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal "arising under" jurisdiction over patent issues is narrow and does not reach a state malpractice claim merely because it requires resolution of a hypothetical patent question.**

Congress gave federal district courts jurisdiction over cases "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), but this Court has long cautioned against reading "arising under" so broadly that any state-created claim touching a federal issue is swept into an exclusive federal forum. Minton's suit against his former lawyers is a Texas tort—legal malpractice—created entirely by Texas law. That the case-within-a-case requires the state court to ask what would have happened had counsel raised the experimental-use exception does not transform the claim into one "arising under" federal patent law.

This Court's well-pleaded-complaint doctrine confirms the point: "a suit arises under the Constitution and laws of the United States only when the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action shows that it is based upon those laws or that Constitution." *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Minton's petition alleges negligence and the elements of Texas malpractice law; it states no patent-law cause of action. Even an embedded federal issue must "really and substantially involve[] a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). The experimental-use question here is a fact-bound, backward-looking inquiry confined to two former litigants; it does not implicate the "validity, construction, or effect" of the patent statute for anyone else.

This Court has likewise cautioned against opening the arising-under door indiscriminately, favoring instead "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). A hypothetical defense never adjudicated by the Patent Office or any patent tribunal is not "substantial" in the sense *Gully* demands; it is one strand in the web of an ordinary malpractice case.

**II. Precedent confirms that suits requiring only incidental application of patent principles remain within the States' traditional jurisdiction.**

Long before the modern "arising under" test was refined, this Court held that suits requiring courts to apply patent-related concepts to resolve contract or property disputes do not thereby become federal patent cases. In *Wilson v. Sandford*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 99 (1850), the Court held that a suit over rights under a patent license was not a case "arising under" the patent laws, because the plaintiff's claim rested on the contract, not the patent grant itself. Similarly, in *Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 282 (1902), the Court held that a suit to recover royalties owed under a patent-license agreement did not arise under the patent laws merely because the underlying agreement concerned a patented invention; the plaintiff's right to relief depended on the contract, not on any federal question of patent validity or infringement. And in *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the Court likewise treated a dispute over rights derived from a patent license as one properly resolved without resort to exclusive federal jurisdiction, because the controversy was governed by ordinary principles of contract law rather than by the patent statute itself.

The same logic controls here. Minton's right to relief flows entirely from Texas's law of professional negligence; the patent question is, at most, background necessary to prove or disprove causation—just as license terms were background to the contract claims in *Wilson*, *Excelsior Wooden Pipe*, and *Pratt*.

**III. Exercising federal jurisdiction here would disturb, rather than preserve, the balance between federal and state judicial responsibilities.**

Embedded-issue jurisdiction is appropriate only where a federal forum may entertain the question without disturbing the balance Congress has struck between state and federal courts. That balance tips toward federal jurisdiction only in the rare case—like *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180 (1921)—where the federal question carries broad, prospective importance, there the constitutionality of a federal bond program affecting countless future transactions. Cf. *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 671–73 (1950) (jurisdiction is not enlarged merely because resolution may turn on an antecedent federal question). No comparable stakes exist here. The Texas court's resolution of Minton's hypothetical defense binds no one but the parties, will not affect the Patent and Trademark Office or other patentees, and will not disturb the uniform administration of the patent laws. Texas courts are fully capable of applying settled patent doctrine to decide this backward-looking malpractice claim, consistent with the States' traditional authority over the practice of law within their borders.

For these reasons, the Texas courts possessed—and should have exercised—jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim.
