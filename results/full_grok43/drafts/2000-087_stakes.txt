ARGUMENT

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the removal period. Statutes must be construed to avoid serious constitutional doubts where an alternative interpretation is “fairly possible.” Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932); see also Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988).

Indefinite civil detention raises grave due-process concerns. “Freedom from bodily restraint has always been at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause from arbitrary governmental action.” Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Detention is permissible only when narrowly tailored to a “sufficiently compelling” governmental interest. Reno v. Flores, 507 U.S. 292, 316 (1993) (O’Connor, J., concurring); see also United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 746 (1987).

Although Congress possesses “broad power” over immigration, Kleindienst v. Mandel, 408 U.S. 753, 766 (1972), that authority is subject to constitutional limits once an alien has effected entry. Landon v. Plasencia, 459 U.S. 21, 32–33 (1982). Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is inapposite; it addressed only “an alien who has not effected an entry.” Id. at 215.

The Fifth Circuit’s reading—that detention continues “as long as ‘good faith efforts to effectuate . . . deportation continue’”—would permit confinement until removal is shown to be impossible, no matter how remote. Zadvydas v. Underdown, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). That standard “demands more than [the] reading of the statute can bear.” Id. at 294. Section 1231(a)(6) therefore must be read to authorize detention only for “a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal.” Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention exceeds statutory authority.
