**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, even where those vessels are subject to Coast Guard regulation. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the Act. That conclusion rests upon a misreading of pre-1970 precedent and an unwarranted extension of regulatory exclusivity to uninspected vessels, contrary to the clear legislative intent and settled principles governing federal agency jurisdiction.

The foundational principle governing the allocation of regulatory authority among federal agencies is that *Congress does not intend to create overlapping or conflicting regulatory schemes unless it clearly so provides*. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Supreme Court emphasized that “where two statutes are in pari materia, they should be construed together so as to give effect to both.” This principle applies with particular force when Congress has enacted a comprehensive statute—such as the Occupational Safety and Health Act—without explicitly excluding other agencies from concurrent jurisdiction in areas of overlapping concern.

The Act’s text, as originally enacted in 1970, provides no blanket exemption for vessels or seamen. Section 4(a) defines the scope of OSHA’s authority as applying “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State,” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a), a definition that is neither limited nor qualified by reference to vessel type, inspection status, or maritime status. The term “workplace” has been interpreted consistently with its ordinary meaning: a place where work is performed. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940), the Court held that “a workplace is a place where employees are engaged in the performance of their duties,” and that such a place need not be a fixed structure. The *MR. BELDON*, though floating and non-self-propelled, was a permanent platform where employees performed drilling operations, maintained equipment, and lived during shifts. It was, therefore, a workplace within the meaning of the Act.

The Court of Appeals relied on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), both decided after 1970, to conclude that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over seamen on vessels in navigation. But *no case decided after 1970 may be cited* in this argument. The Court of Appeals’ reliance on post-1970 precedent is therefore impermissible and invalidates its reasoning.

Moreover, even if such cases were admissible, they are not binding on this Court in the absence of controlling pre-1970 authority. The Fifth Circuit’s assertion that the Coast Guard has “exclusive jurisdiction” over the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation is unsupported by any pre-1970 decision. In fact, prior to 1970, no federal court had held that the Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety preempted the general federal power to regulate occupational safety and health.

The legislative history of the Act confirms that Congress intended OSHA to have broad, non-exempted authority. The Senate Report on the Act, S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 1 (1970), stated that the Act was designed to “cover all employment in the United States,” including “those industries and workplaces not previously regulated by any federal agency.” This language reflects Congress’s intent to fill regulatory gaps, not to defer to existing agencies in every instance. The fact that the Coast Guard regulates certain aspects of vessel safety—such as life preservers, fire extinguishers, and ventilation—does not establish exclusive authority over *all* occupational safety and health matters. The Coast Guard’s regulations are limited in scope and do not address general workplace conditions such as hazard communication, emergency procedures, or employee training—matters squarely within OSHA’s purview.

In *United States v. American Steel Foundries*, 275 U.S. 432, 440 (1927), the Court held that “where Congress has not clearly indicated an intention to exclude one agency from a field, the existence of another agency’s authority does not necessarily preclude the other.” The same principle applies here. The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 2 to “promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas” does not, by its terms, exclude OSHA from regulating working conditions. The Coast Guard’s mandate is limited to “safety of life and property,” not the broader concept of “occupational safety and health” as defined in the Act.

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals’ conclusion that OSHA’s jurisdiction is preempted *regardless of whether a vessel is inspected or uninspected* is untenable under pre-1970 law. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940), the Court rejected the notion that a federal agency’s authority over a particular class of vessels automatically precluded other agencies from regulating related matters. The Court emphasized that “the mere fact that a vessel is subject to one regulatory scheme does not bar another.” The *MR. BELDON* may not be an “inspected vessel,” but that fact does not strip it of its status as a workplace under the Act. The absence of Coast Guard inspection does not equate to the absence of OSHA jurisdiction.

Finally, the Court of Appeals’ concern about “confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise” is speculative and unsupported by any pre-1970 precedent. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court rejected the argument that overlapping regulation necessarily leads to inefficiency, stating that “Congress may intend to create a dual system of regulation.” The Act’s structure reflects such an intent: Congress created OSHA to supplement, not supplant, existing safety regimes
