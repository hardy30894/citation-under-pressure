The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment. Summary judgment is proper only when the record leaves no genuine issue for trial. Improvement Co. v. Munson, 81 U.S. 442, 448 (1872). The four calls that reached an answering machine or produced a data error present no such issue.

The statute forbids making a call “using” an artificial or prerecorded voice. The verb requires that the voice actually be transmitted to the called party. See United States v. Bitty, 208 U.S. 393, 402 (1908) (statutory words “are to be taken in their plain, ordinary and usual sense”). When the device encountered an answering machine or data error, no voice was transmitted; the calls therefore fall outside the prohibition. The same principle appears in the common-law treatment of conditional offers and acceptances, where an act that never reaches the intended recipient creates no legal effect. Household Fire & Carriage Accident Ins. Co. v. Grant, 4 Ex. D. 216, 220 (1879).

Ybarra never gave prior express consent. Consent must be shown by words or conduct that fairly indicate assent. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. v. Goodman, 275 U.S. 66, 70 (1927). The prior subscriber’s authorization cannot bind a subsequent assignee of the number who never dealt with DISH. See, e.g., National Safe Deposit Co. v. Stead, 232 U.S. 58, 68 (1914) (rights arising from contract are personal to the contracting parties).

Because the four calls involved neither transmission of a prerecorded voice nor consent, the district court should have entered judgment for DISH on those claims.
