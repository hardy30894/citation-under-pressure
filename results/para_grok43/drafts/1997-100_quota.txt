The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This guarantee applies with full force once a defendant has been placed in jeopardy and the prosecution has failed to prove its case. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969).

In Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that double-jeopardy protections extend to capital sentencing proceedings that “have the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” Id. at 439. The Court emphasized that when the State assumes the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt facts necessary to impose an enhanced sentence, an adverse finding functions as an acquittal for double-jeopardy purposes. Id. at 444-45. Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), confirmed that the same rule governs any sentencing proceeding possessing those trial-like characteristics, regardless of label. Id. at 209-10.

The California proceeding to prove the prior-conviction allegation under Penal Code section 1025 shares those characteristics. The prosecution was required to prove the existence and seriousness of the 1992 conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, the rules of evidence applied, and the defendant enjoyed the rights of confrontation and cross-examination. These procedures are indistinguishable in relevant respects from those that triggered double-jeopardy protection in Bullington and Rumsey.

Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), and Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), do not control. Those decisions addressed discretionary sentencing determinations made without any requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), recognized that when a statute mandates a separate factual determination carrying a higher maximum penalty, ordinary sentencing flexibility yields to trial-like safeguards. Id. at 610. The same distinction separates this case from United States v. DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. 117 (1980), and United States v. Watts, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), both of which involved sentencing factors decided under a preponderance standard without the full array of procedural protections present here.

Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), expressly left open whether Bullington’s rationale reaches noncapital recidivism findings. Id. at 392. Nothing in the Court’s subsequent decisions has foreclosed that extension. Because the State failed to carry its burden in a proceeding that “resemble[d] a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence,” Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation.
