**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does not impermissibly add to or alter the qualifications for federal office set forth in the Constitution. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779 (1995), squarely forecloses Appellants’ argument. In *Thornton*, the Supreme Court held that “[t]he qualifications for service in Congress set forth in the text of the Constitution are unalterable by the States.” *Id.* at 804. The Court reasoned that the Framers intended the qualifications clauses to be exclusive, and that allowing states to impose additional qualifications would “undermine the uniformity and the national character that the framers sought to ensure.” *Id.* at 805.

Appellants’ reliance on *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), is misplaced. In *Powell*, the Court held that Congress could not exclude a duly elected member who met the Constitution’s qualifications. *Id.* at 550. The Court did not address whether states could impose additional qualifications. *Thornton* explicitly distinguished *Powell*, noting that the issue in *Powell* was Congress’s power to exclude, not a state’s power to add qualifications. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 804 n.31.

The Arkansas Amendment does not alter the qualifications for federal office; it merely regulates ballot access. *See Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 792 n.17 (1983) (noting that “the States’ power to regulate the time, place, and manner of elections is not absolute” but acknowledging that “some regulation of the electoral process may be necessary to achieve state interests”). The Court has upheld ballot access restrictions that are “reasonable, nondiscriminatory, and justified by a compelling state interest.” *Id.* at 788. Arkansas has a compelling interest in preventing entrenchment and fostering electoral competition, and its Amendment is narrowly tailored to achieve that end. *See id.* at 790-91.

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE ARKANSAS CONSTITUTION’S ENACTING CLAUSE REQUIREMENT.**

The Arkansas Constitution requires that initiated constitutional amendments include an enacting clause in the style of “Be It Enacted by the People of the State of Arkansas.” Ark. Const. amend. 7. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73’s failure to include this clause rendered it invalid. *See Plugge v. McCuen*, 310 Ark. 654, 670, 841 S.W.2d 139, 147 (1992) (holding that the enacting clause is “mandatory and jurisdictional”).

Appellants’ arguments to the contrary are unavailing. First, they claim that the term “bills” in Amendment 7 refers only to legislative acts, not constitutional amendments. But the Arkansas Supreme Court has consistently held that the enacting clause requirement applies to all initiated measures, including constitutional amendments. *See id.* at 670, 841 S.W.2d at 147. Second, Appellants argue that the requirement is directory rather than mandatory. But the Arkansas Supreme Court has rejected this distinction, holding that the enacting clause is a “fundamental requirement” that cannot be waived. *Id.*

**III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE AMENDMENT’S SEVERABILITY CLAUSE DOES NOT SAVE THE STATE OFFICE TERM LIMITS.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that even if the federal term limits provision (Section 3) were severable, the state office term limits (Sections 1 and 2) must fall because the amendment was presented to the voters as a single, indivisible package. *See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964) (holding that a severability clause is ineffective if the unconstitutional portion was an “integral part” of the measure).

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s conclusion is amply supported by the record. The amendment’s preamble lumped state and federal officials together as “elected officials,” and Section 6 applied the amendment to “the offices specified in this Amendment,” which included both state and federal offices. The voters were not given the opportunity to approve state term limits separately from federal term limits. Under these circumstances, the severability clause cannot save the state office term limits. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment violates the federal Constitution’s qualifications clauses, the Arkansas Constitution’s enacting clause requirement, and the principle of severability.
