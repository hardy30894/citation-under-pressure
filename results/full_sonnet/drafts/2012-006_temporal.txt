**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE GOVERNMENT MUST PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT EACH DEFENDANT REMAINED A MEMBER OF THE CONSPIRACY WITHIN THE LIMITATIONS PERIOD.**

It is axiomatic that the prosecution bears the burden of proving every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court held that the presumption of innocence continues throughout the entire course of a trial and that "the accused is entitled to acquittal" whenever "upon all the evidence, there is reasonable doubt" as to any element of the offense. *Id.* at 484, 493. That principle applies with full force to the temporal element of a conspiracy charge: whether the particular defendant remained a participant in the unlawful agreement during the period Congress has made prosecutable.

Conspiracy is unlike most substantive offenses in that its existence, and a given defendant's membership in it, is inherently a matter of duration. This Court recognized as much in *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), explaining that a conspiracy "may be a continuing one" that persists "until abandoned or accomplished." *Id.* at 607. Because the crime is defined by its continuation, the question whether the agreement — and the defendant's part in it — continued into the limitations window is not collateral to guilt; it *is* guilt. The government cannot secure a conviction resting on conduct that occurred entirely outside the period Congress has permitted to be prosecuted, and it therefore must prove, as it must prove any other fact essential to the offense, that the defendant's membership persisted into the statutory window.

**II. EVIDENCE OF WITHDRAWAL PRIOR TO THE LIMITATIONS PERIOD REQUIRES THE GOVERNMENT TO PROVE CONTINUED MEMBERSHIP BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT.**

This Court's withdrawal jurisprudence confirms that once a defendant comes forward with evidence that he left the conspiracy, the question of continued participation returns to the government's ledger. In *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court held that a conspirator remains bound by the acts of his co-conspirators until he withdraws, but it also made clear that the entire theory of continuing liability depends on the conspiracy remaining a live, unified enterprise as to that defendant. Withdrawal is significant precisely because it severs the defendant from the ongoing agreement — it does not merely mitigate punishment, it removes him from the crime being committed after that date.

*Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), is directly on point. There the Court held that where a conspiracy has come to an end as to a particular defendant — there, because the objective was achieved — the statute of limitations begins to run from that point, and a conviction resting on the conspiracy's continuation into the limitations period cannot stand absent proof that the defendant's participation persisted. The Court emphasized that "the statute of limitations begins to run" from the moment the conspiracy, as to that individual, terminates, not from the last act of the conspiracy generally. *Id.* at 216. The logic of *Fiswick* applies equally where termination as to a particular defendant results from voluntary withdrawal rather than accomplishment of the criminal object: in either case, the defendant's status as a conspirator within the prosecutable window becomes an open question that the government, not the defendant, must resolve beyond a reasonable doubt.

*Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440 (1949), reinforces this rule. There the Court refused to permit the government to extend the life of a conspiracy through acts occurring after the conspiracy's central objective had been achieved, holding that a "subsidiary conspiracy to conceal" cannot be presumed to continue automatically once the main conspiracy has ended. *Id.* at 443–44. If the government may not simply presume continuation to sweep in post-conspiracy conduct, it follows that the government likewise may not presume continuation to sweep a withdrawn defendant into a limitations period he has already left.

**III. PLACING THE BURDEN ON THE GOVERNMENT IS CONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT'S TREATMENT OF BURDENS ON ELEMENTS VERSUS AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSES.**

This Court's decision in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), does not counsel a different result. *Leland* permitted a State to place the burden of proving insanity on the defendant because insanity does not negate any element of the crime that the State must otherwise prove — the State still had to establish "the elements of the crime charged," including intent, beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 799. Withdrawal before the limitations period is fundamentally different: it goes directly to whether the defendant committed the very offense — continued participation in an ongoing conspiracy — that the indictment charges within the actionable period. Unlike insanity, which excuses conduct otherwise fully proven, timely withdrawal negates the temporal element of the charged conspiracy itself. Under *Davis*, *Kissel*, *Hyde*, *Fiswick*, and *Krulewitch*, that element remains the government's to prove.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, once a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, the burden of persuasion rests with the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The judgment below should be reversed.
