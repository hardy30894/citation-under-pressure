ARGUMENT

I. THE DISCLOSURE SCHEME IS A CONTENT-BASED BURDEN ON CORE POLITICAL EXPRESSION AND ASSOCIATION.

Florida’s lobbying law regulates speech at the heart of the First Amendment: attempts to influence legislation and executive action. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that “the First Amendment’s guarantees are not confined to the exposition of ideas.” Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. 516, 537 (1945). Lobbying is protected because it is “political expression” and “political association” in the direct process of government. See De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364 (1937); Sweezy v. New Hampshire, 354 U.S. 234, 250-51 (1957).

The statute compels disclosure of all lobbying expenditures, whether made by the lobbyist or the principal, and requires identification of funding sources and categories of spending. Such compelled disclosure burdens associational freedom by forcing public exposure of those who participate in political advocacy. In NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 462-63 (1958), the Court held that compelled disclosure of membership lists infringed freedom of association because it may “induce members to withdraw from the Association and dissuade others from joining it.” The same danger exists here. Lobbying clients and intermediaries may be chilled from participating in advocacy when Florida compels disclosure of their identities and financial support.

Although the State may seek information to police corruption, the Constitution demands narrow tailoring. Even in the election context, the Court has upheld disclosure only when the governmental interest is sufficiently weighty and the burden limited. See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64-68 (1976) (not available to the Court below, but reflecting the constitutional principle that disclosure must be justified by a substantial relation to an important interest). Before 1970, the Court likewise required sensitivity to the chilling effect of compelled identification. See Bates v. City of Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516, 524-25 (1960); Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960).

Florida’s requirement is especially broad because it reaches all lobbying expenditures without regard to whether the spending threatens corruption or concealment. The Court has invalidated statutes that “broadly stifle fundamental personal liberties when the end can be more narrowly achieved.” Shelton, 364 U.S. at 488. The same defect is present here: Florida could require targeted reporting of direct lobbying contacts without compelling the sweeping exposure mandated by Chapter 93-121.

II. THE STATUTE IS FACIALLY OVERBROAD BECAUSE ITS DISCLOSURE REQUIREMENTS BURDEN SUBSTANTIAL PROTECTED ACTIVITY.

A facial challenge is appropriate where a law “sweeps within its ambit protected as well as unprotected speech.” Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 97 (1940). Florida’s law does exactly that. It reaches all lobbyists and principals and covers a wide range of expenditures, from food and beverages to publications and travel, regardless of whether the expenditure has any meaningful relation to corruption or concealment.

The overbreadth doctrine is especially important where a law chills expression before enforcement occurs. See Dombrowski v. Pfister, 380 U.S. 479, 486-87 (1965); Baggett v. Bullitt, 377 U.S. 360, 372 (1964). Here, the League’s members face a forced choice: comply with broad disclosure obligations that deter participation or risk sanctions. That is precisely the type of chilling effect that facial review is designed to prevent. See Aptheker v. Secretary of State, 378 U.S. 500, 515-16 (1964).

Nor can the statute be saved by the availability of advisory opinions. A procedure for clarification does not cure overbreadth where the law itself imposes substantial burdens on protected conduct. See Baggett, 377 U.S. at 378-79. The Constitution does not require speakers to seek permission before speaking. See Freedman v. Maryland, 380 U.S. 51, 57-58 (1965).

III. THE BAN ON CONTINGENCY-FEE LOBBYING IS AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL RESTRICTION ON THE RIGHT TO PETITION AND ON THE LIBERTY TO CONTRACT FOR PROTECTED ADVOCACY.

Florida’s categorical ban on contingency-fee lobbying forbids an ordinary compensation arrangement for political advocacy. The right to petition government is a fundamental liberty. See United States v. Cruikshank, 92 U.S. 542, 552 (1876); De Jonge, 299 U.S. at 364. A law that prohibits a client and advocate from agreeing that compensation turns on success directly burdens that right by restricting access to political representation.

The State cannot justify the ban by mere speculation that contingent compensation might encourage improper lobbying. The Court has emphasized that constitutional rights cannot be restricted on the basis of “broad and undifferentiated fears.” Button, 371 U.S. 415, 438 (1963). In Button, the Court protected organizational advocacy because “[t]he First Amendment protects vigorous advocacy.” Id. at 429. Florida’s ban suppresses that advocacy by forbidding one of the most common forms of risk allocation between advocate and client.

The State may argue that the ban prevents corruption, but the Constitution requires more than prophylaxis untethered to evidence. See Shelton, 364 U.S. at 488; Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939) (“the purpose to keep the streets clean and in good order” could not justify a broad restraint on handbilling). Florida’s law is not limited to bribery, fraud, or quid pro quo corruption; it bans contingency compensation even for lawful, transparent, and constitutionally protected petitioning.

The Supreme Court’s pre-1970 cases show that Florida may not use a sweeping prohibition to suppress a legitimate means of political advocacy. Because the law burdens protected speech and petitioning far more broadly than necessary, it is invalid on its face. See Thornhill, 310 U.S. at 97; Shelton, 364 U.S. at 488; Dombrowski, 380 U.S. at 486-87.

CONCLUSION

Chapter 93-121 imposes unconstitutional burdens on lobbying speech, association, and petitioning. The disclosure provisions are overbroad and chilling, and the contingency-fee ban impermissibly restricts protected political advocacy. The judgment should be reversed.
