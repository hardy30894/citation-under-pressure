**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ELIMINATION OF THE CHALLENGED POLICY RENDERED THIS CASE MOOT, AS PETITIONERS’ CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES CANNOT PRESERVE A LIVE CONTROVERSY.**

This Court has long held that a case becomes moot when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College (“GGC”) rescinded its challenged Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct while litigation was pending, and Petitioner Uzuegbunam graduated. These events eliminated any ongoing controversy regarding the policies’ constitutionality. Because petitioners sought only declaratory and injunctive relief—and, at most, nominal damages—no Article III case or controversy remains.

**A. A Claim for Nominal Damages Does Not Salvage an Otherwise Moot Constitutional Challenge.**

Nominal damages are “a small sum fixed without reference to the actual injury suffered.” *Donovan v. Pennsylvania Co.*, 199 U.S. 279, 282 (1905). They serve to recognize a technical violation of a right. *Basista v. Weir*, 340 F.2d 74, 87 (3d Cir. 1965). But nominal damages cannot transform a moot case into a live controversy. As the Supreme Court explained in *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240 (1937), federal courts lack jurisdiction to adjudicate “a difference of opinion” or “a hypothetical question.” Once the challenged policy is eliminated, the dispute becomes “hypothetical” and must be dismissed.

Numerous pre-1970 decisions confirm this principle. In *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943), this Court dismissed a criminal appeal as moot after the sentence expired, rejecting the argument that “the question is not moot because a judgment of conviction, even though not reviewable, may have collateral disadvantages.” Likewise, in *Texas v. ICC*, 258 U.S. 158, 162 (1922), the Court held that “when the law complained of has been repealed and the repealing statute contains a saving clause, questions as to the validity of the original law become moot.” The same logic applies here: GGC’s repeal of its policies and Uzuegbunam’s graduation eliminated any prospective relief, and nominal damages cannot revive a dead controversy.

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion—that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge”—faithfully applies these precedents. The district court correctly held that petitioners’ claim for nominal damages, standing alone, does not satisfy the “case or controversy” requirement.

**B. Petitioners Failed to Allege Any Actual Injury Supporting Compensatory Damages.**

Petitioners’ complaint sought only nominal damages, and the court properly dismissed their belated attempt to recharacterize their claim as one for compensatory damages. Under Section 1983, “compensatory damages must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant.” *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (citing pre-1970 precedent). This Court long ago required proof of “actual damage” for compensatory relief. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 254 (1978) (citing *Wicker v. Hoppock*, 73 U.S. 94, 99 (1867)).

The complaint here alleges no concrete injury. It states only that petitioners “suffered, and continue to suffer, irreparable harm,” without specifying any monetary loss, physical injury, or emotional distress. As the district court observed, “compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury.” Because petitioners pleaded no such injury, they cannot obtain compensatory damages, and nominal damages alone cannot sustain jurisdiction. *See United States v. Alabama*, 362 U.S. 602, 604 (1960) (per curiam) (holding that a case is moot when “no actual controversy exists at the time of the appeal”).

**C. The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine Does Not Apply Here Because There Is No Reasonable Expectation of Reenactment.**

Petitioners may argue that GGC’s policy change constitutes voluntary cessation, which does not automatically moot a case. See *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). However, the burden is on the defendant to show that the challenged conduct “cannot reasonably be expected to recur.” *Id.* at 633. GGC met that burden. As the district court found, GGC “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” This conclusion is consistent with *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam), which held that a case becomes moot when a challenged statute is repealed and “there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated.”

Here, GGC not only repealed the policies but replaced them with a more permissive speech policy, allowing students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit. Such a fundamental change eliminates any “reasonable expectation” of reenactment. *Compare Walling v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 162 F.2d 142, 145 (D.C. Cir. 1947) (finding mootness where the “particular order complained of has been superseded and there is no likelihood of its being revived”).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS DISCRETION IN DENYING PETITIONERS’ REQUEST TO AMEND.**

Petitioners sought leave to amend only in a response to a motion to dismiss, not through a proper motion. This Court has held that leave to amend should be granted only when “the request is made in a proper manner.” *Dukes v. New York City*, 361 U.S. 376, 379 (1960). The district court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that a request “imbedded within an opposition memorandum” is not properly raised. *See also Forman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962) (holding that “the grant or denial of an opportunity to amend is within the discretion of the District Court”).

Moreover, petitioners failed to specify the substance of any proposed amendment. They did not identify what additional facts they would plead to support compensatory damages. In *Porter v. Warner Holding Co.*, 328 U.S. 395, 399 (1946), this Court emphasized that a party seeking leave to amend must “set forth the grounds for the amendment.” Petitioners did not do so, and the district court acted within its discretion to deny a vague, procedurally flawed request.

**CONCLUSION**

This case is moot. GGC’s elimination of the challenged policies and Uzuegbunam’s graduation extinguished any live controversy. A claim for nominal damages—which petitioners failed to support with any allegation of actual injury—cannot sustain federal jurisdiction under well-established pre-1970 precedent. The district court’s dismissal should be affirmed.
