**ARGUMENT**

I. **The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’s law as a valid, content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation.**

The First Amendment permits reasonable, content-neutral regulations of speech in traditional public fora so long as they are narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests and leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 725-30 (2000); *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791, 798-99 (1989). The Massachusetts statute satisfies that standard. It applies on its face to all persons who “knowingly enter or remain” within marked buffer zones adjacent to reproductive health care facilities, regardless of viewpoint. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b), (c). Its object is not to suppress antiabortion expression, but to protect access, safety, and order at clinic entrances, exits, and driveways. That is a classic content-neutral public-safety regulation. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 729-30; *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768-70 (1994).

This Court’s precedents have repeatedly upheld restrictions aimed at clinic access when they burden speech only incidentally. In *Madsen*, the Court sustained a clinic injunction against protesters because the government’s interests in ensuring access and avoiding obstruction justified tailored limits in the immediate vicinity of clinics. 512 U.S. at 768-70. In *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 376-77, 380-81 (1997), the Court approved fixed buffer zones while invalidating only the more intrusive floating bubble zones. And in *Hill*, the Court upheld a statute that restricted certain protest activities within 100 feet of a health-care facility entrance, emphasizing that the law regulated conduct, not message, and left speakers free to communicate in numerous ways. 530 U.S. at 719-25, 729-30.

The Massachusetts law is materially narrower than the statute upheld in *Hill*. It imposes a fixed 35-foot zone, not a floating one. It exempts patients, employees, emergency responders, and persons using the sidewalk or right-of-way solely to reach another destination. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b). And it leaves petitioners ample means to speak, leaflet, pray, display signs, and counsel outside the zone. The First Circuit therefore correctly concluded that the law is narrowly tailored and preserves ample alternative channels. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99; *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726-30.

Petitioners’ principal complaint is that the law prevents them from standing immediately next to clinic entrants. But the First Amendment does not guarantee a speaker’s preferred audience, preferred distance, or preferred method of persuasion. *Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647-49 (1981). Nor does it require the State to permit obstruction or coercive close-quarters tactics simply because some speakers find them effective. *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 380-81; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-70.

II. **If *Hill v. Colorado* applies, it should be followed; if reconsidered, it should not control this case’s outcome.**

Petitioners ask the Court to limit or overrule *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). That request should be rejected. *Hill* is a settled First Amendment decision, and stare decisis has special force in constitutional cases where legislatures have relied on the Court’s ruling. *Planned Parenthood of Se. Pa. v. Casey*, 505 U.S. 833, 854-55 (1992); *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991). *Hill* directly approved a comparable access-protection statute and reasoned that the law was content neutral because it regulated where and how speech occurred, not what was said. 530 U.S. at 719-25.

Petitioners contend that later decisions cast doubt on *Hill*. They do not. Cases such as *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), addressed wholly different settings: political expenditures, disfavored speaker-based regulation, and offensive speech on matters of public concern. None involved fixed public-way restrictions designed to prevent obstruction at clinic entrances. *Snyder*, 562 U.S. at 451-58; *Sorrell*, 564 U.S. at 563-72; *Citizens United*, 558 U.S. at 340-48. Those decisions therefore do not undermine *Hill*’s core holding.

Nor is there any reason to overrule *Hill*. It has proved workable; lower courts have applied it for years; and Congress and the States have relied on its recognition that the government may protect access to health-care facilities through narrowly drawn buffer zones. See *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 725-30; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-70. The Court should therefore reaffirm, not repudiate, the principle that protecting access to clinics is a significant governmental interest that may justify modest limits on speech in the immediate vicinity of clinic entrances. *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768; *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 729-30.

Because the Massachusetts statute is content neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open ample alternative channels, the judgment below should be affirmed.
