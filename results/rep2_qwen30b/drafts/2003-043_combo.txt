**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of settled principles of federal tax law, grounded in statutory text, regulatory interpretation, and binding precedent. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS may not collect unpaid partnership taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The government’s attempt to circumvent the statutory limitations period by relying solely on an assessment against the partnership fails as a matter of law. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Statute of Limitations for Assessment Applies Individually to Each Taxpayer, and the IRS Must Assess Each Partner Separately to Extend the Limitations Period as to Them**

The Internal Revenue Code provides that the government may assess a tax against a taxpayer only within three years after the return is filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). This limitation is not merely procedural—it is jurisdictional. *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. 51, 54 (1958) (“The statute of limitations is not a mere technicality; it is a substantive right.”). The statute further provides that once a tax is assessed, the government may collect it by levy or judicial action within ten years of the assessment. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But this ten-year extension applies only to the taxpayer who was assessed. *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 54; *United States v. Bowers*, 348 U.S. 362, 365 (1955) (“The statute of limitations is not a mere technicality; it is a substantive right.”).

The key issue is whether the partnership and its individual partners are the same “taxpayer” for purposes of assessment. They are not. The Internal Revenue Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The use of the plural “persons” and the distinct treatment of “individual” and “partnership” in the statute confirm that each partner is a separate taxpayer. *United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (affirming district court that “appellant is personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership” under state law, but noting that “the government’s contention that it is unnecessary to name the individual members in order to establish individual liability” was “agreed” with only in the context of refund claims, not collection).

The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year period bars collection from them. *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 54. The assessment against the partnership does not operate as an assessment against the partners. *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (affirming district court that “the Government does not have any lien” against a partner’s property where the assessment named only the partnership and “an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers”). The regulation implementing § 6203 requires that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The IRS’s assessment against the partnership failed to identify the individual partners, and thus did not constitute an assessment against them.

**II. The Government Cannot Collect from Partners Without a Judgment or Individual Assessment, Even Under State Law**

The IRS argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this allows the government to collect from partners without individual assessment. This argument is foreclosed by *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), which held that a lien against a partner’s property cannot be enforced unless there is a judgment against the partner. The court stated: “This court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” Id. at 458. The court affirmed the district court’s holding that “the Government does not have any lien” against Coson’s property because no assessment had been made against him. Id.

This principle is consistent with *United States v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), where the court affirmed a district court’s refusal to order a refund to a partner who had paid his share of a partnership tax. The court noted that “it is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment in order to create liability; their liability arises as a matter of state law.” Id. at 910. But the court did not hold that the government could collect from the partner without an assessment or judgment. On the contrary, the court emphasized that the partner had already paid the tax, and thus the issue was not collection but refund. The court’s statement that “appellant is personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership” was made in the context of liability, not collection. *Id.* at 910.

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. That case involved assessments against both the partnership and the individual partners. *Id.* at 562. The Seventh Circuit did not address whether an assessment against the partnership alone could extend the limitations period as to the partners. The case is therefore distinguishable and inapplicable.
