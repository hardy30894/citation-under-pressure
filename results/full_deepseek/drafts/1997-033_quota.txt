**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) commands that after consolidated pretrial proceedings conclude, each transferred action “shall be remanded by the panel” to its originating district. The word “shall” creates a mandatory duty; the statute leaves no room for a transferee court to retain a case for trial by invoking the general venue transfer provision, 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). *Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992) (courts “must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says”); *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (statutory language “should be conclusive” absent ambiguity). Section 1407(a) contains no exception for self-transfer, and § 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific, later-enacted command.

**I. The Statute Unambiguously Requires Remand**

Congress created the multidistrict litigation mechanism in § 1407 to permit temporary consolidation of pretrial proceedings only. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” remand each action “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Every court of appeals to consider the question—before the Ninth Circuit’s errant decision here—has held that the transferee court lacks authority to retain a case for trial. *In re Fireman’s Fund Ins. Cos.*, 588 F.2d 93, 94–95 (5th Cir. 1979) (remand is “mandatory”); *In re Roberts*, 682 F.2d 105, 108 (3d Cir. 1982) (same); *In re Gen. Motors Corp. Engine Interchange Litig.*, 594 F.2d 1106, 1118 n.15 (7th Cir. 1979) (transferee court “has no power to transfer the case to itself for trial”); *In re Korean Air Lines Disaster of Sept. 1, 1983*, 829 F.2d 1171, 1175 (D.C. Cir. 1987) (Panel “must remand” after pretrial proceedings); *In re Pan Am. Corp.*, 16 F.3d 513, 516 (2d Cir. 1994) (remand is “required”); *In re Food Lion, Inc. Fair Labor Standards Act Litig.*, 73 F.3d 528, 532 (4th Cir. 1996) (same). The Judicial Panel itself has consistently recognized that its authority is limited to pretrial matters. *In re Plumbing Fixture Cases*, 298 F. Supp. 484, 494 (J.P.M.L. 1968) (Panel “has no power to assign a case for trial”); *In re Holiday Magic Sec. & Antitrust Litig.*, 433 F. Supp. 1125, 1126 (J.P.M.L. 1977) (same).

**II. Section 1404(a) Does Not Empower a Transferee Court to Retain a Case for Trial**

The majority below relied on the Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and cases such as *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litig.*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), to hold that a § 1404(a) motion may be entertained before remand. Those decisions, however, did not confront the clear statutory text. *Pfizer* itself acknowledged that its reading was not “required by the language of the statute,” 447 F.2d at 124, and *Fine Paper* simply followed *Pfizer* without independent analysis. The plain language of § 1407(a) controls. *See Ron Pair*, 489 U.S. at 241. Moreover, a § 1404(a) motion is not a “pretrial proceeding” within the meaning of § 1407(a); it is a dispositive venue determination that belongs to the original transferor court after remand. *Cf. In re Patten Sec.*, 798 F.2d 1395, 1398 (Fed. Cir. 1986) (transferee court may not “usurp” the Panel’s remand authority). The Manual for Complex Litigation’s endorsement of the practice does not alter the statute. *See In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, N.H.*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971) (“nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district … for purposes of trial”).

**III. The Practice Subverts Congress’s Policy Choice**

Congress deliberately limited the Panel’s authority to pretrial proceedings to preserve plaintiffs’ right to choose the trial forum. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 90-1130, at 2 (1968) (consolidation “should not be permitted to result in a change of the place of trial”). The self-transfer practice nullifies that right. Here, Lexecon was forced to litigate in Arizona for over a year, then told it would be convenient to try the case there. The Ninth Circuit’s reasoning—that the parties were “already before the federal district court in Arizona,” 61 F.3d at 912—proves too much; it rewards the very delay that § 1407(a) was designed to prevent. The dissent below correctly identified this as an “egregious, oft-repeated error.” *Id.* at 913 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). Only this Court can restore the statutory mandate. *See In re Dow Co. v. United States*, 10 F.3d 968, 975 (3d Cir. 1993) (remand required even if trial in transferee district would be more efficient).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to vacate the § 1404(a) transfer order and direct the district court to remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
