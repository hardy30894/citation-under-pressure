The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969)), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its decisions in Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), provide guidance.

In Bullington, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they "resemble a trial on guilt or innocence" (Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439). The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding involved a trial-like process, with the prosecution presenting evidence and the jury making a determination based on that evidence. Similarly, in Rumsey, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a capital sentencing proceeding in Arizona, noting that the sentencing hearing "had the hallmarks of a trial" (Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209).

The same logic applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, such as the one at issue here. Under California law, a defendant has a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations (Cal. Penal Code § 1025). The prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt (People v. Tenner, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993)), and the defendant has the right to confront witnesses and present evidence (People v. Reed, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224, 228 (1996)). These procedural protections make the sentencing proceeding trial-like, and thus, the Double Jeopardy Clause should apply.

The California Supreme Court's attempt to distinguish Bullington and Rumsey is unpersuasive. The Court's suggestion that the Supreme Court has implied that Bullington does not apply to noncapital cases is unfounded. In Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court merely noted that Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases, but did not hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause is limited to capital cases.

Furthermore, the California Supreme Court's distinction between statutory and constitutional protections is irrelevant. The Supreme Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause only applies to proceedings with constitutionally mandated procedural protections. In fact, Bullington itself involved statutory procedural protections not mandated by the federal Constitution.

The Double Jeopardy Clause should apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings like the one in this case. The trial-like nature of the proceeding, with its attendant procedural protections, makes it "resemble a trial on guilt or innocence" (Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439). Therefore, retrial on the prior conviction allegation is barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause.
