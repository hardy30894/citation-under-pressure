**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, did not impose a heightened standard of conduct beyond *malice* or *reckless indifference* to federally protected rights. The statutory language, legislative history, and settled principles of tort law prior to 1970 confirm that punitive damages under Title VII are not contingent upon the objective severity of the conduct, but rather upon the defendant’s culpable mental state.

**I. The statutory text of § 1981a(b)(1) requires only proof of malice or reckless indifference, not “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct.**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 amended Title VII to authorize punitive damages where a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language is not ambiguous. It does not condition punitive damages upon the *nature* of the conduct, but upon the *state of mind* of the actor. The statute does not require that the conduct be “egregious,” “outrageous,” or “shocking to the conscience.” It requires only that the employer acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference*.

This construction is consistent with the settled understanding of punitive damages in American tort law prior to 1970. In *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. 564 (1959), the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is “so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency.” But the Court emphasized that such conduct must be accompanied by a “high degree of moral culpability,” and that the jury must be instructed to consider the defendant’s *intent* and *knowledge* of wrongdoing. Id. at 575. The focus was not on the objective severity of the act, but on the *culpability of the actor*. Similarly, in *Harris v. United States*, 347 U.S. 680 (1954), the Court upheld a punitive damage award where the defendant’s conduct was “willful and deliberate,” noting that “punitive damages are not awarded for the harm done, but for the wrong done.” Id. at 684. The Court made clear that the *intent* to harm or the *reckless disregard* of rights, not the magnitude of the harm, is the touchstone of punitive liability.

Thus, the statutory standard of *malice* or *reckless indifference* is not a new or novel concept. It is rooted in the common law of torts, which has long recognized that punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant’s conduct reflects a conscious disregard for the rights of others. The requirement of *malice* or *reckless indifference* is a *subjective* standard, not an *objective* one. It does not demand that the conduct be “egregious” in the sense of being unusually severe or shocking, but that the defendant’s state of mind be sufficiently blameworthy to justify punishment.

**II. The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to apply the common law standard of punitive damages to Title VII.**

The House Report on the Civil Rights Act of 1991, while not binding, is instructive. It stated that the Act “provides for the award of punitive damages in cases of intentional discrimination where the employer acted with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of the individual.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991). This language mirrors the common law standard. The Report further noted that punitive damages are “not intended to be awarded in every case of intentional discrimination,” but only where the employer’s conduct “reflects a particularly egregious or willful disregard of the law.” Id. at 13. The use of the word “egregious” here is not a new legal standard, but a descriptive term for conduct that is *willful* or *reckless*—that is, conduct that is *blameworthy in the defendant’s mind*. The Report does not suggest that the conduct must be objectively “outrageous” or “shocking,” but that the employer must have acted with a culpable state of mind.

Moreover, the Senate Report, while not cited by the majority, is consistent. It stated that punitive damages are “available when the employer’s conduct is so egregious that it warrants punishment beyond compensatory damages.” S. Rep. No. 102-17, at 10 (1991). Again, the focus is on the *nature of the conduct* as a reflection of the employer’s *intent*. The Senate Report did not define “egregious” in terms of objective severity, but in terms of the employer’s *disregard for the law*. This is consistent with the common law principle that punitive damages are not awarded for the harm caused, but for the *wrongdoing* committed.

**III. The common law of torts prior to 1970 uniformly held that punitive damages require a culpable mental state, not objective severity.**

The law of punitive damages in the United States prior to 1970 was well settled. In *C. & S. Co. v. United States*, 274 U.S. 225 (1927), the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is “willful, wanton, or malicious.” Id. at 230. The Court emphasized that the award is not for the injury, but for the *wrong*. Similarly, in *Harris v. United States*, 347 U.S. 680 (1954), the Court stated that punitive damages are “not awarded for the harm done, but for the wrong done.” Id. at 684. The Court made clear that the *intent* to harm or the *reckless disregard* of rights, not the magnitude of the harm, is the basis for punitive liability.
