The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard for Exemption 2 is unfounded and contradicts the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent.

The Freedom of Information Act ("FOIA") reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Exemption 2 exempts from disclosure matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this exemption narrowly, holding that it applies to information that is "used for purely internal purposes" and is not of "genuine and significant public interest." Rose, 425 U.S. at 363, 369.

The Ninth Circuit's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard, borrowed from the D.C. Circuit's decision in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C.Cir.1981), is not supported by the statutory text or Supreme Court precedent. The phrase "predominantly internal" is not found in the statute, and the Supreme Court has not endorsed this standard. Instead, the Court has emphasized that FOIA exemptions "must be narrowly construed" and are "explicitly exclusive." U.S. Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting Administrator, FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's application of the "predominantly internal" standard to the ESQD arc maps is flawed. The maps are not "related solely" to internal personnel rules and practices, as they have significant implications for public safety and are not merely internal agency documents. The Navy's own use of the maps, as described by Commander Whitbred, demonstrates their importance for designing and constructing ammunition storage facilities and organizing ammunition operations.

The Ninth Circuit's decision also undermines the purpose of FOIA, which is to provide the public with access to government information. By exempting the ESQD arc maps from disclosure, the court is allowing the Navy to withhold information that is of significant public interest and concern. This is particularly troubling given the Navy's inconsistent treatment of similar information at other bases, such as Bangor.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt the ESQD arc maps from disclosure under Exemption 2 is erroneous and should be reversed. The "predominantly internal" standard is not supported by the statutory text or Supreme Court precedent, and its application to the ESQD arc maps is flawed. The Navy's withholding of this information is not justified under FOIA, and the public has a right to access this information under the Act.
