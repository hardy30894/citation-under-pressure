The D.C. Circuit erred in concluding that the 2004 consent decree resolved Guam’s liability within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring its timely § 107(a) cost-recovery action. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004) (noting that § 107 permits a PRP that has incurred response costs to recover from other PRPs). Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The decree’s plain terms establish that no such resolution occurred.

The decree expressly disclaims liability, stating that it was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3. Courts interpreting materially identical language have held that a disclaimer precludes resolution of liability under § 113(f)(3)(B). See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015); Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). The decree further arose from Clean Water Act claims alleging pollutant discharges, not CERCLA hazardous-substance liability. See United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (emphasizing CERCLA’s distinct focus on hazardous substances). Because the decree addressed only non-CERCLA violations, it could not resolve “liability . . . for some or all of a response action” under CERCLA. Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009).

Statutory structure confirms this reading. Section 113(f)(3)(B) was enacted to encourage CERCLA settlements by providing contribution protection; extending it to CWA decrees would undermine the distinct limitations periods Congress prescribed. See United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 138–39 (2007) (distinguishing § 107 cost recovery from § 113 contribution). The decree’s substantive obligations—closure and cover-system construction—likewise fail to satisfy the statutory trigger, as they were imposed to remedy Clean Water Act violations rather than to allocate CERCLA response costs. See Key Tronic Corp. v. United States, 511 U.S. 809, 815 (1994) (recognizing that CERCLA liability turns on hazardous-substance releases).

Finally, the decree’s entry did not adjudicate any factual or legal issue of CERCLA responsibility. See Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, Inc., 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019). Absent an express resolution of CERCLA liability, Guam retains its § 107(a) claim, which remains timely under the six-year limitations period. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). The judgment below should be reversed.
