## ARGUMENT

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (“Santander”) purchased the petitioners’ defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collected on its own behalf. Because Santander collected debts owed to itself, not to another, it acted as a creditor, not a debt collector. The Fourth Circuit correctly affirmed dismissal.

**I. The plain language of the FDCPA requires that a “debt collector” collect debts “owed or due another.”**

The statute’s threshold definition of “debt collector” includes any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The word “another” is unambiguous: the debt must be owed to a third party at the time of collection. This Court has long held that when statutory language is clear, courts must give effect to its plain meaning. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). “Where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning, the duty of interpretation does not arise.” *Id.* at 485 (quoting *Lake County v. Rollins*, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)). The FDCPA’s definition cannot be stretched to cover a person collecting its own debt. *See United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82 (1932) (statutory words must be given their ordinary meaning).

**II. The common law distinction between a creditor collecting his own debt and a debt collector acting for another confirms the statutory scheme.**

At common law, one who purchases a debt becomes the owner of that debt and may collect it as a creditor. *See Story v. Livingston*, 38 U.S. 359, 371 (1839) (assignee of a debt stands in the shoes of the assignor and may sue in his own name). The FDCPA codifies this distinction. The definition of “creditor” in § 1692a(4) includes any person “to whom a debt is owed.” Santander, having purchased the loans, became the person to whom the debts were owed. Accordingly, it is a creditor, not a debt collector. *See Little v. Hackett*, 116 U.S. 366, 371 (1886) (assignee of a debt is the real party in interest). The petitioners’ argument that default status determines status would collapse this distinction, contrary to the common law principle that ownership of a debt, not its maturity, defines the creditor-debtor relationship. *See National Bank v. Merchants’ National Bank*, 91 U.S. 92, 98 (1875) (holder of a note is the owner and may collect it).

**III. The FDCPA’s exclusions confirm that the default status of a debt is irrelevant to the threshold definition.**

The petitioners rely on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which removes from the definition of “debt collector” a person collecting a debt that “was not in default at the time it was obtained.” But this exclusion applies only to persons who *otherwise* meet the definition of debt collector—i.e., those who collect debts for another. *See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015). Congress did not intend the default status to create a definitional shortcut. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (statutory construction must consider the whole act, not isolated phrases). The exclusion’s limited purpose—to protect loan servicers of non-defaulted debts—is well established. *See S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977)*. This Court has cautioned against reading exclusions as creating affirmative definitions. *See Helvering v. Griffiths*, 318 U.S. 371, 395 (1943) (exclusions from a definition do not create a new category of coverage).

**IV. The FDCPA is a remedial statute, but it must be enforced according to its terms.**

The petitioners invoke the Act’s broad purpose to eliminate abusive debt collection practices. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). But even remedial statutes have limits. *Commissioner v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 563, 571 (1965) (remedial purpose does not override clear statutory language). Here, Congress chose to regulate “debt collectors,” not all persons who collect debts. *See United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 95 (1820) (penal statutes must be strictly construed). The petitioners’ proposed rule—that any purchaser of defaulted debt becomes a debt collector—would sweep in every creditor that buys a non-performing loan, including banks, finance companies, and investors. Nothing in the FDCPA’s text or history supports such a radical expansion. *See The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913) (courts must not add words to a statute).

**V. The petitioners’ “default status” test is inconsistent with the statutory structure.**

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” in two parts: the main text sets forth who is included; the exclusions set forth who is excluded. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The petitioners invert this structure by using an exclusion to create a definition. This Court has rejected such reasoning. *See United States v. Dickerson*, 310 U.S. 554, 561 (1940) (exclusions are not to be read as including matters not within the statutory language). Moreover, the petitioners’ test would produce absurd results: a creditor who purchases a defaulted debt would be a debt collector, but a creditor who purchases the same debt before default would not. Congress could not have intended such an arbitrary distinction. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 518 (1911) (statutes should be interpreted to avoid absurd consequences).

**VI. The Fourth Circuit correctly applied the agency principle underlying the statute.**

The FDCPA was designed to regulate third-party debt collectors acting as agents for creditors. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (defining debt collector as one who collects debts “due another”). This Court has long recognized the distinction between a principal collecting its own debt and an agent collecting for another. *See Interstate Commerce Comm’n v. Baird*, 194 U.S. 25, 44 (1904) (agent collects for principal, not for himself). When Santander purchased the loans, it ceased to act as an agent and became the principal. The petitioners’ allegation that Santander previously serviced the loans as an agent does not change its status after purchase. *See Mackenzie v. Laskey*, 87 U.S. 391, 394 (1874) (a person may act in different capacities at different times).

**VII. The petitioners’ “loophole” argument lacks merit.**

The petitioners contend that permitting Santander to become a creditor upon purchase would create a “loophole” allowing debt collectors to evade the FDCPA by buying the debt. But that argument misapprehends the statute. The FDCPA does not prohibit debt collection; it prohibits abusive practices. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). A creditor that purchases debt and then collects it is subject to state law and other federal statutes, but not the FDCPA’s specific provisions. *See United States v. Patten*, 226 U.S. 525, 541 (1913) (courts should not strain to create liability where Congress has not imposed it). The petitioners’ policy concerns are properly addressed to Congress, not the courts.

**VIII. The petitioners’ interpretation would create an unworkable standard.**

Under the petitioners’ test, every purchaser of a defaulted debt would be a debt collector, regardless of the purchaser’s business model. This would include entities that rarely engage in debt collection. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (requiring regular collection for another). The statute’s “principal purpose” definition would also be undermined. *See id.* (defining debt collector as one whose “principal purpose” is debt collection). This Court has held that statutes should be interpreted to give effect to all their clauses. *See United States v. Patten*, 226 U.S. at 541. The petitioners’ reading would render the “principal purpose” definition superfluous.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed. Santander was collecting its own debts, not debts owed to another, and therefore was not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA.
