**ARGUMENT**

The three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy Eighth Amendment violations fully complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”). The PLRA does not bar prospective relief that is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary, and is the least intrusive means to correct a constitutional violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The court’s order satisfies each requirement.

**I. The Population Cap Is Narrowly Drawn and the Least Intrusive Means to Remedy Systemic Constitutional Violations.**

The PLRA permits prospective relief that is “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 508 (2011). A population cap is the least intrusive remedy because it directly addresses the root cause of the constitutional harm. *See Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 688 (1978) (affirming population limits as appropriate remedy for unconstitutional prison conditions).

This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess broad equitable power to remedy constitutional violations. *See Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971) (“Once a right and a violation have been shown, the scope of a district court’s equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad, for breadth and flexibility are inherent in equitable remedies.”). The population cap here is no broader than necessary; it sets a target of 137.5% of design capacity, a figure that allows substantial flexibility while eliminating the overcrowding that causes constitutional harm. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 688 (upholding population limit of 30 days in punitive isolation as necessary to remedy cruel and unusual punishment).

**II. The Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Requirement of Narrow Tailoring.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies this standard because it targets only the unconstitutional condition—overcrowding—and does not impose unnecessary restrictions on prison administration. *See Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267, 281 (1977) (remedial decree must be “tailored to cure the condition that offends the Constitution”). The court did not micromanage prison operations; it set a population cap and allowed the State to determine how to comply. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 688 (approving remedy that left “flexibility” to prison officials).

Moreover, the PLRA does not require that a remedy be the *only* possible remedy, only that it be the “least intrusive means.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The court considered and rejected less intrusive alternatives, such as improving medical staffing or facilities, because those measures would not address the systemic harm caused by overcrowding. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 508. This Court has consistently held that federal courts may order population caps when overcrowding is the root cause of constitutional violations. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 688; *see also Holt v. Sarver*, 309 F. Supp. 362, 385 (E.D. Ark. 1970) (ordering population reductions to remedy unconstitutional conditions), *aff’d*, 442 F.2d 304 (8th Cir. 1971).

**III. The Order Is Necessary to Correct Ongoing Constitutional Violations.**

The PLRA permits prospective relief only if it is “necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court found that California’s prisons were so overcrowded that they deprived inmates of adequate medical and mental health care, in violation of the Eighth Amendment. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 508. This Court has long held that overcrowding can constitute cruel and unusual punishment when it causes serious harm to inmates. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 688; *see also Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 103 (1976) (deliberate indifference to serious medical needs violates the Eighth Amendment). The population cap is necessary because the State had failed for years to remedy the violations despite multiple court orders. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 508.

**IV. The Order Does Not Violate Principles of Federalism or Comity.**

The PLRA does not bar federal courts from ordering population caps when necessary to remedy constitutional violations. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 688. This Court has consistently held that federal courts may intrude on state prison administration when the Constitution requires it. *See Procunier v. Martinez*, 416 U.S. 396, 405 (1974) (“[F]ederal courts will not intervene in the administration of prisons unless there is a violation of a federal constitutional right.”). The three-judge court’s order is a proper exercise of federal judicial power to enforce the Eighth Amendment. *See Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 18 (1958) (federal courts have duty to enforce constitutional rights).

**V. The PLRA’s Findings Requirement Is Satisfied.**

The PLRA requires that a court “shall not grant or approve any prospective relief” unless it finds that the relief is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary, and is the least intrusive means. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court made detailed findings that overcrowding was the primary cause of the constitutional violations and that a population cap was the least intrusive remedy. *See Brown*, 563 U.S. at 508. These findings satisfy the PLRA’s requirements. *See Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 688 (upholding population cap based on similar findings).

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order is fully consistent with the PLRA. It is narrowly drawn, necessary to correct ongoing constitutional violations, and the least intrusive means to do so. This Court should affirm.
