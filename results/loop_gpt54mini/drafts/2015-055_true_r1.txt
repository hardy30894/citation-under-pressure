**Argument**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively on collateral review because it is a substantive rule under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and not a mere procedural one. Although the Court in *Johnson* invalidated the ACCA residual clause as unconstitutionally vague, the retroactivity question is governed by longstanding precedent holding that new substantive rules apply to cases on collateral review. See *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments in part and dissenting in part); *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965); *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966); *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969); *Warden, Lewisburg Penitentiary v. Marrero*, 417 U.S. 653 (1974); *United States v. The Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103 (1801).

The controlling principle is that when a decision changes the substantive reach of a criminal statute, it applies retroactively because it changes the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971). That is exactly what *Johnson* did. Before *Johnson*, the residual clause extended ACCA punishment to convictions that otherwise involved conduct that presented a serious potential risk of physical injury to another. After *Johnson*, that clause can no longer lawfully be used at all. The result is that some prisoners previously sentenced under ACCA no longer fall within the statute’s mandatory minimum. A decision that removes a category of convictions from the statute’s coverage is substantive, not procedural. See *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971); *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965).

This Court has long recognized that retroactivity is especially appropriate where a new rule reflects a determination that the State lacked power to impose the challenged punishment. In *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), the Court held that a conviction under an unconstitutional statute cannot stand. In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), the Court likewise recognized that a court exceeds its lawful power when it imposes punishment not authorized by statute. *Johnson* invalidated a portion of ACCA’s definition of “violent felony,” which means the government lacked authority to impose enhanced punishment based on that clause. A prisoner serving a sentence enhanced by an invalid clause is therefore detained under a sentence the law does not permit. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874); *United States v. Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103 (1801).

The government may argue that *Johnson* announced only a constitutional “procedure” for classifying prior convictions. That argument fails because *Johnson* did not regulate how courts conduct sentencing; it eliminated one category of predicate offense from ACCA altogether. Where a new rule narrows the statute’s reach, it is substantive and retroactive. See *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971); *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). The same distinction appears in the Court’s older retroactivity cases, which turn on whether a new rule changes the legal consequences of prior conduct, not merely the mode of adjudication. See *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965); *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966); *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969).

Nor is there any serious fairness concern in applying *Johnson* retroactively. The Due Process Clause forbids punishment based on vague criminal laws. A conviction or sentence resting on a void provision cannot be insulated from collateral review by the happenstance of timing. As the Court stated in *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), an unconstitutional law is “no law.” That principle compels relief here because Welch’s ACCA sentence was enhanced under a clause the Supreme Court has now held invalid.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed, and Welch’s sentence vacated.
