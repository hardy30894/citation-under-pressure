**ARGUMENT**

I. Milford’s Denial of Access Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because It Was Based on Subject Matter, Not Viewpoint, and Was Viewpoint-Neutral.

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s application was constitutional because it was based on the Club’s subject matter—religious worship and instruction—rather than its viewpoint. *See Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (“[T]he government must abstain from regulating speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction.”). The Club’s proposed activities—singing religious songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying—are quintessentially religious in nature and fall outside the scope of Milford’s community use policy, which permits only “social, civic and recreational meetings” and excludes religious purposes. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (holding that a school’s denial of access to a religious group for after-hours use was unconstitutional where the policy was not viewpoint-neutral); *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 512 (2d Cir. 2000) (affirming summary judgment where the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious” and thus outside the scope of the policy).

Milford’s policy is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restriction on a limited public forum. *See Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983) (explaining that a limited public forum is “created for a limited purpose” and the government may impose “reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions” on speech). The policy explicitly excludes religious worship and instruction, and the Club’s proposed activities fall squarely within that exclusion. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512 (“The Club’s activities are not merely discussions of morals and character from a religious viewpoint; they are religious instruction and prayer.”). The Club’s attempt to recast its activities as “moral and character development” is unavailing, as the Supreme Court has recognized that religious speech cannot be transmuted into secular speech by relabeling. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (“There is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech’—and become unprotected ‘worship.’”).

II. Milford’s Denial Was Justified by the Establishment Clause Because Permitting the Club’s Activities Would Constitute Government Endorsement of Religion.

Even if the Club’s speech were deemed secular, Milford permissibly excluded it to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. *See Board of Educ. of Kiryas Joel Vill. Sch. Dist. v. Grumet*, 512 U.S. 687, 696 (1994) (“The Religion Clauses prevent the government from ‘mak[ing][] a law respecting an establishment of religion’ or ‘prohibit[ing] the free exercise thereof.’”); *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394-95 (upholding a school’s refusal to allow a religious group to use its facilities for religious purposes to avoid Establishment Clause concerns). The Club’s activities—prayer, scripture memorization, and evangelical instruction—would convey a message of school endorsement of Christianity, particularly given the Club’s use of school facilities and the impressionable age of its audience. *See Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308 (2000) (holding that school-sponsored prayer violates the Establishment Clause); *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (same).

The Second Circuit correctly distinguished this case from *Lamb’s Chapel*, where the Court held that a school’s refusal to allow a religious group to use its facilities for after-hours meetings violated the Free Speech Clause. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the proposed use was a film series discussing family values from a Christian perspective, which the Court deemed secular enough to warrant access. *Id.* at 388-89. Here, by contrast, the Club’s activities are explicitly religious worship and instruction, not merely discussions of morality from a religious perspective. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. Milford’s policy is thus constitutional under both the Free Speech and Establishment Clauses.

III. The Club’s Reliance on *Rosenberger* Is Misplaced Because That Case Involved Viewpoint Discrimination, Not Subject Matter Exclusion.

The Club erroneously relies on *Rosenberger* for the proposition that Milford’s denial was unconstitutional. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829, involved a university’s refusal to fund a student-run religious magazine, which the Court held was viewpoint discrimination. Here, Milford’s policy excludes all religious worship and instruction, regardless of viewpoint. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. The Club’s attempt to recast its activities as secular is unavailing, as the Supreme Court has made clear that religious speech cannot be transmuted into secular speech by relabeling. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s application did not violate the First Amendment, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
