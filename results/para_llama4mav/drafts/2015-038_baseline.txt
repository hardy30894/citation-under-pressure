The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) grants the National Park Service (NPS) authority to regulate activities within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including those on non-federal lands. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) supports the NPS's regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within CSUs.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon's interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute's plain text. The provision states that "only those lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] which are public lands ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). However, this language does not preclude the NPS from exercising regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs.

In Katie John III, this Court held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not limit the NPS's authority to regulate non-federal lands within CSUs. 720 F.3d 1214 (9th Cir.2013). The Court's decision is consistent with the statute's language and legislative history, which indicate that Congress intended to preserve the NPS's authority to regulate activities within CSUs, regardless of land ownership.

Sturgeon's claim that the NPS lacks authority to regulate his use of a hovercraft on state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley River National Preserve is without merit. The NPS's hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of its regulatory authority under ANILCA, and the Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees.

The State of Alaska's challenge to the NPS's regulatory authority also fails. Alaska lacks standing to bring this challenge, as it has not demonstrated a concrete and particularized injury. The Ninth Circuit correctly vacated the district court's judgment as to Alaska and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

The NPS's regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs is consistent with the principles of federalism and the property clause of the Constitution. See Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529 (1976). The Property Clause grants Congress the power to regulate federal property, including national parks. The NPS's authority to regulate activities within CSUs is a valid exercise of this power.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) and the statute's legislative history support the NPS's regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs. Sturgeon's claim is without merit, and Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS's regulatory authority.
