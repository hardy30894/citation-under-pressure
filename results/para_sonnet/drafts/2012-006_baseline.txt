**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires the Government to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That Petitioners' Membership in the Conspiracy Continued Into the Limitations Period.**

The Due Process Clause requires the government to prove "every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which [a defendant] is charged" beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This is not a mere procedural nicety; it is "a prime instrument for reducing the risk of convictions resting on factual error" and reflects "a fundamental value determination of our society that it is far worse to convict an innocent man than to let a guilty man go free." *Id.* at 372 (Harlan, J., concurring). Where a fact is essential to establishing criminal liability—rather than merely mitigating an otherwise complete offense—the burden of proving that fact cannot be shifted to the accused. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 698 (1975) (state may not require defendant to disprove a fact that "can serve to reduce" the degree of the crime, because doing so "effectively shifts the burden of persuasion" on an element).

Conspiracy is a continuing offense; the crime is not complete at any single moment but persists until the conspiracy's objects are achieved or abandoned. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) ("the offense is complete when the [agreement] is made ... but it is continuous, and does not end with the making of the agreement"). Because the statute of limitations for conspiracy runs from the last act in furtherance of the agreement rather than its inception, membership *during* the limitations period is not an incidental detail—it is the fact that transforms conduct occurring years earlier into a currently prosecutable crime. Cf. *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970) (statutes of limitations "are to be liberally interpreted in favor of repose," and it is the government's burden to show the offense was "committed within the period of limitations"). Just as the government must prove that a completed-conduct crime occurred within the limitations period, it must equally prove that a continuing-conduct crime like conspiracy remained ongoing—with the defendant as a member—into that period.

**II. Evidence of Withdrawal Places the Burden of Persuasion on the Government Because It Negates an Element of Continuing Membership, Rather Than Establishing a True Affirmative Defense.**

The government may permissibly place the burden of persuasion on a defendant only for genuine affirmative defenses—matters that excuse or justify conduct that otherwise satisfies every element of the offense. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977) (state may require defendant to prove an affirmative defense so long as it "does not serve to negative any facts of the crime which the State is to prove in order to convict"). Withdrawal is different in kind. It does not excuse continued participation in an ongoing conspiracy; it demonstrates that the very fact the government must prove—continuing membership—did not exist during the limitations window. *See Hyde*, 225 U.S. at 369 (a conspirator remains criminally liable for acts of the conspiracy "unless, before the act done, he repudiates the conspiracy," at which point his membership, and hence the basis for continuing liability, ends).

Once a defendant produces evidence that he left the conspiracy before the limitations period began, the prosecution's theory of continuing membership is directly controverted. To require the defendant to *prove* withdrawal by a preponderance—rather than requiring the government to disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt—effectively relieves the government of proving an essential temporal element: that petitioners remained members of the conspiracy when the limitations clock was running. That is precisely the burden-shifting this Court condemned in *Mullaney*, 421 U.S. at 703–04, where requiring a defendant to prove heat of passion by a preponderance unconstitutionally "requir[ed] the defendant to negate" a fact bearing directly on the degree of the offense charged. The same logic controls here: requiring petitioners to prove withdrawal, rather than requiring the government to disprove it, requires the defendant to negate a fact—continuing membership within the limitations period—that is indispensable to conviction.

**III. Sound Policy and the Structure of the Statute of Limitations Confirm That the Government Must Bear This Burden.**

Statutes of limitations "protect individuals from having to defend themselves against charges when the basic facts may have become obscured by the passage of time," *Toussie*, 397 U.S. at 114–15, and this concern is most acute in conspiracy prosecutions, where an accused's connection to a scheme may have ended years before trial. If the burden rests on the defendant merely to introduce "some evidence" of withdrawal, while the ultimate burden of persuasion remains with the defendant, the practical effect is to conscript stale, decayed evidence against the very party the limitations period was designed to protect. That result inverts the presumption of innocence, which "lies at the foundation of the administration of our criminal law." *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895).

Nor does placing the ultimate burden on the government impose an undue evidentiary hardship. The government retains the initial and primary burden of proving the conspiracy's existence and each defendant's membership at all relevant times. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464–65 (1978), recognized only that a defendant asserting withdrawal must come forward with evidence of an affirmative act of withdrawal—a production burden, not a persuasion burden. Once that evidence is presented, fairness and the constitutional principles of *Winship* and *Mullaney* require the government, which alone bears the burden of proving guilt, to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners' membership persisted into the limitations period.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should hold that once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the limitations period, the burden of persuasion shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period.
