**ARGUMENT**

**I. The plain, mandatory text of § 1407(a) forecloses the practice below.**

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statutory text is unambiguous, and this Court has long held that "the sole function of the courts" in such circumstances "is to enforce [the statute] according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Where, as here, "the language of the statute is plain, and admits of no more than one meaning," there is "no occasion to resort to the rules of construction." *United States v. Missouri Pacific R.R. Co.*, 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929).

The word "shall" is not surplusage; it is a command. When Congress uses "shall" rather than "may," it removes the matter from the realm of discretion. *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947) (distinguishing mandatory from permissive statutory language). This Court applied that same principle to reject an argument that a mandatory statutory procedure could be treated as merely directory, explaining that the statute's terms "must be complied with" and cannot be relaxed by a court that finds compliance inconvenient. *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Section 1407(a) contains no exception permitting a transferee court to disregard remand once pretrial proceedings conclude, and no court may supply one. Every word of the statute must be given effect. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) (it is "our duty to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute") (quoting *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883)). A construction permitting the transferee court to retain the case for trial renders the remand command a nullity, since the very command whose text is "shall be remanded" would in practice mean "may be remanded, unless the transferee judge prefers to keep the case." That reading violates the settled duty to give operative effect to every word Congress enacted. *Montclair*, 107 U.S. at 152; *Menasche*, 348 U.S. at 538–39.

**II. A specific statutory command controls over the general grant of transfer authority in § 1404(a).**

Respondent does not dispute that § 1407(a) commands remand; it argues only that § 1404(a)'s general authority to transfer "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice" permits the transferee court to transfer the case to itself, thereby avoiding remand altogether. But § 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision of nationwide application, while § 1407(a) is a specific provision governing the narrow category of multidistrict proceedings, complete with its own mandatory remand requirement. This Court has repeatedly refused to permit a general statute to override the specific command of a special one absent a clear indication that Congress intended that result.

In *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222 (1957), the Court confronted an analogous problem: whether the general venue statute for corporations could be read to expand venue in patent infringement suits beyond the specific venue statute Congress had enacted for that class of cases. The Court held that the specific provision controlled, because Congress had enacted the special venue statute for patent suits as a particular, deliberate limitation, and nothing indicated an intent to have the general provision override it. *Id.* at 228–29. The same logic applies here. Congress enacted § 1407(a) as a careful, particular scheme for coordinating pretrial proceedings while preserving the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial. It cannot be presumed that Congress, having gone to the trouble of writing a mandatory remand provision into that specific scheme, silently intended the general transfer provision of § 1404(a) to swallow it whole. If Congress had wished to allow transferee courts to retain multidistrict cases for trial, it could easily have said so, just as it specified in § 1407(a) that transfer was for "pretrial proceedings" only. Courts may not enlarge a statute under the guise of interpreting it. *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926) (rejecting a construction that would, "in effect," be "an enlargement" of the statute "by the court").

Nor can respondent take comfort from decisions applying § 1404(a) transfer authority to specific venue statutes elsewhere in the Judicial Code. In *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), this Court held that § 1404(a) could operate alongside the specific venue provision of the Federal Employers' Liability Act because nothing in that Act's venue provision purported to withdraw the ordinary discretionary transfer power Congress had preserved for the convenience of the parties. *Id.* at 58–61. Here, by contrast, § 1407(a) does not merely coexist with § 1404(a); it contains an express, mandatory directive—remand "shall" occur—that directly conflicts with any claimed power to transfer the case to the transferee court itself for trial. *Collett* thus confirms, rather than undermines, the rule that a specific and mandatory command displaces a general grant of discretion where the two cannot be reconciled.

**III. Considerations of convenience cannot supplant the statute's command.**

The court of appeals did not conclude that the district court's order was correct; it merely declined, on an interlocutory petition, to say the error was "clear." That is no endorsement of the practice, and it cannot substitute for a construction faithful to the statute's text. Whatever administrative convenience transferee courts may find in retaining cases for trial, "the duty of interpretation does not arise" where the statutory command is plain. *Missouri Pacific*, 278 U.S. at 278. Congress, not the judiciary, balances the convenience of coordinated proceedings against the plaintiff's right to trial in the chosen forum, and it did so by requiring remand. The judgment below should be reversed.
