ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying Sieben’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because Feltmann failed to present legally sufficient evidence that Sieben’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for her discharge were pretexts for sex discrimination or retaliation. Under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), once an employer articulates a legitimate reason, the presumption of discrimination drops away and the plaintiff must prove both that the reason is false and that the true reason was unlawful discrimination. St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993). Feltmann did neither.

**I. Sieben’s articulated reasons were true and nondiscriminatory.**

Sieben discharged Feltmann because she had the lowest average gross profit per vehicle of any consultant for five consecutive months, because she failed to follow managerial directives about her sales methods, and because she repeatedly violated a vehicle-use policy after written warning. These are quintessential legitimate business reasons. The record confirms them: her profit average was lowest from December 1992 through April 1993; Rich sent her a January memo criticizing her “work ethic”; he sent a February memo specifically prohibiting her from taking her demo car outside the St. Louis area; and she admitted she continued the weekly 300-mile trips for two more months. Moreover, male consultant Rick Beutel was also placed on probation for low sales performance, undermining any inference that discipline was gender-based. See Hutson v. McDonnell Douglas Corp., 63 F.3d 771, 781 (8th Cir. 1995) (plaintiff must show the employer’s “articulated reason is false”).

Feltmann’s Pinnacle Club award does not contradict the objective profit data. A salesperson can sell many vehicles at low margins, and Rich testified that her failure to explain vehicle features prevented her from commanding higher prices. Courts do not sit as “super-personnel departments.” Rothmeier v. Investment Advisers, Inc., 85 F.3d 1328, 1336 (8th Cir. 1996). Thus Sieben’s business judgment was reasonable and lawful.

**II. Feltmann offered no evidence of pretext or discriminatory animus.**

Her evidence of sex discrimination consisted of (1) her status as the only female consultant, (2) one offensive remark by an employee at a different franchise, and (3) her exclusion from a voluntary sales trip. None establishes pretext. The remark was made by Gordon Anzalone, who was not in her chain of command and not a decisionmaker. Sieben promptly investigated, reprimanded him, and offered to fire him—hardly evidence of discriminatory animus. The Chicago trip was not a term or condition of employment; Rich first offered it to the two highest producers, and when they declined, he did not offer it to anyone. Feltmann was therefore not singled out. Being the only female consultant is insufficient, without more, to prove discrimination. See Johnson v. Bunny Bread Co., 646 F.2d 1250, 1257 (8th Cir. 1981).

Feltmann could not show that Rich’s or the Capps’ actions were motivated by her gender. The memos, probation, and discharge all tracked specific, documented performance and policy violations. She produced no evidence that any male consultant who engaged in similar conduct was treated more favorably. In fact, Beutel—a male—was disciplined for the identical low-sales issue.

**III. The retaliation claim fails for lack of any causal connection.**

A retaliation plaintiff must prove that her protected complaint was a determining factor in the adverse action. Gray v. University of Arkansas, 947 F.2d 863, 868 (8th Cir. 1991). Here, Sieben responded to her complaint promptly and favorably: the owners met with her, reprimanded Anzalone, and offered to terminate him. There was no subsequent adverse action until her discharge months later, after she continued to violate a known policy and to post poor financial results. Temporal proximity alone—especially over a four-month gap—cannot establish causation. See Jackson v. St. Joseph State Hosp., 840 F.2d 1387, 1391 (8th Cir. 1988). The evidence instead showed that Rich and the Capps acted on lawful, specific business concerns that arose independently after the complaint. Feltmann’s own admission that she continued using her demo car for months after the written warning is undisputed and fatal to her retaliation claim. An employer cannot retain a nonperforming, policy-violating employee on pain of retaliation liability whenever the employee once complained. Johnson, 646 F.2d at 1257.

Because Feltmann’s evidence was insufficient to support either a discrimination or retaliation verdict, the district court erred in denying Sieben’s motion for judgment as a matter of law. Sieben respectfully requests that the judgment be reversed and judgment be entered in its favor, or, alternatively, that a new trial be granted.
