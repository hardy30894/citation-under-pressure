**ARGUMENT**

**The Bureau of Prisons’ Classification of Appellant’s Offense as a “Crime of Violence” Contradicts the Plain Language of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) and Exceeds Its Statutory Authority.**

The sole question presented is whether a drug conspiracy sentence enhanced for possession of a firearm may be deemed a “crime of violence” under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), thereby disqualifying an inmate from early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). It cannot.

**I. The Sentencing Enhancement Does Not Alter the Character of the Underlying Offense.**

The offense of conviction is conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846. That offense does not have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force. *See United States v. Nardello*, 393 U.S. 286, 296 (1969) (holding that the definition of an offense turns on its statutory elements, not the conduct of a particular defendant). The sentencing enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 for possession of a firearm is a discretionary adjustment, not an element of the crime. *Cf. United States v. Hester*, 325 F.2d 654, 656 (9th Cir. 1963) (holding that a sentencing factor does not change the nature of the underlying conviction). A conviction remains what the statute defines; no judicial finding at sentencing can retroactively convert a nonviolent conspiracy into a violent one. *See Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1873) (holding that courts cannot punish beyond the statutory definition of the offense).

**II. The BOP’s Program Statement Impermissibly Expands the Definition of “Crime of Violence.”**

Congress limited the reduction to prisoners convicted of a “nonviolent offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The BOP regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, defines a “crime of violence” by reference to 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). That statute requires either (A) an element of force or (B) an offense that “by its nature” involves a substantial risk of force. Congress’s use of “nature” refers to the inherent characteristics of the offense, not the accidental presence of a weapon during its commission. *See United States v. Evans*, 333 U.S. 483, 486 (1948) (holding that statutory words must be given their ordinary meaning). A drug conspiracy does not inherently involve force. *See United States v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 563, 565 (1968) (holding that the risk of force must be inherent in the crime’s commission, not merely conjectural).

The BOP’s Program Statement erroneously equates a sentencing enhancement with the nature of the offense. This contravenes the principle that an agency’s interpretation cannot override the plain language of a statute. *See United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260, 265 (1954) (holding that agency discretion must be exercised consistent with congressional intent). The enhancement for possessing a weapon does not transform a conspiracy into a crime that “by its nature” risks force. *See United States v. Grady*, 225 F.2d 410, 414 (3d Cir. 1955) (holding that a conspiracy’s nature is determined by its object, not collateral conduct).

**III. The BOP’s Interpretation Deprives Appellant of a Congressionally Intended Benefit.**

Congress enacted § 3621(e)(2)(B) to encourage rehabilitation and reduce recidivism. *See United States v. R.L.C.*, 373 U.S. 21, 28 (1963) (holding that remedial statutes should be liberally construed). Appellant fully satisfied the program’s requirements. To deny him the benefit based on a sentencing adjustment unrelated to his conviction undermines Congress’s purpose. *See United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 22 (1948) (holding that lenity requires resolving statutory ambiguity in favor of the accused).

Moreover, the BOP’s rule violates the principle that an agency may not impose a punishment greater than that authorized by statute. *See Ex parte Reed*, 100 U.S. 13, 18 (1879). The enhancement was a factor for the sentencing court alone; the BOP cannot use it to impose a de facto second disqualification.

Accordingly, this Court should hold that a drug conspiracy conviction, even with a weapon enhancement, is not a “crime of violence” under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), and direct the BOP to grant Appellant the statutory early release.
