# ARGUMENT

## I. The Reinstatement Statute Should Not Be Construed to Reach Reentries That Predate Its Enactment

When a statute is silent as to its temporal reach and its application to past conduct would attach new burdens to actions already taken, this Court has long instructed that the statute is presumed prospective. As the Court explained in *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190 (1913), "the first rule of construction is that legislation must be considered as addressed to the future, not to the past," and a retrospective operation will not be given to a statute affecting antecedent conduct "unless such be the unequivocal and inflexible import of the terms, and the manifest intention of the legislature." *Id.* at 199. Section 241(a)(5) contains no such unequivocal command reaching reentries occurring before its 1997 effective date. Congress's silence should not be read to override the settled presumption against retroactivity.

That presumption carries special force in the immigration context, where this Court has repeatedly resolved statutory ambiguity in the alien's favor. In *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), the Court refused to read a deportation statute expansively, explaining that "deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile," and that courts should not assume Congress intended the harshest of several possible readings. *Id.* at 10. The Court applied the identical canon in *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), construing an immigration exclusion statute to avoid disrupting rights an alien had already acquired before the statute's passage, and again in *Costello v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 376 U.S. 120 (1964), where doubts about the reach of a deportation ground were resolved against the government. These cases establish a consistent interpretive rule: where, as here, a reinstatement provision would strip an alien of the opportunity to seek adjustment of status based on a marriage to a citizen entered into years after his reentry, the statute should not be read to reach back and extinguish that opportunity absent explicit congressional direction. No such direction appears in Section 241(a)(5), and the statute should therefore be construed to apply only to reentries occurring after its enactment.

## II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute to Fernandez-Vargas's 1982 Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute's text could be stretched to cover pre-enactment reentries, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas violates the settled principle that a law may not attach new and disfavored legal consequences to completed private conduct without offending due process. This Court has invalidated retroactive statutes precisely on that ground. In *Untermyer v. Anderson*, 276 U.S. 440 (1928), the Court held that a statute could not retroactively impose a tax burden on a transaction already completed under the prior legal regime, because doing so disturbed reasonable expectations formed in reliance on existing law. The same logic controls here: Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982, married a United States citizen in 2001, and sought adjustment of status under the law as it then existed—only to have Section 241(a)(5) retroactively strip him of any opportunity for relief based on conduct, his reentry, that occurred fifteen years before the statute existed.

The government cannot escape this conclusion by invoking Congress's plenary power over immigration. This Court has been careful to note that even where Congress may prospectively regulate the conditions of an alien's presence, aliens within the United States remain persons entitled to due process protection. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), the Court held that aliens "shall not be... deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law," *id.* at 238, and in *Yamataya v. Fisher*, 189 U.S. 86 (1903), the Court confirmed that administrative officers executing immigration statutes may not disregard the "fundamental principles that inhere in 'due process of law.'" *Id.* at 100–101. Those principles are violated when a statute, applied retroactively, forecloses relief for conduct that was lawful, or at least not subject to this categorical bar, when it occurred.

The government's reliance on *Galvan v. Press*, 347 U.S. 522 (1954), and *Harisiades v. Shaughnessy*, 342 U.S. 580 (1952), is misplaced. Those cases upheld retroactive application of deportation grounds tied to an alien's own culpable conduct—membership in the Communist Party—conduct Congress had determined warranted removal regardless of when it occurred. Neither case addressed a provision that retroactively extinguishes an alien's ability to seek adjustment of status based on a subsequent marriage to a citizen, a status entirely independent of the reentry itself. Fernandez-Vargas's expectation of relief arose not from his 1982 reentry but from his 2001 marriage; applying Section 241(a)(5) to bar that relief based solely on the date of an already-completed reentry imposes a new disability on a settled fact pattern in a manner these earlier decisions never sanctioned.

For these reasons, Section 241(a)(5) should not be applied to Fernandez-Vargas's pre-1997 reentry, and if it is, that application is impermissibly retroactive.
