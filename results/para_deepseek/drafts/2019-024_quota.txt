**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a plaintiff know the challenged investment was “imprudent” contradicts the statutory text and this Court’s precedents.**

Section 1113(2) bars suit filed more than three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The “breach or violation” under § 1104 is the fiduciary’s act—here, the decision to invest in alternative assets—not the legal evaluation of whether that decision satisfied the prudent investor standard. Congress drew a line between actual knowledge and constructive knowledge by repealing the provision that would have started the clock upon filing of a report from which a plaintiff “could reasonably be expected” to have learned of the breach. That repeal did not import a requirement that the plaintiff know the legal significance of the facts. Rather, as the Ninth Circuit itself long held, “the statute of limitations is triggered by a claimant’s knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by his knowledge of the law.” *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (quoting *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985)). In *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), actual knowledge existed when plan administrators knew the insurer would retain a substantial portion of funds, not when they realized that retention was legally prohibited. And *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991), measured the period from “the earliest date on which plaintiff knew of the breach,” not from the date plaintiff understood the breach was actionable.

This Court has consistently rejected the notion that a limitations clock awaits legal enlightenment. In *United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 122 (1979), the Court held that a claim accrues when the plaintiff knows “the critical facts” of his injury and its cause, even if he does not know those facts constitute a legal wrong. Likewise, in *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633, 653 (2010), the Court stated that a plaintiff need only possess “facts that would lead a reasonable person to investigate,” not knowledge of a legal theory. By requiring Sulyma to know both that the investments occurred and that they were “imprudent,” the Ninth Circuit demanded knowledge of a legal conclusion and effectively resurrected the constructive-knowledge rule Congress repealed in 1987.

**II. A plaintiff cannot defeat the statute of limitations by failing to read or recall disclosures he actually accessed.**

The Ninth Circuit compounded its error by treating Sulyma’s testimony—that he did not recall reading the documents—as a genuine dispute of material fact. Actual knowledge is not confined to direct admissions; it may be inferred from a plaintiff’s possession of and attention to relevant information. *Staehr v. Hartford Fin. Servs. Grp., Inc.*, 547 F.3d 406, 411 (2d Cir. 2008). Intel produced undisputed evidence that Sulyma accessed the websites containing Fund Fact Sheets, annual disclosures, and notices describing both the alternative investments and the strategy behind them. Those documents disclosed precisely the facts Sulyma now claims were hidden. A plaintiff cannot manufacture a triable issue by testifying that he “cannot recall” information he actually viewed. *See TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 28 (2001) (limitations provisions should not be read to extend indefinitely where Congress has provided a specific rule).

The Ninth Circuit’s two-part formulation is also internally incoherent. Sulyma admits he knew his retirement accounts were invested in the challenged funds. He knew the fees increased and performance lagged. The fiduciary “breach” he alleges is not the existence of alternative investments, but the failure prudently to manage them. *See Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523, 532 (2015) (fiduciaries must “conduct their own independent evaluation” of investments). Under the Ninth Circuit’s test, no § 1104 claim could ever be time-barred unless the plaintiff had obtained expert legal advice or a court filing identifying the imprudence. *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1341–42 (9th Cir. 1994), itself demonstrates that actual knowledge turns on knowledge of the underlying facts—not on a legal label. A rule that rewards deliberate ignorance and selective memory does not vindicate ERISA’s remedial purposes; it converts the three-year statute of limitations into a tolling provision at the plaintiff’s election.

Because Sulyma knew the facts of the plan’s investments and the disclosed strategy behind them, the three-year period began when Intel made those disclosures. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
