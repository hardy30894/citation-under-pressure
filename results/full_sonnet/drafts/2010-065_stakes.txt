**ARGUMENT**

**Petitioner Lacks Standing to Challenge Her Conviction on Tenth Amendment Grounds Because the Amendment Protects the Sovereignty of the States, Not the Personal Liberty Interests of Individual Defendants.**

**A. The Tenth Amendment Reserves Power to the States as Sovereigns, and This Court Has Already Held That Private Parties Have No Standing to Invoke It.**

This Court confronted the precise question presented here more than seventy years ago and answered it. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that a federal agency's sale of electricity invaded matters reserved to the States under the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected the challenge because the private litigants had no stake in the States' reserved powers: "there is no objection to the Authority's operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment." *Id.* at 144. That holding rests on a simple structural premise—the Tenth Amendment "states but a truism" about the relationship between two sovereigns, and its protections belong to the States themselves. A private party's assertion of that interest is, at best, borrowed and, at worst, illusory.

Petitioner's conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 does not transform her into a surrogate for the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. She was prosecuted by the United States for conduct that also violated state law; the Commonwealth has taken no position that the federal statute intrudes on its sovereignty, much less sought to vindicate any such interest here. Under *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, that absence is dispositive.

**B. The Weight of Authority in the Courts of Appeals Confirms That Individual Criminal Defendants May Not Raise Tenth Amendment Claims on the States' Behalf.**

Consistent with *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, a substantial majority of the circuits to consider the question have held that private parties—including criminal defendants—lack standing to challenge federal statutes as invasions of state sovereignty. *See United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004). The First Circuit's explanation in *Medeiros* is instructive: the Tenth Amendment's structural protections are enforced through the States' own political and legal processes, and "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general," such that if a State "refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." 431 F.3d at 35. Petitioner's remedy, if any, lies in persuading the Commonwealth to object—not in personally asserting a sovereign interest that belongs to the State alone.

**C. Permitting Petitioner to Raise This Claim Would Convert a Structural Federalism Limit into a Generalized Grievance Unmoored from Article III.**

This Court's standing jurisprudence requires that a litigant assert a judicially cognizable, particularized interest of her own, not a generalized grievance about the allocation of governmental power. *See Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923) (a party's interest in the manner in which government power is exercised "is comparable to that of any other citizen" and insufficient to found a case or controversy). Because "the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States," *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945), a defendant's complaint that Congress exceeded its delegated powers is, at bottom, a complaint about the distribution of authority between two governments to which she is not a party. That grievance is the State's to raise, not hers.

Moreover, this Court's jurisdictional inquiries must precede the merits. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Allowing Petitioner to litigate the constitutionality of § 229 under the Tenth Amendment would require this Court to reach a merits question—whether the Necessary and Proper Clause and the Treaty Power, as construed in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), permit Congress to enact the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act without a jurisdictional element—before ever determining that Petitioner has a cognizable stake in that question. Article III does not permit that sequence.

**D. The Political Safeguards of Federalism, Not Individual Criminal Defendants, Protect the States' Reserved Powers.**

Finally, the structural check on federal overreach into matters of local concern operates chiefly through the States' representation in the national political process, not through defensive litigation by individuals convicted of federal crimes. Recognizing standing here would allow any defendant prosecuted under any federal criminal statute to relitigate, as a matter of course, the outer boundaries of Congress's Article I powers—an outcome inconsistent with the limited, sovereign-to-sovereign nature of the Tenth Amendment's guarantee as this Court described it in *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, 306 U.S. at 144.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
