**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That Petitioners’ Claims for Nominal Damages Cannot Save Their Otherwise Moot Claims.**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), controls this case. There, the court held that a request for nominal damages cannot preserve a live controversy when the underlying constitutional challenge to a policy has been rendered moot by repeal or revision. *Id.* at 1255–56. The court reasoned that nominal damages—“awarded when a legal right is technically violated but no actual harm is shown”—do not redress a cognizable injury sufficient to maintain Article III standing. *Id.* at 1255 (quoting *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978)). Because Petitioners’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were mooted by Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) revision of its policies, their request for nominal damages cannot revive those claims.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165 (2013), is misplaced. In *Chafin*, the Supreme Court held that a case was not moot despite the father’s return to Scotland because the dispute over child custody remained live. *Id.* at 172. That case involved a continuing controversy over a legal right, not a request for nominal damages to preserve a moot constitutional challenge. Unlike *Chafin*, Petitioners here seek nominal damages for a past violation of their First Amendment rights—rights that are no longer at issue because GGC’s revised policies permit the very speech they sought to challenge. *See id.* at 172–73 (distinguishing cases where the controversy is no longer live).

Petitioners’ attempt to distinguish *Flanigan’s* on the ground that their challenge was “as-applied” rather than “facial” is unavailing. The Eleventh Circuit has made clear that *Flanigan’s* rule applies equally to both types of challenges. *See Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (rejecting the argument that an as-applied challenge could be saved by nominal damages). The court explained that “[t]he mere fact that a plaintiff challenges a policy as applied to him does not transform nominal damages into compensatory damages.” *Id.* at 1256. Petitioners’ as-applied challenge to GGC’s prior policies is no different: once those policies were repealed, their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief became moot, and nominal damages cannot revive them.

**II. Petitioners’ Claims Are Not Saved by Their Alleged Request for Compensatory Damages.**

Petitioners argue that their complaint sufficiently pleaded a request for compensatory damages, thereby preserving a live controversy. The district court, however, correctly construed the complaint as seeking only nominal damages. *See Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (holding that compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on “actual injury” such as monetary loss or emotional distress). The complaint’s vague references to “monetary damages” and “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence” were insufficient to plead compensatory damages, particularly where Petitioners failed to allege any concrete injury. *See id.* at 1230–31.

The Eleventh Circuit has repeatedly held that a plaintiff cannot bootstrap a moot claim by tacking on a request for nominal damages after the fact. *See Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255–56; *see also Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018) (requiring specificity in proposed amendments). Petitioners’ belated attempt to recharacterize their damages claim as compensatory is no exception. The district court did not err in construing the complaint against them, as it was required to do at the motion-to-dismiss stage. *Chaparro v. Carnival Corp.*, 693 F.3d 1333, 1335 (11th Cir. 2012).

**III. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend.**

Petitioners argue that the district court abused its discretion by denying their request to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages. However, the court correctly noted that Petitioners’ attempt to amend was procedurally improper. *See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (holding that a request for leave to amend must be made in a proper motion, not embedded in a response to a motion to dismiss). Moreover, Petitioners failed to specify the substance of their proposed amendment, as required by Rule 15. *See id.* at 1157.

Even if the court had erred, Petitioners could have sought leave to amend after judgment under Rule 59(e) or Rule 60(b). *See Czeremcha v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers*, 724 F.2d 1552, 1556 (11th Cir. 1984). They did not do so. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying their untimely and procedurally defective request.

**IV. The Court of Appeals’ Decision Is Consistent with Supreme Court Precedent.**

Petitioners’ reliance on *Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth*, 529 U.S. 217 (2000), is misplaced. In *Southworth*, the Supreme Court addressed whether a university’s mandatory student activity fee could be used to fund political and ideological speech without violating the First Amendment. *Id.* at 224. The Court held that so long as the fee system was viewpoint-neutral, it did not violate the First Amendment. *Id.* at 233. That case did not address mootness or the effect of nominal damages on a moot controversy.

Similarly, *Healy v. James*, 408 U.S. 169 (1972), and *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), involved challenges to university policies that were still in effect at the time of adjudication. *Healy*, 408 U.S. at 171; *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 265. They do not support Petitioners’ argument that nominal damages can preserve a moot claim.

**Conclusion**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Petitioners’ claims for nominal damages cannot save their otherwise moot constitutional challenge. The district court did not err in dismissing the case as moot, and its denial of leave to amend was not an abuse of discretion. The judgment below should be affirmed.
