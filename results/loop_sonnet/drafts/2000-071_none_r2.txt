# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Policy Reasonably Confines Its Limited Forum to Secular Community Uses; Excluding Religious Worship Defines the Forum's Boundaries and Does Not Discriminate Against a Viewpoint.

A school building is not a street or park held "immemorially . . . for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is dedicated during the day to instruction and, after hours, to a discrete set of "social, civic and recreational" community uses. Because the building is not a traditional public forum, the school retains substantial authority to define what falls within the limited access it has chosen to grant. This Court has recognized that "the State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). Opening property for some uses does not obligate the government to accommodate every use a citizen might wish to make of it. *Id.* The principle is longstanding: a legislature's decision to forbid a particular use of public property is "no more an infringement of the rights of a member of the public than for the owner of a private house to forbid" that same use of his own premises. *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897). Regulation of access to public property is likewise constitutionally sound where it is administered evenhandedly, without discrimination among speakers, to serve legitimate ends in maintaining order and the purpose for which the property is held. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941).

Milford's Policy operates on precisely this permissible logic. It excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—irrespective of which faith or sect might seek to conduct it, while leaving open a wide range of civic and recreational activities. That is a reasonable definition of subject matter, not viewpoint suppression. The record leaves no doubt which category the Club's meetings occupy: they open and close in prayer, teach memorized scripture, and culminate in a "challenge and invitation" through which children are asked to accept Christ as personal savior and to recite a prayer of conversion. This is not the teaching of morals and character informed by religious perspective; it is religious instruction and worship of exactly the kind the Policy reserves against, regardless of the sect involved. Confining a limited civic forum to exclude that category is a permissible boundary on the forum's scope, fully consistent with the school's authority to preserve its property for its intended use. *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Directed at School-Age Children, Immediately After the Close of the School Day.

Even if the Policy's application to the Club implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that hosting the Club's activities risked an Establishment Clause violation was reasonable and independently sufficient to justify exclusion. This Court has consistently treated the public schoolhouse as uniquely sensitive Establishment Clause terrain because of the presence of young, impressionable children. "[I]t is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). At the same time, the State may not pursue programs that establish "a 'religion of secularism'"; neutrality between religion and irreligion is the touchstone within the schoolhouse. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963).

That concern is heightened, not diminished, when religious instruction occurs inside the school building itself, immediately following the close of the school day, in the very classrooms the children occupy each morning. This Court has held that such use of "the tax-established and tax-supported public school system to aid religious groups to spread their faith . . . falls squarely under the ban" of the First Amendment. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948). The Establishment Clause demands neutrality in both directions: the State need not be religion's adversary, but "state power is no more to be used so as to handicap religions than it is to favor them." *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947). And while this Court has recognized that the Religion Clauses do not compel hostility to religion, and that "[t]he First Amendment . . . does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State," *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952), *Zorach* upheld released-time religious instruction only because it occurred *off* school property, avoiding any appearance that the school itself sponsored the religious exercise. *Id.* at 308–09. Milford's building, by contrast, is the very site of instruction, and "the First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968).

Given the Club's own description of its activities—prayer, scripture memorization, and an invitation to conversion directed at grade-school children—conducted in Milford's classrooms directly after school, the school reasonably concluded that permitting the Club to meet on its premises would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that *Engel*, *Schempp*, *McCollum*, and *Everson* forbid. That independent justification supports the judgment below.
