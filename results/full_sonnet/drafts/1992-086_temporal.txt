# ARGUMENT

## I. The District's Exclusion of Lamb's Chapel Solely Because of the Religious Viewpoint of Its Speech Violates the Free Speech Clause.

The First Amendment forbids government from opening a forum for the discussion of a subject and then excluding a particular speaker because government officials disapprove of the viewpoint that speaker would express. This Court settled that principle decades ago and has never wavered from it. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court reversed convictions arising from a city's denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses, holding that "[t]he right to equal protection of the laws, in the exercise of those freedoms of speech and religion protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments, is a right belonging to petitioners as individuals" and that a permit scheme administered so as to discriminate against unpopular religious speech cannot stand. *Id.* at 272. Likewise in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), the Court struck down the revocation of a preacher's permit to speak on public streets, explaining that "the danger of unfettered use of ordinances such as this one is that they may be found to be readily susceptible to improper application to speech." *Id.* at 294. And in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), the Court held that a city could not selectively enforce a park ordinance against a Jehovah's Witness meeting while permitting other assemblies, for "[t]here is no showing that the [meeting] was different from other religious assemblages." *Id.* at 69.

These cases establish a controlling rule: once government opens property to speech on a subject, it may not slam the door on a speaker simply because that speaker's message is religious. That is precisely what the Center Moriches Union Free School District did here. The District permitted use of its facilities by outside organizations to address matters of family life, child rearing, and community values — the very subject of Lamb's Chapel's proposed film series. It excluded Lamb's Chapel only because the Dobson film series approached that subject "from a Christian perspective." This is viewpoint discrimination in its purest form, condemned by *Niemotko*, *Kunz*, and *Fowler* alike.

The school setting does not diminish this protection. In *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), this Court held that expressive activity on school premises may not be suppressed merely because officials disagree with or are uncomfortable with its viewpoint, stating that "state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism" and that school officials do not possess "absolute authority over their students." *Id.* at 511. If students themselves retain First Amendment rights within the schoolhouse, a fortiori the District may not, by blanket rule, exclude community speakers from an after-hours forum solely because their message is religious in character.

Film is fully protected expression, entitled to the same First Amendment safeguards as the spoken or printed word. In *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495 (1952), this Court held unconstitutional a state's denial of a license to exhibit a film on the ground that it was "sacrilegious," explaining that "[i]t is not the business of government in our nation to suppress real or imagined attacks upon a particular religious doctrine." *Id.* at 505. The District's rejection of the Dobson film series — precisely because it examined family life "from a Christian perspective" — is the mirror image of the censorship this Court condemned in *Burstyn*. The government may no more punish a film for being religious than for being "sacrilegious"; both restrictions single out speech for disfavor based on its religious content, in violation of the Free Speech Clause.

## II. The Establishment Clause Does Not Require, and in Fact Forbids, This Kind of Discrimination Against Religious Speech.

The District has suggested that the Establishment Clause compelled its exclusion of Lamb's Chapel. That premise is mistaken. This Court has long recognized that the Establishment Clause does not require government to be hostile to religion or to treat religious speech as a second-class category unworthy of benefits extended to secular speech. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Court upheld the extension of a generally available public benefit — bus transportation reimbursement — to parents of parochial school children, explaining that the Establishment Clause "requires the state to be a neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers," neither favoring nor disfavoring religion. *Id.* at 18. Similarly, in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), the Court sustained a released-time program for religious instruction, observing that "[w]e are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being" and that the Constitution "does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Id.* at 312-13.

Neutrality, not discrimination, is the touchstone. Allowing Lamb's Chapel the same access to school facilities afforded to other community groups discussing family and social issues would not establish religion — it would simply treat religious speech even-handedly, exactly as *Everson* and *Zorach* require. The District's rule, by contrast, does not maintain neutrality; it enacts hostility toward religion, penalizing Lamb's Chapel's message solely because it is religious. That is the very evil the Free Speech Clause — and the neutrality principle of the Establishment Clause — forbids.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
