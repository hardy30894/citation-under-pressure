# ARGUMENT

## I. THE BOARD'S "GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT" STANDARD FOR POLLING IS ARBITRARY BECAUSE IT RENDERS THE POLLING DEVICE SUPERFLUOUS

An agency's interpretation of its governing statute must be reasonable to merit deference. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 844 (1984). Where an agency's rule is internally incoherent, it fails even the deferential arbitrary-and-capricious standard of judicial review. Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (agency action is arbitrary if the agency "entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem" or offered an explanation that "runs counter to the evidence").

The Board's polling standard suffers from precisely this defect. The Board requires an employer to possess the same quantum of evidence—"objective considerations" sufficient to raise a "reasonable doubt" about the union's majority status—before it may conduct a poll as it requires before the employer may simply withdraw recognition outright. See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). Three courts of appeals recognized the irrationality of this equivalence and rejected it, holding that an employer may poll on a lesser showing of "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support." Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); accord Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981).

The logic compelling this result is straightforward. If an employer already possesses evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition unilaterally, it has no need to conduct a poll at all—it may simply act on that evidence. The poll becomes a "useful and legitimate tool," Mingtree, 736 F.2d at 1299, only when the employer's evidence, while substantial, does not rise to the level required for outright withdrawal. As the Sixth Circuit explained, an employer facing this evidentiary gap confronts a genuine dilemma: it risks an unfair-labor-practice charge for bargaining with a minority union if it continues recognition, or a separate unfair-labor-practice charge for withdrawing recognition without sufficient proof if it does not. Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 867. Polling resolves this dilemma by allowing the employer to test its doubt through a Struksnes-compliant secret ballot. See Struksnes Construction Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967).

The Board's own precedent concedes the problem. In Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), the Board acknowledged that it "favored elections over polling," describing RM elections as the preferred mechanism for testing union support because of their procedural safeguards. Yet if RM elections are preferred precisely because they offer more protection than unsupervised polling, logical consistency demands that the evidentiary threshold for the less-protected procedure—polling—be no higher, and arguably lower, than the threshold for the more-protected procedure. The Board's insistence on an identical standard for both inverts this relationship and cannot be reconciled with its own stated rationale.

This Court has instructed that Board rules must rest on reasoned decisionmaking that reflects the practical realities of labor relations. See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987) (deferring to Board standards only where they represent a reasonable accommodation of competing interests). The Board's polling standard, by collapsing the distinction between polling and withdrawal, offers employers illusory protection while doing nothing to serve the Act's goal of industrial stability. An employer with doubt insufficient to withdraw recognition, but substantial enough to warrant testing, is left with no lawful mechanism to resolve that doubt short of guessing—precisely the arbitrary result the Act's stability principles were designed to avoid. Cf. NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 596–600 (1969) (recognizing employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment through means other than unfair-labor-practice litigation).

## II. THE BOARD'S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

Even under the Board's own standard, its factual finding cannot stand. Judicial review of Board factual findings asks whether they are supported by "substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole." Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial evidence "must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." Id. at 488.

The Board disregarded uncontroverted testimony from multiple employees—including union steward Ron Mohr, who stated that he did not believe employees wanted the union—by labeling such statements "unverified" or "off-the-cuff" without record support for that characterization. An agency may not simply discount evidence that undermines its conclusion without explanation grounded in the record. State Farm, 463 U.S. at 43. Where, as here, the bargaining unit itself was substantially reconstituted—drawing only 32 of 45 predecessor employees, with all new hires among successor employees—the presumption of continuing majority support rests on a materially weaker factual foundation than in the ordinary successor case addressed in Fall River, 482 U.S. at 41 n.8. The Board's mechanical application of its reasonable-doubt standard, without accounting for this diminished evidentiary base, fails the substantial-evidence test this Court articulated in Universal Camera.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
