**ARGUMENT**

Bond lacks standing to mount her Tenth Amendment attack on 18 U.S.C. § 229. The Supreme Court has held that a private party, “absent the states or their officers,” has “no standing” to raise a Tenth Amendment objection to federal action. *Tennessee Elec. Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Auth.*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). That principle controls here. Bond is a criminal defendant seeking to invalidate her conviction under a federal statute; she does not assert, and the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania has not asserted, any sovereign injury. Under *Tennessee Electric*, Bond may not convert a state-federal structural objection into a personal defense to a federal prosecution.

This Court and others have treated *Tennessee Electric* as binding. The Supreme Court has emphasized that courts must resolve jurisdictional questions before reaching the merits. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95, 101-02 (1998). And the First Circuit correctly recognized that *Tennessee Electric*’s statement on Tenth Amendment standing was not dicta, but an alternative holding. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33-35 (1st Cir. 2005), cert. denied, 548 U.S. 904 (2006). Bond’s attempt to recast the issue as a merits defense cannot avoid the threshold jurisdictional bar. See *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 101-02.

Bond’s contrary authorities do not justify departure from the Supreme Court’s rule. The Seventh Circuit in *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703-04 (7th Cir. 1999), cert. denied, 528 U.S. 1116 (2000), allowed private standing, but it did so by reading structural federalism principles more broadly than *Tennessee Electric* permits. Likewise, the Eleventh Circuit’s older decision in *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), predated and did not grapple with the Supreme Court’s explicit standing holding. More recent decisions have recognized the force of *Tennessee Electric* and denied private-party standing. See *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006), cert. denied, 549 U.S. 1171 (2007); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33-36; *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004), cert. denied, 543 U.S. 874 (2004).

Bond’s contention that *Tennessee Electric* is limited to civil suits is incorrect. The standing principle is not confined to one procedural posture. The Court’s statement was grounded in the Constitution’s allocation of power between the States and the Federal Government, not in the identity of the litigant. See *Tennessee Elec. Power*, 306 U.S. at 143-44. The same logic applies with even greater force in a criminal case, where Congress has enacted a generally applicable statute and the defendant seeks to invoke the Tenth Amendment on the State’s behalf. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that constitutional structure does not confer roving litigant status on private parties. *Raines v. Byrd*, 521 U.S. 811, 819-20 (1997); *Allen v. Wright*, 468 U.S. 737, 751 (1984).

Nor does *Bond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 844 (2014), alter the standing analysis here. The Court there construed the statute narrowly to avoid the constitutional question, but it did not reject the antecedent principle that standing and justiciability must be established before reaching merits issues. See *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 94-95. When a case can be resolved on nonconstitutional grounds, courts should do so. *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Auth.*, 297 U.S. 288, 341-48 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring). Here, however, Bond seeks the opposite: she asks the Court to disregard the jurisdictional limits recognized in *Tennessee Electric* and decide a structural constitutional claim she is not entitled to press.

Bond also cannot rely on the notion that her criminal conviction itself supplies standing. Injury in fact is not enough where the asserted right belongs to the States as sovereigns. The Tenth Amendment “is but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). And federal criminal defendants routinely may challenge statutes only insofar as they infringe their own constitutional rights. See *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987); *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 607-18 (2000). Bond has no individualized federalism right to insist that Pennsylvania, rather than the United States, prosecute her conduct.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed on standing grounds alone. *Tennessee Electric* remains controlling, and Bond, a private defendant with no state authorization, may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to defeat her valid federal conviction.
