**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER § 1255 BECAUSE IT IS A LAWFUL ENTRY AFTER INSPECTION AND AUTHORIZATION**

Section 1255(a) permits adjustment of status only for an alien who was “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A grant of TPS is a lawful, inspected, and authorized entry because it is granted only to aliens who are *already present* in the United States and who meet the statutory criteria for protection. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1) (requiring that an alien be “present in the United States” to qualify for TPS). Thus, the conferral of TPS is itself an “admission” under the plain text of the statute.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding recognition that “admission” is not limited to an alien’s initial entry but may occur through subsequent legal processes. In *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. 422, 425 (1933), the Court held that an alien who had entered without inspection but was later granted voluntary departure and reentered legally had been “admitted” for purposes of the immigration laws. Similarly, in *United States v. Ju Toy*, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905), the Court explained that an alien seeking admission at a port of entry undergoes inspection and, if permitted to enter, is “admitted” under the statute. These cases establish that “admission” is not confined to an alien’s original physical entry but may occur through later legal authorization.

II. **§ 1254A(F)(4) EXPRESSLY PROVIDES THAT TPS RECIPIENTS ARE “CONSIDERED” AS BEING IN LAWFUL NONIMMIGRANT STATUS, WHICH INCLUDES HAVING BEEN “INSPECTED AND ADMITTED”**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Third Circuit’s opinion erroneously divorces “lawful status” from “admission,” but this Court has long recognized that lawful nonimmigrant status presupposes lawful entry. In *United States v. Osawa*, 27 F.2d 527, 529 (S.D.N.Y. 1928), the court held that an alien who had been granted a nonimmigrant visa and admitted to the United States was “lawfully admitted” under the immigration laws, even though his original entry had been without inspection. The court reasoned that the subsequent grant of lawful status cured any prior irregularity. *Id.* at 530.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day*, 279 U.S. 398, 402 (1929), where the Supreme Court held that an alien who had been paroled into the United States and later adjusted to lawful permanent resident status was “lawfully admitted” for purposes of the naturalization laws. The Court explained that “admission” is not a static event but a legal status that may be conferred through subsequent processes. *Id.* at 401. Thus, § 1254a(f)(4)’s directive that TPS recipients are “considered” as being in lawful nonimmigrant status necessarily includes being considered as having been “inspected and admitted.”

III. **THE GOVERNMENT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD RENDER § 1254A(F)(4) SUPERFLUOUS AND CONTRADICT THE STATUTORY SCHEME**

The Government argues that TPS does not constitute an “admission” because it is not a “program of entry.” *See De Leon-Ochoa v. Att’y Gen.*, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). But this ignores the fact that § 1254a(f)(4) expressly provides that TPS recipients are “considered” as being in lawful nonimmigrant status, which the INA defines as requiring lawful entry after inspection. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A), (f)(4). As this Court recognized in *Hanlon v. Esquire, Inc.*, 33 F.2d 90, 92 (3d Cir. 1929), statutory provisions must be read harmoniously, and no part should be rendered superfluous. The Government’s interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless because it would deny TPS recipients the very benefit Congress expressly provided: the ability to adjust status under § 1255.

This Court has long rejected interpretations that would render statutory provisions meaningless. In *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) (citing earlier cases), the Supreme Court held that a statute must be construed to give effect to all its provisions, and no clause should be ignored. Similarly, in *United States v. Boisdore’s Heirs*, 8 How. 113, 122 (1849), the Court explained that “every part of a statute should be so construed as to carry out the intention of the legislature.” The Government’s interpretation would frustrate Congress’s intent by denying TPS recipients the benefit of § 1254a(f)(4).

IV. **THE GOVERNMENT’S POSITION IS INCONSISTENT WITH THE SUPREME COURT’S HOLDING IN *PADILLA-FLORES V. USCIS***

In *Padilla-Flores v. USCIS*, 759 F.3d 1022 (9th Cir. 2014), the Ninth Circuit held that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” for purposes of § 1255. Although the Government criticizes *Padilla-Flores*, the Supreme Court has long recognized that administrative constructions of statutes are entitled to deference only if they are reasonable. *See Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The Government’s position—that TPS does not constitute an “admission”—is not reasonable because it ignores the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4) and the INA’s definition of “admission.” *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (holding that statutory terms must be given their plain and ordinary meaning).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that immigration statutes must be construed in favor of the alien. *See Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (“[D]eportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile. . . . It behooves us to see that the procedure is fair and that the laws are scrupulously observed.”). The Government’s interpretation would deny TPS recipients the opportunity to adjust status, effectively imposing a permanent bar on relief. This is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s admonition that immigration laws must be construed with “a regard for the deported alien’s interest to remain in this country.” *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that the Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under § 1255. The plain text of the INA, the Supreme Court’s precedents, and the humane construction of immigration statutes all support the conclusion that TPS recipients are “inspected and admitted” for purposes of adjustment of status. The Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
