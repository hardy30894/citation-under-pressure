**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal appellate court reviewing for plain error may consider matters outside the trial record to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The answer is yes. The plain-error inquiry is not confined to evidence presented to the jury; the court may examine the entire record, including prior convictions and other reliable materials, to assess whether the error actually prejudiced the defendant.

**I. The Plain-Error Standard Permits Consideration of the Full Record, Including Matters Outside the Trial Evidence**

Plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) requires the defendant to show that the error affected his substantial rights—i.e., that it resulted in an actual miscarriage of justice. This Court has long recognized that appellate courts may look beyond the trial record to determine whether a defendant was prejudiced by an error. In *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court considered whether a sentencing court’s misunderstanding of a mandatory-minimum statute violated due process. The Court examined the state-court record, including the sentencing judge’s statements, to determine whether the defendant was actually prejudiced. *Id.* at 731–33. The inquiry was not limited to the evidence at trial; the Court considered the entire proceeding. Similarly, in *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), the Court reviewed the admission of a prior conviction into evidence, looking at the record of that conviction—not just the trial evidence—to determine whether the prior conviction was constitutionally valid. *Id.* at 114–15. The Court noted that the “record of the prior conviction” was properly before the appellate court for review. *Id.* at 115. These cases confirm that appellate courts may consider the full record, including evidence of prior convictions that was never presented to the jury, when assessing prejudice.

**II. The Court of Appeals Properly Considered Greer’s Prior Convictions to Determine Whether the *Rehaif* Error Affected His Substantial Rights**

In *Rehaif v. United States*, the Court held that the government must prove that the defendant knew he was a felon at the time of possession. The Eleventh Circuit found that the district court plainly erred by failing to instruct the jury on that knowledge element. But the court then examined Greer’s prior convictions—matters outside the trial record—to determine whether Greer could show that the error affected his substantial rights. That approach is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court reviewed the voluntariness of a guilty plea based on the record of the plea colloquy, not just the trial evidence. *Id.* at 242–44. The Court considered the “record” of the plea proceeding, which included matters outside the trial itself. *Id.* at 244. Likewise, in *Garland v. Washington*, 232 U.S. 642 (1914), the Court held that a formal defect in arraignment could be cured by later proceedings, and the appellate court could examine the entire record to determine whether the defendant was actually prejudiced. *Id.* at 645–46. The Court stated that “the objection was merely a formal one” and that the record showed the defendant was not harmed. *Id.* at 645. These cases demonstrate that the appellate court may look beyond the trial evidence to assess prejudice.

**III. The Use of Prior Convictions to Assess Prejudice Is Consistent with the Court’s Treatment of Habitual-Offender Statutes**

In cases involving recidivist enhancements, this Court has repeatedly approved the consideration of prior convictions to determine whether a defendant was properly sentenced. In *In re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95 (1895), the Court considered the admission of a prior conviction record to prove a second-offense larceny charge. *Id.* at 96–97. The Court held that the prior conviction was properly admissible as part of the government’s case-in-chief, even though it was not relevant to the underlying offense. *Id.* at 97. Under this logic, a prior conviction may be considered on appeal to determine whether the defendant knew of his felon status. Similarly, in *Murphy v. Massachusetts*, 177 U.S. 155 (1900), the Court upheld a resentencing that corrected an earlier error, noting that the appellate court could examine the “record of the case” to determine the proper sentence. *Id.* at 157–58. The Court did not restrict the record to trial evidence alone.

**IV. The Court’s Precedents Reject a Rigid Limitation to the Trial Record When Assessing Prejudice**

This Court has consistently held that the harmless-error and plain-error inquiries are not confined to the evidence presented at trial. In *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), the Court vacated a judgment and remanded for the state courts to consider whether an evidentiary error affected the jury’s verdict. *Id.* at 797–98. The Court contemplated that the state courts would examine the “record” in detail, including matters outside the trial evidence, to determine prejudice. *Id.* at 798. In *Eskridge v. Washington State Board of Prison Terms & Paroles*, 357 U.S. 214 (1958), the Court held that an indigent defendant was entitled to a free transcript to pursue an appeal. *Id.* at 216. The Court recognized that an appellate court needs the full record—including trial proceedings and any other relevant materials—to assess whether errors were prejudicial. *Id.* at 215–16. These cases establish that the appellate court’s review is not limited to the trial record.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit properly considered Greer’s prior convictions to determine that the *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights. The plain-error standard permits the court to examine the entire record, including matters outside the trial evidence, to assess whether the error resulted in a miscarriage of justice. The judgment should be affirmed.
