**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BASED ON A NARROW, FORMULAIC INTERPRETATION OF “SEXUALLY OFFENSIVE ACTS,” CONTRARY TO THIS CIRCUIT’S CLEAR PRECEDENT.

The district court erred in concluding that Moll’s hostile work environment claim failed because she did not allege any “sexually offensive acts” within the applicable statute of limitations. That conclusion rests on a fundamental misreading of *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365 (2d Cir. 2002), and ignores the well-established principle that a hostile work environment claim may be based on a pattern of conduct that, while facially sex-neutral, is motivated by sex and creates a work environment objectively hostile to women. See *id.* at 375 (“A hostile work environment claim may be based on conduct that is not overtly sexual but is nonetheless motivated by sex and creates a work environment that is hostile to women.”).

The district court applied a rigid, form-over-substance test, requiring that each incident be “sexually offensive” in nature to qualify as part of a hostile environment. This approach is contrary to *Alfano*, which explicitly rejected such a narrow interpretation. In *Alfano*, this Court held that “a hostile work environment may be established by conduct that is not overtly sexual but is nonetheless motivated by sex.” *Id.* at 375. The Court emphasized that “the focus is not on whether the conduct is sexually explicit, but whether it is sufficiently severe or pervasive to create an environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive, and whether the conduct is based on sex.” *Id.* at 374.

Here, Moll’s allegations, when viewed in their totality, establish a pattern of conduct that was both objectively hostile and subjectively offensive, and that was motivated by her sex. The district court improperly isolated individual incidents and failed to consider the cumulative effect of the conduct. Moll alleged that Daniel Irving, her supervisor, left her three inappropriate notes in 1998 and 1999, including one stating he thought about her while showering—conduct that, while not overtly sexual in language, was clearly sexually suggestive and targeted at her as a woman. *See id.* at 375 (holding that “a note stating ‘I think about you when I’m in the shower’” could support a hostile environment claim).

More significantly, Moll alleged a pattern of discriminatory treatment that was not overtly sexual but was nonetheless rooted in sex. She was denied the ability to work from home—despite her male colleagues being permitted to do so—after she requested time off to care for a sick child. She was denied vacation time on July 5, 2002, while male colleagues with less tenure were granted the same request. She was excluded from work-related social events, including professional hockey games, which were attended by her male coworkers. These incidents, though facially neutral, were part of a broader pattern of differential treatment that, when viewed in context, created a work environment that a reasonable woman would find hostile and abusive.

The district court’s error was compounded by its failure to consider the temporal continuity of the conduct. Moll’s allegations span from 1998 to 2003, with the most recent incidents occurring within the limitations period. *See 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-5(e)(1)* (statute of limitations for Title VII claims is 180 days after the last discriminatory act). The district court dismissed claims based on conduct occurring before the limitations period, but failed to recognize that a hostile work environment claim is not time-barred if the conduct is part of a continuing pattern. *See Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 787 (1998)* (“A hostile environment claim is not time-barred if the last act of harassment falls within the limitations period, even if earlier acts occurred outside the period.”).

Moreover, the district court erred in treating Moll’s allegations of disparate treatment—such as being denied promotions while male colleagues were promoted—as irrelevant to her hostile environment claim. This is a fundamental misunderstanding of the law. *See Alfano*, 294 F.3d at 375 (“Discriminatory treatment and hostile environment claims are not mutually exclusive; they may coexist and support one another.”). The denial of promotions, the refusal to allow remote work, and the exclusion from social events all contributed to a work environment that was hostile to women and created a perception of unequal treatment based on sex.

The district court’s narrow interpretation of “sexually offensive acts” effectively eviscerates the protections of Title VII by allowing employers to insulate themselves from liability by using facially neutral conduct to create a hostile environment. This is precisely the kind of result *Alfano* sought to prevent. The Second Circuit has made clear that “the inquiry is not whether the conduct is sexually explicit, but whether it is motivated by sex and creates a hostile environment.” *Id.* at 375.

Accordingly, the district court’s dismissal of Moll’s hostile work environment claim was error. The allegations, when viewed in their totality, are sufficient to survive summary judgment. The court must consider the cumulative impact of the conduct, not isolate individual incidents based on a rigid definition of “sexually offensive.” The judgment must be vacated and the case remanded for further proceedings.

**For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court is reversed insofar as it granted Verizon’s motion for summary judgment on Moll’s hostile work environment claim.**
