The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted expansion of the Equal Protection Clause.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954) (striking down segregation in public schools); McLaughlin v. Florida, 379 U.S. 184 (1964) (invalidating a statute prohibiting interracial cohabitation). The Clause is not a general prohibition on arbitrary or irrational government action.

In cases where a plaintiff alleges that they have been singled out for adverse treatment, the Supreme Court has generally required that the plaintiff demonstrate membership in a protected class or group. See, e.g., Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448 (1962) (requiring a showing of selective prosecution based on an unjustifiable standard such as race or religion). The Seventh Circuit's "class of one" theory dispenses with this requirement, allowing a plaintiff to state an equal protection claim based solely on the allegation that they were treated differently from others without a rational basis.

This Court has never recognized a "class of one" theory of equal protection liability. In fact, the Court has cautioned against expanding the Equal Protection Clause beyond its traditional boundaries. See, e.g., Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256 (1979) (rejecting an equal protection challenge to a state hiring preference for veterans, despite its disproportionate impact on women).

The Seventh Circuit's holding is also inconsistent with the Court's decisions in cases such as FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307 (1993), which emphasize the rational basis review applicable to equal protection claims. Under rational basis review, a government action is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. The Seventh Circuit's "class of one" theory, by contrast, appears to impose a more stringent standard, allowing liability based on a showing of irrational or malicious intent.

In this case, respondent Olech's claim that the Village of Willowbrook demanded an additional 18-foot easement from her, while requiring only a 15-foot easement from other property owners, does not state a cognizable equal protection claim. Olech does not allege membership in a protected class or group, and her claim is therefore not cognizable under the Equal Protection Clause.

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to dismiss Olech's equal protection claim for failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.
