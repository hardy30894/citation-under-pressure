# ARGUMENT

## I. Under This Court's Precedents, A State Procedural Default Bars Federal Habeas Review Only Where The Petitioner Himself Has Deliberately Bypassed State Procedures.

This Court has long recognized that "the doctrine under which state procedural defaults are held to constitute an adequate and independent state law ground barring direct Supreme Court review is not to be extended to limit the power granted the federal courts under the federal habeas statute." Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 399 (1963). The writ of habeas corpus exists to test "the very truth and substance of the causes" of a prisoner's detention, Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 458 (1953), and Congress vested the federal courts with power to look through state procedural rulings to the merits of federal constitutional claims. See Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 311-312 (1963).

Fay v. Noia held that a state procedural default forfeits federal habeas review only when it results from "the considered choice of the petitioner" — a "deliberate by-passing of state procedures." 372 U.S. at 439. The standard for such a forfeiture is borrowed from the law of waiver of constitutional rights generally: it requires "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). A default attributable not to the prisoner's own considered choice but to the failure of counsel cannot, under this standard, be equated with the personal, deliberate bypass that Fay v. Noia requires. Where "the prisoner deliberately by-passes the orderly procedure of the state courts," federal relief may be foreclosed; but "if he did not, it cannot be." Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. at 438.

## II. Because A Defendant Has No Personal Voice In Appellate Counsel's Selection Of Issues, Counsel's Default Of A Claim Cannot Be Treated As The Petitioner's Own Deliberate Bypass.

This Court has repeatedly held that a criminal defendant's right to counsel on a first appeal is essential to the fair administration of justice. Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 357-358 (1963) (an indigent's "one and only appeal" cannot be adequate "without the help of an attorney"). And the assistance guaranteed must be genuine advocacy, not a token presence — counsel's role is to function "in the active role of an advocate," Anders v. California, 386 U.S. 738, 744 (1967), evaluating the record and presenting the defendant's strongest claims.

It follows that when appellate counsel fails to raise a meritorious claim, the omission is counsel's failure, not the defendant's. A defendant does not "choose" which issues counsel omits from a brief any more than the indigent defendant in Douglas chose to proceed without a brief at all. Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), teaches that "lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries," precisely because a layman cannot be expected to identify or preserve his own legal claims. The same logic that condemns denying counsel altogether condemns treating counsel's unilateral omission as though it were the defendant's personal, deliberate abandonment of a claim.

Respondent's later, defaulted assertion that his appellate counsel was ineffective in failing to raise the sufficiency claim is, at bottom, a claim that the default of the sufficiency claim was never his own considered choice at all. To require that this excusing claim itself have been perfectly exhausted — on pain of losing the power to excuse the underlying default — visits upon the defendant a second layer of forfeiture for a lawyer's failure that the defendant, by definition, could not personally control. Nothing in Fay v. Noia's deliberate-bypass standard, or in Brown v. Allen's insistence that federal courts independently secure federal rights, 344 U.S. at 486-487, suggests that the failure of a claim of ineffective assistance to be preserved should itself be attributed to the petitioner as a deliberate bypass, when the very premise of the claim is that counsel — not the petitioner — controlled the presentation of issues on appeal.

## III. Requiring Independent Exhaustion Of A Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim Before It May Serve As Cause Would Undermine The Guarantee Of Effective Appellate Advocacy Recognized In Douglas And Anders.

If a defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim cannot excuse the default of the underlying claim unless the ineffectiveness claim was itself properly exhausted, defendants whose appellate counsel failed them would frequently find themselves without any forum in which the systemic denial of effective appellate advocacy could be vindicated. That result cannot be reconciled with this Court's recognition that the right to counsel on appeal, like the right to trial counsel, is not "citizens' [sic] observance of a mere formality" but a substantive guarantee against "conviction[s] which lack[] the fundamental fairness essential to the very concept of justice." Betts v. Brady is not controlling here, but the principle articulated in Gideon, 372 U.S. at 344, that the guiding hand of counsel is necessary at every step where rights may be lost, applies with full force to appellate representation under Douglas, 372 U.S. at 357.

Because Carpenter's default of the sufficiency claim was the product of appellate counsel's own failure — not Carpenter's personal, deliberate bypass under Fay v. Noia — the Sixth Circuit correctly held that the ineffective-assistance claim, though itself defaulted, could serve as cause. The judgment below should be affirmed.
