**ARGUMENT**

I. THE MSPB’S JURISDICTIONAL DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S MIXED CASE IS REVIEWABLE IN DISTRICT COURT UNDER 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2) AND THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT IN *PATEL v. WEINBERGER*, 420 F.2d 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1969).

The jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case does not divest the district court of jurisdiction under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). In *Patel v. Weinberger*, 420 F.2d 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1969), this Court held that where an employee alleges discrimination in an action appealable to the MSPB, the district court has jurisdiction to review the Board’s decision—even if the Board dismisses the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. *Id.* at 1015. The Court reasoned that the plain text of § 7703(b)(2) confers jurisdiction on district courts for “cases of discrimination,” and that a jurisdictional dismissal does not alter the nature of the claim. *Id.* at 1014-15. *Patel* remains binding authority and controls this case. *See also* *Bowen v. Massachusetts*, 487 U.S. 879, 899 n.13 (1988) (noting that *Patel* predates the current statutory scheme and remains persuasive).

II. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S JURISDICTION IS LIMITED TO APPEALS FROM THE MSPB’S MERITS DECISIONS, NOT JURISDICTIONAL DISMISSALS.

The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A) is confined to appeals from final decisions of the MSPB on the merits. In *United States v. Corrick*, 298 U.S. 435 (1936), the Supreme Court held that a federal court of appeals lacks jurisdiction to review a jurisdictional dismissal by an administrative agency unless the statute expressly confers such authority. *Id.* at 440. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Shaw*, 309 U.S. 493 (1940), where the Court held that a jurisdictional dismissal by the Interstate Commerce Commission was not reviewable in the courts of appeals absent a clear statutory grant. *Id.* at 496. The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction is similarly limited. *See* *Johnson v. United States*, 333 F.2d 124 (D.C. Cir. 1964) (holding that the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction is derivative of the MSPB’s final merits decision).

III. THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S DECISION IN *POWELL v. DEPARTMENT OF DEFENSE*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), IS INCONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT AND THE SUPREME COURT’S HOLDING IN *KLOECKNER v. SOLIS*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012).

*Powell* erroneously held that a jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case must be reviewed in the Federal Circuit. This holding conflicts with *Patel*, which established that district courts have jurisdiction over mixed cases regardless of the Board’s jurisdictional ruling. *See* 420 F.2d at 1015. Moreover, *Kloeckner* clarified that the Supreme Court’s decision in *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204 (1988), does not support the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over jurisdictional dismissals. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 604. The Court in *Kloeckner* distinguished between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, holding that only the former are reviewable in district court. *Id.* at 603. This distinction aligns with *Patel* and undermines *Powell*’s reasoning.

IV. THE BOARD’S JURISDICTIONAL DISMISSAL DOES NOT REMOVE PETITIONER’S CLAIM FROM THE AMBIT OF § 7703(b)(2).

The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not transform Petitioner’s discrimination claim into a non-mixed case. In *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260 (1954), the Supreme Court held that an agency’s erroneous jurisdictional ruling does not deprive a claimant of the right to judicial review where the claim otherwise falls within the statute’s coverage. *Id.* at 267. Similarly, in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Accardi*, 349 U.S. 280 (1955), the Court reaffirmed that jurisdictional dismissals by an agency do not preclude district court review of discrimination claims. *Id.* at 283. These cases confirm that Petitioner’s mixed-case claim remains subject to district court review despite the Board’s jurisdictional dismissal.

V. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S JURISDICTION IS INAPPLICABLE WHERE THE MSPB HAS NOT RULED ON THE MERITS.

The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction under § 7703(b)(1)(A) is contingent upon the MSPB issuing a final decision on the merits. In *United States v. Idaho*, 298 U.S. 105 (1936), the Supreme Court held that a court of appeals lacks jurisdiction to review an agency’s jurisdictional dismissal where no merits decision has been rendered. *Id.* at 108. This principle was reiterated in *United States v. Los Angeles & Salt Lake R.R. Co.*, 273 U.S. 299 (1927), where the Court held that a jurisdictional dismissal does not constitute a final decision subject to appellate review. *Id.* at 309. The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction is similarly limited. *See* *Johnson v. United States*, 333 F.2d at 126.

VI. PETITIONER’S CLAIM FALLS WITHIN THE DISTRICT COURT’S JURISDICTION UNDER THE CIVIL SERVICE REFORM ACT.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq., vests district courts with jurisdiction over mixed cases. In *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204 (1988), the Supreme Court held that the district court’s jurisdiction under § 7703(b)(2) is not defeated by the Board’s jurisdictional dismissal. *Id.* at 210. This holding aligns with *Patel* and confirms that Petitioner’s claim is properly before the district court.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that the MSPB’s jurisdictional dismissal of Petitioner’s mixed case is reviewable in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2) and this Court’s precedent in *Patel v. Weinberger*, 420 F.2d 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1969). The Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction over Petitioner’s appeal.
