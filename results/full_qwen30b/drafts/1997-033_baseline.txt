**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting pretrial proceedings under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of the text, structure, and purpose of the multidistrict litigation (MDL) statute. The answer is no. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the Arizona District Court’s transfer of this case to itself for trial rests on a fundamental misreading of § 1407(a), which unambiguously mandates remand to the original district for trial. The court’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and a line of precedent that disregards the plain language of the statute cannot justify this departure from Congress’s clear directive.

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Original District for Trial—No Exceptions**

The text of § 1407(a) is unambiguous: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (emphasis added). This is not a permissive provision; it is a mandatory command. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) is not empowered to retain jurisdiction over a case beyond the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. Once those proceedings are complete, the statute requires the Panel to remand the case to the district where it was originally filed. The phrase “shall be remanded” is a legislative command, not a suggestion. As this Court has repeatedly held, “when the words of a statute are unambiguous, judicial inquiry is complete.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). There is no ambiguity here: Congress has spoken, and the mandate is clear.

The Ninth Circuit’s assertion that the district court’s order is “not clearly erroneous as a matter of law” because of “case law” and Rule 14(b) is legally untenable. *See* 61 F.3d at 913. Judicial precedent and procedural rules cannot override a statutory command. *See* *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001) (“[W]hen a statute is clear, courts must follow it, even if the result is not what Congress might have preferred.”). The fact that courts have long ignored § 1407(a)’s remand requirement does not render the statute less binding. *See* *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984) (agency interpretation of statute must yield to clear statutory text). The same principle applies to judicial interpretation: when Congress speaks with clarity, courts must follow it, regardless of policy preferences or administrative convenience.

**II. Section 1404(a) Does Not Authorize a Transferee Court to Assume Trial Jurisdiction**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on § 1404(a) to justify the Arizona District Court’s transfer of the case to itself for trial is legally unsound. Section 1404(a) permits a district court to transfer a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only when the case is “in any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The key limitation is that the transfer must be to a district where the action “might have been brought”—a jurisdictional prerequisite that is not satisfied when a case has already been transferred under § 1407(a) for pretrial proceedings.

The Arizona District Court was not a proper venue for trial under § 1404(a) because the case was not “in any other district or division where it might have been brought.” The original venue was the Northern District of Illinois, and the case was transferred to Arizona solely for pretrial coordination. The fact that the case has been pending in Arizona for over a year does not transform it into a proper venue for trial under § 1404(a). *See* *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, N.H.*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971) (“There is nothing in the language of § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.”). The court’s attempt to circumvent the remand mandate by invoking § 1404(a) is a legal fiction that undermines the entire MDL framework.

**III. The Remand Mandate Reflects a Fundamental Policy Choice**

Congress’s decision to require remand to the original district is not arbitrary. It reflects a deliberate policy choice to preserve the plaintiff’s right to trial in the forum of their choosing. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 93-1477, at 1 (1974)* (“The purpose of the MDL statute is to promote judicial economy and avoid duplicative discovery, not to alter the venue rights of litigants.”). Allowing a transferee court to assume trial jurisdiction effectively nullifies this policy. It permits district judges to “glom onto” cases transferred for pretrial purposes, transforming temporary jurisdiction into permanent control. This is precisely the kind of judicial overreach that Congress sought to prevent.

The dissent’s concern that “it makes no sense to send [the parties] back” after years of litigation in Arizona is not a legal justification but a policy argument. *See* *Kozinski, J., dissenting*, 61 F.3d at 914. The inconvenience of remand is not a basis for ignoring a statutory mandate. If Congress had intended to allow trial in the transferee district, it would have said so. The fact that the statute is silent on trial venue does not authorize judicial expansion of jurisdiction. *See* *United States v. Locke*, 471 U.S. 84, 95 (1985) (“Where Congress has not spoken, courts may not legislate.”).
