**Argument**

Section 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. A conviction resting only on an objective “reasonable person” standard cannot stand, because criminal punishment generally requires a culpable mental state, and this Court has long refused to infer felony liability from ambiguous words alone. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250-52 (1952); Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101-07 (1945); United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, 251-52 (1922).

1. **The statute’s text and history require mens rea.**  
Section 875(c) prohibits anyone who “transmits” a communication “containing any threat” to injure another person. 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). Nothing in that text dispenses with the ordinary presumption that Congress legislates against a background of mens rea. Morissette, 342 U.S. at 250-52. This Court has repeatedly held that “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal.” Id. at 252. Where Congress omits an express mental-state term, courts should not transform a threat statute into a strict-liability offense. Id. at 263-64; see also Nash v. United States, 229 U.S. 373, 376-78 (1913) (reading statute to require culpability where criminal liability would otherwise sweep too broadly).  

The government’s objective-only theory would punish speech the speaker did not mean as threatening, including political hyperbole, artistic expression, or grim humor. That result is inconsistent with the settled rule that ambiguous conduct is not enough to brand a citizen a criminal. Boyce Motor Lines, Inc. v. United States, 342 U.S. 337, 340-42 (1952); Lambert v. California, 355 U.S. 225, 228-30 (1957).

2. **“True threats” have always been understood as purposeful threats, not merely frightening words.**  
This Court’s early threat cases focus on whether the defendant communicated a real, intentional menace. In Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 707-08 (1969), the Court treated context as decisive and reversed a conviction where the statement was political “crude offensive method” rather than a true threat. Watts confirms that the First Amendment does not permit punishment unless the communication is genuinely threatening in the speaker’s own sense, not just susceptible to a fearful reading by a listener.  

Likewise, in the line of cases protecting expression from criminal sanctions, this Court has required that speech be judged in context and with attention to meaning, not isolated words. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 308-11 (1940); Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. 516, 537-38 (1945). A subjective-intent requirement best fits that tradition because it distinguishes threats from protected advocacy, satire, and emotional venting.

3. **An objective standard creates unconstitutional vagueness and overbreadth.**  
The Constitution forbids laws that leave ordinary people uncertain about what conduct is criminal. Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391-96 (1926); Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453-58 (1939). An objective-only threat statute makes criminal liability turn on after-the-fact reactions of listeners and juries, not on what the defendant actually meant. That invites arbitrary enforcement and chills speech. Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 258-62 (1937); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-70 (1931).  

The Third Circuit’s rule is especially dangerous because emotional, artistic, and political speech often uses violent imagery without any intent to threaten. Under the court below’s approach, the more disturbing the rhetoric, the greater the risk of criminal punishment—exactly the kind of chilling effect the First Amendment forbids. See Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson, 283 U.S. 697, 708-23 (1931); Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263-63 (1941).

4. **Subjective intent is the narrowest rule faithful to constitutional text and criminal-law principles.**  
Requiring proof that the defendant intended his words as a threat does not immunize genuine menace. The government may prove intent from context, repetition, prior conduct, and the words themselves. But it must prove that the defendant meant to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person could have felt alarm. See Screws, 325 U.S. at 101-07; Morissette, 342 U.S. at 263-64.  

That rule is also administrable. Juries routinely decide intent from circumstantial evidence. See Bailey v. Alabama, 219 U.S. 219, 242-44 (1911). The only question is whether the government must prove the defendant’s culpable mental state. For a criminal threat statute carrying imprisonment, the answer must be yes.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
