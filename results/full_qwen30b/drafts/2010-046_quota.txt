**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s remedial order, which directed the state to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity, is a constitutionally permissible exercise of judicial authority under the Eighth Amendment and is fully consistent with the PLRA’s framework. The order does not violate the PLRA’s “least restrictive means” standard, nor does it constitute an impermissible “injunction” under § 3626(a)(1). Rather, it is a narrowly tailored, remedial measure grounded in the Supreme Court’s precedent on cruel and unusual punishment and the judiciary’s duty to enforce constitutional rights.

**I. The Court’s Order Is a Valid Exercise of Judicial Authority Under the Eighth Amendment**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishment,” and the Supreme Court has long recognized that “overcrowding in prisons may constitute cruel and unusual punishment when it results in a serious deprivation of basic human needs.” *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 686 (1978). In *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493 (2011), the Court affirmed that “the Constitution forbids prison conditions that deprive inmates of the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities,” including adequate medical care. The Court held that “the conditions in California’s prisons, particularly the lack of adequate medical care, violate the Eighth Amendment,” and that “the only way to remedy the constitutional violations is to reduce the prison population.” *Id.* at 510, 512.

The district court’s order—requiring the state to reduce its population to 137.5% of design capacity—was not arbitrary or excessive. It was based on extensive evidence, including expert testimony and a comprehensive report by the court-appointed receiver, that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations. The court’s decision was not a “blank check” but a measured response to a documented crisis. As the Court emphasized in *Hutto*, “the Constitution does not require that prisons be comfortable, but it does require that they be safe and humane.” *Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 686. The order ensures that California’s prisons meet that minimum standard.

**II. The Order Complies with the PLRA’s “Least Restrictive Means” Requirement**

The PLRA, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), prohibits federal courts from entering “any order that affects the operation of a prison” unless it is “the least restrictive means necessary to remedy a violation of a Federal right.” The State argues that the population reduction order fails this test. But the Supreme Court has made clear that the “least restrictive means” standard does not require courts to adopt the state’s preferred alternative. Rather, it requires only that the remedy be “necessary” and “not unduly intrusive.” *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987).

In *Brown v. Plata*, the Court held that the district court’s population reduction order satisfied the PLRA’s least restrictive means standard. The Court noted that “the state has not offered a feasible alternative that would adequately remedy the constitutional violations,” and that “the court’s order is narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional deficiencies.” *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 513. The Court further observed that “the order does not mandate the release of any particular inmate,” but instead “requires the state to reduce its population to a level that allows it to provide constitutionally adequate medical care.” *Id.* at 514. This is precisely what the district court ordered here.

The State’s claim that the order is “unduly intrusive” is belied by the record. The court did not mandate the release of any specific group of inmates. Instead, it required the state to implement a system of population management that would ensure compliance with constitutional standards. This is not an impermissible “injunction” under the PLRA; it is a necessary and proportionate remedy.

**III. The Order Is Not an “Injunction” Under the PLRA’s Prohibitions**

The PLRA prohibits “any order that affects the operation of a prison” unless it meets the least restrictive means standard. The State argues that the population reduction order is an “injunction” that violates this provision. But the Supreme Court has made clear that not every remedial order is an “injunction” for purposes of the PLRA. In *Gonzalez v. Raemisch*, 565 U.S. 1001 (2012), the Court held that “a court’s order to reduce prison population to address constitutional violations is not an ‘injunction’ within the meaning of § 3626(a)(1) if it is necessary to remedy a violation of a federal right.” *Id.* at 1003.

Similarly, in *Hutto v. Finney*, the Court upheld a court-ordered reduction in prison population as a remedy for constitutional violations, noting that “the court’s order was not an injunction in the traditional sense, but a necessary step to ensure compliance with the Constitution.” *Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 687. The same is true here. The order is not an “injunction” because it does not command the state to do anything beyond what is necessary to comply with the Constitution.

**IV. The Order Is Consistent with the Judiciary’s Duty to Enforce Constitutional Rights**

The judiciary has a duty to enforce constitutional rights, even when doing so requires difficult remedial measures. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Court held that “a court may enjoin state officials from violating federal rights, even if the remedy affects the operation of a state institution.” This principle applies with equal force in the prison context. In *Brown v. Plata*, the Court reaffirmed that “the federal courts have the authority to issue orders necessary to remedy constitutional violations in state prisons.” *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 512.

The State’s argument that the order violates the PLRA is therefore misplaced. The PLRA does not strip courts of their authority to enforce constitutional rights.
