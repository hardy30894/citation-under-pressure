**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that the formulation of a State's test of criminal responsibility is a matter of legislative policy in which the States retain "wide latitude." *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows that a State may also determine the procedural channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, leaving such judgments to the States absent a compelling showing that due process requires otherwise. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense, as historically understood since *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent element-by-element. Likewise, in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court addressed insanity as a distinct issue governing criminal responsibility, separate from the prosecution's independent burden to prove the elements of the offense. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation between the insanity inquiry and the mens rea inquiry.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not violate "a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937).

Nor does the Fourteenth Amendment impose a rigid uniformity on state criminal procedure. As this Court explained in *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), due process embodies "a concept less rigid and more fluid than those envisaged in other specific and particular provisions of the Bill of Rights," *id.* at 462, permitting the States considerable room to adapt their trial procedures, including the rules governing the admission and use of evidence.

This deference extends to a State's authority to structure how evidence bearing on mental condition is received and applied, so long as the resulting procedure does not amount to conduct that "shocks the conscience." *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence—directing the jury to the historically established channel of the insanity defense—falls comfortably within that discretion. The rule does not exclude the evidence altogether; it merely assigns it to the framework in which such evidence has traditionally been considered.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), confirms this result. There, the Court explained that "the aim of the requirement of due process is not to exclude presumptively accurate evidence but to prevent fundamental unfairness in the use of evidence, whether true or false." *Id.* at 236. Arizona did not exclude Clark's psychiatric evidence; the trial court considered it fully in adjudicating his insanity claim. The court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to negate the mens rea element the State was otherwise required to prove. Such channeling does not create the kind of fundamental unfairness condemned in *Lisenba*.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in evaluating his insanity claim; the court did not bar the evidence from the proceeding, but limited the purpose for which it could be used—precisely the sort of evidentiary line-drawing sanctioned in *Betts*, *Rochin*, and *Lisenba*, and consistent with the substantive latitude confirmed in *Leland* and *Powell*. Arizona's rule reflects the traditional separation between the insanity defense, rooted in *M'Naghten's Case* and *Davis*, and the prosecution's independent burden to prove criminal intent. Nothing in this framework offends "a principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people... as to be ranked as fundamental." *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325.

For these reasons, Arizona's rule confining Clark's psychiatric evidence to his insanity defense did not violate the Due Process Clause, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
