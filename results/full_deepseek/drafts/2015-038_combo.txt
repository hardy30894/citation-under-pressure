**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service properly enforced its hovercraft regulation on state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act does not strip the Service of authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within conservation system units. The plain text, structure, and constitutional backdrop confirm that the Service retains regulatory jurisdiction over all lands and waters within park boundaries.

**I. The Plain Text of ANILCA § 103(c) Does Not Prohibit Regulation of Non-Federal Lands**

Section 103(c) provides that “only” provisions that apply to “public lands” shall apply to lands “conveyed to the State.” But the term “public lands” in ANILCA is defined broadly to include all lands within conservation system units, regardless of ownership. *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3102(3). A statute’s plain language controls. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Where Congress defined “public lands” to encompass the entire unit, it cannot be read to create a carve-out for state-owned parcels. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is a regulation of “public lands” as defined, and thus lawfully applies to the Nation River within the Preserve.

**II. The Hovercraft Ban Regulates Use, Not Land Ownership**

Even if § 103(c) limited regulations that “apply solely to public lands,” the hovercraft ban does not apply solely to land. It regulates the operation of a vehicle on navigable waters—a quintessentially federal subject. The Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate navigable waters and the vessels that ply them. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1, 193 (1824). The Nation River is a navigable waterway; the hovercraft ban therefore falls within federal commerce power. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). The Service’s regulation is a permissible exercise of that authority, independent of land ownership.

**III. The Federal Government Retains Plenary Authority Over Navigable Waters**

The State’s title to submerged lands does not extinguish federal regulatory authority. The United States has long exercised control over navigable waters to protect commerce, navigation, and public uses. *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 703 (1899). That authority includes the power to prohibit certain vessels, like hovercrafts, that pose unique risks to public safety and park resources. *United States v. Winans*, 198 U.S. 371, 381 (1905) (recognizing federal easement over navigable waters for public purposes). The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of that dominant federal power.

**IV. The Property Clause Authorizes Regulation of Adjacent Non-Federal Lands**

The Property Clause gives Congress plenary power to manage federal lands and to protect them from harm originating on adjacent non-federal lands. *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897). Here, the hovercraft ban protects the Yukon-Charley Preserve as a whole. The ban applies uniformly throughout the unit, ensuring that activities on state-owned waters do not impair federal resources. *United States v. Gratiot*, 39 U.S. 526, 537 (1840) (Congress has “complete power” over public lands). The Service’s regulation is a reasonable exercise of that protective authority.

**V. The Supremacy Clause Precludes State Interference**

To the extent Alaska claims authority to exempt hovercrafts from the ban, that claim conflicts with valid federal law. Under the Supremacy Clause, federal regulations preempt inconsistent state law. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). The hovercraft ban is a federal regulation of general applicability within the Preserve; Alaska cannot carve out its own lands from its reach. The State’s asserted interest in managing its waters must yield to the paramount federal interest in preserving national park resources.

**VI. Congress Did Not Intend ANILCA to Displace NPS Authority**

ANILCA was enacted to protect Alaska’s conservation system units. Reading § 103(c) to gut the Service’s regulatory authority over non-federal lands would undermine that purpose. This Court should avoid interpretations that lead to absurd results. *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 490. The Service’s interpretation—that it retains jurisdiction over all lands within park boundaries—is the only one consistent with the statute’s text and Congress’s intent.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that NPS may regulate hovercrafts on state-owned navigable waters within the Yukon-Charley Preserve. The judgment should be affirmed.
