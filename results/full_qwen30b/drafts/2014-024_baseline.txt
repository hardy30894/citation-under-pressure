**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—lies at the heart of the First Amendment’s protection against criminalizing speech absent a clear intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective “true threat” standard, which permits conviction based solely on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat, violates the constitutional guarantee of due process and contravenes the Supreme Court’s definitive holding in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003). This Court should reverse.

### I. The Third Circuit’s Objective Standard Violates the First Amendment’s Requirement of Subjective Intent

The First Amendment protects speech, even speech that is offensive or disturbing, unless it falls within a narrow exception. One such exception is the “true threat,” which is not protected because it is “a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Id.* at 359. But the Supreme Court has made clear that the true threat exception is not coextensive with all speech that might be perceived as threatening by a reasonable observer. Rather, it requires proof of the speaker’s *subjective intent* to threaten.

In *Virginia v. Black*, the Court held that “a true threat is a statement in which the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The Court rejected the notion that a threat could be established by an objective standard alone, stating that “the First Amendment protects even the most offensive speech in order to preserve the robust marketplace of ideas.” *Id.* at 358. The Court emphasized that “the speaker must have intended to threaten,” and that “a threat is not a threat unless the speaker means it to be one.” *Id.* at 359.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that a statement is a true threat if a reasonable person would foresee it as threatening, regardless of the speaker’s intent—directly contradicts *Black*. By allowing conviction based on an objective standard, the court effectively criminalizes speech that a speaker may believe is merely expressive, satirical, or rhetorical. This is precisely the kind of overbroad criminalization the First Amendment was designed to prevent.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. *Kosma* predated *Black* and did not address the subjective intent requirement. Moreover, *Kosma* did not purport to define the constitutional limits of the true threat doctrine; it merely applied a pre-*Black* understanding of the term. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Black* overruled any precedent inconsistent with the requirement of subjective intent. As this Court has repeatedly held, “a decision of this Court controls the interpretation of federal law, even when a circuit court has previously interpreted the same statute.” *United States v. Johnson*, 529 U.S. 569, 575 (2000).

### II. The Objective Standard Creates a Severe Risk of Criminalizing Protected Speech

The Third Circuit’s objective standard invites the criminalization of speech that is clearly protected under the First Amendment. Elonis’s Facebook posts—though disturbing in tone—were presented in the form of rap lyrics, hyperbolic expressions, and satirical commentary. He testified that he was writing “rap” and “poetry,” not making literal threats. His wife, who had never seen him write such lyrics before, testified that she took them seriously not because of their form, but because of their content.

Yet under the Third Circuit’s standard, the form of the speech is irrelevant. A reasonable person might interpret the posts as threatening, even if the speaker did not intend them as such. This is precisely the danger the First Amendment guards against. As this Court has warned, “the danger of overcriminalization is greatest when the law criminalizes speech based on its effect rather than its intent.” *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 472 (2010). The risk of punishing protected expression is not hypothetical—it is real and pervasive.

The Court has long recognized that “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive, unpleasant, or even repugnant.” *Texas v. Johnson*, 491 U.S. 397, 414 (1989). To allow conviction based on an objective standard is to permit the government to punish speech based on how it is received, not how it is intended. That is a fundamental violation of due process and the core values of free expression.

### III. The Government’s Interest in Preventing Fear Does Not Justify an Objective Standard

The Third Circuit justified its objective standard on the ground that the “true threat” exception exists to protect individuals from the fear of violence. But this reasoning misapprehends the nature of the First Amendment’s protection. The Constitution does not permit the government to punish speech simply because it causes fear. Rather, it permits punishment only when the speaker *intends* to cause fear through a serious threat of violence.

The government’s interest in preventing fear is not a license to criminalize speech that is merely offensive or alarming. As the Court stated in *Black*, “the First Amendment protects even the most offensive speech in order to preserve the robust marketplace of ideas.” *538 U.S. at 358*. The fear of violence is not a justification for punishing speech that is not intended to threaten. The government may protect individuals from actual threats, but it may not punish speech that is only perceived as threatening by a reasonable observer.

### Conclusion

The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard for true threats violates the First Amendment’s requirement of subjective intent. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Virginia v. Black* made clear that a true threat requires a speaker’s intent to threaten. The government must prove that the defendant *meant* to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person might have perceived the statement as threatening. Reversal is necessary to preserve the constitutional protection of free speech.

For these reasons, the petition should be granted.
