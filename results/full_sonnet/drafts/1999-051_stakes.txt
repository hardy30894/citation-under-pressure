**ARGUMENT**

**I. Ineffective assistance of counsel is "cause" for a procedural default because it is a constitutional violation attributable to the State, not to the petitioner.**

This Court has long recognized that a procedural default may be excused where the prisoner demonstrates "cause" for the default and "prejudice" resulting from it. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). "Cause" exists where "some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts to comply with the State's procedural rule." *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). The Court in *Carrier* held that "ineffective assistance of counsel . . . is cause for a procedural default," *id.* at 488, because a lawyer's constitutionally deficient performance is not fairly chargeable to the client. As the Court explained in *Coleman v. Thompson*, "[a]ttorney error that constitutes ineffective assistance of counsel is cause for a procedural default." 501 U.S. 722, 753 (1991). By contrast, ordinary attorney error "must be attributed to the prisoner" because "the attorney is the petitioner's agent." *Id.* (quoting *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488).

The controlling distinction, then, is not whether the ineffective-assistance claim has itself survived every procedural hurdle in state court, but whether the underlying conduct amounts to a *constitutional* violation independent of the prisoner's own choices. Appellate counsel's failure to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was such a violation. A defendant is constitutionally entitled to effective assistance on a first appeal as of right, for such an appeal "is not adjudicated in accord with due process of law if the appellant does not have the effective assistance of an attorney." *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 396 (1985). Once counsel's performance falls below the *Evitts* standard, the resulting default of the sufficiency claim is the State's doing, not Carpenter's — regardless of the procedural fate of the ineffective-assistance claim in a later, state post-conviction proceeding.

**II. Requiring the "cause" claim itself to be free of procedural default imposes an unwarranted, potentially infinite layering of exhaustion requirements that this Court's cause-and-prejudice jurisprudence does not demand.**

The cause-and-prejudice standard is an equitable accommodation between the State's legitimate interest in the finality of its judgments and the prisoner's interest in federal review of federal constitutional violations. *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 90-91. This Court has been careful not to convert that accommodation into "a rigid rule" that forecloses relief through technical formalism. *Id.* at 91. Consistent with that flexibility, the Court has excused defaults for reasons having nothing to do with whether the excusing circumstance was itself preserved — for example, where "the legal basis for a claim was not reasonably available to counsel," a novel-claim rationale that asks only about the objective unavailability of the claim, not about whether *that* unavailability was separately litigated. *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1, 16 (1984).

If a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause unless the prisoner first shows cause and prejudice for defaulting *that* claim, the logic does not stop there: the second-order cause claim could likewise be defaulted, requiring yet another showing of cause and prejudice, and so on. Nothing in *Carrier* or *Coleman* contemplates such an infinite regress. Those cases speak of ineffective assistance as cause in unadorned terms — "ineffective assistance of counsel . . . is cause for a procedural default," *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488 — without conditioning that rule on the ineffective-assistance claim's own procedural pedigree. To read such a condition into the cause inquiry would transform a doctrine meant to protect against unfairness into a trap that swallows the very claims it was designed to preserve.

**III. Sound administration of habeas corpus counsels against requiring double exhaustion of an ineffective-assistance claim before it may excuse a separate default.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that habeas procedure should not be so rigid as to defeat the writ's core purpose of correcting constitutional violations that affect the fairness of a conviction. *See McCleskey v. Zant*, 499 U.S. 467, 493-94 (1991) (balancing finality against the need to correct fundamental constitutional errors). Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim was raised at the first opportunity available to him with new counsel — through an application to reopen his direct appeal. The Ohio courts declined that application as untimely, but the timeliness ruling does not erase the underlying constitutional reality that appellate counsel failed to challenge a conviction unsupported by sufficient evidence. Where, as here, the same systemic failure of counsel infects both the direct appeal and the ability to timely raise that failure, sound practice — and this Court's cases treating ineffective assistance as an external, State-attributable cause — support permitting the defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim without an additional showing of cause for its own default.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
