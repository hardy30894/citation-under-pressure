**ARGUMENT**

**I. CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) Provides the Exclusive Remedy for Guam’s Costs Incurred Pursuant to a Judicially Approved Settlement**

The court of appeals correctly held that CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides the exclusive remedy for Guam’s costs incurred pursuant to the 2004 Consent Decree. Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits a party that has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” to seek contribution from any person not party to the settlement. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has recognized that § 113(f)(3)(B) “provides two express avenues for contribution,” *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004), and that a settling party may seek contribution from non-settling parties, *id.* at 168. The D.C. Circuit has further explained that § 113(f)(3)(B) “insulates a settling party from further contribution liability,” *United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d 1073, 1080 (D.C. Cir. 2017), and that a qualifying settlement triggers the three-year statute of limitations for contribution claims, *id.* at 1081.

Guam’s argument that the Consent Decree did not “resolve” its liability is unavailing. The D.C. Circuit has held that a consent decree resolves liability where it imposes obligations consistent with a finding of liability, even if the parties disclaim liability. *See United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d at 1080 (citing *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1113, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017)). The Consent Decree here required Guam to close the Ordot Dump, design and install a cover system, and pay a civil penalty—obligations consistent with liability for environmental contamination. *See* Consent Decree, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam) (J.A. 90). The disclaimer of liability in the Consent Decree does not alter this conclusion. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding that a disclaimer of liability did not preclude a finding that liability was resolved where the settlement imposed obligations consistent with liability).

**II. The Consent Decree Qualifies as a “Settlement” Under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B)**

The Consent Decree is a “settlement” within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has held that § 113(f)(3)(B) applies to “administrative or judicially approved settlements,” *Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167, and the D.C. Circuit has explained that a consent decree approved by a court qualifies as a judicially approved settlement, *United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d at 1080. The Consent Decree here was approved by the District Court of Guam and required Guam to undertake specific obligations in exchange for the dismissal of EPA’s Clean Water Act claims. *See* Consent Decree (J.A. 90). This constitutes a judicially approved settlement under § 113(f)(3)(B).

Guam’s argument that the Consent Decree does not qualify as a settlement because it arose from Clean Water Act claims is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has held that CERCLA liability may arise from the same conduct that gives rise to liability under other environmental statutes. *See Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 814 (1994). The D.C. Circuit has further explained that a settlement resolving liability for response costs under one statute may trigger contribution rights under CERCLA. *See United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d at 1080. The Consent Decree here resolved Guam’s liability for response costs related to the Ordot Dump, and thus qualifies as a settlement under § 113(f)(3)(B).

**III. The Three-Year Statute of Limitations for Contribution Claims Bars Guam’s Suit**

The three-year statute of limitations for contribution claims under § 113(g)(3) bars Guam’s suit. The Supreme Court has held that the statute of limitations for contribution claims begins to run when the settlement is entered. *See Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 170. The Consent Decree was entered in 2004, and Guam did not file its suit until 2017—well beyond the three-year limitations period. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). The D.C. Circuit has further explained that the statute of limitations for contribution claims is not tolled by the filing of a cost-recovery action under § 107. *See United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d at 1081.

Guam’s argument that the statute of limitations should be equitably tolled is unavailing. The Supreme Court has held that equitable tolling is not available where the plaintiff has failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing its claims. *See Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990). Guam’s delay in filing suit—13 years after the Consent Decree and 9 years after the limitations period expired—demonstrates a lack of due diligence. *See id.* The D.C. Circuit has further explained that equitable tolling is not available where the plaintiff has failed to demonstrate extraordinary circumstances. *See Pace v. Burgess*, 920 F.3d 1000, 1005 (D.C. Cir. 2019). Guam has not demonstrated extraordinary circumstances warranting equitable tolling.

**IV. Guam’s § 107 Cost-Recovery Claim Is Precluded by § 113(f)(3)(B)**

Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery claim is precluded by § 113(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has held that § 113(f)(3)(B) provides the exclusive remedy for a settling party’s costs incurred pursuant to a settlement. *See Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167. The D.C. Circuit has further explained that a settling party may not pursue a § 107 cost-recovery claim where § 113(f)(3)(B) provides an alternative remedy. *See United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d at 1080. The Consent Decree here resolved Guam’s liability for response costs, and thus § 113(f)(3)(B) provides the exclusive remedy for Guam’s costs. *See id.*

Guam’s argument that § 107 provides an alternative remedy is unavailing. The Supreme Court has held that § 107 and § 113 are mutually exclusive remedies, and that a settling party may not pursue a § 107 claim where § 113 provides an alternative remedy. *See Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167. The D.C. Circuit has further explained that a settling party may not pursue a § 107 claim where the settlement resolves liability for response costs. *See United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d at 1080. The Consent Decree here resolves Guam’s liability for response costs, and thus § 107 is not available as an alternative remedy. *See id.*
