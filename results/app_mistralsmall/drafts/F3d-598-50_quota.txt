**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING TESTIMONY THAT IMPROPERLY VOUCHED FOR THE APPELLEES’ CREDIBILITY AND USURPED THE JURY’S ROLE IN DETERMINING PROBABLE CAUSE.**

The admission of testimony that directly vouched for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, and opined on the existence of probable cause, violated fundamental evidentiary rules and deprived Appellants of a fair trial. *See United States v. Abel*, 469 U.S. 45, 54 (1984) (holding that a witness may not “bolster” another witness’s credibility before it has been attacked); *United States v. Mejia*, 545 F.3d 179, 202 (2d Cir. 2008) (prohibiting witnesses from expressing opinions on the credibility of other witnesses). The Second Circuit has repeatedly cautioned against such testimony, emphasizing that jurors are fully capable of assessing credibility without extrinsic validation. *See United States v. Rosa*, 11 F.3d 315, 334 (2d Cir. 1993) (reversing a conviction where a prosecutor improperly vouched for a witness’s credibility).

Even more egregious was the admission of testimony that amounted to legal conclusions on probable cause. *See United States v. Rigas*, 490 F.3d 208, 223 (2d Cir. 2007) (holding that witnesses may not testify as to whether probable cause existed). The District Court allowed Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant to opine that the officers’ actions were justified, effectively instructing the jury on the legal standard rather than permitting them to apply it. *See id.* This violated the rule that jurors, not witnesses, determine whether facts satisfy legal standards. *See United States v. Glenn*, 312 F.3d 58, 64 (2d Cir. 2002).

The cumulative effect of these errors was highly prejudicial. The jury was exposed to repeated assertions that the officers’ version of events was credible and legally sound, while Appellants’ account was implicitly undermined. *See Hynes v. Coughlin*, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996) (requiring reversal where improperly admitted evidence “so infected the trial that the resulting conviction violates due process”). The errors were not harmless, as they directly addressed the two central issues in the case: credibility and probable cause. *See id.*

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY DENIED APPELLANTS’ MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW, AS THE SECURITY CAMERA PHOTOGRAPHS DO NOT IRRECONCILABLY CONTRADICT APPELLEES’ ACCOUNT.**

Appellants argue that the security camera photographs so plainly contradict the officers’ testimony that no reasonable jury could credit their version of events. However, the photographs are ambiguous and do not definitively resolve the dispute. *See Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007) (holding that a video recording did not conclusively establish the plaintiff’s version of events). The Second Circuit has recognized that photographs, like other evidence, may be subject to differing interpretations. *See United States v. MacPherson*, 424 F.3d 183, 187 (2d Cir. 2005). Thus, the District Court properly denied Appellants’ motion for judgment as a matter of law.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY THAT OBSTRUCTING GOVERNMENTAL ADMINISTRATION REQUIRES A LAWFUL ARREST.**

Under New York law, obstructing governmental administration (OGA) requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *See People v. Case*, 42 N.Y.2d 98, 101 (1977). The District Court’s failure to so instruct the jury was error, as it permitted the jury to convict Higgenbottom even if they found the officers’ arrest was unlawful. *See id.* The Second Circuit has held that jury instructions must accurately reflect the law, and failure to do so is reversible error. *See United States v. Park*, 421 F.3d 220, 225 (2d Cir. 2005).

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY ON PUNITIVE DAMAGES WHERE APPLICABLE.**

Appellants presented evidence that Ramos and Rivera acted with malice in fabricating charges against Cameron. Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is motivated by evil motive or intent. *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). The Second Circuit has held that punitive damages are appropriate where the evidence shows “reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights.” *See Robison v. Via*, 821 F.2d 913, 923 (2d Cir. 1987). The District Court’s refusal to instruct the jury on punitive damages was error, as Appellants’ evidence supported such an instruction. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
