Respondent’s judgment should be affirmed because the Constitution fixes exclusive qualifications for Members of Congress, and Arkansas may not add to them by initiative or constitutional amendment.

Article I is explicit. For the House, “No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2. For the Senate, “No Person shall be a Senator who shall not have attained to the Age of thirty Years, and been nine Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State for which he shall be chosen.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3. Those textually enumerated qualifications are exclusive. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522-48 (1969).

Powell is controlling. There, the Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected Member who met the Constitution’s qualifications, because the Constitution “fixes the qualifications of Members of Congress” and neither House may “add to” them. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. at 522, 548. The Court examined the text, structure, and history of Article I and concluded that the Framers deliberately chose a limited, fixed list. Id. at 528-31, 540-48. That reasoning forecloses Arkansas’s attempt to bar ballot access to otherwise qualified candidates solely because they have already served a specified number of terms. A state may not do indirectly, through a ballot disqualification, what the Constitution forbids it to do directly. See id. at 550-51.

The historical understanding confirms the point. In Federalist No. 52, Madison explained that the qualifications for the House were meant to be few and uniform, and in Federalist No. 60 he emphasized that the constitutional specifications of qualification were not left to ordinary political manipulation. The Constitution’s uniformity would be defeated if each state could superadd its own terms-limits qualification for national office. See Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. at 547-48.

Arkansas’s measure is not merely a ballot regulation. It declares that a person who has served the specified number of terms “shall not” be certified or placed on the ballot. That is a substantive qualification for office, not a neutral regulation of election machinery. The Court has long recognized that states may regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections, U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, but that power does not extend to altering qualifications fixed by the Constitution. Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355, 366-67 (1932); Burroughs v. United States, 290 U.S. 534, 544-46 (1934). Article I, section 4, permits states to prescribe procedural rules; it does not authorize them to disqualify whole classes of otherwise eligible candidates.

Nor can Arkansas justify Section 3 as an exercise of reserved powers. The Tenth Amendment reserves only powers not delegated to the United States or prohibited to the states. U.S. Const. amend. X. But the power to define qualifications for Members of Congress was not reserved; it was expressly taken up and fixed by Article I. See Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. at 548-49. When the Constitution speaks directly, state power must yield. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 176-78 (1803).

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s severability ruling should also be affirmed if challenged. A severability clause cannot save a measure where the valid and invalid parts were presented to the electorate as a single package and the court cannot say the voters would have approved the valid portions alone. Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 312-13 (1936); Carter v. State of Arkansas, 240 Ark. 565, 401 S.W.2d 747 (1966). Here, the federal and state term-limit provisions were joined in one amendment and marketed together as one “Term Limitation Amendment.” The lower court reasonably concluded that the package was inseverable.

Finally, this case presents no occasion for judicial deference to political innovation. The issue is not whether term limits are wise, but whether a state may amend the federal Constitution by indirection. It may not. In the words of Powell, the Constitution “is a limitation upon the power of the Congress and not a grant of power to Congress.” 395 U.S. at 521. The same principle binds the States. Because Arkansas’s Amendment 73 adds qualifications the Constitution does not permit, Section 3 is invalid. The judgment should be affirmed.
