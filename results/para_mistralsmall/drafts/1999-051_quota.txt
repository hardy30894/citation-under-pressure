**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

This Court has long held that a prisoner seeking federal habeas relief must first fairly present his federal claims to the state courts. *O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 844 (1999) (“[A] state prisoner must give the state courts an opportunity to act on his federal claims before he presents those claims to a federal habeas court.”). When a prisoner fails to do so, his claims are procedurally defaulted, and he may not obtain federal review unless he demonstrates cause for the default and actual prejudice therefrom. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). The cause-and-prejudice standard is “a prerequisite to federal habeas review of procedurally defaulted claims.” *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 622 (1998).

The Court has recognized a narrow exception to the cause requirement: when a prisoner’s constitutional right to effective assistance of counsel was violated by the very counsel whose ineffectiveness is alleged to excuse the default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) (“[I]f the procedural default is the result of ineffective assistance of counsel, the Sixth Amendment itself requires that responsibility for the default be imputed to the State.”). This exception, however, applies only when the ineffective-assistance claim itself has not been procedurally defaulted. *Id.* at 489 (“[T]he mere fact that counsel failed to raise a claim in an earlier proceeding does not establish cause for a procedural default in a later federal habeas proceeding.”).

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that an ineffective-assistance claim may excuse the default of another claim regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim itself is procedurally defaulted. *Carpenter v. Mohr*, 170 F.3d 915, 919 (6th Cir. 1999) (“[T]he cause requirement is satisfied if the petitioner can show that his appellate counsel was ineffective in failing to raise the claim on direct appeal, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim itself has been procedurally defaulted.”). This holding conflicts with *Carrier* and its progeny. *See, e.g., Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446, 451 (2000) (per curiam) (“[T]he Court of Appeals erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of another claim.”).

**II. The Sixth Circuit’s holding is inconsistent with this Court’s decisions applying the cause-and-prejudice standard.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the cause-and-prejudice standard is strictly applied to ensure that federal habeas review does not become a substitute for ordinary error correction. *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623 (“[T]he cause-and-prejudice requirement is grounded in concerns of comity and federalism.”). The Sixth Circuit’s rule would eviscerate that standard by allowing prisoners to bootstrap procedurally defaulted claims through defaulted ineffective-assistance claims. *See Smith v. Robbins*, 528 U.S. 259, 288 (2000) (“[T]he cause-and-prejudice standard ensures that habeas petitioners do not ‘sandbag’ the state courts by failing to raise claims there and then raising them in federal court.”).

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s approach would encourage prisoners to delay raising ineffective-assistance claims, knowing that such claims could later be used to excuse defaults on other claims. *See Murray*, 477 U.S. at 490 (“[T]he cause requirement would serve no purpose if it could be satisfied by a showing that a prisoner’s counsel failed to raise a claim in an earlier proceeding.”). This would undermine the finality of state court judgments and the orderly administration of justice. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750 (“[T]he States’ interest in finality is important, and the cause-and-prejudice standard furthers that interest.”).

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s holding is unsupported by the decisions of other circuits.**

The Sixth Circuit’s rule is an outlier. Every other circuit to address this issue has held that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim. *See, e.g., United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1982) (noting that the cause requirement “prevents a prisoner from using federal habeas as a delayed appeal”); *United States v. Cook*, 45 F.3d 388, 392 (10th Cir. 1995) (“[A]n ineffective-assistance claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim.”); *United States v. Simmonds*, 111 F.3d 737, 745 (11th Cir. 1997) (same).

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Circuit’s holding is contrary to this Court’s decisions and the overwhelming weight of authority from other circuits. It should be reversed.
