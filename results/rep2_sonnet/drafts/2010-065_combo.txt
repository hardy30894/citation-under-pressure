# ARGUMENT

## I. A Private Litigant Lacks Standing to Raise a Tenth Amendment Challenge to a Federal Criminal Statute

This Court's decision in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), forecloses Bond's claim. There, private utility companies argued that TVA's sale of power violated the Tenth Amendment by intruding on matters reserved to the states. This Court rejected the claim on standing grounds, holding that private parties, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. That holding controls here. Bond, like the utility companies in *Tennessee Electric*, is a private party asserting that a federal statute intrudes upon the reserved powers of a sovereign—Pennsylvania—that has not joined her suit and has raised no objection of its own.

The rationale for this rule is structural. The Tenth Amendment protects the relationship between the federal government and the several States; it does not create a personal right enforceable by any individual who finds federal regulation inconvenient. As this Court explained in *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *Id.* at 124. It is a rule of construction describing the allocation of sovereign authority, not a font of individual rights parallel to the personal liberties secured elsewhere in the Bill of Rights. Where the alleged constitutional injury runs to the sovereign interest of a State, it is the State—not a private defendant—that possesses the interest necessary to invoke it.

## II. Bond's Asserted Injury Is Not the Kind of Concrete, Particularized Interest That Confers Standing

This Court has long insisted that a party invoking the judicial power must show more than a generalized grievance shared by the public at large. In *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), the Court dismissed a taxpayer's challenge to a federal appropriations statute allegedly intruding on matters reserved to the States, explaining that the plaintiff's "interest in the moneys of the Treasury...is shared with millions of others" and is "comparatively minute and indeterminable." *Id.* at 487. The same is true of Bond's asserted interest in vindicating Pennsylvania's sovereign prerogatives. She does not claim that the Commonwealth's own regulatory authority has been impaired in any way that harms her distinctly from any other citizen; she claims only that the federal government prosecuted her under a statute that, in her view, a State should have applied instead. That is precisely the kind of diffuse, structural grievance that *Mellon* held insufficient to support standing.

This Court reached a similar conclusion in the analogous context of competitor standing. In *Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes*, 302 U.S. 464 (1938), and *Perkins v. Lukens Steel Co.*, 310 U.S. 113 (1940), the Court held that private parties lacked standing to challenge federal action allegedly exceeding constitutional authority where their asserted injuries did not flow from an invasion of a legally protected interest belonging to them. The same logic applies with even greater force here: Bond's alleged injury—prosecution under a statute she believes exceeds Congress's enumerated powers—is fully redressable through the ordinary constitutional doctrines that do protect individual defendants, such as due process and vagueness challenges, which she has separately raised. She need not be permitted to borrow the Commonwealth's sovereign interest to accomplish the same end.

## III. Permitting a Structural Challenge of This Kind Would Displace the State's Own Prerogative to Assert or Waive Its Sovereign Interest

The Tenth Amendment's structural protections belong to the States as sovereigns, and it is the prerogative of the State, not a private citizen, to decide whether and how to assert them. This principle finds support in *Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433 (1939), where this Court examined whether a private plaintiff could invoke a structural constitutional provision belonging to a legislative body, and confined standing to those whose official interest was directly implicated by the alleged violation. *Id.* at 438. Pennsylvania has never suggested that § 229's application to Bond's conduct offends its sovereignty, nor has it sought to prosecute this case itself. Indeed, this Court has recognized that Congress may implement treaty obligations through legislation "necessary and proper" to that end, and that such legislation may address matters that would otherwise fall within the traditional police power of the States. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920); *see also Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945) (noting that "administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States"). Where, as here, the sovereign whose interest is allegedly threatened has not objected, and where this Court's own precedents in *Tennessee Electric* and *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 341 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (counseling that courts should not decide constitutional questions broader than necessary), counsel restraint, there is no occasion for a federal court to adjudicate a structural federalism claim that the State itself has not raised.

## Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed insofar as it held that Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds.
