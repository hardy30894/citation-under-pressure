**ARGUMENT**

I. THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE SPEAKER SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENTS TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS THREATS.

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam). But the Constitution does not permit conviction for speech merely because a listener feels threatened. *Id.* at 708. To avoid criminalizing speech that is protected by the First Amendment, the government must prove that the speaker subjectively intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Id.*

This Court has long held that the First Amendment requires proof of a culpable mental state before speech may be punished. *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931) (holding that a statute banning red flags as “emblems of opposition to organized government” was unconstitutionally vague and overbroad); *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242, 258 (1937) (holding that a statute criminalizing membership in an organization advocating violent overthrow of the government was unconstitutionally vague).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard—under which a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat—*United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991)—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), this Court cautioned that speech may not be punished merely because it stirs people to anger or invites dispute. The Third Circuit’s standard would permit conviction for speech that a reasonable listener might misinterpret as threatening, even if the speaker did not intend it as such. That is precisely the danger the First Amendment was meant to avoid. *Id.*

This Court has repeatedly required proof of a defendant’s subjective intent when speech is at issue. In *Garrison v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 64, 74 (1964), the Court held that the First Amendment bars punishment for criticism of public officials unless the speaker acted with “actual malice.” Similarly, in *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 279–80 (1964), the Court required proof that the speaker knew his statements were false or acted with reckless disregard of their truth or falsity. These cases reflect a fundamental principle: the First Amendment demands that the government prove a culpable mental state before it may punish speech. *Id.*

The Third Circuit’s objective standard flips this principle on its head. It permits conviction for speech that a reasonable person might find threatening, regardless of the speaker’s intent. That is not the law. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707. The First Amendment requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Id.*

II. VIRGINIA v. BLACK DOES NOT ALTER THIS ANALYSIS.

The Third Circuit relied on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), to uphold Elonis’s conviction. But *Black* does not support the Third Circuit’s objective standard. In *Black*, this Court held that a statute prohibiting cross burning could be constitutional if it required proof that the defendant intended his conduct to intimidate. *Id.* at 365. The Court did not address whether an objective standard would suffice. *Id.* at 365–66. Indeed, the Court expressly declined to decide whether the First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent in true-threat cases. *Id.* at 365 (“In this case we consider whether the Commonwealth of Virginia's statute banning cross burning with ‘an intent to intimidate a person or group of persons’ violates the First Amendment.”).

The Court’s decision in *Black* is consistent with its earlier precedents requiring proof of a culpable mental state. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 365. The Third Circuit’s objective standard, by contrast, permits conviction for speech that a reasonable person might find threatening, regardless of the speaker’s intent. That is not the law. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707.

III. THE GOVERNMENT’S PROPOSED STANDARD WOULD CHILL PROTECTED SPEECH.

The government’s objective standard would chill protected speech. If speakers may be punished for statements that a reasonable person might find threatening, they will hesitate to engage in robust debate, satire, or hyperbole. *Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4. This is particularly true in the context of online speech, where tone and context are easily misinterpreted. *Id.* The First Amendment does not permit such a chilling effect. *Id.*

This Court has long recognized the danger of punishing speech based on its tendency to cause fear. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that speech may not be punished unless it is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action. The government’s objective standard would permit punishment for speech that merely causes fear, even if it does not incite lawless action. That is not the law. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The First Amendment requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. The Third Circuit’s objective standard is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. The Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with the First Amendment.
