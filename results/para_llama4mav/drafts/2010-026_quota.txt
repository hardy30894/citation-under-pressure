The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard for Exemption 2 is unfounded and contradicts the statutory language and Supreme Court precedent.

Exemption 2 shields matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this exemption narrowly, emphasizing that it applies to information that is not of "genuine and significant public interest." Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 363 (1976). The Court has also highlighted the importance of the risk of circumvention of agency regulation in determining whether information falls within Exemption 2. Id. at 369.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on the D.C. Circuit's decision in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C.Cir.1981) (en banc), is misplaced. While Crooker formulated the "predominantly internal" standard, it did so in the context of law enforcement materials, which are not at issue here. Moreover, the Ninth Circuit's own precedent in Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir.1980), does not support the adoption of this standard. Hardy held that "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2." Id. at 656. This does not imply that all "predominantly internal" materials are exempt.

The ESQD arc maps at issue are not "related solely" to internal personnel rules and practices. They are used to design and construct ammunition storage facilities and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety. As such, they are of significant public interest, particularly given the potential risks associated with the storage of explosives near populated areas. The Navy's own actions, including its decision to release similar documents related to the Bangor base, suggest that the ESQD arc maps are not sensitive information that requires protection.

Furthermore, Exemption 7(F), which protects information compiled for law enforcement purposes that could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual, is not applicable here. The ESQD arc maps were not compiled for law enforcement purposes, as required by Exemption 7. See John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146, 156 (1989); Church of Scientology v. United States Department of the Army, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979).

The Navy's concerns about national security are not supported by the fact that the ESQD arc maps have not been classified. Exemption 1 of FOIA specifically exempts classified matters "kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). If the disclosure of the ESQD arc maps is as dangerous as the Navy claims, it is acting irresponsibly by not classifying them. See U.S. Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (emphasizing that FOIA exemptions are "explicitly exclusive").

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt the ESQD arc maps from disclosure under Exemption 2 is unfounded and contradicts the statutory language and Supreme Court precedent. The documents are not "related solely" to internal personnel rules and practices, and their disclosure does not present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation. The Navy's concerns about national security are not supported by the lack of classification, and Exemption 7(F) is not applicable. Therefore, the ESQD arc maps should be disclosed under FOIA.
