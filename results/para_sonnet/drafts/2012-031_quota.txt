# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Properly Applied Rule 23(b)(3)'s Predominance Requirement Through the "Rigorous Analysis" This Court and the Third Circuit Require.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b)(3) demands that "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." This Court has confirmed that district courts must undertake a "rigorous analysis" of whether the prerequisites of Rule 23 are satisfied, and that this analysis will "frequently" entail "some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim." *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551, 2552 n.6 (2011). Critically, however, *Wal-Mart* did not transform certification into a full merits adjudication; it merely confirmed that courts may examine evidence bearing on both class requirements and merits where the two are intertwined. *See id.*

The District Court did precisely what *Wal-Mart* and *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), require. Over a four-day evidentiary hearing, the court examined thirty-two expert reports, live testimony, and voluminous deposition evidence before concluding that Respondents had shown "by a preponderance of the evidence" that antitrust impact and damages methodology were susceptible to class-wide proof. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). This is the exact evidentiary showing *Hydrogen Peroxide* demands: "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations," which "may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits." 552 F.3d at 317 (quoting *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 166, 168 (3d Cir. 2001)). Nothing in *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156 (1974), forbids this approach; *Eisen* merely prohibits "a merits inquiry for any other pretrial purpose." *Wal-Mart*, 131 S. Ct. at 2552 n.6.

## II. Antitrust Law Has Never Required Mathematical Certainty in Damages, and a Common Methodology for Calculating Class-Wide Damages Satisfies Rule 23(b)(3).

This Court has long recognized that antitrust plaintiffs need not prove damages with precision. In *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931), the Court held that although "the damages may not be determined by mere speculation or guess," a plaintiff need only furnish a basis for a "reasonable estimate" of harm once the fact of injury is established. *Id.* at 563. That principle was reaffirmed in *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100 (1969), where the Court explained that antitrust damages are inherently difficult to ascertain with exactness and that this difficulty does not bar recovery, so long as a jury can arrive at an estimate that is not "the product of mere speculation." *Id.* at 123.

Dr. McClave's regression-based damages model, credited by the District Court as a "common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis," *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154, satisfies this standard. The model isolated Comcast's alleged anticompetitive clustering—the deterrence of overbuilder entry—as a common cause of supracompetitive prices paid by all class members. That the ultimate damages figure will be calculated collectively rather than household-by-household does not defeat predominance; it is a permissible byproduct of the common conduct alleged. Requiring perfect precision at the certification stage would impose a burden even greater than that borne by antitrust plaintiffs at trial under *Story Parchment* and *Zenith Radio*.

## III. The District Court's Factual Findings Warrant Deference Under the Clearly Erroneous Standard.

Class certification orders are reviewed for abuse of discretion, and underlying factual findings are reviewed for clear error. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312. That standard is demanding. A finding of fact is clearly erroneous only when the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The Third Circuit may reverse only where the District Court's determination "is completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility" or "bears no rational relationship to the supportive evidentiary data." *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972).

This Court's decision in *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), forecloses the relief Comcast seeks. There the Court explained that appellate courts must not "reverse the finding of the trier of fact simply because it is convinced that it would have decided the case differently," and that "[w]here there are two permissible views of the evidence, the factfinder's choice between them cannot be clearly erroneous." *Id.* at 573–74. The District Court here weighed competing expert testimony across a four-day hearing and reached a plausible account of the record. Under *Anderson*, *Gypsum*, and *Krasnov*, that finding must stand even if this Court, sitting as fact-finder, might have weighed the evidence differently.

## CONCLUSION

The District Court conducted the rigorous, evidence-based analysis that *Wal-Mart*, *Hydrogen Peroxide*, and *Eisen* require, and its findings on predominance are entitled to deference under *Anderson*, *Gypsum*, and *Krasnov*. Because a common methodology for calculating class-wide damages satisfies the standards of *Story Parchment* and *Zenith Radio*, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
