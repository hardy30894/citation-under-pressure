**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARDING APPLE DAMAGES BASED ON SAMSUNG’S ENTIRE PROFITS WHERE THE INFRINGED DESIGN PATENTS COVER ONLY A MINOR COMPONENT OF THE ACCUSED PRODUCTS.**

A. **Under § 289 of the Patent Act, Damages Must Be Limited to the Portion of Profits Attributable to the Infringed Design.**

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that a design patent owner is entitled to “the total profit” made from the infringing article of manufacture. 35 U.S.C. § 289. However, this Court has long held that the “article of manufacture” to which damages are tied is not the entire product sold to consumers, but rather the component that incorporates the patented design. *Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co.*, 786 F.3d 983, 990 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (en banc) (citing *Dolan v. Detroit, Toledo & Ironton R.R. Co.*, 126 F. 625, 626 (6th Cir. 1903)). Thus, damages under § 289 are limited to the profits attributable to the infringed design, not the entire product. *Id.* at 991.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co.*, 839 F.3d 103 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (en banc), where the Court held that the “article of manufacture” is the component that embodies the patented design, not the entire smartphone. *Id.* at 108. The Court explicitly rejected the argument that the entire product’s profits could be awarded where the design patent covered only a minor component. *Id.* at 109.

B. **The District Court’s Award of Total Profits Violates the Statutory and Constitutional Limits on Patent Damages.**

The Supreme Court has long held that patent damages must be “reasonable” and tied to the value of the invention, not the entire product. *General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp.*, 461 U.S. 648, 654 (1983). The Federal Circuit has applied this principle in design patent cases, requiring apportionment where the patented design is only a component of the accused product. *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 990-91.

Here, the infringed design patents cover only specific design elements of the iPhone, such as the front face and graphical user interface. These elements are minor components of Samsung’s smartphones, which include numerous other features unrelated to the patented designs. The district court’s award of total profits—without apportionment—violates the statutory and constitutional limits on patent damages.

C. **The Federal Circuit’s Precedent Requires Apportionment in Design Patent Cases.**

In *Apple*, the Federal Circuit held that damages under § 289 must be limited to the profits attributable to the infringed design, not the entire product. *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 990-91. The Court rejected the argument that the entire product’s profits could be awarded where the design patent covered only a minor component. *Id.* at 109.

Similarly, in *Dolan*, the Sixth Circuit held that damages for design patent infringement must be limited to the profits attributable to the infringed design, not the entire product. *Dolan*, 126 F. at 626. The Court reasoned that damages must be tied to the value of the invention, not the entire product. *Id.*

D. **The District Court’s Award of Total Profits Is Unsupported by the Evidence.**

The district court’s award of total profits was based on the erroneous legal premise that § 289 permits recovery of the entire product’s profits where the design patent covers only a minor component. However, the evidence shows that the infringed design patents cover only minor components of Samsung’s smartphones, such as the front face and graphical user interface. The district court’s award of total profits—without apportionment—is unsupported by the evidence and violates the statutory and constitutional limits on patent damages.

II. **THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY ON THE REQUIREMENT OF APPORTIONMENT.**

A. **The District Court’s Failure to Instruct the Jury on Apportionment Violated Samsung’s Right to a Fair Trial.**

The Seventh Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in patent cases, and this right includes the right to have the jury instructed on the correct legal standard. *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. 370, 376 (1996). Here, the district court failed to instruct the jury on the requirement of apportionment under § 289, depriving Samsung of its right to a fair trial.

B. **The Federal Circuit’s Precedent Requires Jury Instructions on Apportionment in Design Patent Cases.**

In *Apple*, the Federal Circuit held that damages under § 289 must be limited to the profits attributable to the infringed design, not the entire product. *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 990-91. The Court reasoned that the jury must be instructed on the requirement of apportionment to ensure that damages are tied to the value of the invention, not the entire product. *Id.* at 991.

Similarly, in *Dolan*, the Sixth Circuit held that damages for design patent infringement must be limited to the profits attributable to the infringed design, not the entire product. *Dolan*, 126 F. at 626. The Court reasoned that the jury must be instructed on the requirement of apportionment to ensure that damages are tied to the value of the invention, not the entire product. *Id.*

C. **The District Court’s Failure to Instruct the Jury on Apportionment Requires Reversal.**

The district court’s failure to instruct the jury on the requirement of apportionment violated Samsung’s right to a fair trial and requires reversal. The Federal Circuit’s precedent requires jury instructions on apportionment in design patent cases, and the district court’s failure to provide such instructions was reversible error. *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 990-91; *Dolan*, 126 F. at 626.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Samsung respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for a new trial on damages, with instructions that damages be limited to the portion of Samsung’s profits attributable to the infringed design patents.
