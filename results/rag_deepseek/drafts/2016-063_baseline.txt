**ARGUMENT**

The MSPB’s dismissal of Mr. Perry’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction was a decision disposing of a “mixed case,” and thus, under *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), judicial review lies exclusively in federal district court, not the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

**I. The Plain Text of the CSRA Directs Mixed Cases to District Court, Regardless of the Basis for the Board’s Dismissal**

The Civil Service Reform Act defines a “mixed case” as one in which the employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” enumerated federal laws. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). When the Board “decides” such a case, “the employee may obtain judicial review … in a district court.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 42-43.

In *Kloeckner*, this Court held that a “decision” includes a dismissal on procedural grounds. *Id.* at 47. The Court reasoned that the statute “makes no distinction between decisions on the merits and decisions on procedural grounds.” *Id.* Neither does it distinguish between decisions on procedural grounds and decisions on jurisdictional grounds. The statute speaks broadly of the Board “deciding” the case. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(3). A dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is a “decision” no less than a dismissal for untimeliness. Because the Board “decided” Mr. Perry’s mixed case, review must occur in district court.

**II. *Kloeckner* Displaces the D.C. Circuit’s Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals**

The D.C. Circuit below attempted to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that it addressed only procedural dismissals, whereas this case involves a jurisdictional dismissal. That distinction collapses under the logic of *Kloeckner*.

First, the *Kloeckner* Court expressly rejected the argument that the forum for review depends on whether the Board reached the merits. *Id.* at 50-51. The same reasoning compels the conclusion that the forum for review also cannot depend on *why* the Board failed to reach the merits. As the Court explained, “the text of § 7702(a)(3) says nothing about either type of dismissal.” *Id.* at 50. The word “decides” encompasses all dismissals.

Second, the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Dep’t of Def.*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced because *Powell* cannot survive *Kloeckner*. In *Powell*, the court held that a jurisdictional dismissal is not a mixed case because the employee was not “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Board.” *Id.* at 599. But *Kloeckner* made clear that an employee *has* been affected by an appealable action if the action—here, removal and suspension—is of the type that could be appealed to the Board. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 44. The fact that the Board later determined that the employee’s *particular* case was not appealable does not retroactively strip the action of its appealable character. As the *Kloeckner* Court noted, “the employee’s action must be appealable when measured against the statute’s definition of covered actions—not later, after the Board has dismissed the appeal.” *Id.* at 45.

Third, the D.C. Circuit’s concern that the Board cannot waive jurisdictional requirements but can waive procedural ones is irrelevant. The statute focuses on the nature of the *action*, not the Board’s flexibility in adjudicating it. An action like removal is appealable regardless of whether the employee later agrees to it. The Board’s jurisdiction over voluntary actions is a merits inquiry, not a true jurisdictional question. *See Elgin v. Dep’t of the Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1, 15 (2012) (noting that the Board’s jurisdiction is defined by the type of action, not the employee’s consent). If the Board erred in finding Mr. Perry’s retirement voluntary, that error pertains to the merits of his discrimination claim, which belongs in district court.

**III. The Purpose of the CSRA Favors District Court Review for Jurisdictional Dismissals in Mixed Cases**

The CSRA’s framework ensures that discrimination claims are decided by district courts, which have expertise in such matters, rather than the Federal Circuit. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50. That purpose is defeated if jurisdictional dismissals shunt discrimination claims to the Federal Circuit, which rarely hears such claims. Mr. Perry’s case illustrates the problem: He alleges that his settlement was coerced due to race, age, and disability discrimination. Those claims require factual development and legal analysis best suited for district court. A jurisdictional ruling by the Board should not deprive him of that forum.

Moreover, requiring review in district court promotes judicial efficiency by avoiding the “complicated and needless” litigation over which court has jurisdiction. *Id.* at 49. The D.C. Circuit’s approach forces employees to guess whether the Board’s eventual ruling will be treated as jurisdictional or procedural, leading to wasteful transfers like this one.

**IV. The Federal Circuit Lacks Exclusive Jurisdiction Over This Appeal**

The general rule that Board decisions are reviewed in the Federal Circuit, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), yields to the specific rule for mixed cases, *id.* § 7703(b)(2). *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 45. Because Mr. Perry brought a mixed case, the Federal Circuit has no jurisdiction. *United States Postal Serv. v. Gregory*, 534 U.S. 1, 6-7 (2001) (reaffirming that the Board’s jurisdiction is statutory and must be strictly construed). The case should be transferred to the appropriate district court.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that MSPB decisions dismissing mixed cases on jurisdictional grounds are reviewable in federal district court, not the Federal Circuit.
