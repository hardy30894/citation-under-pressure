## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Holding That Bryant Lacks Standing to Enforce the Arbitration Award

Bryant, as the intended beneficiary of the collective bargaining agreement’s grievance and arbitration provisions, has standing to enforce the arbitration award in his favor. A union member may sue to enforce an arbitration award when the union has breached its duty of fair representation or when the member is a third-party beneficiary of the agreement. *See Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 186 (1967) (permitting an employee to sue where the union’s conduct is arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith). Here, the union actively assisted Bryant by filing the grievance and obtaining the award, yet Bell Atlantic refused to comply. The award is a contractual obligation that Bryant may enforce directly. *See United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 596 (1960) (courts enforce arbitration awards as part of the collective bargaining agreement). The district court’s contrary holding ignores Bryant’s status as an intended beneficiary and the union’s failure to bring its own enforcement action. Bryant has standing.

### II. The District Court Misapplied the Burden-Shifting Framework for Title VII and Section 1981 Claims

The district court failed to apply the Supreme Court’s clarification in *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000), that a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with evidence that the employer’s proffered reason is pretextual, is sufficient to survive summary judgment. Under *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973), and *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253–54 (1981), Bryant established a prima facie case of discrimination based on race, color, and gender by showing he is a member of a protected class, was qualified, suffered an adverse employment action, and was treated less favorably than similarly situated employees outside his class. Bell Atlantic asserted that Bryant was terminated for insubordination—failing to work assigned overtime. Bryant presented evidence that the articulated reason was pretextual: (1) Bell Atlantic refused the union’s offer to have co-workers perform Bryant’s overtime; (2) Bell Atlantic’s proposed accommodations were impracticable given Bryant’s child-care responsibilities; (3) no other employee was disciplined for refusing overtime due to child-care conflicts; and (4) the company’s policy was applied inflexibly despite its discretion to accommodate. Under *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 148, “a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer’s asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated.” The district court erroneously required Bryant to produce additional direct evidence of discrimination, thereby misapplying the burden-shifting framework.

### III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment

The record contains multiple genuine disputes of material fact that must be resolved by a jury. First, whether Bell Atlantic’s proffered reason for termination—insubordination—was pretextual is disputed. Bryant adduced evidence that the company refused a reasonable accommodation offered by the union, that it imposed discipline inconsistently, and that the policy was enforced in a manner that had a disparate impact on employees with child-care responsibilities. *See Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 151 (pretext may be inferred from inconsistencies in the employer’s explanation). Second, the district court ignored evidence that Bryant’s gender (male, single parent) and race were motivating factors. Bryant testified that other employees, particularly white and female employees, were accommodated when they faced similar conflicts. Such comparative evidence creates a factual dispute. *See Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 255–56 (plaintiff may show that employer treated similarly situated employees more favorably). Third, the district court failed to consider that the arbitration award in Bryant’s favor—finding that his termination violated the collective bargaining agreement—constitutes evidence that Bell Atlantic’s actions were not legitimate. An arbitration decision that the employer lacked just cause for discharge is relevant to the pretext inquiry. *See Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co.*, 415 U.S. 36, 60 (1974) (arbitration decision may be considered as evidence in Title VII suit). These factual disputes—whether the employer’s reason was false, whether other employees were treated differently, and whether the arbitration award undermines the employer’s explanation—are all material and require trial.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s summary judgment and remand for further proceedings on Bryant’s claims to enforce the arbitration award and for discrimination and retaliation under Title VII and Section 1981.
