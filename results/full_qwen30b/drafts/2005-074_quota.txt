**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B), DOES NOT APPLY RETROACTIVELY TO DEPORTATION ORDERS ISSUED BEFORE ITS ENACTMENT, AND ITS APPLICATION TO FERNANDEZ-VARGAS IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE.

The question presented is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), intended for Section 241(a)(5)—now codified as 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)(B)—to apply to aliens who re-entered the United States *before* the statute’s effective date in 1997. The Tenth Circuit held that it does, and that this application is not impermissibly retroactive. That conclusion is contrary to settled principles of constitutional law and this Court’s precedent, which prohibit retroactive application of statutes that impair settled expectations and impose new legal burdens without fair notice.

The core of the retroactivity inquiry is whether the statute “alters the legal consequences of past conduct.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994). When a statute “changes the rules of decision,” it operates retroactively and must be scrutinized under the *Landgraf* framework. Here, the reinstatement statute does precisely that: it strips an alien of the right to seek relief—such as adjustment of status—that was available at the time of the prior deportation and re-entry. The statute was not in effect when Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the U.S. in 1982, nor when he lived in the country for nearly two decades. During that time, he reasonably believed he could seek relief under the INA, including through marriage to a U.S. citizen. The law did not then prohibit such relief for re-entrants with prior removal orders. To now apply the statute to him is to impose a new penalty for conduct that was lawful at the time.

This Court has long held that “a person may not be punished for an act which, at the time it was committed, was not a crime.” *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 440 (1965). While the reinstatement statute is not a criminal penalty, it imposes a *legal consequence*—deportation without any form of relief—that was not available when the conduct occurred. The alien’s re-entry in 1982 was not a violation of a law that then barred all relief. The INA at that time permitted adjustment of status for certain aliens, including those who had been previously removed, provided they met the statutory requirements. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1982)*. The fact that Congress later eliminated that right for re-entrants with prior orders does not justify applying the new rule to conduct that occurred before the rule existed.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537 (1982), is misplaced. In *Johnson*, the Court upheld the retroactive application of a sentencing enhancement because the defendant had been convicted under a statute that did not then contain the enhancement. But the Court emphasized that the defendant had “no legitimate claim of entitlement” to a particular sentence. *Id.* at 550. Here, Fernandez-Vargas *did* have a legitimate claim of entitlement: he had lived in the U.S. for nearly 20 years, married a U.S. citizen, and applied for adjustment of status under a regime that permitted such relief. The Tenth Circuit’s assertion that he had “no protectable expectation” of adjustment is factually and legally incorrect. *See* *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 445 F.3d 1258, 1262 (10th Cir. 2006). The expectation was not merely speculative—it was grounded in the law as it existed at the time of his re-entry.

This Court has repeatedly held that “a statute that alters the legal consequences of past conduct is retroactive.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. The reinstatement statute does so by denying relief that was available when the re-entry occurred. *See* *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. at 440; *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433, 440 (1922) (a statute that “changes the legal consequences of past conduct” is retroactive). The Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding undermines the principle that “no one should be deprived of a right which the law has given him.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 343 U.S. 128, 131 (1952).

Moreover, the statute’s application here violates the principle of *fair notice*. An alien cannot be expected to anticipate that a law enacted in 1997 would strip him of a right he reasonably believed he possessed. *See* *United States v. Celestine*, 526 U.S. 102, 108 (1999) (a statute that “imposes new obligations” without notice is impermissibly retroactive). The fact that the statute was enacted after Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the U.S. and lived here for decades makes the application particularly harsh. He relied on the law as it was, and Congress cannot now punish him for that reliance.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), is also inapposite. In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that a statute eliminating discretionary relief for certain aliens could not be applied retroactively because it “deprived aliens of a right they had acquired under the law.” *Id.* at 315. The Court emphasized that “the right to seek discretionary relief is not a mere privilege, but a matter of substantial importance to the alien.” *Id.* at 316. Fernandez-Vargas had such a right when he applied for adjustment of status. The reinstatement statute extinguished it retroactively.
