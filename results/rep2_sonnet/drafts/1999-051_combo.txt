# ARGUMENT

## I. A Procedural Default Attributable to Counsel's Constitutionally Deficient Performance Excuses a Habeas Petitioner's Default, Regardless of the Procedural Posture of the Ineffective-Assistance Claim Itself.

This Court's habeas jurisprudence has long distinguished between defaults that are attributable to a petitioner's own considered choice and those imposed on him by forces beyond his control. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that a state prisoner forfeits federal review of a constitutional claim only where he has made "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege"—what the Court termed "deliberate by-passing of state procedures." *Id.* at 439. The default bars federal review "only" when it "result[s] from the deliberate choice of the petitioner himself." *Id.* Carpenter made no such choice. He is not a lawyer; the decision not to raise the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal was made by counsel appointed to represent him, without consultation or authorization. Under *Fay*, that is precisely the kind of default that federal habeas review must reach.

This principle rests on a broader premise that has guided this Court since *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938): waiver of a constitutional right must be "an intelligent and competent waiver by the accused," and courts must "indulge every reasonable presumption against" finding such a waiver. *Id.* at 464, 468. Carpenter's appellate attorney did not knowingly and intelligently relinquish the sufficiency claim after weighing its merits; he failed to raise it because he rendered constitutionally deficient assistance. A lawyer's oversight is not the client's informed choice, and it cannot be treated as one consistent with *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), where the Court insisted that waiver of fundamental rights be established by "clear and convincing evidence" of a defendant's own considered decision. *Id.* at 723-24 (plurality opinion).

## II. Denying Excuse for the Default Would Gut the Right to Effective Appellate Counsel That This Court Guaranteed in *Douglas v. California*.

The petitioner's rule—that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim can never serve as cause unless that claim has independently survived state procedural bars—would strip the right to effective appellate counsel of practical meaning. This Court held in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), that a State that provides a right of appeal must furnish indigent defendants with counsel to give that appeal substance, because "the type of an appeal ... presents a situation in which an indigent, in effect, is denied ... the full appellate review that non-indigent defendants have." *Id.* at 358. That guarantee, and the parallel guarantee of trial counsel recognized in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), would be illusory if a State could insulate an appellate lawyer's constitutional failure from federal review simply by imposing a second procedural bar on the very claim that seeks to vindicate the underlying right. Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim is not incidental to his defaulted sufficiency claim—it is the constitutional injury that produced the default. To require him to have separately, and successfully, litigated that injury through every layer of state procedure before it may excuse the default it caused is to convert *Douglas*'s promise of effective appellate advocacy into a procedural trap.

## III. This Court's Traditional Approach to Habeas Corpus Looks to the Substance of the Constitutional Violation, Not the Procedural Regularity With Which It Was Presented.

The office of the writ of habeas corpus, as this Court explained in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), is to test whether a prisoner is held "in violation of the Constitution," and federal courts sitting in habeas have "the power ... to look behind" state procedural determinations "when the facts warrant." *Id.* at 458, 463 (opinion of Reed, J.). Consistent with that principle, the Court in *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), reaffirmed that the federal habeas court's duty to independently examine a constitutional claim exists precisely because state-court procedural rulings are not always an adequate proxy for whether the underlying federal right was honored. *Id.* at 312. Similarly, in *House v. Mayo*, 324 U.S. 42 (1945), the Court refused to permit a state procedural technicality to foreclose consideration of a federal constitutional claim where the prisoner had not been given a fair opportunity to have the claim heard. *Id.* at 44-45. And in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), the Court made clear that the exhaustion requirement exists to give state courts a fair opportunity to correct constitutional errors—not to erect an absolute bar divorced from the reality of how the error arose. *Id.* at 116-17.

These cases establish that procedural regularity is a means to the end of vindicating federal constitutional rights, not an end in itself. Carpenter's counsel's failure to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence was itself a violation of the Sixth Amendment. That violation is the "cause" for the later default, full stop. Nothing in this Court's precedent requires that the cause itself first survive an independent procedural gauntlet before it may be recognized as cause. To hold otherwise would multiply procedural bars without any corresponding assurance that the underlying constitutional violation was ever fairly addressed—precisely the result *Fay*, *Brown*, and *House* sought to avoid.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit.
