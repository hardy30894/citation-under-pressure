ARGUMENT

Punitive damages under Title VII require proof that the employer’s conduct was egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind. This Court has long held that exemplary damages are “never to be allowed, unless there is misconduct beyond that which is implied by the act itself.” Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875). The remedy is “confined to cases where the act complained of has been characterized by some peculiar circumstance of aggravation.” Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858). It is awarded only “where the injury has been wanton, or where the negligence has been gross.” Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893).

These standards trace to the common-law rule that punitive damages punish “a wrongdoer for some act of an aggravated character.” Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851). The aggravating element must appear in the conduct itself; a bare finding of intent or recklessness does not suffice. See The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 558 (1818) (exemplary damages reserved for “gross and wanton outrage”). Because Title VII’s punitive-damages provision incorporates this common-law tradition, an award cannot rest on a showing of malice or reckless indifference alone. The employer’s actions must independently exhibit the “peculiar circumstance of aggravation” that has always been required. Quigley, 62 U.S. at 214.

Kolstad’s evidence, even if credited, demonstrates at most ordinary intentional discrimination. It supplies no proof of the additional aggravating conduct demanded by the governing precedents. The district court therefore correctly refused to submit the punitive-damages claim to the jury.
