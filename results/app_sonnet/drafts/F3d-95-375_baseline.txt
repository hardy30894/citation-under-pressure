**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Court Has Jurisdiction Notwithstanding the Timing of the Notice of Appeal.**

Appellants filed their notice of appeal on October 19, 1994, after the district court's summary judgment order resolved the claims of all Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs, but before the district court entered its final order disposing of the Bozé Plaintiffs' claims on March 1, 1995. This sequence does not deprive the Court of jurisdiction. In *FirsTier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co.*, 498 U.S. 269 (1991), the Supreme Court held that "a notice of appeal filed after the announcement of a decision or order — but before the entry of the judgment or order — is treated as filed on the date of and after the entry." *Id.* at 276. Because the district court's summary judgment ruling left nothing further to decide as to Appellants, and the subsequent final order merely disposed of separate, unrelated claims by the Bozé Plaintiffs, Appellants' notice of appeal ripened upon entry of the March 1, 1995 order. No new notice was required.

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Striking the Testimony of Four Expert Witnesses as a Discovery Sanction.**

Dismissal-type sanctions that deprive a party of the ability to prove its case are reserved for the most flagrant cases. In *National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc.*, 427 U.S. 639 (1976), the Supreme Court cautioned that such sanctions must be evaluated against the full record and are appropriate only where the conduct is characterized by "flagrant bad faith" and "callous disregard" of responsibilities. *Id.* at 643. This Court has likewise required that before imposing sanctions tantamount to dismissal, a court must find that the violation was willful and that lesser sanctions would not serve the interests of justice. *Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs., Inc.*, 765 F.2d 511, 514 (5th Cir. 1985).

Here, the record does not establish the requisite bad faith. Plaintiffs' counsel moved—albeit belatedly—for a protective order asserting that their experts had not yet formulated opinions, a substantive concern the district court did not squarely address before ordering depositions completed by July 30, 1993. While noncompliance with that second deadline was itself improper, striking the testimony of four experts outright, without considering intermediate remedies such as cost-shifting, a further short extension, or in camera review of the experts' preparedness, was disproportionate. The district court's warning that experts would be struck if they "have not had enough time to examine all the documents," (Vol. 16, June 3, 1993, Tr. at 31-32), reflects precisely the kind of preparation concern that a continuance, not exclusion, is designed to cure. Because the district court failed to consider lesser sanctions on this record, the exclusion order should be vacated.

**III. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under Daubert.**

In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), the Supreme Court charged trial courts with a "gatekeeping" function to ensure that expert testimony is both relevant and reliable, assessing factors such as testability, peer review, error rates, and general acceptance. *Id.* at 592-94. Daubert's inquiry, however, is "flexible," and its focus "must be solely on principles and methodology, not on the conclusions that they generate." *Id.* at 594-95. This Court has applied that standard in toxic tort cases involving causation testimony, recognizing that experts may rely on epidemiological and toxicological literature, differential diagnosis, and clinical experience to support causation opinions. *Christophersen v. Allied-Signal Corp.*, 939 F.2d 1106, 1109-10 (5th Cir. 1991) (en banc).

The district court's wholesale exclusion of Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's testimony did not adequately credit the methodological bases underlying their opinions regarding chemical exposure and resulting injury. To the extent the district court's ruling rested on disagreement with their conclusions rather than the soundness of their methodology, it exceeded the proper Daubert inquiry. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 595. At minimum, the district court should have conducted a more searching examination of the specific methodologies employed rather than excluding the witnesses in blanket fashion following the discovery dispute.

**IV. Summary Judgment Was Improperly Granted.**

Summary judgment is appropriate only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and... the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law." Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). In ruling on such a motion, the court must view the evidence and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party, and may not weigh the evidence or determine its truth. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Because the district court's summary judgment ruling rested entirely on the erroneous exclusion of Appellants' expert testimony described above, the predicate for summary judgment collapses once that testimony is restored. With competent expert evidence of exposure and causation properly before the court, genuine issues of material fact remain as to both personal injury and property damage claims, precluding judgment as a matter of law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's orders striking Appellants' expert testimony, excluding Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson under *Daubert*, and granting summary judgment, and remand for further proceedings.
