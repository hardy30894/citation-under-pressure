The plain language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's use of the word "shall" indicates a clear congressional intent to limit the authority of the transferee court to pretrial proceedings. See Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 343 (1960) (emphasizing the importance of statutory language in determining the scope of a district court's authority).

Despite this clear statutory directive, the District of Arizona invoked 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial. However, § 1404(a) does not override the specific mandate of § 1407(a). The two statutes serve distinct purposes: § 1407(a) governs the transfer of cases for pretrial proceedings, while § 1404(a) allows for the transfer of cases for trial. See Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 634-636 (1964) (discussing the legislative history and purpose of § 1404(a)).

The Ninth Circuit's decision affirming the District Court's order is erroneous because it ignores the plain language of § 1407(a) and relies on case law that has disregarded the statutory mandate. See, e.g., Pfizer v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir.1971). As Judge Kozinski noted in his dissent, the federal courts have "simply disregarded the statutory mandate" and justified their departure on grounds of policy or convenience. See Lexecon, Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach, 61 F.3d 911, 915 (9th Cir. 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting).

The Supreme Court has consistently emphasized the importance of adhering to statutory language and congressional intent. See, e.g., Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22, 31 (1988) (noting that "federal law, specifically 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), governs the District Court's decision whether to give effect to the parties' forum-selection clause"). In this case, the plain language of § 1407(a) requires that the case be remanded to the original district for trial.

Furthermore, allowing a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial would undermine the policy choice reflected in § 1407(a). Congress has determined that cases should be transferred to remote locations only for the duration of pretrial procedures, and that plaintiffs should retain their right to select the forum for trial. See Lexecon, Inc., 61 F.3d at 916 (Kozinski, J., dissenting).

In conclusion, the District Court's invocation of § 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial was erroneous, and the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of that decision should be reversed. The plain language of § 1407(a) and the policy choice reflected in that statute require that the case be remanded to the original district for trial.
