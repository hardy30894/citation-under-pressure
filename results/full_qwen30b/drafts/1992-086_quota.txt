**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from its after-hours facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech and equal protection. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its auditorium for a nonsectarian, family-oriented film series—conducted entirely during non-school hours, with no disruption to school operations, and open to the public—constitutes impermissible content-based discrimination in a limited public forum. The Second Circuit’s contrary conclusion, resting on a flawed interpretation of *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37 (1983), and an erroneous application of *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), must be reversed.

The school district’s facilities constitute a **limited public forum**. As the Supreme Court has held, “a public forum may be created by government policy or practice, even if not explicitly designated as such.” *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45. The District has permitted numerous non-religious groups to use its facilities for after-hours events—such as the Southern Harmonizers’ musical performance and the “Psychology and The Unknown” lecture series sponsored by the public library. These uses demonstrate that the District has opened its facilities to the public for expressive activity, thereby creating a limited public forum. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985) (recognizing that “a limited public forum” arises when government “opens a forum for public expression”).

Once a limited public forum is created, the government may not exclude speech based on its **content** or **viewpoint**. *See Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45; *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a film series on family values—featuring Dr. James Dobson, a nationally recognized expert on child rearing—was based solely on the religious *content* of the message. The film’s description explicitly states it “reminds parents of society’s slide toward humanism” and emphasizes “Christian values” as a counterbalance. Yet the District denied access not because the film was sectarian, but because it was *religious in content*. This is precisely the kind of content-based discrimination the First Amendment forbids. *See *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273 (“The University may not deny access to student groups on the basis of the religious content of their speech.”).

The District’s argument that no prior religious use occurred is irrelevant. The existence of a limited public forum does not depend on whether religious groups have previously used the space. Rather, it depends on whether the government has opened the forum to *similar* groups. *See *Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 247 (1990) (“The Equal Access Act requires that a school that opens its doors to noncurriculum-related student groups must allow religious groups equal access.”). In *Mergens*, the Court held that a high school’s policy of allowing student groups to meet during non-instructional time created a limited public forum, and that denying a Christian club access based on its religious content violated the Equal Access Act—and, by extension, the First Amendment. *Id.* at 247–48. The same logic applies here: the District has allowed secular groups to use its facilities for expressive purposes; it may not exclude religious groups solely because of the content of their speech.

The District’s reliance on *Deeper Life Fellowship v. Board of Education*, 938 F.2d 1475 (2d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. That case upheld a school district’s refusal to allow a religious group to use facilities where no prior non-religious groups had been permitted to use the space. *Id.* at 1480. But here, unlike in *Deeper Life*, the District has repeatedly allowed non-religious groups to use its facilities. The mere fact that the District has not allowed religious groups to use the space in the past does not justify a categorical exclusion when the forum is already open to others. *See *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46 (“The First Amendment does not require the government to open a forum for public expression, but if it does, it may not discriminate against particular viewpoints.”).

Moreover, the District’s refusal violates the **Equal Protection Clause**. The government may not treat similarly situated speakers differently based on the content of their speech. *See *Roberts v. United States Jaycees*, 468 U.S. 609, 622 (1984) (“The First Amendment protects the right to associate for the purpose of engaging in expressive activities.”). The District allows secular groups to use its facilities for educational and cultural events, yet denies access to a religious group presenting a similar message on a shared topic—family values. This differential treatment based on religious content is unconstitutional. *See *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (plurality opinion) (“The government may not deny access to a forum simply because the speech is religious.”).

Finally, the District’s policy cannot be justified under the **Establishment Clause**. The government may not suppress religious speech to avoid the appearance of endorsing religion. *See *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395 (“The Establishment Clause does not permit the government to suppress religious expression simply because it is religious.”). The District’s refusal to allow the film series is not a neutral regulation; it is a content-based exclusion that burdens religious expression while allowing secular speech. *See *Good News Club v. Milford Central Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 107 (2001) (“The government may not exclude religious groups from a public forum simply because of the religious nature of their message.”).
