The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows plaintiffs to recover punitive damages if they demonstrate that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The legislative history of the Act indicates that Congress intended to limit punitive damages to "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.).

The Courts of Appeals have long recognized that punitive damages require a higher level of culpability than mere intentional discrimination. In Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996), the Seventh Circuit held that an award of punitive damages under § 1981a must surmount a "higher hurdle" than mere proof of intentional discrimination. Similarly, in Kim v. Nash Finch Co., 123 F.3d 1046, 1063 (8th Cir. 1997), the Eighth Circuit stated that punitive damages are available only in cases where the defendant's conduct is "outrageous" or demonstrates "reckless or callous indifference" to the plaintiff's rights.

The Court of Appeals' decision in this case, however, suggests that punitive damages are available whenever a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination and the defendant acts with "reckless indifference" to the plaintiff's federally protected rights. Kolstad v. American Dental Ass'n, 108 F.3d 1431, 1438 (D.C. Cir. 1997). This interpretation is inconsistent with the legislative history and the case law from other circuits.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that punitive damages are intended to punish and deter egregious conduct. In Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983), the Court held that punitive damages are available under § 1983 when the defendant's conduct is "reckless or callous" or involves "evil motive or intent." Similarly, in Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 21-22 (1991), the Court emphasized that punitive damages must be "reasonably related to the reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct."

Other circuits have applied similar standards in the context of § 1981, which also allows for punitive damages. In Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy-Province of Detroit, Inc., 816 F.2d 1104, 1108-09 (6th Cir. 1987), the Sixth Circuit held that punitive damages are available under § 1981 when the defendant's conduct is "malicious, wanton, or oppressive." See also Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co., 817 F.2d 1290, 1295-96 (7th Cir. 1987) (punitive damages available under § 1981 when defendant's conduct is "egregious" or demonstrates "reckless or callous indifference").

The ADA's conduct in this case, while found to be discriminatory, does not rise to the level of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct required to sustain an award of punitive damages. The jury's finding of intentional discrimination is not sufficient, on its own, to support an award of punitive damages. See St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (proof of intentional discrimination is not sufficient to compel an award of punitive damages).

In conclusion, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. This interpretation is consistent with the legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 and the case law from other circuits. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973); Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994); Texas Dep't of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248 (1981).
