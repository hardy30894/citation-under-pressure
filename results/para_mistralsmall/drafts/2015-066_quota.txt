**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Applies to Tribal Court Convictions Used as Predicate Offenses in Federal Prosecutions Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right attaches at every “critical stage” of a criminal proceeding, *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 224 (1967), including trials and guilty plea proceedings, *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 30 (1972). The Supreme Court has made clear that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state prosecutions but extend to proceedings where the defendant faces potential imprisonment. *See Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344-45 (1963) (holding the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies to state felony prosecutions); *Argersinger*, 407 U.S. at 37 (extending the right to misdemeanor cases where imprisonment is imposed).

Tribal courts are not exempt from these constitutional guarantees when their convictions are used to establish predicate offenses in federal prosecutions. The Sixth Amendment’s reach is not circumscribed by the jurisdictional source of a prior conviction. *See Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967) (holding that a prior uncounseled conviction used to enhance punishment in a state prosecution violated the Sixth Amendment, even though the prior conviction occurred in another state). The Court in *Burgett* reasoned that the use of an uncounseled conviction as a predicate offense “stamps the conviction as at least presumptively suspect.” *Id.* at 115. This principle applies with equal force when the predicate conviction arises from a tribal court proceeding. *See Ant v. United States*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394-95 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that uncounseled tribal court convictions could not be used as predicate offenses in a federal prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because they violated the Sixth Amendment).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), correctly applied this precedent. The court recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state courts but extend to tribal courts when their convictions are used to establish elements of a federal offense. *Id.* at 677. The court further noted that the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) does not provide a right to counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, and thus tribal court convictions obtained without counsel do not satisfy constitutional due process requirements. *Id.* at 675-76.

II. **The Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause prohibits the use of unreliable convictions to establish elements of a federal offense. *See United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 447 (1972) (holding that a sentencing court may not rely on a prior uncounseled conviction to enhance a sentence because such convictions are presumptively unreliable). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the government may not use convictions obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment to enhance punishment. *See Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115; *Tucker*, 404 U.S. at 447.

The same principle applies when uncounseled tribal court convictions are used to establish predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that the reliability concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel are equally applicable when uncounseled convictions are used to prove an essential element of a federal offense. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 678-79 (citing *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), for the proposition that the government must prove each element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt). The court further noted that the use of uncounseled convictions to establish guilt conflicts with the heightened reliability requirements of the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution. *Id.* at 678.

III. **The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Does Not Conflict with *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994).**

The government argues that *Nichols* permits the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions. *Nichols*, however, involved the use of an uncounseled conviction to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, not to establish an essential element of a federal offense. *Id.* at 746-47. The Court in *Nichols* expressly distinguished between the guilt and sentencing phases of a criminal prosecution, noting that the government’s burden of proof is lower at sentencing. *Id.* at 748. The Court further acknowledged that sentencing courts may consider the reliability of prior convictions and may depart from the Sentencing Guidelines if a conviction is unreliable. *Id.* at 747-48.

The Ninth Circuit correctly distinguished *Nichols* in *Bryant*, recognizing that the use of uncounseled convictions to establish an essential element of a federal offense implicates different constitutional concerns than their use in sentencing. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 678-79. The court noted that the government must prove each element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt, and thus the reliability concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel are heightened during the guilt phase. *Id.* at 678 (citing *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364).

IV. **The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with the Supreme Court’s Precedent on Recidivist Statutes.**

The government’s reliance on *Nichols* is misplaced because *Nichols* did not address the use of prior convictions to establish elements of a federal offense. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that recidivist statutes penalize the defendant’s current offense, not the prior convictions themselves. *See Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747 (citing *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008)). The Court has also held that the government may not use prior convictions obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment to enhance punishment. *See Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115; *Tucker*, 404 U.S. at 447.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Bryant* is consistent with these principles. The court recognized that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions to establish predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) implicates the same constitutional concerns as the use of such convictions to enhance punishment. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 678-79. The court further noted that the government’s burden of proof is higher during the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution, and thus the reliability concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel are heightened. *Id.* at 678.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel and the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. The decision is consistent with the Supreme Court’s precedent on the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel, the reliability of prior convictions, and the use of recidivist statutes. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Ninth Circuit.
