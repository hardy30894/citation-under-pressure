The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. A state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel cannot constitute cause to excuse the default of an underlying constitutional claim.

Federal habeas review is unavailable where a prisoner has failed to present his federal claim to the state courts in the manner prescribed by state law. Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886). This rule rests on “the principles of comity and federalism.” Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950). It applies with full force to claims that appellate counsel rendered ineffective assistance. Because such a claim is itself a distinct constitutional contention, it must be presented to the state courts in accordance with state procedural rules. See Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953) (opinion of Frankfurter, J.) (“The state court must have been given ‘a fair opportunity’ to consider the federal question.”).

Where the prisoner has defaulted the ineffective-assistance claim, that default is not cured merely because the claim is offered as cause for another default. The prisoner must still demonstrate that some objective factor external to the defense, and not fairly attributable to him, prevented timely presentation of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. No such external impediment appears when the only asserted cause is the defaulted ineffective-assistance allegation. To hold otherwise would permit successive layers of default to evade the exhaustion requirement, undermining the rule that “the state courts should have the first opportunity to review” federal claims. Darr, 339 U.S. at 204.

The court of appeals’ contrary conclusion—that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may nevertheless serve as cause—contradicts these settled principles. It would allow a prisoner to obtain federal review of a defaulted sufficiency claim simply by asserting, without having timely presented to the state courts, that appellate counsel should have raised it. Such a result “would make the state adjudication a mere prologue to federal adjudication.” Brown, 344 U.S. at 487. The writ of habeas corpus was never intended to operate in that manner.

Accordingly, because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself procedurally defaulted, it cannot excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The writ should not have issued.
