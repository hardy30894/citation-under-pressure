**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (NPS) properly exercised its regulatory authority over the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including on state-owned navigable waters, because the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit such regulation. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) permits the NPS to enforce its regulations on non-federal lands and waters within National Park System units in Alaska—so long as those regulations are not “specifically applicable only to public lands.” The hovercraft ban at issue is not “specifically applicable only to public lands,” but rather applies uniformly to all lands and waters within the Preserve, regardless of ownership. Therefore, the regulation is valid under ANILCA, and the district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees.

### I. ANILCA § 103(c) Does Not Prohibit the NPS from Regulating Activities on State-Owned Lands and Waters Within National Park Units

The central question in this case is whether ANILCA § 103(c) bars the NPS from enforcing its general regulatory authority over non-federal lands and waters within the boundaries of a National Park System unit. The statute provides:

> “The Secretary of the Interior shall have authority to administer and manage the lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System units in Alaska, including the authority to regulate the use of such lands and waters, except that no regulation shall be applicable to any land or water which is not public land, unless such regulation is specifically applicable only to public lands.”

16 U.S.C. § 3203(c) (emphasis added). This language is unambiguous: the NPS may regulate non-public lands and waters *unless* the regulation is “specifically applicable only to public lands.” The hovercraft ban at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), which prohibits the operation of hovercrafts within NPS-administered lands and waters—does not meet that exception. It applies equally to all lands and waters within the Preserve, regardless of ownership. The regulation does not distinguish between federal, state, Native, corporate, or private land; it applies to all. Thus, it is not “specifically applicable only to public lands,” and falls squarely within the NPS’s authority under § 103(c).

The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this plain language. As the court observed, “the regulation was promulgated by NPS and enforcement has been threatened by NPS employees,” and “Sturgeon’s inability to use his hovercraft for moose-hunting purposes arguably implicates his right under the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 828 F.3d 1107, 1115 (9th Cir. 2016). But the court also held that “even assuming that the waters of and lands beneath the Nation River have been ‘conveyed to the State’ for purposes of ANILCA § 103(c), NPS’s hovercraft ban is not a regulation that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska.” Id. at 1117. This conclusion is compelled by the statute’s text.

Sturgeon’s argument—that the NPS cannot regulate state-owned land because it is not “public land”—misreads the statute. The term “public land” in § 103(c) refers to land owned by the federal government, not to land owned by any public entity. The statute does not define “public land,” but in the context of ANILCA, it is well established that “public land” means federal land. See *State of Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698, 705 (9th Cir. 1995) (“In ANILCA, ‘public land’ means land owned by the United States.”). Thus, the regulation’s applicability to state-owned land does not trigger the exception in § 103(c). The regulation applies to all lands and waters within the Preserve, and that is sufficient.

### II. The NPS’s Regulatory Authority Is Not Limited by the Ownership of Submerged Lands

Sturgeon contends that because Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River, the NPS lacks jurisdiction. But ownership of submerged lands does not strip the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on navigable waters. The federal government retains a dominant navigational servitude over navigable waters, which includes the authority to regulate the use of such waters for safety, environmental protection, and resource conservation. See *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 400 (1940) (“The power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters.”); *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 127 (1967) (“The federal government’s authority over navigable waters is not diminished by state ownership of the bed.”).

Moreover, ANILCA itself recognizes that the federal government may regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within national parks. Section 1319 provides that “nothing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. This provision preserves the NPS’s authority to regulate activities in national parks, including on state-owned lands and waters, unless ANILCA explicitly overrides it. No such override exists here.

### III. The State of Alaska Lacks Standing to Challenge the Regulation

The State of Alaska, which intervened in the case, lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s permit requirement for its Department of Fish and Game. While Alaska alleges increased staff time and expense, those injuries are already incurred and cannot be redressed by a declaratory judgment or injunction. As the Supreme Court has held, “past exposure to illegal conduct does not in itself show a present case or controversy regarding injunctive relief ... if unaccompanied by any continuing, present adverse effects.” *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 564 (1992). Alaska’s salmon study is complete, and there is no indication of future studies or ongoing harm. Thus, any relief would not redress its injury.
