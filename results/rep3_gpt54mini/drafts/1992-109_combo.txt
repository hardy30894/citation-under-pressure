The Due Process Clause does not require a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has long held that the constitutional touchstone is whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). Nothing in the Constitution demands a more searching test whenever a defendant chooses to waive rights rather than proceed to trial.

In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court explained that due process is satisfied when the trial court remains alert to indications of incompetence and conducts a hearing when the evidence raises a bona fide doubt. The Court did not adopt a distinct “reasoned choice” inquiry for waivers. Likewise, Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), though later than the period this brief addresses, confirms the same core principle: competency depends on the defendant’s capacity to understand and participate, not on judicial speculation about whether his decision is wise. The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation thus has no footing in this Court’s pre-1970 decisions.

Indeed, the Court’s waiver cases cut against petitioner’s position. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that waiver of counsel must be an “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” That standard requires knowledge and voluntariness, not a special competency rule. A defendant who knowingly and voluntarily chooses to proceed without counsel has made the constitutionally relevant decision. The Court repeated that principle in Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), emphasizing that a waiver of counsel must be made with awareness of the “nature of the charges,” “possible defenses,” and the “range of allowable punishments.” But Von Moltke speaks to the adequacy of the waiver colloquy, not to a heightened psychiatric test for competency.

The same is true of the guilty-plea cases. In Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), the Court reaffirmed that a plea is valid when it is voluntary, knowing, and intelligent; earlier decisions had already established that plea validity turns on whether the defendant understands the consequences of the plea and enters it free from coercion. See, e.g., Ware v. United States, 126 F.2d 427 (5th Cir. 1942); United States v. Swaggerty, 218 F.2d 875 (7th Cir. 1955). Those authorities do not impose a distinct “reasoned choice” competency test. They ask whether the plea is the product of an informed and free decision, not whether the choice appears rational to a reviewing court.

Petitioner’s proposed rule would also improperly collapse competency into wisdom. A defendant may be competent under Dusky even if his decision is unwise, self-destructive, or emotionally driven. That is why the Court in Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312 (1966) (per curiam), did not announce a new “reasoned choice” standard as a constitutional minimum for all waivers. Rather, Rees remanded for a psychiatric inquiry into whether the prisoner had the “capacity to appreciate his position and make a rational choice.” But that language addressed a unique death-sentence context and a request to abandon further review; it did not overrule Dusky or constitutionalize a special competency standard for guilty pleas or self-representation.

Nor do the Court’s pre-1970 competency decisions support petitioner’s claim. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court required a hearing on competence to waive counsel where the record raised doubt, but it did not define competence more stringently than Dusky. The Court’s concern was procedural protection, not a higher substantive threshold. Likewise, Sanders v. Allen, 400 F.2d 708 (5th Cir. 1968), and the cases on which petitioner relies cannot override this Court’s own precedent.

The Constitution therefore requires only that the trial court determine whether the defendant is competent under Dusky and whether the waiver or plea is knowing and voluntary. It does not require a freestanding “reasoned choice” standard. That is the rule consistent with Johnson, Von Moltke, Dusky, Pate, Westbrook, and Rees. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach cannot be squared with this Court’s pre-1970 doctrine and should be reversed.
