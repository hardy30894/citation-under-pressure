**ARGUMENT**

**I. Title VII’s Punitive-Damages Provision Requires Proof of “Malice” or “Reckless Indifference,” Not Independent “Egregiousness.”**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) governs punitive damages in Title VII cases. It authorizes such awards only where the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” Congress did not condition punitive relief on a separate showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct divorced from the employer’s state of mind. *See* § 1981a(b)(1). This Court has long held that statutory text is the “best evidence of [legislative] intent.” *United States v. Am. Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Where Congress has spoken clearly, courts may not engraft additional requirements onto the statute. *Id.* at 544.

This Court’s precedents confirm that punitive damages hinge on the defendant’s mental state, not the severity of its conduct. In *Lake Shore & Mich. S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), the Court upheld punitive damages where the defendant acted “wantonly, or oppressively, or with such malice as implies a spirit of mischief or criminal indifference to civil obligations.” The Court did not require proof that the defendant’s conduct was independently “egregious.” *Id.* Similarly, in *Day v. Woodworth*, 13 How. 363, 371 (1851), the Court recognized that punitive damages may be awarded for “gross negligence” or “wanton wrongs,” but emphasized that the focus remains on the defendant’s culpable state of mind. *Id.* These cases establish that punitive damages are not reserved for the most extreme factual scenarios; they attach where the defendant’s wrongdoing reflects a sufficiently blameworthy mental state.

**II. The Legislative History of the 1991 Civil Rights Act Does Not Impose an “Egregiousness” Requirement.**

The legislative history cited by petitioner does not alter the plain text of § 1981a(b)(1). The House Report stated that punitive damages under Title VII require “an even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 55 (1991). But this statement does not impose a freestanding “egregiousness” requirement. Instead, it clarifies that punitive damages are not available for mere negligence or good-faith mistakes. *See id.* This interpretation aligns with this Court’s longstanding rule that punitive damages are reserved for conduct evincing “malice” or “reckless indifference.” *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107; *Woodworth*, 13 How. at 371.

Moreover, the House Report’s reference to a “higher standard” does not contradict the statutory text. Congress expressly defined the standard as “malice or reckless indifference.” § 1981a(b)(1). The House Report’s commentary merely explains that this standard is more demanding than the threshold showing of intentional discrimination required for liability. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 55. It does not impose an additional, independent requirement of “egregious” conduct.

**III. Pre-1991 Case Law Confirms That Punitive Damages Turn on the Defendant’s Mental State, Not the Severity of Its Conduct.**

Before the 1991 Act, courts routinely awarded punitive damages under § 1981 (which, like Title VII, prohibits intentional discrimination) where the defendant acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference,” without requiring proof that the conduct was independently “egregious.” In *Curtis Publ’g Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 157 (1967), the Court upheld a punitive-damages award against a publisher for publishing a false and defamatory story about a public figure, emphasizing that the jury could infer “reckless disregard” from the defendant’s conduct. The Court did not require proof that the conduct was independently “egregious.” *Id.* at 157-58.

Similarly, in *St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727, 731 (1968), the Court held that reckless disregard of the truth could support punitive damages, even where the defendant did not act with actual malice. The Court explained that punitive damages are appropriate where the defendant’s conduct reflects “a high degree of awareness of ... probable falsity.” *Id.* at 731. Again, the focus was on the defendant’s mental state, not the severity of its conduct.

These cases confirm that punitive damages are not reserved for the most extreme factual scenarios. They attach where the defendant’s wrongdoing reflects a sufficiently blameworthy mental state—i.e., “malice” or “reckless indifference.” *See* § 1981a(b)(1). The absence of an independent “egregiousness” requirement is consistent with this Court’s longstanding precedents.

**IV. The District Court Properly Concluded That Petitioner Failed to Demonstrate “Malice” or “Reckless Indifference.”**

The District Court correctly held that petitioner failed to demonstrate that the ADA acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to her federally protected rights. The evidence showed that the ADA’s decisionmakers considered legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for promoting Spangler over Kolstad—namely, Spangler’s greater experience in health care reform and legislative drafting. *See Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 912 F. Supp. 13, 14 (D.D.C. 1996). The fact that the jury rejected these reasons as pretextual does not transform the ADA’s conduct into “malice” or “reckless indifference.” *See* § 1981a(b)(1); *H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I)*, at 55.

This Court has long held that punitive damages are not available for mere errors in judgment or negligence. *See Woodworth*, 13 How. at 371; *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107. The ADA’s conduct, even if discriminatory, did not evince the requisite mental state for punitive damages. The District Court therefore properly denied petitioner’s punitive-damages claim.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The ADA did not act with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to petitioner’s federally protected rights, and the District Court properly denied her punitive-damages claim.
