**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in concluding that the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the '566 application under 35 U.S.C. § 120. That determination turns on whether the intervening applications—specifically the '838 and '768 applications—sufficiently incorporated the '579 application by reference such that the chain of continuity was preserved. The Board’s conclusion rests on a fundamental misinterpretation of the law governing incorporation by reference and the requirements for claiming benefit under § 120. As this court held in *Harari I*, the proper standard is whether the identity of the incorporated reference is clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented. 602 F.3d at 1352–53. Under that standard, the Board failed to apply the correct legal framework and instead engaged in a rigid, form-over-substance analysis that disregards the purpose of the patent system to promote clarity and fairness in disclosure.

The key issue is not whether the '579 application was “filed on the same day” as the '880 application—clearly it was not—but whether the '838 and '768 applications, which were part of the chain leading to the '880 application, incorporated the '579 application by reference with sufficient clarity to satisfy § 120. The Board’s conclusion that the '838 and '768 applications failed to incorporate the '579 application rests on a misreading of *In re Narkis*, 869 F.2d 1454 (Fed. Cir. 1989), which stands for the proposition that “a reference incorporated by reference must be clearly identified in the specification.” Id. at 1457. However, *Narkis* does not require that the incorporated reference be identified in the same manner as it was in the original application; rather, it requires that the reference be “clearly identified” to a reasonable examiner. Id. The critical inquiry is not form, but whether the disclosure, taken as a whole, enables a reasonable examiner to understand what is being incorporated.

Here, the '838 application was a divisional of the '566 application and included the same incorporation statement from the '566 application, which explicitly referenced the '579 application by serial number and filing date. J.A. 1204. The '768 application, a continuation of the '838 application, incorporated the '838 application by reference and thus inherited the incorporation statement. The '708 application, a continuation of the '768 application, likewise incorporated the '768 application and thus carried forward the incorporation. The '880 application, a continuation of the '708 application, included the same incorporation language, now updated in the preliminary amendment to reflect the correct serial number and filing date of the '579 application. The transmittal sheet and the preliminary amendment, when viewed together, make clear that the applicant intended to incorporate the '579 application and that the reference was not ambiguous.

The Board’s contrary conclusion—that the '838 and '768 applications failed to incorporate the '579 application because they did not independently identify it by serial number and filing date—ignores the well-established principle that a continuation application may incorporate by reference material from a prior application, even if the prior application’s incorporation statement is not repeated verbatim. See *In re Rinehart*, 531 F.2d 1048, 1053 (C.C.P.A. 1976) (“A continuation application may incorporate by reference material from a prior application, even if the prior application’s incorporation statement is not repeated.”). The purpose of incorporation by reference is to avoid redundancy and to allow applicants to rely on prior disclosures without reiterating them. To require each application in a chain to independently identify every incorporated reference would defeat that purpose and impose an unreasonable burden on applicants.

Moreover, the Board’s approach is inconsistent with *Harari I*, where this court emphasized that the test is whether the identity of the incorporated reference is clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented. 602 F.3d at 1352–53. The transmittal sheet accompanying the '880 application explicitly identified the application as a continuation of the '708 application, which itself was a continuation of the '768 application, which was a continuation of the '838 application, which was a divisional of the '566 application. J.A. 1204. The preliminary amendment further clarified that the incorporation statement referred to the '579 application by serial number and filing date. A reasonable examiner, reviewing the entire record, would have understood that the '579 application was intended to be incorporated by reference.

The Board’s decision undermines the predictability and stability of the patent system. It creates a trap for applicants who rely on the doctrine of incorporation by reference to streamline their disclosures. If each application in a chain must independently identify every incorporated reference, applicants will be forced to repeat lengthy disclosures, increasing costs and reducing clarity. This is contrary to the policy of the Patent Act, which encourages efficient and clear disclosure.

For these reasons, the Board erred in concluding that the '838 and '768 applications failed to incorporate the '579 application by reference. The chain of continuity was preserved, and the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the April 13, 1989 filing date of the '566 application. The judgment of the Board must be reversed.
