The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law as surrogate federal law on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit's contrary holding, which allows state law to apply whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law, is inconsistent with the OCSLA's text and the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the statute.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the OCS "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "applicable" in this context, but the Fifth Circuit has held that state law is "applicable" only when there is a gap or void in federal law. See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). This interpretation is consistent with the OCSLA's purpose of providing a comprehensive body of law for the OCS.

The Ninth Circuit erred in rejecting the "gap-filling" requirement and holding that state law applies as long as it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. See Newton v. Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd., No. 17-55580 (9th Cir. 2019). This interpretation would allow state law to supplant federal law on the OCS, even when federal law provides a comprehensive scheme, like the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs wage and hour claims, leaving no room for state law to fill gaps. See 29 U.S.C. § 201, et seq.

The Ninth Circuit's holding is also inconsistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969). While Rodrigue held that state law could apply on the OCS, it did so in the context of a gap in federal law. The Death on the High Seas Act (High Seas Act) did not provide a remedy for the survivors of workers who died on fixed drilling platforms, and the OCSLA filled this gap by incorporating state law. See Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 366.

In contrast, the FLSA provides a comprehensive scheme for wage and hour claims, and California's wage and hour laws are not necessary to fill a gap in federal law. The district court correctly held that Newton's claims under California law were preempted by the FLSA. See Newton v. Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd., No. CV 15-9021 (C.D. Cal. 2017).

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's judgment should be reinstated. The OCSLA permits the application of state law on the OCS only when there is a gap in federal law, and the FLSA provides a comprehensive scheme that governs wage and hour claims.
