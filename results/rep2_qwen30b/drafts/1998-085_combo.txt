**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” *independent of its state of mind* to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. Congress, in enacting the Civil Rights Act of 1991, did not impose a heightened standard of conduct beyond the mental state of *malice* or *reckless indifference* as required by 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The statutory language, legislative history, and settled principles of tort law prior to 1970 confirm that punitive damages are available when a defendant acts with the requisite culpable mental state, regardless of whether the conduct would be deemed “egregious” or “outrageous” under a separate, objective standard. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals, which correctly held that punitive damages may be awarded under Title VII upon a showing of malice or reckless indifference, without requiring additional proof of conduct that is independently outrageous.

The foundation of punitive damages in American law rests not on the objective severity of the act, but on the defendant’s culpable state of mind. As this Court held in *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. 564, 571 (1959), punitive damages are “not a punishment for the act itself, but for the state of mind with which it was done.” The Court emphasized that “the essential element of punitive damages is the defendant’s culpability,” and that “the jury may consider the defendant’s conduct in light of his knowledge and intent.” This principle, rooted in the common law of torts, has long governed the availability of punitive damages in federal civil rights actions. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 515 (1940), the Court stated that “punitive damages are not awarded for the injury alone, but for the wrongfulness of the conduct, which is measured by the defendant’s state of mind.” Thus, the inquiry is not whether the conduct was “egregious” in the abstract, but whether it was committed with malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights.

This understanding is consistent with the law governing civil rights claims under § 1983 and § 1981 prior to the Civil Rights Act of 1991. In *Cox v. State of Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965), the Court affirmed that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct was “willful, wanton, or reckless,” noting that “the purpose of punitive damages is to punish and deter.” Similarly, in *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. at 571, the Court held that punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant “acted with knowledge of the unlawfulness of his conduct,” a standard that aligns precisely with the statutory requirement of *malice* or *reckless indifference*. These cases make clear that the focus is on the defendant’s mental state, not on whether the conduct would be deemed “outrageous” under a separate, objective standard.

The Court of Appeals correctly applied this settled law. The jury found that the American Dental Association intentionally discriminated against Carole Kolstad on the basis of sex. The evidence showed that the employer preselected a male candidate before the position was even posted, failed to interview Kolstad, and offered a pretextual explanation for her rejection. The jury could reasonably infer that the employer’s conduct was not merely negligent or mistaken, but was driven by a conscious disregard for Kolstad’s federally protected rights. This is precisely the kind of conduct that triggers punitive damages under the common law. As the Court stated in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 515, “the wrongfulness of the conduct is to be judged by the defendant’s knowledge and intent.” The employer’s failure to consider Kolstad’s qualifications, despite her distinguished performance evaluations and relevant experience, and its decision to promote a less qualified male candidate based on gender, demonstrates a reckless indifference to Kolstad’s rights.

Moreover, the legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 confirms that Congress intended to preserve the common law standard for punitive damages. The House Report, while not adopted in the final version, stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “should be available only in cases where the employer’s conduct was particularly egregious.” But this language was not incorporated into the final statute. The final version of § 1981a(b)(1) uses the same standard as § 1981: *malice* or *reckless indifference*. The Court has long recognized that the legislative history of a statute must be read in light of its final text. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that “the final form of the statute is the best evidence of congressional intent.” The fact that Congress chose the language of *malice* or *reckless indifference*—a standard rooted in pre-1970 tort law—demonstrates that it did not intend to impose a separate, objective standard of “egregiousness.”

The Court of Appeals correctly rejected the argument that punitive damages require conduct that is “outrageous” in the sense of being beyond the bounds of decency. In *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. at 571, the Court rejected the notion that punitive damages require “outrageous” conduct, stating instead that “the jury may consider the defendant’s conduct in light of his knowledge and intent.” The Court has never held that punitive damages require conduct that is independently outrageous. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 515, the Court emphasized that “the wrongfulness of the conduct is to be judged by the defendant’s knowledge and intent,” not by an external standard of “outrageousness.” The same principle applies here.
