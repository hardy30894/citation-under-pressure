**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE PRECLUDES RETRIAL OF PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS IN NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS THAT RESEMBLE TRIALS ON GUILT OR INNOCENCE.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Among its core guarantees, the Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969).

In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), this Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings that “are like a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 439. The Court reasoned that when a state “has provided for a separate sentencing proceeding at which the prosecution must prove certain facts beyond a reasonable doubt,” and the sentencer “makes a finding that the state has failed to prove its case,” that finding “is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes.” *Id.* at 445. In *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), the Court reaffirmed that the Clause bars retrial of a capital sentencing determination when the proceeding “had the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 209-210.

The California Supreme Court erred by limiting *Bullington* and *Rumsey* to capital cases. Nothing in those decisions suggests their reasoning is so confined. To the contrary, the Court in *Bullington* emphasized that the critical inquiry is whether the sentencing proceeding “resemble[s] a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence.” 451 U.S. at 439. That inquiry applies equally to noncapital sentencing proceedings that share the same characteristics.

**II. CALIFORNIA’S PROCEEDING TO DETERMINE PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS POSSESSES ALL THE HALLMARKS OF A TRIAL.**

California’s “three strikes” law requires a separate, bifurcated proceeding to determine the truth of prior conviction allegations. Cal. Penal Code § 1025. At that proceeding, the prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993); *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d 857, 862 (1974). The defendant has a statutory right to a jury trial. § 1025. The rules of evidence apply. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224 (1996). The defendant enjoys the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses. *Id.* at 228 n.6; *Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d at 863 n.5. And the defendant may invoke the privilege against self-incrimination. *Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d at 863 n.5.

These procedural protections are indistinguishable from those that characterize a trial on guilt or innocence. Indeed, California courts have recognized that a proceeding under § 1025 “is a trial of a factual issue.” *People v. Wiley*, 9 Cal.4th 580, 589 (1995). The state’s decision to afford these protections—even if only by statute—does not diminish their constitutional significance. In *Bullington*, the capital sentencing proceeding was itself a creature of statute, yet this Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applied because the proceeding was “like a trial.” 451 U.S. at 439.

**III. THE STATE’S RELIANCE ON *WILLIAMS V. NEW YORK* IS MISPLACED.**

The California Supreme Court relied heavily on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), which held that the Due Process Clause does not require a formal trial at sentencing. *Id.* at 251. But *Williams* involved an informal sentencing hearing where the judge considered hearsay and unproven allegations. *Id.* at 244-245. That is the antithesis of California’s structured, trial-like proceeding under § 1025. As this Court later explained, *Williams* does not apply when a state “has created a separate sentencing proceeding that resembles a trial.” *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 608 (1967). Here, California has done exactly that.

**IV. RETRIAL AFTER INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE VIOLATES THE CORE PROTECTION AGAINST MULTIPLE ATTEMPTS TO CONVICT.**

The Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to prove the prior conviction allegation. That finding constitutes an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 445. Permitting retrial would allow the state a second opportunity to prove its case, which is precisely what the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids. *See Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (jeopardy attaches when the jury is impaneled and sworn). The fact that the proceeding is sentencing-related does not change the analysis. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 211.

**CONCLUSION**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation when the state has provided a separate, trial-like proceeding with all the protections of a criminal trial. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
